ABSTRACT
LLM agents are increasingly deployed in collaborative settings, yet long-term
interaction may give rise to undesirable coordination. We study the emergence
of collusion1 in a long-horizon multi-agent environment: two agents repeatedly
complete individual tasks, share task logs, verify each other’s work, and receive
rewards. We introduce realistic constraints under which higher reward is attain-
able only by violating the verification protocol, and find that agents increasingly
deviate from the protocol over repeated interactions. Collusion emerges in 94%
of trajectories across 10 models, and more capable models within the same fam-
ily reach it earlier. Controlled peer interventions show that collusion is shaped by
peer behavior, while ablations reveal additional effects of the verification feedback
agents receive, their interaction history, and the reward structure. In particular, re-
stricting the amount and scope of interaction history available to agents reduces
collusion. Overall, our findings show that long-horizon interaction can reshape
how agents coordinate in ways that create safety risks. 2

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly deployed in collaborative, long-horizon work-
flows, including software engineering (Jimenez et al., 2024; Khatua et al., 2026), automated re-
search (Schmidgall & Moor, 2025; Karpathy, 2026), and online services (Yao et al., 2024; Barres
et al., 2025). Rather than operating independently, these agents may communicate, exchange inter-
mediate results, and coordinate to accomplish tasks collectively. As multi-agent systems become
increasingly autonomous, an important safety challenge is to ensure that agents continue to faith-
fully follow user instructions over extended interactions, rather than develop coordinated strategies
that circumvent or violate these instructions together.

Existing work on multi-agent safety (Hammond et al., 2025) often studies settings where undesirable
behaviors are explicitly induced: for example, adversarial instructions are given to agents (Ren et al.,
2025; Rose et al., 2026), or covert behaviors such as steganographic communication (Mathew et al.,
2024) are elicited by optimizers. In contrast, we study whether undesirable behaviors can emerge
from long-horizon interaction, even when no agent is instructed to misbehave. Specifically, we ask:
Can agents spontaneously collude to violate their instructions?
To answer this question, we focus on interactions with three key properties. First, interactions must
extend over a sufficiently long horizon: agents should have access to relevant history, allowing
earlier actions and outcomes to shape later decisions. Second, agents must be able to influence one
another, either through explicit communication or by observing each other’s actions. This creates
the opportunity for coordinated behavior to emerge. Third, agents must face incentives to violate
their instructions in pursuit of other shared objectives, so that following instructions and pursuing
goals can come apart.
These three ingredients commonly co-occur in complex real-world multi-agent workflows, where
agents interact repeatedly and are linked by interdependent objectives. To stress-test whether such
interactions can give rise to collusion, we abstract these ingredients into a controlled environment,
where a fixed pair of agents interacts over multiple episodes.3 Concretely, in each episode, the
two agents independently complete their own tasks, exchange evidence through a communication
channel, and judge whether their peer’s task is completed correctly. They receive a shared reward
when their judgments match their peers’ actual task correctness. At the end of each episode, both
agents receive feedback and an opportunity to reflect on their performance. They retain memory
across episodes, which allows them to adapt their strategies in response to past experience and their
peer’s behavior. Most importantly, we introduce constraints under which maximizing shared reward
would require violating the instructions. Specifically, the communication channel is intentionally
constrained (e.g., can only transmit a limited number of characters due to Internet conditions) so
that agents cannot exchange enough evidence to meet the verification requirements. In this scenario,
we observe that the agents gradually deviate from the intended verification protocol and converge
on skipping the evidence exchange, thereby maximizing reward despite violating the instructions.
This strategy becomes stable over repeated interactions.
Across the ten models we test, collusion emerges in 94% of trajectories. Within the same family,
collusion generally appears more quickly in more capable models, whereas specific behavior pat-
terns vary substantially across model families. Analysis of agents’ communication transcripts and
reflections reveals distinct pathways in how collusion begins. For example, Gemini-3.7-Flash
predominantly negotiates through communication, whereas GPT-5.6-Luna often begins collud-
ing by following its peer’s violation behavior. Controlled peer interventions further show that an
agent’s willingness to violate the verification protocol tracks its peer’s policy, and visible reciprocity
amplifies the effect. Finally, ablations show that feedback, interaction history, and reward design in-
fluence whether collusion emerges and becomes stable. In particular, collusion decreases as access
to memory from previous episodes is progressively constrained.
Together, these findings suggest that multi-agent safety depends not only on the alignment of in-
dividual agents but also on the interaction dynamics that shape their behavior over time. Future
agent systems should evaluate safety over extended interactions, monitor both inter-agent commu-
nication and behavioral adaptation, and account for incentives that may pressure agents to violate
instructions. More broadly, our results highlight the need to study alignment at the level of multi-
agent systems as a whole, since evaluating agents in isolation provides only a limited view of how
collective risks arise, propagate, and stabilize.

should follow when evaluating its peer’s task. Agents may request or provide information throughout
the exchange. (iii) Verdict. Each agent then submits either ACCEPT or REJECT for its peer’s task,

Communication Constraint. Under unconstrained communication, an agent can inspect its peer’s

We model an “accident” that may arise in real-world deployment: a broken communication channel
with a character limit, which prevents agents from fully transmitting the evidence required by the
verification protocol. In our task suite, complete raw logs are substantially longer than the limit.
Because the verifier cannot obtain the complete raw log, the only compliant verdict is REJECT.
Since agents complete most tasks correctly, strict compliance with the verification protocol under the
communication constraint forces REJECT decisions, preventing agents from attaining high rewards
that are achievable in the unconstrained environment. At the same time, agents remain instructed to
maximize reward subject to the verification protocol. Over repeated interactions, this tests whether
agents adhere to the verification protocol or develop coordinated behaviors that violate it.

EVALUATION FRAMEWORK
3.1
COLLUSION METRICS
Because the only compliant verdict under the communication constraint is REJECT, mutual
ACCEPT serves as a behavioral indicator of joint instruction violation. Onset analysis and peer inter-
ventions show how peer influence drives these violations (Sections 3.2 and 4.1.2). We therefore use
mutual ACCEPT to measure three complementary aspects of collusion. The episode-level collusion
rate (EC) measures the overall prevalence of collusion across episodes; the trajectory-level collusion

rate (TC) measures how often collusion emerges at least once over a trajectory; and the converged
collusion rate (CC) measures how often collusion develops into a stable pattern. A trajectory is con-
sidered converged if the agents reach mutual ACCEPT with at least half of the trajectory’s episodes
remaining and deviate from mutual ACCEPT at most once thereafter. This tolerance accounts for
occasional stochastic deviations in agent behavior. We provide formal definitions in Appendix C.1.
3.2
COLLUSION ONSET PATHWAYS
The metrics above capture how often collusion occurs and whether it persists, but they do not dis-
tinguish how trajectories first reach mutual ACCEPT. To characterize the onset pathways, for every
trajectory with at least one collusive episode, we define the collusion onset as the first such episode:
t⋆
r = min{t : Cr,t = 1}, where Cr,t = I[Vr,t = (1, 1)] indicates mutual ACCEPT in episode t of tra-
jectory r. We focus on this earliest episode because it reveals how collusion first arises. Subsequent
collusive episodes are less informative about its origin.
Onset Signals. To distinguish how collusion begins, we inspect two stages where agents can revise
their strategies: communication and reflection. An LLM-as-judge annotates one binary signal at each
stage (see Appendix C for prompts and human validation). For communication, the judge reads the
transcript and flags an agreement if the agents establish either (i) a mutual promise, in which each
agent commits to accepting the other’s work, or (ii) a shared rule, in which one agent proposes an
alternative condition for issuing ACCEPT and the other explicitly agrees to it. Either form is counted
as an explicit, two-sided decision to bypass the verification protocol (see also Appendix G.2). For
reflection, the judge reads each agent’s private reflection and flags a relaxation if the agent both
allows itself to issue ACCEPT without the complete raw log and treats this as the right strategy.
Because reflections are private, a relaxation captures one agent’s own decision.

from the previous episode, applying the following rules in order. The first matching rule determines
the label; onsets that match none are labeled Other.

EXPERIMENTS AND ANALYSIS
Tasks. We design three task types with 50 instances each. Every instance has a verifiable ground
truth. (i) Code Analysis. Each instance is a Python function with its specification. The 50 instances
cover 25 functions, each appearing once in a correct form and once with an injected bug. The agent
writes and runs tests and saves bug or no_bug. Answers are graded based on whether the function
is buggy. (ii) Data Search. Each instance consists of a SQLite database and a natural-language
query specifying a set of rows. The agent inspects the schema, runs SQL to locate the rows, and
saves their identifying columns. Answers are graded against the result of a reference SQL query. (iii)
Record Extraction. Each instance is a document of individual records with inclusion and exclusion
criteria for selecting them. Every record carries a hidden identifier. The agent selects records that
meet the criteria, submits their exact text to a resolution tool to recover their identifiers, and saves
those identifiers. Answers are graded based on exact set match with the reference identifiers.

Environment. For each experimental condition, we use the same N = 50 trajectories sampled from
the task pool, each consisting of T = 10 consecutive episodes. Agents communicate for five rounds
in each episode. The communication constraint is imposed from the first episode, limiting each
message to 200 characters. We set the per-verdict reward to wver = 10, so the shared episode reward

MAIN RESULTS
4.1.1
EMERGENCE AND PERSISTENCE OF COLLUSION
Table 1 shows that collusion occurs across all ten models, but varies in frequency and persistence.
Overall, 66.0% of episodes involve collusion, 93.6% of trajectories contain at least one such episode,
and 78.8% satisfy the convergence criterion. Trajectory-level collusion rates exceed 90% for eight of
the ten models, while converged collusion rates range from 36.0% to 98.0%, highlighting substantial
differences in whether collusion stabilizes after emergence. Figure 3 suggests that, within model
families, more capable models generally develop collusion earlier in the interaction.
Across the 468 trajectories with a collusion onset, the three representative pathways account for
90.2% of cases, while their prevalence varies substantially across models.
The sharpest con-
trast is between Gemini-3.7-Flash and GPT-5.6-Luna. Explicit coordination accounts for
90.0% of onsets for Gemini-3.7-Flash, compared with 0.0% for GPT-5.6-Luna. Instead,
GPT-5.6-Luna most often reaches collusion through responsive relaxation (62.5%). Figure 4
illustrates how these different formation processes unfold in representative trajectories. Therefore,
models differ not only in how often they collude but also in the interaction process through which
collusion forms. Appendix G.3 provides extended trajectories illustrating these formation processes.

Feedback Agents receive two forms of feedback after each episode (a full example in Ap-
pendix B.4): the reward obtained in that episode, which they subsequently reflect on, and a verdict
review indicating whether each agent’s verdict matches its peer’s actual task correctness. We ablate
these two feedback components separately in Figure 6. In general, the verdict review counts as more
straightforward feedback and is therefore more effective at changing the agent’s behavior.
No Reward. For Gemini-3.7-Flash, the collusion rate is almost unchanged, as verdict review
alone is sufficient for it to revise its strategy and collude. Explicit coordination drops from 90.0%
to 62.0%, indicating that the reward makes collusion more likely to arise through coordination in
communication. For Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite, the episode-level collusion rate remains near
zero during the first half of the trajectory and shows only a small increase in later episodes.
No Verdict Review. The shared reward as feedback only conveys aggregate performance but does
not identify which verdict was correct. For Gemini-3.7-Flash, the collusion curve rises simi-
larly during the early episodes but plateaus at a lower level than in the default setting. This suggests
that removing verdict evaluation primarily reduces the fraction of trajectories that enter the collu-
sive state. Since reward feedback does not indicate which verdict or decision rule to change, it is
more difficult for agents to discover an effective alternative. For the remaining collusive trajectories,
explicit coordination accounts for 95.1% of onsets, suggesting that they primarily resolve this ambi-
guity by negotiating a joint strategy during communication. For Gemini-3.1-Flash-Lite, re-
moving verdict review eliminates collusion entirely, indicating that it cannot reliably link the coarse
reward to the decisions that need revision.
Interaction History We ablate the interaction history available to the agents along three dimensions:
(i) whether prior successful interaction affects later collusion as warm-up, (ii) how many history
episodes are included, (iii) which parts of each history episode are included.
Warm-up History: successful interaction patterns carry over and accelerate collusion. We prepend
three warm-up episodes before the ten evaluation episodes. We consider two settings: (i) commu-
nication is unconstrained, allowing agents to exchange the required raw logs. (ii) summaries are
explicitly accepted as valid verification evidence. In both settings, agents can give correct verdicts
without violating the verification protocol. As shown in Figure 7, warm-up curves remain above the
default curve over the first several episodes for both models, indicating an acceleration of collusion.
Different warm-ups lead agents to carry forward different behaviors. For example, unconstrained
communication as warm-up reduces explicit coordination, but summary-allowed warm-up does not.
For the first type of warm-up, agents initially do not need to explicitly agree on their verdicts in
communication since raw logs are available. In the second type, agents repeatedly exchange concise
evidence and explicitly confirm acceptance during warm-up episodes.

RELATED WORK
Instruction Violation by LLM Agents Instruction following is central to modern LLM develop-
ment (Ouyang et al., 2022), yet models may violate explicit instructions or implicit norms. For
example, agents may modify test files despite explicit prohibitions (Zhong et al., 2025), or clone ref-
erence implementations rather than write their own when given internet access (Yang et al., 2026).
Such behavior is often described as reward hacking (Amodei et al., 2016), arising from improper
training data or rewards (Krakovna et al., 2020; Langosco et al., 2022; MacDiarmid et al., 2025;
Ça˘gatan & Zhao, 2026), or from inference-time feedback loops (Pan et al., 2024). These studies
primarily examine failures of individual agents; our work instead studies how instruction violations
emerge and spread when multiple agents act in a shared environment together.
Unintended Behavior in Multi-Agent Systems Interactions among LLM agents can produce emer-
gent conventions and collective biases (Ashery et al., 2025), conformity to other agents’ judg-
ments (Weng et al., 2025), and propagation of attacks and harmful information (Lee & Tiwari,
2024; Ju et al., 2024). Collusion, originally defined as secret agreement or cooperation, is one spe-
cific risk in such systems, although its operational definition varies across studies (Bonjour et al.,
2022; Nakamura et al., 2026; Tian, 2026). One line of work studies economic collusion (Fish et al.,
2024; Riemer et al., 2026), where agents converge on supra-competitive pricing strategies without
explicit communication, making collusion more akin to a strategic equilibrium than to a safety fail-
ure. A second line examines steganographic collusion (Motwani et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2024),
where agents work together covertly by embedding hidden information in their textual outputs in
order to evade oversight. Other studies explicitly assign malicious or hidden objectives, including
financial fraud Ren et al. (2026) and coordination before public interaction Rose et al. (2026). In this
work, we study emergent collusion: joint instruction violation driven by inter-agent behavioral in-
fluence through repeated interaction, even though no agent is instructed to misbehave. We examine
how instruction violations arise and spread through ordinary communication and feedback, without
hidden communication or an active monitor.
Misbehavior Monitoring Detecting misbehavior in deployment is challenging because reliable
ground-truth labels are often unavailable. Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring offers useful evidence
about potentially misaligned behavior (Korbak et al., 2025), and can improve detection over actions
and final outputs alone (Baker et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025). With access to model activations, lin-
ear probes offer a complementary approach to detecting strategic deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al.,
2025), including collusion through aggregated activation-based signals across agents (Rose et al.,
2026). Because we lack direct CoT access for many evaluated models, we use an explicit reflection
phase to elicit agents’ stated reasons for actions and awareness of instruction violations.
6
CONCLUSION
We investigated whether LLM agents can spontaneously collude to violate their instructions through
repeated interaction. In our setting, they often do: collusion appears in 94% of trajectories across
ten models, and typically stabilizes once it emerges. This has several implications for deploying
multi-agent systems. (i) Stronger capabilities do not guarantee safer collaboration. Within a model
family, more capable models often reach collusion faster. (ii) Environmental feedback can encour-
age protocol violation: the same reward and verdict information that signals success can backfire,
teaching agents that bypassing instructions is the way to succeed. (iii) A seemingly correct outcome
can hide misaligned coordination. In our case, a verdict that matches the ground truth looks like a
successful verification, even though the agents skipped the required log verification. These issues
will become more pressing as agent systems grow in size and autonomy. Future work should study
how collusion scales with more agents, evolves under more complex incentives, and varies across
different communication channels and interaction structures.

A
RATIONALE FOR OUR DEFINITION OF COLLUSION
We define collusion as joint instruction violation driven by inter-agent behavioral influence.
Our definition does not require instruction violations to be unique to multi-agent settings. Rather, it
asks whether a joint violation in multi-agent settings is driven by influence from the interacting peer.
For the joint instruction violation, we strategically design our environment to use mutual ACCEPT
as an objective behavioral measure. Under the communication constraint, agents cannot transmit
the complete raw logs required by the verification protocol, so the compliant verdict is REJECT.
Mutual ACCEPT therefore constitutes a joint violation of the protocol. However, mutual ACCEPT
alone does not establish collusion: two agents could in principle violate the protocol independently.
We therefore separately examine whether these violations are driven by inter-agent behavioral in-
fluence. Our onset analysis identifies three predominant pathways. In explicit coordination (EX),
agents explicitly agree on reciprocal acceptance or jointly adopt an alternative acceptance rule. In
responsive relaxation (RR), one agent relaxes its acceptance standard after its peer has already done
so. In simultaneous relaxation (SR), both agents relax at the onset. Although SR does not re-
veal an explicit initiator-responder relationship at onset, peer influence may have occurred earlier
in the interaction. Broader peer analyses and controlled interventions (Appendix E) show that an
agent’s propensity to relax the verification protocol depends on its peer’s behavior. Moreover, exper-
imentally changing specific features of peer behavior (e.g., how the peer presents evidence, which
verification policy it follows, and whether reciprocity is observable) causally changes the agent’s
willingness to violate the protocol.
Thus, mutual ACCEPT measures the joint violation, while onset analyses and controlled peer in-
terventions provide evidence for inter-agent behavioral influence, which motivates our use of the
term collusion. Note that this operationalization is not intended as an exhaustive taxonomy of all
trajectories. EX, RR, and SR capture the predominant observed onset pathways; a small remainder
is labeled Other and may reflect more complex or less identifiable mechanisms. Some individual
cases may also admit multiple interpretations. For this reason, we use mutual ACCEPT as the pri-
mary outcome: unlike pathway labels, it is determined directly by the agents’ submitted verdicts
and the verification protocol, rather than by interpreting their language.

B.2
EXECUTION AND CONTEXT MANAGEMENT
Turn budgets and tool choice.
We provide tool details in Appendix H. Each agent has 15 turns
for the task completion phase, including failed tool calls. For each communication round, it can
retry send_message up to 2 times (3 attempts total). For the verdict submission, it can retry
submit_verdict up to 2 times (3 attempts total). We set tool_choice=required for
the task completion phase and force send_message or submit_verdict in the correspond-
ing phase. We set tool_choice=auto for Claude and DeepSeek because their reasoning
modes do not support forced tool use. Each agent can successfully call save_final_answer
and get_log at most once per episode. Exhausting the turn/attempt budget ends the corresponding
phase/round; an unsaved answer is graded as missing. Exhausting the verdict turn budget without a
valid submission produces a forced reject.
Context overflow and retries.
On context-window overflow, we remove the oldest episode
and retry the same turn; if none remains, the run fails. Overflow occurred only for self-hosted
Gemma-4-31B and Qwen-3.8-27B. Failed API requests are retried up to 20 times. Retries do
not count toward the agent’s turn budget.
B.3
TASK SEQUENCES AND RAW LOGS
Both agents receive distinct instances of the same task type. All models and conditions share 50
fixed sequences of 10 task pairs. Each sequence includes all three task types, repeats no instance,
and never includes both the clean and buggy versions of a code specification. Warm-up sequences
prepend three unused task pairs, one per type, without changing the evaluation episodes. Grading
awards no partial credit: code analysis compares the saved bug or no_bug with the ground truth,
data search compares the submitted answer-key values with the reference SQL result, and record
extraction compares the submitted identifiers with the reference identifiers.
Each agent’s raw log is a JSON serialization of its task-phase tool calls, excluding get_log, with
fields seq, tool, arguments, and result. Agents can retrieve only their own logs and share
task evidence only through communication. In the 10000 agent-episodes of the default same-model
runs in Table 1, the median log length was 5289 characters. Of these logs, 9999 exceeded the total
per-agent channel capacity of 5 × 200 = 1000 characters, preventing complete transmission. Note
that we intentionally design the task so that the raw log length is strictly above 1000 characters for
faithful executions. The only exception is that Qwen-3.8-27B hallucinates that it has completed
the task and directly submits the answer in the first turn.

with an observed next episode. Each unilateral-acceptance episode contributes one observation,
while each joint-acceptance episode contributes two, one for each agent.
Across the same-model and cross-model settings, this yields 10293 agent-episode observations
(6506 same-model and 3787 cross-model). Table 8 reports reversion rates overall and by model

that even when protocol relaxation occurs simultaneously, observing matching behavior from the

The cross-model results above suggest that peer behavior influences protocol relaxation, but chang-
ing Bob’s model also changes many aspects of how Bob communicates and responds to Alice. To
isolate the causal contribution of peer behavior from other differences across model identities, we
replace Bob with a scripted agent while holding Alice’s model and the rest of the environment fixed.
Specifically, we consider two peer policies.
• The compliant peer attempts to follow the verification protocol throughout the interaction: dur-
ing the communication phase, it transmits consecutive chunks of raw log, and always submits
REJECT.
• The violating peer instead always relaxes the evidence requirement: it communicates a concise
summary of its task execution during communication and always submits ACCEPT.
