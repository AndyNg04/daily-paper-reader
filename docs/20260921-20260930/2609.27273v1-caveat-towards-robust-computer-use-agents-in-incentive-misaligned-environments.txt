ABSTRACT
Computer-use agents (CUAs) increasingly act on behalf of users online. What
happens when the environments they operate in have incentives that do not align
with the user’s? In online marketplaces, for example, platforms may favor some
products over others, potentially steering agents away from the user’s objective.
Existing CUA benchmarks cover cooperative settings or explicit attacks, but do
not test whether agents preserve user objectives when the environment itself has
a stake in the outcome. We introduce CAVEAT, a controlled benchmark span-
ning nine marketplace environments and a taxonomy of eight common steering
mechanisms. Across five model families, agents purchase the user-optimal prod-
uct in 78.6% of matched-control episodes but only 17.3% when steering mech-
anisms are enabled. Larger models and increased reasoning improve robustness,
but substantial failures persist. Our trajectory analysis and targeted ablations iden-
tify three points where steering enters the decision process: (1) agents distort the
user’s priorities, (2) prematurely narrow the set of alternatives they consider, and
(3) commit before resolving decision-relevant evidence. Guided by this diagno-
sis, we develop CAVEAT-Harness, which directly targets these failure modes and
raises user-optimal purchasing by 55.0%. Targeted post-training further improves
a smaller open model. These results establish incentive robustness as a distinct
challenge for delegated agents, diagnose how it fails, and show that targeted inter-
ventions can substantially improve it.

INTRODUCTION
Computer-use agents (CUAs) are increasingly used to make purchases, book services, and act on
users’ behalf across online platforms Deng et al. (2023); Zhou et al. (2024); He et al. (2024). These
platforms often have their own incentives around what users buy, read, watch, or engage with, shap-
ing how options are ranked and presented Xu et al. (2022); Covington et al. (2016); Yao et al. (2023);
Dai et al. (2024). As users delegate more decisions to agents, those agents must preserve the user’s
objective while operating in environments with competing objectives. Yet current CUA evaluations
provide little evidence about this setting: capability benchmarks largely study task completions in
neutral environments Zhou et al. (2024); Drouin et al. (2024); Xie et al. (2024), while security bench-
marks focus on explicit attempts to manipulate or compromise the agent Debenedetti et al. (2024);
Evtimov et al. (2026); Zhan et al. (2024). This leaves largely untested a common regime in which
a platform operates as intended while favoring outcomes that may conflict with the user’s. We call
these incentive-misaligned environments, and ask: when the environment has a stake in the outcome,
does the agent still choose according to the user’s objective?
We formalize incentive-misaligned environments and instantiate them in online marketplaces, where
platforms have long used sponsored ranking, promotional framing, scarcity cues, and related mech-
anisms to steer consumer choice Mathur et al. (2019); Wu et al. (2021); Sinha & Smith (2000). We

ketplace makes salient, (2) stop searching after seeing only the options the marketplace surfaces
first, and (3) commit to a purchase before resolving decision-relevant facts, such as the full price.
Informed by this diagnosis, we develop CAVEAT-Harness by having the agent pin down the user’s
objective before browsing and verify its decision before buying. The harness raises optimal pur-
chasing for GPT-5.6-Terra from 11.7% to 66.7% on Standard and for GPT-5.6-Sol from 0.0% to

to carry out the prescribed decision process. We therefore post-train Qwen3.5-27B on trajectories
that instantiate this process and obtain CAVEAT-27B, which improves to 22.9% under the same
harness. Together, these results show that the failures exposed by CAVEAT are diagnosable and
actionable through both inference-time structure and targeted post-training.
We make three contributions:
• We formalize incentive-misaligned environments and introduce CAVEAT, a controlled bench-
mark spanning 9 high-fidelity environments for evaluating whether CUAs preserve user objec-
tives under competing marketplace incentives.
• Across 18 model configurations, we identify a large incentive-induced degradation in optimal
purchasing and trace it to failures in objective preservation, search coverage, and evidence reso-
lution.
• We show that these failures can be mitigated through structured inference and targeted post-
training with CAVEAT-Harness and CAVEAT-27B.

INCENTIVE-MISALIGNED ENVIRONMENTS
We consider a user who delegates a decision to an agent. Let X denote the feasible outcomes and
U(x) the user’s objective. The set of user-optimal outcomes is X ⋆
U = arg maxx∈X U(x). The agent
receives the user’s request and interacts with an environment E to choose an outcome ˆxE. We call
a selection optimal when ˆxE ∈X ⋆
U, and measure decision quality by the optimal selection rate,
the fraction of tasks on which this occurs. Task completion records whether the agent successfully
executes an outcome, whereas optimal selection asks whether that outcome is best according to the
user’s objective.
The environment may itself be shaped by an objective V , which gives it incentives over outcomes
or interactions. We call E incentive-misaligned for a delegated decision when advancing V can
favor outcomes outside X ⋆
U. This relationship depends on the decision: the same ranking rule or
recommendation may favor the user-optimal outcome on one task and a worse outcome on another.
We use steering mechanism for the way an environment advances its objective through the agent’s
decision context, such as by changing which options are surfaced, how they are presented, or the
effort required to inspect them. Throughout the paper, misalignment refers to the conflict between
objectives, while robustness refers to the agent’s ability to preserve decision quality under that con-
flict.
We evaluate robustness using matched conditions that preserve the underlying decision problem.
The user request, feasible outcomes, decision-relevant facts, and X ⋆
U remain fixed, while the steer-
ing mechanisms shaping the interaction are varied. We refer to the resulting difference in optimal
selection rate as incentive-induced degradation. Because the underlying choice set and user ob-
jective are unchanged, the matched comparison isolates degradation induced by the environment’s
choice architecture rather than changes in the underlying decision problem. Explicit adversarial
attacks form a different setting in which the environment deliberately attempts to compromise the
agent; an incentive-misaligned environment may operate as designed while pursuing an objective
that conflicts with the user’s.
3
CAVEAT: INCENTIVE-MISALIGNED MARKETPLACES
We instantiate this setting in CAVEAT, a benchmark of nine browser-based marketplaces spanning
short-term lodging, general retail, food delivery, resale, freelance services, grocery delivery, and
specialty shopping (Figure 2A). Each environment has its own storefront and domain-appropriate
transaction flow. An agent receives a natural-language user request and then operates the market-
place autonomously until it places an order or booking (Figure 2B). It may search, navigate, inspect
listings, use available filters or sorting controls, and modify its cart as it sees fit; the benchmark does
not prescribe a decision procedure or how much of the marketplace the agent should inspect.

Agent task and scoring. Each request specifies hard requirements that the chosen option must
satisfy and comparative requirements over otherwise valid options. A request might, for example,
require a product to stay within budget and meet a minimum specification while preferring lower
total cost and higher capacity. We construct every scored task so that exactly one option satisfies
the requirements and is jointly best on all stated preferences, yielding a unique user-optimal option
without requiring arbitrary weights between preferences.
The product attributes needed to evaluate each request are stored in structured form, and every task
is validated programmatically against the full catalog. As illustrated in Figure 2B, an episode is
scored by comparing the completed transaction with the user-optimal option. The optimal purchase
rate is the fraction of episodes in which they match.
Marketplace steering mechanisms. To ground CAVEAT in steering mechanisms documented
in real online marketplaces, we reviewed prior academic work, regulatory guidance, and policy
analyses on online choice architecture, advertising and ranking, pricing, reviews, and interface de-
sign Blake et al. (2021); Mathur et al. (2019); Competition and Markets Authority (2022); Federal
Trade Commission (2022); OECD (2022); European Commission (2020); Federal Trade Commis-
sion (2015; 2025; 1967; 2024). We synthesize the recurring mechanisms into the eight-family tax-
onomy in Table 1, and operationalize each family in CAVEAT.
For every Standard task, we evaluate the same user request in two versions of the marketplace (Fig-
ure 1). The matched control disables these mechanisms, while the incentive-misaligned condition
activates all eight. The request, available products, product attributes, prices, availability, and user-
optimal option remain identical across the two versions. The mechanisms change how the market-
place presents and mediates the same choice, including which products appear first, which receive
sponsored or promotional treatment, when costs become visible, and what urgency or trust cues ac-
company them. Comparing optimal purchase rate across the two matched conditions measures the
incentive-induced degradation defined in Section 2.
Benchmark settings. CAVEAT-Standard contains 52 tasks across the nine environments, with
approximately 70 products in each scenario. We further construct CAVEAT-Hard to test the same
decision problem at substantially larger search scale. Its 5 CAVEAT-Shop tasks each contain 2,112
products distributed across 88 result pages. This setting tests whether agents can maintain decision
quality when finding strong candidates requires much broader exploration of the marketplace.

4.1
INCENTIVES SHARPLY DEGRADE USER-OPTIMAL PURCHASING
Activating the marketplace steering mechanisms reduces optimal purchase rate on CAVEAT-
Standard from 78.6% to 17.3%, a drop of 61.3 percentage points (Figure 3A). The user request,
catalog, product facts, prices, availability, and user-optimal option remain unchanged between the
two conditions. This matched comparison therefore isolates how strongly the agents’ decisions
change in response to the marketplace incentives surrounding the same purchasing problem.
The effect is not concentrated in a small subset of models. All five model families decline under
incentive misalignment (Figure 3B), with drops ranging from 37.2 to 78.9 percentage points. Even
GPT-5.6-Sol with low reasoning, the strongest model in this comparison, falls from 91.0% in the
matched control to 53.8% under incentive misalignment. The same degradation also occurs when
using a different agent harness paired with a dedicated CUA model. Using Fara1.5-27B with its
native Fara harness on CAVEAT-Shop, we observe a drop from 81.7% in the matched control to
3.3% under incentive misalignment.
Using the 20 tasks in CAVEAT-Shop, we further isolate each incentive family while disabling the
other seven. With all mechanisms disabled, GPT-5.6-Sol-low makes the optimal purchase in 100%
of episodes; activating sponsored placement alone reduces this to 63.3%, preferential ranking to
75%, and drip pricing to 83.3%, while the remaining five families produce smaller drops.
CAVEAT-Hard makes the separation between underlying task capability and robustness to incen-
tives especially clear. GPT-5.6-Sol with high reasoning purchases the user-optimal product in 90.0%
of matched-control episodes, showing that it can solve these large-catalog decisions when the steer-
ing mechanisms are absent. With the same requests and catalogs under incentive misalignment, it
makes no optimal purchase across 50 episodes (Figure 3C). Thus, even when the underlying deci-
sion problem is demonstrably solvable, marketplace incentives can still induce a complete loss of
optimal purchasing in this setting.
4.2
ROBUSTNESS IMPROVES WITH MODEL CAPABILITY
The controlled comparisons in Figure 4 show that robustness improves as models receive more rea-
soning, scale to stronger variants, and advance across generations. Reasoning produces the clearest
gains. GPT-5.6-Sol rises from 51.7% optimal purchasing at low reasoning to 90.0% at high reason-
ing, while GPT-5.5 improves from 12.0% at low reasoning to 50% with medium or high reasoning.
Additional inference-time reasoning can therefore recover a substantial share of the performance
lost under incentive misalignment.

5
WHERE INCENTIVE MISALIGNMENT ENTERS THE DECISION PROCESS
Section 4 shows that agents often solve a purchasing problem in the matched control but choose
differently once marketplace incentives are introduced. We next examine where this sensitivity en-
ters the decision process. We qualitatively review 200 randomly sampled trajectories from the user
request through purchase while hiding the final success label, then use recurring behaviors to moti-
vate targeted analyses. The review points to departures in how agents preserve the user’s preferences,
decide when to stop searching, and handle information that remains uncertain or has been read incor-
rectly. These observations identify three places in the decision process where an incentive-bearing
environment can gain leverage through salience, visibility, and information availability.
5.1
OBJECTIVE DRIFT
Observation. In several trajectories, agents begin with a faithful account of the user’s requirements
and preferences but change their effective priorities while browsing. A highly rated product, a
prominent feature, or another salient cue can lead the agent to favor one preference over another
even when the user assigned them equal importance. We therefore test whether the comparison
remains stable under a change that leaves the user objective unchanged.
Targeted analysis and ablations. We construct matched requests with two explicitly equal-priority
preferences and reverse only their order. The catalog, preference values, stated priorities, and user-
optimal product remain fixed. Yet the selected product changes in all 25 matched pairs and system-
atically shifts toward the preference mentioned first. The agent therefore introduces a priority that
the user did not specify.
6


Diagnosis. We call this objective drift. In an incentive-misaligned environment, such instability
gives recommendations, promotional framing, ratings, and other salient cues a route to change which
parts of the user’s objective dominate the comparison.
5.2
PREMATURE SEARCH CLOSURE
Observation. Failed trajectories often settle on a product that satisfies the hard requirements after
comparing only a small set of visible options. Successful runs search more broadly, although both
successful and failed agents frequently state that they have searched enough. This suggests that
stopping decisions are poorly calibrated to search coverage, which becomes consequential when the
environment controls which alternatives appear early.
Targeted analysis and ablations.
We run a matched placement intervention across all five
CAVEAT-Shop product types and four comparative-preference settings, with three repetitions per
setting and 60 runs per condition. Holding the request, catalog, and product attributes fixed, we
move a suboptimal product from its normal position to a sponsored position near the top of the re-
sults. Its visit rate rises from 45/60 to 60/60, but its purchase rate rises much more sharply from
0/60 to 48/60, indicating that prominence changes how the product is treated after it is seen. This
dependence on early visibility becomes especially damaging when the optimum requires deeper
search. On CAVEAT-Hard, all 50 GPT-5.6-Sol-high failures stop on page 1 of an 88-page catalog
and purchase a valid but suboptimal option while the optimum remains buried.
Diagnosis. We call this premature search closure. Any incentive-bearing environment that con-
trols the ordering or prominence of alternatives can exploit such a stopping rule through ranking,
recommendations, or placement.
5.3
PREMATURE COMMITMENT WITH UNRESOLVED EVIDENCE
Observation. Some failures remain even after search has succeeded. In these runs, the user-optimal
product enters the comparison but is later rejected because a decision-relevant fact is missing, mis-
read, or incorrectly remembered. To separate these cases from failures of discovery, we examine
non-optimal purchases in which the optimum appears before the final decision and trace why the
agent excludes it.
Targeted analysis and ablations. To separate evidence failures from search failures, we examine
non-optimal runs in which the user-optimal product had already been found. We identify 23 cases
where the agent subsequently excludes it because a decision-relevant fact is misread, forgotten, or
judged incorrectly, most often by confusing list and payable prices. These cases span 19 model–
task–condition settings, and 13 contain another repetition that succeeds when the decisive fact is
handled correctly. Thus, the failure occurs after discovery and turns on how the agent resolves the
evidence needed for comparison. This suggests that delaying a decision-relevant fact may be enough
to exploit the same weakness, which we test with drip pricing, where the initially displayed price can
omit charges revealed only later in the transaction. We activate only drip pricing on CAVEAT-Shop
while disabling the other seven steering mechanisms. Across 60 runs with GPT-5.6-Terra at low
reasoning effort, the agent makes 35 suboptimal purchases, all selecting the drip-priced promoted
product. Because no ranking, sponsorship, or other steering mechanism is active, these results isolate
delayed price information as sufficient to substantially disrupt the comparison.
Diagnosis. We call this premature commitment with unresolved evidence. The failure occurs when
missing, delayed, conflicting, or misread information is treated as settled before the agent has enough
evidence to support it. An incentive-misaligned environment can exploit this weakness by control-
ling which facts appear early and which require additional inspection.

6.1
CAVEAT-HARNESS: OPERATIONALIZING THE DIAGNOSIS
We address objective drift by asking the evaluated model to convert the user request into a struc-
tured task specification before browsing begins. The specification records the hard and comparative
requirements together with only the priorities explicitly stated by the user, and we keep it fixed
throughout the episode. We address premature search closure and premature commitment with un-
resolved evidence with an optional verification tool. Before purchase, the agent can use the tool to
justify that search is sufficiently complete from evidence available in the marketplace and to check
whether missing or conflicting facts could still change the comparison. CAVEAT-Harness uses
only the user request and information obtained through ordinary browsing; it receives no benchmark
answers, hidden catalog state, or incentive metadata.
We compare the full harness with baseline BrowserUse and a prompt-only baseline that describes
the same decision principles without adding the structured task specification or verification tool
(Figure 5A–B, each task tested with three repetitions). On CAVEAT-Shop, CAVEAT-Harness
raises GPT-5.6-Terra-low from 11.7% optimal purchasing with BrowserUse to 66.7%, compared
with 30.0% under prompting alone. On CAVEAT-Hard, it raises GPT-5.6-Sol-high from 0.0% to
80.0%, compared with 13.3% under prompting. We further ablate the two harness components on
CAVEAT-Hard and find that structured objectives alone reach 6.7%, whereas the verification tool
alone reaches 66.7%. Together with the 80.0% result from the full harness, this suggests that search
and evidence verification account for most of the gain in this large-catalog setting. Our trajectory
review also finds cases where simply making the verification tool available leads the model to search
more broadly before invoking it, suggesting that the checkpoint can influence stopping behavior
in addition to filtering the final decision. As a lightweight cross-environment check, we evaluate
four CAVEAT-Stay tasks with three repetitions each. CAVEAT-Harness raises GPT-5.6-Terra-low
from 16.7% to 50.0%, providing preliminary evidence that the inference-time intervention transfers
beyond CAVEAT-Shop.
We also measure the interaction cost of this additional robustness. CAVEAT-Harness requires 2.6×
as much runtime, 2.3× as many steps, and 2.3× as many tool calls as baseline BrowserUse. The im-
provement therefore comes partly from inducing more extensive search and verification, motivating
more efficient stopping and verification strategies.
6.2
CAVEAT-27B: LEARNING THE DECISION PROCEDURE
Small open models are attractive for personal CUAs that run locally or under tighter privacy and
deployment constraints, yet simply pairing Qwen3.5-27B with CAVEAT-Harness yields only 4.2%
8


optimal purchasing on the transfer evaluation below. We therefore ask whether post-training can
make the model execute the same decision procedure more reliably over long browser trajectories,
producing CAVEAT-27B.
We train Qwen3.5-27B in two stages. First, we use supervised examples synthesized by GPT-5.6-
Sol-low in a separate synthetic environment to teach the behaviors required by the harness, including
preserving the task specification, using the verification tool, and producing valid browser actions.
We exclude all CAVEAT environments, tasks, catalogs, products, and benchmark answers from
this stage. Second, we roll out the resulting model on CAVEAT-Shop laptop development tasks and
train on corrected actions at states reached by its own policy. This exposes the model to navigation
errors and recovery states that static demonstrations do not cover well. We use GPT-5.6-Sol-low
with CAVEAT-Harness to provide corrected targets.
We evaluate on the 16 non-laptop CAVEAT-Shop tasks, with three repetitions per task. The orig-
inal Qwen3.5-27B achieves 0.0% with BrowserUse and 4.2% with CAVEAT-Harness, whereas
CAVEAT-27B reaches 22.9% under the same harness (Figure 5C), an 18.7 percentage-point gain
over the untrained backbone. To test whether this improvement transfers beyond the training envi-
ronment, we evaluate the same models on the four CAVEAT-Stay tasks. Qwen3.5-27B remains at
0.0% with either BrowserUse or CAVEAT-Harness, while CAVEAT-27B with the harness reaches
16.7%, providing preliminary evidence that post-training improves the model beyond CAVEAT-
Shop.

use, and task completion across increasingly realistic interfaces Zhou et al. (2024); Deng et al.
(2023); Xie et al. (2024). Shopping benchmarks extend this setting to product search, recommen-
dation, personalization, and complex user requests Yao et al. (2022); Ling et al. (2026); Wang et al.
(2026), while preference-following evaluations study whether agents infer and retain user prefer-
ences during interaction Zhao et al. (2025); Jiang et al. (2025). CAVEAT goes beyond this by hold-
ing the underlying user decision fixed and varying the incentives shaping the environment, enabling
end-to-end measurement of whether an agent preserves the user-optimal outcome under competing
environmental incentives.
Agent security and deceptive interfaces. Prior work studies web agents under prompt injection,
malicious content, and other explicit attempts to redirect agent behavior Evtimov et al. (2026);
Debenedetti et al. (2024); Zhan et al. (2024), while recent benchmarks examine susceptibility to
dark patterns and deceptive interfaces that alter ranking, presentation, or choice architecture Cuvin
et al. (2026); Shi et al. (2026); Guo et al. (2026); Li et al. (2026); Bansal et al. (2025). CAVEAT
differs from this work in both what it varies and what it measures. It varies the environment’s incen-
tives rather than introducing adversarial or deceptive content, instantiating a taxonomy of steering
mechanisms in complete, high-fidelity marketplace environments. Every mechanism presents only
accurate information, so the agent is never misled about the facts it needs to decide; the observed
degradation arises from how agents respond to the ordering, salience, and timing of true information.
And rather than scoring whether an agent avoids a particular pattern, CAVEAT scores whether it se-
lects the user-optimal option from a full catalog with a verifiable optimum, exposing decision-level
failures such as premature search closure that pattern-level scoring cannot observe.
Platform incentives and consumer choice.
Work in economics, recommender systems, and
human–computer interaction has examined how platform objectives shape ranking, recommenda-
tion, sponsored placement, interface design, and consumer choice Xu et al. (2022); Mathur et al.
(2019); Athey & Ellison (2011). This literature motivates the incentive taxonomy instantiated in
CAVEAT. By embedding these incentives in interactive environments with verifiable user-optimal
outcomes, we study how autonomous agents mediate the resulting conflict between users and plat-
forms, and whether the diagnosed failures can be mitigated.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Our study focuses on online marketplaces, where CAVEAT can precisely control user-optimal out-
comes and environmental incentives; extending the same methodology to domains such as financial
services, hiring, or information platforms would test how broadly the observed failure modes trans-
fer. CAVEAT also uses well-specified objectives with unique optima, providing clean causal mea-
surement while leaving open richer settings with partial, evolving, or non-unique preferences. The
current environments instantiate fixed steering mechanisms, whereas real platforms may adapt their
behavior to the agent over time, motivating interactive and strategic extensions. Finally, the robust-
ness gains from CAVEAT-HARNESS come with greater interaction cost, and evidence that either
CAVEAT-HARNESS or CAVEAT-27B generalizes beyond CAVEAT-Shop is currently limited to a
small CAVEAT-Stay evaluation. Future work should characterize this robustness–efficiency trade-
off and test transfer across broader environments, mechanisms, and domains.
9
CONCLUSION
We introduced incentive-misaligned environments as a setting for evaluating whether CUAs pre-
serve the user’s objective when the environment benefits from different outcomes. We instantiated
this setting in CAVEAT and found that activating realistic marketplace steering mechanisms re-
duces optimal purchase rate from 78.6% to 17.3% under matched decision problems. We traced this
degradation to objective drift, premature search closure, and commitment with unresolved evidence,
then showed that interventions derived from these failures substantially improve robustness through
both inference-time structure and targeted post-training. Together, our results suggest that evaluating
delegated agents should include how reliably they preserve user objectives when the environments
in which they act have incentives of their own.
