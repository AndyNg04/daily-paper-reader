## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are increas-ingly used as collaborative assistants, yet domi-nant NLP evaluation practices remain centered on aggregate metrics such as accuracy and flu-ency. These approaches often overlook behav-iors that are critical in human-facing settings (e.g., consistency across multiple turns and it-erative refinement). In this paper, we examine limitations of current NLP evaluation practices and introduce TCR, a structured framework for evaluating human–AI interaction using ed-ucational LLM assistants as an illustrative ex-ample. TCR emphasizes dimensions such as transparency, consistency, and refinement. We further present structured evaluation prompts and illustrative interaction examples demon-strating how structured evaluation can comple-ment aggregate metrics and LLM-as-a-judge approaches. Our work highlights the need for more human-centered evaluation practices for interactive LLM systems. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly adopted in human-facing settings, yet their evalua-tion remains largely governed by general-purpose NLP benchmarks. While these benchmarks sup-port scalable and standardized comparison, they often provide limited insight into interactive be-haviors that emerge in real-world use(Ding and Magerko, 2026). This paper examines gaps in cur-rent LLM evaluation practices and introduces TCR (Transparency, Consistency, and Refinement) ,a structured framework for analyzing model be-haviors in multi-turn human–AI interaction. Using educational settings as an illustrative example, we discuss how structured evaluation may reveal limi-tations often overlooked by dominant NLP evalu-ation approaches and outline implications for the

We take the position that aggregate-centric evalua-tion may overlook critical behaviors in multi-turn interaction, highlighting the need for more inter-pretable behavioral evaluation. Second, we introduce TCR, a structured framework that sup-ports interaction-level evaluation beyond aggregate correctness metrics for interactive LLM systems. 

## 2 Current Model Evaluation 

2.1 Aggregate Evaluation 

Dominant NLP evaluation practices have tradition-ally emphasized correctness-oriented metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F-score measures (Hutchinson et al., 2022). These approaches pri-marily rely on reference-based metrics, such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) and ROUGE (Lin, 2004), as well as reference-free approaches in-cluding LLM-as-a-judge frameworks. While these methods support scalable and standardized com-parison, they remain limited for evaluating open-ended and human-facing generation tasks(Kashid and Bhattacharyya). LLM-as-a-judge frameworks have recently gained prominence due to their scalability(Wang et al., 2018, 2019). However, prior work shows that LLM judges exhibit systematic biases that can dis-tort evaluation outcomes (Li et al., 2025; Szyman-ski et al., 2025). In many cases, these approaches continue to prioritize surface-level fluency and pref-erence signals while providing limited insight into how interaction quality evolves across multi-turn and human-facing contexts. Benchmarks such as MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023) and Arena-Hard-Auto (Li et al., 2024) exemplify this trend by em-phasizing conversational performance while pro-viding limited insight into model behavior across

real-world interaction contexts. While single-turn evaluation has long been dom-inant, recent work shows that static benchmarks can obscure demographic biases and failures in AI-mediated interaction(Star, 2026), Recent work has increasingly explored multi-turn and long-horizon evaluation settings beyond static single-turn benchmarks. but often remain centered on preference modeling, or aggregate outcome opti-mization rather than broader human-centered in-teraction qualities (Chen et al., 2026; Abdulhai et al., 2026). Prior work further emphasizes that meaningful evaluation should account for broader contextual and human-centered considerations be-yond benchmark performance (Selbst et al., 2019; Mitchell et al., 2019; Raji et al., 2021). Together, these considerations highlight that meaningful eval-uation should account not only for performance outcomes, but also for the broader assumptions, behaviors, and consequences that emerge when language models are deployed in human-facing set-tings. 

2.2 Human-Facing Evaluation 

Prior research argues that evaluating machine learn-ing systems requires attention not only to model outputs, but also to human behavior, institutional practices, and deployment contexts (Ji et al., 2023; Hendrycks et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2024). Inter-active benchmarks such as Chatbot Arena (Chi-ang et al., 2024) introduce more dynamic evalua-tion settings, but often remain outcome-centric and rely heavily on aggregate success metrics. Human-facing LLM systems frequently require contex-tual guidance, iterative interaction, and adaptation across multiple turns. However, dominant evalu-ation approaches still emphasize correctness and fluency metrics, providing limited insight into in-teraction quality and evolving user context. Ed-ucational settings provide an illustrative example of these limitations because effective instructional support depends on iterative interaction, contex-tual guidance, and learner-centered communica-tion (Vygotsky and Cole, 1978; Kapur, 2008; Ding et al., 2025). Correctness alone provides limited in-sight into whether AI systems support understand-ing, refinement, or productive interaction over time (Shneiderman, 2022; Friedman et al., 2002). Re-cent work explores multi-turn tutoring evaluation and personalized interaction modeling in educa-tional AI. However, these approaches often remain centered on aggregate optimization and reward-based evaluation rather than structured analysis of interaction quality (Abdulhai et al., 2026; Wan et al., 2026). Educational settings therefore illus-trate why human-facing multi-turn interaction may require more interpretable and process-oriented evaluation approaches. Existing evaluation approaches for human-facing LLM systems are often either aggregate-centric or too coarse to capture model behaviors in authentic multi-turn settings. Unlike prior work that evaluates isolated usability or explainability factors, TCR focuses specifically on interaction-level behaviors emerging across multi-turn human– AI interaction. This position paper builds on a prior scoping review examining how AI agents are eval-uated in human-facing environments (Levac et al., 2010). The review analyzed 126 sources across HCI, AI, and education venues and identified recur-ring evaluation themes(Ding and Magerko, 2025). Rather than proposing a broad evaluation taxon-omy, this paper focuses on interaction quality in human-facing multi-turn LLM systems using edu-cation as an illustrative example. 

## 3 TCR Evaluation Framework 

3.1 Framework Overview 

We introduce TCR as a lightweight framework for evaluating model behaviors in multi-turn human-facing AI systems. Rather than focusing only on traditional aggregate correctness or fluency, TCR emphasizes how models support users through iter-ative interaction, contextual guidance, and refine-ment across turns. The framework focuses on be-haviors that are often overlooked in dominant NLP evaluation practices, particularly in settings where users rely on AI systems for explanation, clarifi-cation, and ongoing support. TCR also includes structured evaluation prompts designed to support more interpretable and process-oriented assessment practices. As shown in Table 1, TCR emphasizes dynamic multi-turn behaviors that are often over-looked by traditional evaluation approaches. 

3.1.1 Evaluation Dimensions 

TCR focuses on three behavioral dimensions that commonly emerge in human-facing multi-turn in-teraction: transparency, consistency, and refine-ment. These dimensions are presented as an inten-tionally scoped framework rather than an exhaus-tive taxonomy, while remaining extensible across additional domains and interaction contexts. Transparency Transparency refers to whether an agent communicates reasoning in clear and in-terpretable ways (Nauta et al., 2023; Arrieta et al., 2020; Guidotti and Ruggieri, 2019). In educational settings, explanations are part of the learning pro-cess rather than auxiliary outputs. Relevant indi-cators include reasoning clarity, traceability, and alignment between explanations and task outcomes (Silva et al., 2023; Bommasani et al., 2021; Brans-ford et al., 2000; Amershi et al., 2019; Radanliev, 2025; Kim et al., 2026). 

Consistency Consistency refers to the stabil-ity of system behavior across similar conditions (Nauta et al., 2023; Nielsen, 1995). While bench-marks often report aggregate scores, they may overlook behavioral variance across prompts, runs, or evaluators. Relevant indicators include out-put stability and sensitivity to prompt perturba-tions(Carvalho et al., 2019; Robnik-Šikonja and Bohanec, 2018; Vanden Abeele et al., 2012; Lu and Song, 2025; Shen et al., 2025). 

Refinement Refinement refers to an agent’s abil-ity to support iterative interaction through clarifica-tion, revision, and feedback over time (Wang et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2024). Unlike static single-turn evaluation, educational interaction is inherently it-erative. Relevant indicators include responsiveness to feedback and coherence across revisions(Guo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Nam et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2025). 4.1 Implication 1: Limitations in LLM-as-a-Judge Systems 

LLM-as-a-judge frameworks offer scalability and efficiency for automated evaluation. However, our analysis identifies limitations in their applicability to human-facing evaluation settings. Evaluating interaction quality often requires sensitivity to con-text, user goals, and iterative interaction patterns that cannot be reliably inferred through fluency-and similarity-based evaluation alone. In our illus-trative example, LLM judges may prioritize techni-cally correct responses while overlooking contex-tual misalignment, inconsistent behavior, or ineffec-tive refinement support. These patterns suggest that LLM-as-a-Judge Systems provide only a partial view of interaction quality and highlight the need for hybrid evaluation approaches that combine au-tomated auditing with structured human-centered assessment. 

4.2 Implication 2: Evaluating Multi-Turn Interaction 

Current evaluation approaches often collapse multi-turn interaction into aggregate correctness or pref-erence signals, masking behaviors that emerge through refinement, clarification, and contextual adaptation over time. While these approaches sup-port scalable comparison, they provide limited in-sight into how interactions evolve through dialogue, revision, and iterative refinement. These limita-tions highlight the sociotechnical nature of evalu-ation, which is shaped by human values, institu-tional expectations, and contextual interaction. As illustrated in our educational example, structure evaluation through structured evaluation prompts helps surface assumptions that are often hidden by aggregate evaluation practices and makes model behavior more transparent in interactive contexts. Together, these implications suggest that future model evaluation methods should incorporate con-textual evaluation, multi-turn consistency analysis, and hybrid human–AI assessment approaches. 

## 5 Limitations and Future Work 

While TCR is intentionally scoped in this work, future work may further validate and extend the framework through large-scale empirical studies and more accessible evaluation practices for diverse users and interaction contexts.
