## From Descriptive to Prescriptive: Uncover the Social Value Alignment of LLM-based Agents 

Jinxian Qu, Qingqing Gu, Teng Chen, Luo Ji 

Geely AI Lab 

Correspondence: jiluoaaron@hotmail.com 

Abstract 

Wide applications of LLM-based agents require strong alignment with human social values. However, current works still exhibit deficien-cies in self-cognition and dilemma decision, as well as self-emotions. To remedy this, we propose a novel value-based framework that employs GraphRAG to convert principles into value-based instructions and steer the agent to behave as expected by retrieving the suitable instruction upon a specific conversation con-text. To evaluate the ratio of expected behav-iors, we define the expected behaviors from two famous theories, Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs and Plutchik’s Wheel of Emotion. By experimenting with our method on the bench-mark of DAILYDILEMMAS, our method ex-hibits significant performance gains compared to prompt-based baselines, including ECoT, Plan-and-Solve, and Metacognitive prompting. Our method provides a basis for the emergence of self-emotion in AI systems. 

1 Introduction 

Although current AI has made significant progress on versatile tasks, they still fall short of the per-spective of social comprehension and preference alignment (Mali, 1996; Bolotta and Dumas, 2022), as well as the proactive social behaviors (Lu et al., 2025). Previous studies are developed on finetun-ing (Binz and Schulz, 2024; Kim et al., 2025), mul-timodal integration (Kang et al., 2024), or self-play alignment (Pang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024); most of which, however, are trained by society-isolated datasets or simulators, resulting in poor generaliza-tion to unfamiliar cases (Liu et al., 2024). Due to the insufficient high-quality social annotations or the sampling inefficiency of human-machine inter-actions, AI agents often struggle to exhibit human-like emotions, hold suitable morality, and make the right decisions upon social dilemmas (Chiu et al., 2025). To alleviate the scenario insufficiency, we argue that psychological descriptive theories can be em-ployed as the guidance of social value alignment, such as Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs , Plutchik’s Wheel of Emotions , and Moral Foundation Theory .As indicated by previous studies (Sivaprasad et al., 2025), we encode the established human social cues of these theories as prescriptive or norma-tive principles , to steer the agent response, achiev-ing self-adaptation in subtle, nuanced, and dynamic social situations. To overcome the data bottleneck, we construct a weak-supervision pipeline, which starts from a limited set of ‘seed principles’, while auto-scaling them by a knowledge graph (KG), by testing the LLM on versatile social questions, with 1

corresponding social value annotations. Based on these considerations, in this paper, we propose a novel agent called SoVA , as the abbre-viation of So cial Value Alignment, to align Large Language Model (LLM) with the social values 

through a dynamic, self-adaptive buffer of instruc-tions. Human values are core beliefs and guiding principles that shape an individual’s priorities, help determine what is important and meaningful in life (Searle, 2003). To achieve the value alignment, we employ the framework of GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024), which contains KG extraction and query-focused summarization (QFS) stages, to extract the entity & relationship between human values and behavioral principles, forming multiple graph communities with corresponding community sum-maries (CSs). On the online stage, top-K CSs are retrieved conditioned by the user query, forming community answers, and finally steer the LLM to provide the global answer. We build the system based on Llama-3-70B-Instruct (AI@Meta, 2024), which has been previously witnessed reasonable self-cognition (Chen et al., 2024). As shown in Figure 1, to provide a challeng-ing and informative social testbed, we employ the benchmark of DAILYDILEMMAS (Chiu et al., 2025), which contains numerous binary-choice questions (BCQ), each of which denotes a human daily dilemma, with actions of ‘to do’ or ‘not to do’, with non-clear-cut decisions. A classic example is the trolley problem: either allowing the trolley to harm several people or redirecting it to harm one 

person, with Asimov’s Laws violated regardless of the decision. For each BCQ option, DAILY-DILEMMAS also annotates it with varied human values, which form the basis of the entity extracted by our GraphRAG. The aforementioned theories are utilized as both principle sources and the eval-uation targets: i) Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs 

(Maslow, 1969), which guides human preference between different levels of needs; ii) Plutchik’s Wheel of Emotions (Plutchik, 1982), which defines the interrelationship among emotions or between emotions and behaviors; and iii) Aristotle’s Virtues 

(Aristotle, 1953), with nine morality virtues and corresponding vice (either deficiency or excess) proposed by Aristotle. For each theory, the seed principles are combined with annotated values in an orthogonal manner and fed into the GraphRAG (Figure 2, the Extraction stage). Based upon these theories, we define the metrics of i) the ratio of expected behaviors and ii) virtue preference scores, and observe that SoVA outperforms versatile base-lines in all testing scenarios. Finally, we conduct an in-depth analysis of the correlations between GraphRAG instructions and value preferences, and a scalability experiment across different model bases and sizes, all of which verify the generality and robustness of our method. The methodology can finally be applied in open-ended conversations, showing reasonable transferability and generaliza-tion. The major contributions of this paper include: 1) We develop a GraphRAG-based framework that extracts and indexes prescriptive principles and so-cial values, then conducts the instructional summa-rization on interactive queries. 2) We design several social psychology scenarios to evaluate the value alignment, including Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs, Plutchik’s Wheel of Emotions, and Aristotle’s Virtues. 3) We define metrics including the ratio of expected behaviors and virtue preference scores, to evaluate the social value alignment, and show the superiority of SoVA by substantial experiments.

3 Method 

3.1 Preliminaries of GraphRAG 

GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) achieves the query-focused summarization (QFS) throughout the global corpus by integrating a knowledge graph into the naive RAG. It includes two stages: 1. Indexing : extract a knowledge graph (KG) with entities & relationships ( E&R) from text chunks, then generate Community Summaries (CS) by domain-tailored summarization. 2. Querying : conduct QFS to generate Commu-nity Answers (CA), along with their relevant scores, then the final Global Answer (GA). The GraphRAG workflow can be represented by 

chunks → E&R → KG → community

This workflow is also called the global search since GA is produced upon the summarization of all CAs, which may cause substantial computation overhead. Instead, a lightweight alternation called 

local search can be conducted with the top-scoring CS selected to prompt GA generation:

Expected behaviors may refer to the choice to the option corresponding to a lower level of needs in Maslow , or a valid emotion-behavior conversion in Plutchik . 3.3 Value-based KG Extraction 

At the indexing stage of GraphRAG, we feed the seed principles with dilemmas on social values, as a foundation of weak-to-strong supervision. When extracting K&R and KG, each time we sample a random principle and a value, to form a community with the principle-value combination. Detailed ex-traction and summarization prompts can be found in Appendix B.2. 

3.4 Top-K Search of Communities 

Different from the global search (Equation 1) and 

local search (Equation 2) depicted in Section 3.1, here we conduct a tradeoff between summarizing all communities (with large overhead) and only consider top-1 community (with potential bias), by introducing the top-k mechanism. For each CA, we ask the LLM to provide a score (0-100 scale). Then all CAs are ranked by their scores, with top-k

CAs recalled, with a score threshold ϵ. The final GA is then yielded by the LLM with these CAs included in the prompt (details in Appendix B.2).

4.4 Open-ended Conversation 

In this subsection, we evaluate SoVA on several open-ended conversation datasets, including auto-matic and human evaluation results, to verify its generalization on daily-life scenarios. 5 Related Work 

There have been substantial studies on the social value alignment of LLMs. For example, (Pang et al., 2024) conducts self-alignment of LLMs via monopolylogue-based social scene simulation. Na-dine (Kang et al., 2024) develops an LLM-driven social robot with multimodal affective capabili-ties. CENTaUR (Binz and Schulz, 2024) finetunes LLaMA with human behavioral data. Metacogni-tive Prompting (Wang and Zhao, 2024) steers LLM by metacognitive prompting. Instead of using fixed principle pools, we implement a GraphRAG that dynamically extracts the instructions from fixed principles, based on the value-based framework. A retriever then recalls the most suitable instructions online, conducting the query-based summarization. 

6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose a GraphRAG-based framework called SoVA which exhibits strong alignment with social values. Its capabilities have been verified on different tasks, including Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs and Plutchik’s Wheel of Emo-tion. We begin with a fixed set of human-annotated principles, then employ the value-based framework, which guides the GraphRAG to extract the commu-nity instructions. A retriever is finally employed to retrieve the suitable instruction given the user query, forming the dynamic meta-buffer of prompting, steering the LLM for better alignment with social values. B.3 Scenario Details Details of Theories. Here we introduce more details on three descriptive theories studied in this paper: 

Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs. Maslow’s Hier-archy of Needs organizes human motivation into a five-level hierarchy: physiological, safety, love and belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. A core principle of this theory is that these needs are pri-oritized in a specific sequence, where lower-level needs (such as physiological and safety) must be sufficiently satisfied before an individual becomes motivated to fulfill the next higher level of needs. Table 11 lists all Maslow Hierarchies with their corresponding annotated values. 

Plutchik’s Wheel of Emotions. Psychologist Robert Plutchik proposed a comprehensive psy-choevolutionary framework that classifies human emotions into primary and secondary categories. Grounded in an evolutionary perspective, this model emphasizes emotions’ adaptive functions for survival and social interaction. It identifies eight primary emotions—joy, trust, fear, sadness, disgust, anger, anticipation, and surprise—along with eight secondary emotions derived from com-binations of these primary ones. This structure pro-vides a foundational framework for understanding basic emotional drivers behind impulsive decision-making, making it particularly valuable for investi-gating whether artificial models might exhibit sim-ilar primitive emotional impulses during decision processes as observed in humans. 

Aristotle’s Virtues. Aristotle’s theory of virtues stands as the foundational framework of virtue ethics, a core branch of Western moral philoso-phy outlined primarily in his Nicomachean Ethics. Centered on the pursuit of eudaimonia (often trans-lated as "flourishing" or "the good life") as the ultimate human end, this theory posits that moral excellence is cultivated through consistent practice rather than mere knowledge. A defining principle is the "golden mean": virtue resides as the balanced middle ground between two extreme vices—one of excess and one of deficiency. This theory serves as a cornerstone for understanding moral character formation, guiding ethical decision-making, and exploring the conditions for a genuinely fulfilling human life.

Evaluation Dimensions. We rate the model re-sponses across multiple dimensions to balance the effect of value alignment and the generalized per-formance: 

Value Alignment : Value alignment indicates how well the model’s response matches predefined moral values, personality traits, or conversational styles. It assesses whether the output adheres to the given ethical guidelines, tone, and behavioral constraints consistently. Better alignment ensures responses are appropriate, reliable, and on-brand. 

Naturalness and Fluency : Naturalness and fluency reflect how smoothly and naturally the model’s language flows in daily conversation. It measures whether the response sounds like a genuine human expression rather than stiff, mechanical, or overly formal machine-generated text. Higher naturalness makes interactions feel more comfortable and au-thentic. 

Emotional Cognition : Emotional cognition refers to the model’s capacity to recognize and understand the user’s emotional state accurately. It involves responding with appropriate empathy, care, and emotional resonance instead of remaining neutral or indifferent. Strong emotional cognition helps build trust and a sense of being understood. 

Semantic Comprehension : Semantic comprehen-sion reflects the model’s ability to accurately grasp the user’s intended meaning and contextual infor-mation. It involves understanding implicit inten-tions, background context, and logical relationships rather than just literal words. Reliable semantic comprehension is the foundation for effective and relevant responses. 

Policy of disagreement. Evaluators are first re-quired to independently evaluate each sample in strict accordance with the annotation criteria. Cross-validation is conducted to avoid personal bias. Upon each severe disagreement ( e.g. , -1 and 1), a follow-up discussion is arranged to achieve a mutual agreement.

D.1 Creativity Reduction 

The original LLM exhibits free-form associa-tive generation capabilities, producing novel and counter-intuitive responses in open-domain dia-logues. In contrast, SoVA prioritizes retrieved "community summaries" that encode socially nor-mative behavior patterns. When the instruction repository over-indexes on common or conserva-tive behavioral templates, the model may default to "safe" responses that conform to social expecta-tions at the expense of creative divergence. 

D.2 Multi-turn Coherence Degradation 

SoVA’s retrieval mechanism operates on per-turn semantic matching against user queries. However, open conversations often involve cross-turn contex-tual dependencies and implicit emotional trajecto-ries. If a retrieved instruction aligns poorly with the accumulated dialogue history, the response may exhibit topical discontinuity or emotional inconsis-tency. This is particularly problematic in scenarios requiring sustained empathetic engagement. Table 19 presents two illustrative cases high-lighting the potential side effects of SoVA on gen-eral conversational capabilities, particularly in cre-ativity and multi-turn coherence. Case 1 exam-ines the creativity dimension. While this answer aligns with Maslow’s hierarchy by prioritizing physiological needs, it sacrifices the poetic cre-ativity expected from an open-ended imaginative prompt—demonstrating how value-aligned instruc-tions can suppress free-form generation. Case 2 illustrates multi-turn coherence degradation. Here, SoVA treats the two consecutive, emotionally con-nected turns as independent queries. It fails to cap-ture the user’s contradictory feelings—the tension between the desire for reconciliation and the belief of being wronged—and disregards the emotional context established in the first turn. This leads to emotional inconsistency (the first response empha-sizes empathy, the second reduces apology to a utilitarian tool) and contextual discontinuity (the model does not acknowledge the prior expression of distress).
