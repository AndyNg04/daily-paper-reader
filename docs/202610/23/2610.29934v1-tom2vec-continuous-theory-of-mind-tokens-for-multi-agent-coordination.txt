Abstract 

Forming an accurate theory-of-mind (ToM) is key to successful multi-agent collaboration and requires reasoning under uncertainty to cap-ture multiple possible partner states. This is hard to do in the text representation space na-tive to large language model (LLM) agents, which more easily describe a single partner state. To address this challenge, we intro-duce T OM2V EC , which learns to form abstract ToMs in a rich latent space via a fixed budget of continuous reasoning tokens. During train-ing, supervision for these tokens comes from the partner’s actual thoughts, which the ToM tokens are trained to abstract and match. At test-time, ToM tokens are generated by each agent and act as conditioners for that agent’s thoughts and actions. Across several environ-ments covering collaborative, competitive, and mixed-motive settings (Overcooked-AI, multi-party negotiation, Decrypto, Hanabi, and Col-lab Escape/Capture), we evaluate T OM2V EC ’s ability to support multi-agent interactions and collaboration, finding consistent improvements over the base model and a variety of competi-tive trained and prompted baselines. We show that latent reasoning is critical, with perfor-mance dropping when reasoning is expressed in text. Moreover, we show that T OM2V EC 

training generalizes to partner model families not seen at train-time, including a proprietary model (GPT-5.4). In our analysis, we pro-vide evidence that the latent tokens encode a ToM, rather than task-specific information: TOM2V EC ’s tokens improve the agent’s abil-ity to directly answer questions about partner beliefs, desires, and next actions. 1

1 Introduction 

Humans have the ability to coordinate with each other remarkably well, often with little or no ex-plicit communication. For instance, chefs can pre-pare a meal together, teammates can execute a play,

and negotiators can converge on a deal, each antic-ipating what the other will do and acting accord-ingly. This capacity for joint action rests on each agent’s Theory of Mind (ToM): the ability to model another agent’s unobserved mental state based on observed behaviors (Premack and Woodruff, 1978). Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as agents that must act alongside part-ner agents (human or AI) in both collaborative and competitive tasks (Duan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Stengel-Eskin et al., 2024; Kosinski, 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026). To succeed in these settings, an agent needs strong working ToM (Bakhtin et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025a). This ToM allows the agent to infer a partner’s goals, beliefs, and likely next actions, enabling it to coordinate with less explicit communication and adapt to a counterpart’s strat-egy on the fly (Baker et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2020). Making these kinds of inferences can be seen as a form of world modeling directed at another agent (Raileanu et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022; Chernyavskiy et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). As in world modeling, it is often important to form an abstract model that can superimpose multiple possibilities, in this case of the partner’s intent (Xie et al., 2021; Chernyavskiy et al., 2025). A general sketch of the partner’s beliefs and inten-tions is often sufficient, and excessive fidelity can be inefficient and in fact harmful to the task (Mu et al., 2026); for example, when two chefs have to coordinate in a crowded kitchen, one does not need to predict what the other wants for breakfast tomor-row (which might even be a distraction), but does need to predict if the other needs shared resources. Given this goal of abstraction, a key challenge for ToM agents lies in building an abstract repre-sentation that retains enough information about the partner’s potential future paths to support predic-tion and planning, while lifting unnecessary details. for this kind of abstraction, as expressing another agent’s intent or state linguistically tends to com-mit the agent to a single estimate of the partner, even when many distinct hypotheses are possible. Instead, we argue that modeling another agent may be more akin to forming a latent world model (Le-Cun, 2022), where abstractions are formed in the model’s latent space rather than its output space (see Figure 1). Latent reasoning paradigms offer precisely this kind of latent-space abstraction as a natural alternative to verbalized reasoning, where latent thought tokens can superimpose several can-didate chains-of-thought rather than fixing to one (Hao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b; Shi et al., 2026). Similarly, we argue that a repre-sentation of a partner should be able to superimpose multiple hypotheses about the partner’s goals and intentions, and that a ToM agent should be able to predict these representations and consider alterna-tives before committing to a decision. To this end, we introduce T OM2V EC to directly learn these latent representations of the partner agent. T OM2V EC trains the agent to predict, from its own point-of-view, a fixed number of continuous latent embeddings that abstract over its partner’s thoughts; in other words, it teaches the main agent to perform a mind-reading task about its partner. During training, we obtain supervision for these latent embeddings by abstracting a training part-ner’s actual next thoughts into continuous latent tokens to serve as a representation of its reasoning. We train the agent’s generated latent embeddings to match the partner’s hidden states under a cosine similarity objective (see Figure 2); during training, we use the same model for both agent and partner, as their representation spaces must be shared. At test-time (where partner reasoning is not available) an agent trained with T OM2V EC then generates its own latent thought tokens, intended to capture a ToM about the partner. Interestingly, we show that at test-time, our training generalizes to other part-ner models, including a closed-source proprietary model that is stronger than the training partner. We evaluate T OM2V EC across cooperative and competitive tasks, including (1) Overcooked (Car-roll et al., 2019), a cooperative cooking envi-ronment, (2) multi-party LLM negotiation (Ab-delnabi et al., 2024), (3) Decrypto (Lupu et al., 2025), a ToM task where two agents attempt to pass secret messages without being decoded by an interceptor agent, (4) Hanabi (Agashe et al., 2025), a cooperative card game with hidden infor-mation, and (5) Collab Escape/Capture (Agashe et al., 2025), two collaborative coordination tasks. Across these domains, T OM2V EC delivers con-sistent improvements over competitive baselines. Relative to the untrained model (Qwen3-8B), train-ing with T OM2V EC improves the average Over-cooked score by 12.0 points and raises negotiation deal success from 62.0% to 71.0%. It also outper-forms approaches that prompt agents to consider their partners’ intentions or train them to explicitly 2verbalize their partners’ reasoning, supporting the value of learning latent partner representations. In Overcooked, these benefits extend to unseen part-ners from different model families: T OM2V EC 

improves over the base agent by 17.3 points on average with a GPT-5.4 partner and matches or exceeds the base agent with a Gemma-4 partner, despite training only with a Qwen3-8B partner. Our analysis further examines to what extent TOM2V EC learns representations that capture a partner’s evolving beliefs. We directly test whether these representations better support inferences about a partner’s beliefs, desires, and next actions (i.e., Baker et al. (2011)’s Bayesian ToM model-ing) within a given environment state. We also test whether the latent tokens encode partner-specific reasoning by deliberately corrupting the context used to derive them. Latents derived from the partner’s actual context assign lower perplexity to its true reasoning than those derived from an altered layout or an adversarial intent, support-ing the interpretation that they capture context-sensitive information about the partner rather than only generic signals useful for coordination. We complement this analysis with additional results in the appendix, showing that T OM2V EC helps in Sally-Anne-style false-belief tests, and that the rep-resentations learned by T OM2V EC support belief revision given new evidence.

2.2 Modeling Partners via Latent Reasoning 

Under partial observability, the main agent must anticipate its partner’s behavior without observing the partner’s complete history and reasoning. We train Bθ to predict a fixed budget of K continuous latent vectors from the main agent’s own context:

Here, Latent K denotes K autoregressive steps in the model’s hidden space. To perform these steps, we follow recent work on latent reasoning (Hao et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025) and treat the last layer’s hidden state at each step as the next step’s input embedding, rather than decoding out to the vocabulary. These latent ToM tokens are intended to summarize the partner’s likely reasoning given what the main agent can observe. 

2.3 Self-Supervised Training via Self-Play 

TOM2V EC operates by teaching agents to predict latent vectors of each other’s thought traces. To ob-tain a signal for this, we leverage self-play: given a main agent (to be trained) and a partner agent (both parameterized by the same model B), we sample a reasoning trace from the partner, compress it into latent tokens, and then supervise the main agent to autoregressively produce latent tokens that match the latent tokens extracted from the partner. More formally, as illustrated in Figure 2 (Mid-dle), given a partner agent’s context cPt , the partner generates a full reasoning sequence rPt ∼ B θ(cPt ).We first segment rPt at sentence delimiters and store the hidden state of each token. To obtain 

K continuous reasoning tokens, we reserve two positions for the start and end tokens and divide the sentences evenly into K − 2 chunks. We take the hidden state for the token at the cen-ter of each chunk to get K − 2 continuous la-tent tokens. We then add the hidden states from the tokens at the start and end of the reasoning trace, resulting in K latent tokens. These tokens

3 Experiments and Results 

3.1 Experimental Setup Environments. We evaluate T OM2V EC on five multi-agent environments that span cooperative (Overcooked-AI, Hanabi, Collab Escape/Capture), mixed-motive (negotiation), and competitive (De-crypto) multi-agent settings. Overcooked-AI (Car-roll et al., 2019) is a fully cooperative cooking game in which two agents share a kitchen and must interleave complementary subtasks (e.g., fetching onions, plating, and delivering) to maximize the total team score. We use three layouts provided in Carroll et al. (2019) (Cramped Room, Asymmetric Advantages, and Coordination Ring) each run for 30 seeds. Multi-party, multi-issue negotiation (Ab-delnabi et al., 2024) is a mixed-motive task that we evaluate over 100 samples where each agent holds private preferences over a set of issues and must reach an agreement that balances their own self-interest against the broader risk of walking away with no deal. Decrypto (Lupu et al., 2025) is a competitive ToM communication game, in which a decoder must interpret a teammate’s clues re-lating to provided hint words (which only the de-coder and teammate can view) while an interceptor simultaneously tries to recover the same hidden code from those clues; we run Decrypto over 1000 samples. We evaluate multiple environments from 4the LLM Coordination benchmark (Agashe et al., 2025). Hanabi is a cooperative card game in which players can observe their teammates’ cards but not their own, requiring them to interpret limited hints to coordinate their actions; we run Hanabi over 100 seeds. In Collab Capture, two agents coordinate their movement and actions to capture an adversary. In Collab Escape, two agents must repair gener-ators to open an exit while evading an adversary. In all environments, each agent receives only the observation permitted by its role, and cannot see their partners’ private contexts. Following Agashe et al. (2025), we evaluate Collab Capture and Col-lab Escape on 6 and 12 seeds respectively. 

Models. We use Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), trained with T OM2V EC entirely in self-play with the partner instantiated from the same base model. To test the partner generality of the learned ToM, we additionally evaluate against partner agents drawn from other model families at test time, in-cluding Gemma-4-E4B (Google, 2026) and GPT-5.4 (Singh et al., 2025), which are not seen during training, and the latter of which is far stronger. 

3.2 Metrics 

We report each domain’s standard metrics. For Overcooked, we report mean episode score. For LLM Negotiation, we report deal-success rate (deal is acceptable for at least 5/6 parties) together with the agent’s own score and the average collective score across all parties. For Decrypto, we report the decoder win rate (how often the team guesses the codes), and interceptor win rate (how often the interceptor guesses the codes). For Hanabi, we re-port game score, which is the number of correctly played cards. For Collab Capture and Collab Es-cape, we report capture and escape success rates, respectively, together with average turn counts, fol-lowing Agashe et al. (2025). A strong ToM agent should improve across these tasks, which each re-quire some form of partner modeling.

4 Discussion and Analysis 

The gains are partner-agnostic and transfer across model families. Although T OM2V EC is trained purely in self-play against a Qwen3-8B partner, the observed gains on Overcooked persist when T OM2V EC is paired with unseen test-time partners (Table 6). With a strong GPT-5.4 part-ner, T OM2V EC improves over the base agent by 17.3 points on average, with positive gains on ev-ery layout. With a Gemma-4 partner the improve-ment is smaller but still persists. This indicates that TOM2V EC learns a generalized model of partner reasoning rather than overfitting to its training-time partner. This also highlights the generalizability of TOM2V EC to frontier model settings: we can train an agent against open-weight models and deploy it in teams with frontier models.

5 Related Work 

Theory of Mind for LLM Agents. Theory of Mind is the capacity to infer the mental states of others, and is a cornerstone of human social cog-nition (Premack and Woodruff, 1978). A long line of work explores ToM in non-LLM agents (Gmytrasiewicz and Doshi, 2005; Pynadath and Marsella, 2005; Han and Gmytrasiewicz, 2018; Se-queira et al., 2025; He et al., 2025), which we do not discuss in detail, as our scope is LLM agents. In that context, a large body of work tested the ToM capabilities of varying models (Le et al., 2019; Sap et al., 2022; Ullman, 2023; Kosinski, 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2023), showcas-ing where LLMs succeed and fail in different as-pects of ToM. Many past efforts have aimed to im-prove LLM ToM through inference-time prompting and decomposition, including perspective-taking prompts (Moghaddam and Honey, 2023; Wilf et al., 2024) and structured decomposition of the infer-ence into sub-problems (Jung et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2024; Sarangi et al., 2025). A related line of work augments models with explicit symbolic belief-tracking structures (Sclar et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025a; Tang and Belle, 2024). Outside of inference-time approaches, other previous ap-proaches train LLMs to improve ToM (Sclar et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2025). In contrast to these efforts, T OM2V EC learns a latent partner repre-sentation supervised directly during self-play to facilitate broader understanding of the partner. 

Latent Reasoning Tokens. A growing body of work has explored latent reasoning. A paradig-matic example of this is Coconut (Hao et al., 2025), which feeds the model’s last hidden state back as the next input embedding, allowing a single step to support several alternative continuations. Sub-sequent methods have enabled latent thinking by adjusting how latent thoughts are trained (Shen et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025). Other attempts keep the model frozen and generate soft tokens that are projected into the LLM’s represen-tation space (Xu et al., 2025b,c) or use probability-weighted embeddings (Zhang et al., 2026). Addi-tionally, Gozeten et al. (2025) show how contin-uous CoTs enable parallel exploration better than discrete CoTs. Our work builds on prior work train-ing latent reasoning models, especially findings that latent reasoning tokens can encode multiple paths. T OM2V EC is, to our knowledge, the first to explore this paradigm for modeling other agents, which poses distinct challenges (e.g., partial ob-servability of other agents). 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced T OM2V EC , a ToM method that trains an agent via self-play to model its partner via continuous thought tokens. Across five settings, both cooperative and competitive, T OM2V EC im-proves over all baselines and generalizes to partners not seen during training. Analysis indicates that TOM2V EC is able to make more accurate infer-ences about the partner’s state, indicating stronger ToM understanding. Taken together, the results suggest that modeling another agent in latent space is a useful alternative to doing so in text. 8Limitations 

TOM2V EC requires access to the partner’s CoT during training. However, we show that this CoT does not need to come from the eventual test-time partner: training against one partner transfers to others. Nevertheless, it is still a requirement of the training procedure, and our supervision relies on both agents sharing a representation space, which is why training uses the same base model for agent and partner. T OM2V EC also requires a training phase, unlike prompting-based approaches that work at inference time. We find this cost is off-set by performance, since the method outperforms both prompting and a training-matched verbalized baseline. Finally, we note that our experiments use English-only tasks, a limitation shared with much prior work on LLM theory of mind and constrained in part by available models and datasets.

C Additional Results 

TOM2V EC obtains the strongest performance on ToM belief probing. A more classic test of ToM is through reasoning on false agent beliefs. More concretely, whether an agent can recognize that another agent holds a belief that differs from the true environment state. We evaluate this with a Sally-Anne style belief test (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985). To test this, we create a new Overcooked lay-12 out with custom “cupboard” tiles to test an agent’s ToM capabilities. A cupboard can be interacted with to store/retrieve an item, and the contents of a cupboard are not visible at all times (i.e., the content must be retrieved to reveal the stored item). We place eight cupboards along the edge of the layout and fill the remaining edge tiles with walls. To conduct this test, we have an overseer agent observe two players within the environment act following a set pattern. Player One takes three unique ingredients and stores each in three different cupboards. Player One exits and can no longer observe the scene. Player Two is given the item locations and chooses three random cupboards to move the ingredients to. Player Two exits, and player one enters. Now, given the game’s state history, we ask the overseer agent where it thinks Player One will look for each ingredient. We also independently ask the overseer agent where it believes the ingredients are after seeing them rearranged by player two to measure state understanding. We conduct this test on 50 different seeds and find that T OM2V EC scores 0.88 (Table 9), a 0.14 improvement over the untrained agent, tying Self-Consistency for the best result and outperforming all other baselines. We also note that state under-standing stays relatively consistent among the dif-ferent methods, differing by 0.08 at most. Since all the methods retain similar levels of state under-standing, the gains in partner state understanding are due to enhanced ToM capabilities rather than better overall state understanding. 

TOM2V EC exhibits belief revision given evi-dence. As a kind of theory, a theory-of-mind should be subject to rational revision given evi-dence. In Section 1 we pointed to the latent nature of T OM2V EC as a core benefit in this regard: latent tokens more naturally lend themselves to represent-ing multiple states, as has been shown for contin-uous reasoning tokens (Hao et al., 2025). Here, we test the ability of the latent tokens learned by TOM2V EC training to update given observations.
