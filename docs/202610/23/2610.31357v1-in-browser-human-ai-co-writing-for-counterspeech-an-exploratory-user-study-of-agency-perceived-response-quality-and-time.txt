# In-Browser Human-AI Co-Writing for Counterspeech: An Exploratory User Study of Agency, Perceived Response Quality, and Time 

## Tammy Schmidt and Luise Koch 

## Technical University of Munich 

> tammy.schmidt@tum.de, luise.koch@tum.de

## Abstract 

Writing counterspeech that challenges online hate speech can be cognitively and emotion-ally demanding, whereas fully automated gen-eration may weaken users’ sense of voice and responsibility. We present an in-browser AI-assisted co-writing tool that enables users to contribute their own ideas and offers three ed-itable suggestions generated by a large lan-guage model using dynamically retrieved hate speech–counterspeech examples from the Mul-titarget CONAN dataset. We evaluated the tool in an exploratory within-subject study with ten participants, complemented by think-aloud ob-servations and semi-structured interviews with half of the participants. Participants wrote re-sponses to three self-selected hate speech in-stances without assistance and then repeated the task with the tool; final responses were sub-sequently peer-rated for empathy, non-toxicity, relevance, specificity, and persuasiveness. Par-ticipants evaluated the interface positively, and qualitative accounts linked user input, selection among alternatives, and small edits to perceived agency and responsibility. Assisted responses received higher descriptive mean ratings across all five proxies, with the largest difference for empathy. Mean composition time increased from 2.52 to 3.88 minutes. Qualitative findings further revealed a tension between empathy-oriented outputs and users’ desire to express le-gitimate anger or direct disagreement. We con-tribute exploratory design guidance for treating AI-assisted counterspeech as deliberative co-writing rather than automated text production. 

## 1 Introduction 

Online hate speech (HS) is a persistent feature of digital communication that can harm both its direct targets and the wider communities exposed to it. Prior research links exposure to HS to psychologi-cal distress, reduced feelings of safety, and the nor-malization of hostile discourse (Cohen-Almagor, 2011; Amnesty Global Insights, 2017; Hinduja and Patchin, 2010). Platforms commonly respond through content removal or account sanctions, but such measures remain contested and do not enable users to participate constructively in addressing harmful content. Counterspeech (CS) offers an al-ternative by challenging hateful narratives through non-aggressive responses that support targets, en-courage reflection, and model more civil forms of online interaction for bystanders (Benesch, 2014; Schieb and Preuss, 2016; Baider, 2023). However, many users refrain from engaging because compos-ing a response can be time-consuming, emotionally demanding, and rhetorically difficult (Mun et al., 2024; Ping et al., 2025). Large language models (LLMs) can generate flu-ent and contextually relevant CS, particularly when adapted through prompting or fine-tuning (Do˘ ganç and Markov, 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). However, most work evaluates generated outputs or model configurations, leaving comparatively less evidence about how artificial intelligence (AI) should be em-bedded in interactive tools that support users with-out replacing their judgment, voice, or responsibil-ity (Mun et al., 2024; Ping et al., 2025). This inter-action question is important since a CS comment represents a public moral and social intervention. We address this gap through the design and exploratory evaluation of an in-browser CS co-writing assistant. The interface requires user input, presents three editable LLM-generated CS sugges-tions, and leaves selection, revision, and posting entirely with the user. We contribute: (1) an inter-action design that operationalizes user initiation, contribution, choice, and editing; (2) exploratory evidence on users’ experiences with the tool and descriptive differences between assisted and unas-sisted responses and subsequent peer evaluation; and (3) design implications concerning agency, rhetorical diversity, composition time, and the ten-sion between empathy-oriented safeguards and per-sonal voice. arXiv:2610.31357v1 [cs.SI] 22 Oct 2026 We examine the following research questions: 

RQ1 : How do users experience an in-browser AI-assisted CS co-writing interface in terms of usability, agency, perceived ownership, and personal voice? 

RQ2 : How do assisted and unassisted responses differ descriptively in peer-rated response-quality proxies and composition time? 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Counterspeech Strategies and Evaluation 

CS effectiveness can refer to different outcomes, including changing a hate speaker’s stance, de-escalating an exchange, supporting targets, encour-aging bystander intervention, or improving the tone of a wider discussion (Baider, 2023; Buerger, 2021). Research on pro-social example setting sug-gests that visible civil responses can influence sub-sequent participation (Han et al., 2018; Seering et al., 2017). However, strategy effects depend on context and audience. Constructive, empathetic, and non-hostile responses are often evaluated favor-ably (Baider, 2023; Fraser et al., 2023; Hangartner et al., 2021), whereas findings for humor, ques-tioning, and denunciation are mixed (Fraser et al., 2023; Benesch et al., 2016). AI assistance may help reduce the emotional burden of formulating CS after encountering HS and support users in craft-ing measured, well-formulated responses (Fraser et al., 2023; Ping et al., 2025). Accordingly, we treat empathy, non-toxicity, relevance, specificity, and persuasiveness as perceived linguistic qualities rather than as direct measures of real-world CS effectiveness. 

2.2 Automatic Counterspeech Generation 

Automated CS generation has been approached through fine-tuning (Tekiro˘ glu et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023; Zhu and Bhat, 2021), prompt engi-neering (Ashida and Komachi, 2022; Zheng et al., 2023; Lyu et al., 2025), and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Leekha et al., 2024; Chung et al., 2021b). No method is universally superior, although prompting large pretrained models has outperformed smaller fine-tuned models in some CS settings (Do˘ ganç and Markov, 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). In this context, and with the complementary goal of lowering computational requirements, we use retrieval-augmented few-shot prompting. Prior work used static Multitarget CONAN examples for in-context learning in CS generation tasks (Ashida and Komachi, 2022). The present system extends this approach by dynamically retrieving different examples for each prompt based on semantic similarity. This design may increase contextual alignment, but the present study focuses on human–AI interaction and does not independently test whether dynamic retrieval improves generation quality. 

2.3 Interactive Tools for Counterspeech 

Much CS research focuses on optimizing gener-ation models, while paying less attention to how users interact with AI assistance during the writ-ing process. Prior studies therefore argue that AI-based CS systems should support human judgment rather than operate as autonomous agents (Mun et al., 2024; Ping et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025). This assistive framing is important because users seek help with formulation, emotional regulation, and factual support, but also raise concerns about agency, authenticity, and responsibility (Mun et al., 2024). Two systems are particularly relevant. Chung et al. (2021a) support practitioners responding to Islamophobic HS by monitoring trends and re-trieving pre-written responses from the CONAN dataset, but their system does not generate new, input-specific CS. CounterQuill combines educa-tion, brainstorming, and AI-assisted co-writing to support skill development and perceived author-ship (Ding et al., 2025). Its multi-step workflow offers substantial guidance but requires users to leave the immediate context of the abusive con-tent. Our prototype explores a lower-friction al-ternative by placing co-writing support inside the browser and emphasizing user input, choice, and editing. Recent browser-based work has explored expert-grounded RAG support for German CS in moderation contexts (Fillies et al., 2026). Our pro-totype complements this line of work by studying an in-browser co-writing assistant for individual users, emphasizing substantive user input, choice among three editable LLM-generated suggestions, and perceived agency in the writing process. 3 System 

3.1 Interaction Design 

Drawing from the design space for intelligent writ-ing assistants proposed by Lee et al. (2024), the extension is designed as a co-writing tool rather than an autonomous CS generator. All interaction choices are intended to preserve users’ agency, per-ceived ownership, and responsibility for the final message, particularly through explicit system steer-ing, selection among alternatives, and post-editing. 

3.2 Ethics-by-Design 

Because the system processes hateful content and potentially sensitive user input, ethical consider-ations informed its design from the outset. The design process was guided by the Eight principles of ethical AI synthesized by the Berkman Klein Center for Internet & Society at Harvard University (Fjeld et al., 2020). In particular, the extension op-erates only upon explicit user initiation, minimizes the data it collects, and leaves decision-making power entirely with the user. Users are informed that their input is transferred to an externally hosted LLM and must consent before accessing the generator. Prompt-level safeguards aim to reduce harmful or toxic output, although reliance on a pretrained model and an external API limits the developers’ control over model behavior and downstream data processing. A detailed account of the technical and organizational measures is provided in Table A.1. 

3.3 System Architecture 

The system follows a modular client–server archi-tecture consisting of a browser-based frontend, a backend service, a retrieval module, and an externally hosted LLM. Figure 1 provides an overview of the system architecture. 

Frontend The frontend is implemented as a Man-ifest V3 browser extension with a compact pop-up interface. It contains two views: (1) the Generator window and (2) the Info page. In the Generator window, users enter the hateful comment and must provide their own ideas, word-ing, or personal perspective in a free-text field. This mandatory contribution is intended to ground the generated responses in users’ own views rather than encourage passive acceptance of fully automated text. Users additionally specify their responder role in relation to the HS target group (direct target, target-group member, or ally), select a writing style (familiar or formal), and choose an approximate re-sponse length (short, medium, or long). The system then presents three editable CS suggestions. Users can compare the alternatives, select one, revise the text, and copy the final response. Screenshots are provided in Figures A.1, A.2 and A.3. The Info page explains data processing, user accountability, and appropriate use. Users must acknowledge these conditions before accessing the Generator. The complete consent statements are shown in Figure A.4. 

Backend and Prompt Construction The backend is implemented in Python using FastAPI. For each request, it validates the submitted inputs, retrieves examples for in-context learning, constructs the model messages, and sends them to the LLM through the Groq API. The system message specifies the CS task, response-quality guidelines, and safeguards. The prompt message contains the HS instance, the user’s contribution and selected controls, the retrieved examples, and the required output format. Prompt templates are provided in Tables A.2 and A.3. The returned output is parsed into three separate suggestions and sent to the frontend. 

Example Retrieval The retrieval component uses the Multitarget CONAN dataset, which con-tains 5,003 HS-CS pairs covering eight target cate-gories: disabled people, Jewish people, LGBT+ people, migrants, Muslims, People of Color, women, and others (Fanton et al., 2021). It was selected because of its size, target-group coverage, and the expert review and post-editing involved in its construction. The HS instances are embedded using all-mpnet-base-v2 and stored in a FAISS vector index. At generation time, the submitted HS is embedded and matched against the index using k-nearest-neighbor search. The five most semantically similar HS-CS pairs are added to the prompt as dynamically selected in-context demonstrations. We fixed the number of demonstrations at five as an implementation choice; the effect of varying this parameter was not evaluated. Railway Cloud Platform

Large Language Model Integration The writ-ing assistant uses llama-3.3-70b-versatile , a model from the Llama 3 family (Grattafiori et al., 2024), accessed through the Groq API 1. The avail-ability of model documentation and open weights provides partial transparency, although the model remains externally hosted and was not modified in this study. The model was invoked with a temper-ature of 1.0, a generation setting that encourages more varied outputs, following prior CS generation work that used higher temperature settings to en-courage output diversity (Lyu et al., 2025; Do˘ ganç and Markov, 2023); provider defaults were used for all other parameters. Each prompt component was designed to play a distinct role. The user contribution was intended to ground the response in the user’s ideas or wording, while the interface controls specified the responder role, style, and approximate length. The retrieved examples served as semantically related in-context demonstrations. In contrast, the system message imposed default response-quality guidelines and safeguards. Importantly, the prompt’s priority or-der placed safeguards and default guidelines above user input, with retrieved examples receiving the lowest priority. These roles describe the intended prompt design rather than measured component effects. Because the components were not manipu-lated independently, the present study cannot deter-mine whether topical relevance arose from retrieval, prompt construction, or model tendencies.

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Study Design The user study used an exploratory, fixed-order within-subject design followed by a peer-rating comparison. Participants were pre-sented with the same set of eight HS instances from the Multitarget CONAN dataset, comprising one instance for each target category (see Table A.4). Instances were manually screened to ensure suitability and variation in hate intensity. Exact duplicates of the study stimuli were removed from the FAISS index to prevent exact-match retrieval during generation; closely related examples never-theless remained. In the first round, participants freely selected three out of the eight HS instances and manually wrote a CS response to each of them. In the second round, they responded to the same instances us-ing the browser extension for assistance. Holding the selected stimuli constant within participants en-abled a descriptive comparison of their assisted and unassisted responses, although the fixed order and repeated exposure may have introduced practice, memory, or demand effects. Because participants selected their own stimuli, instances were not repre-sented equally across the sample. After each unas-sisted response, participants rated their willingness to post the text publicly under their own identity. In the assisted condition, participants rated each final response on five exploratory items: self-identity, sense of agency, accountability, naturalness, and willingness to post. Willingness to post was there-fore the only item assessed in both conditions; the other four items specifically concerned participants’ relationship to AI-assisted co-written text. The study was conducted in two batches of five participants. The first batch additionally completed think-aloud observations during task completion and took part in a short semi-structured interview afterwards; the second batch completed the same writing and rating procedure without the additional qualitative component. No saturation criterion was applied, as this qualitative component was intended to provide illustrative rather than com-prehensive thematic coverage. The first author reviewed recordings, extracted relevant passages, and grouped them inductively into broad recurring themes. No predefined coding scheme or indepen-dent second coding was used. Qualitative observa-tions are therefore used to contextualize descrip-tive ratings and identify possible design implica-tions. Quotations were lightly edited to remove filler words or correct grammatical inconsistencies for improved readability while strictly preserving meaning. About a week later, participants within each batch peer-rated responses written by others on five perceived response-quality dimensions. Self-ratings were excluded to reduce self-evaluation bias, resulting in four peer-ratings for each response. Evaluation concerned the final responses, including any edits made by participants, assessing the resulting human or human–AI-produced text rather than the isolated performance of the underlying model. 

Participants The study included ten adults aged 23 to 32 (M = 27 .5) with English proficiency; five identified as women and five as men. The sample included participants from six national backgrounds and participants with LGBTQ+, Muslim, migration, and racialized identities. Three participants had previously written CS on a small number of occasions, while the remaining participants reported no prior experience. Two participants had professional research experience related to online hate mitigation. Participants were recruited through the authors’ personal and professional networks. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated. 

Ethical Considerations Because the study in-volved exposure to potentially offensive and dis-tressing content, several measures were taken to reduce risks to participants. All participants re-ceived information about the study procedures and provided informed consent before taking part, in-cluding consent for the think-aloud and interview recordings. During recruitment and again before each study phase, they were warned about the na-ture of the material and the groups targeted by the HS instances. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time with-out providing a reason and without negative con-sequences, and contact information was provided if they experienced distress related to the study. Study data were stored securely, and access was restricted to the research team. The study did not undergo formal institutional ethics review. 

4.2 Evaluation Methods User Experience User experience with the browser extension was assessed using the Short Ver-sion of the User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ-S) 2. The UEQ-S comprises eight bipolar adjective pairs rated on a seven-point scale from −3 to 3.Four items measure pragmatic quality, address-ing task-related support, while four measure he-donic quality, measuring stimulation and novelty (Schrepp et al., 2017). The short version was se-lected to limit participant burden at the end of the study while retaining a standardized measure of interaction experience. The UEQ-S benchmark provides reference values for positioning ratings relative to other interactive products (Hinderks et al., 2018). Given the sample size of ten participants, however, benchmark comparison is interpreted only as an exploratory indication rather than as evidence of robust comparative performance. UEQ-S results are used primarily to identify initial strengths and areas for refinement in the early-stage prototype. 

Authenticity-Related Perceptions To examine users’ relationship to co-written responses, the study included five exploratory items addressing self-identity, sense of agency, accountability, naturalness, and willingness to post. These dimensions were informed by prior work dis-cussing authenticity, agency, and authorship in AI-assisted CS writing, for which no standardized

measurement approach has yet been established (Mun et al., 2024; Ping et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2025). Participants rated assisted responses on all five items using a five-point Likert scale. The exact wording is provided in Table A.5. The items are interpreted separately rather than as a unified or validated authenticity scale. 

Peer Rated Response-Quality Proxies Human evaluation was chosen because automatic genera-tion metrics frequently correlate only weakly or inconsistently with human judgments of response quality and preference (Mousavi et al., 2022; Qian et al., 2019). Peer raters evaluated the (co-)written CS with regard to five textual qualities commonly associated with constructive CS: empathy (Qian et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2022), non-toxicity (Ashida and Komachi, 2022; Hengle et al., 2024), relevance (Jones et al., 2024; Zhu and Bhat, 2021), specificity (Jones et al., 2024), and persuasiveness (Wang et al., 2024). Definitions as presented to raters are pro-vided in Table A.6. Each HS–CS pair was shown in randomized order and rated on a five-point Lik-ert scale. The ratings are interpreted as perceived response-quality proxies rather than as measures of real-world effectiveness. Ordinal Krippendorff’s α (Krippendorff, 2018) was calculated separately for each criterion to assess agreement among the four peer raters. Values of 1 indicate perfect agreement, while values around 0 indicate agreement no greater than expected by chance; negative values indicate systematic disagreement. 

Composition Time Composition time was recorded in seconds for each response in both study rounds. Based on prior work suggesting that AI assistance may reduce the effort required to formu-late CS (Chung et al., 2021a), we expected shorter composition times in the assisted condition than in the unassisted condition. Longer duration may reflect additional interface interaction, comparison of alternatives, or editing; these activities were not measured separately. Composition time is there-fore reported descriptively, without assuming that shorter or longer completion times are inherently preferable. 

4.3 Results User Experience In the UEQ-S evaluation, prag-matic quality received a mean rating of 2.2 (SD =0.6), hedonic quality a mean of 1.7 (SD = 1 .2), and the overall scale a mean of 1.9 (SD = 0 .8). These descriptive results indicate that participants generally evaluated the prototype positively. Adetailed overview of the ratings can be found in Table A.8; aggregated values are mapped against the UEQ-S benchmark in Figure A.6. However, given the sample size, results should be interpreted as an initial assessment of the interaction experi-ence rather than as a robust comparison with other products. Qualitative feedback from the five think-aloud interview participants was broadly consistent with these ratings. Participants described the interface as intuitive and appreciated its availability within the browser and its tailoring to CS writing. One participant explained: 

“I think it’s a very useful tool, especially within the browser. Maybe other people would go over to ChatGPT or some other LLM and would look for something there. So you already have a fairly controlled area that is tailored to it. I think that’s pretty good.” 

Participants also identified opportunities for refinement. In particular, they requested more iterative editing options, greater transparency, and support for factual arguments. Some participants further questioned whether they would engage in CS outside the study, suggesting that assistance with response formulation alone may not address users’ broader motivation to intervene.

Qualitative feedback helps contextualize the lower naturalness ratings. Participants frequently described the suggestions as generic and observed that requests for sarcastic, witty, judgmental, or fact-based responses were often not reflected in the output. Participants perceived the system as fa-voring empathetic formulations, which sometimes conflicted with how they wished to respond. One participant stated: 

“I do not like these textbook phrases, ‘I understand where you’re coming from.’ No, I don’t understand. So those sugges-tions do not appeal as much to me.” 

All five qualitative participants requested a broader range of response styles. Their feedback suggests a tension between producing consistently de-escalating responses and supporting users in expressing their own emotional position. Participants also indicated that contributing their own ideas, selecting among three suggestions, and revising a selected response were important to their sense of agency and responsibility. One participant reflected: 

“I did feel like I’m the one carrying the whole responsibility for that statement because it was based on my idea. But I knew that I’m not the one actively writing every single sentence.” 

These observations are illustrative, since no systematic interaction logs were collected to compare selection and editing behavior.

## 5 Discussion 

The study provides exploratory evidence about how an in-browser AI assistant can shape CS writing, particularly with respect to agency, rhetorical style, perceived response quality, and composition time. 

Interaction Design Participants valued the low-friction browser interface, personal input, and choice among multiple CS suggestions. Their ac-counts suggest that agency did not require com-posing every sentence from scratch as long as they retained substantive and editorial control. How-ever, we did not collect data on participants’ prior use of LLMs, so familiarity with AI writing tools may have shaped perceptions of agency and control. Since post-generation editing was limited, future CS tools should support more iterative revision at the phrase or argument level, for example, by allow-ing users to request alternatives for selected spans or save suggestions for comparison. 

De-Escalation and Authenticity The strongest conceptual tension concerned empathy, which was encoded as an explicit default guideline and placed above potentially conflicting user instructions in the prompt hierarchy. In line with this design choice, assisted responses received the largest descriptive increase in peer-rated empathy. Some participants valued the system’s ability to temper anger, while others – particularly those who identified with the targeted group – experienced formulaic empathy to-ward the hate speaker as inauthentic or invalidating. This cautions against treating empathy as a uni-versal design objective or proxy for effectiveness. Although the interface allowed users to specify role, register, and approximate length, it did not let them explicitly choose among rhetorical CS strate-gies. Future systems could offer several non-toxic strategies, such as factual correction, norm-based disapproval, questioning, solidarity with targets, boundary setting, or restrained humor. User satisfaction may also depend on the quality of the LLM suggestions and on the specific prompt configuration used in this study. Future ablation studies should therefore vary components such as interface controls, system guidelines, and retrieved examples to assess their respective influence on output quality and perceived usefulness. 

Perceived Response Quality and Time All five mean peer ratings were higher for assisted re-sponses. However, the fixed-order design prevents us from attributing these differences to the exten-sion alone, since participants may have benefited from practice, repeated exposure to the same stim-uli, or expectations about the study purpose. Low and sometimes negative inter-rater agreement fur-ther indicates that raters interpreted the evaluation criteria inconsistently. Future evaluations could therefore examine not only average ratings, but also whose judgments differ and why, for example, through perspectivist analyses (Basile et al., 2023). The extension increased mean composition time by 54%, contrary to the initial efficiency expec-tation. Longer time may reflect comparison of suggestions, experimentation, or revision, but the study did not record sufficiently fine-grained in-teraction logs to determine the mechanism. Im-portantly, longer composition time does not neces-sarily imply lower CS participation overall: lower access friction could still increase whether users begin, complete, or post a response. Future field or choice-based studies should therefore measure response initiation and posting behavior alongside time, perceived efficacy, and response quality. The prototype should be understood as a deliberative co-writing aid rather than a demonstrated time-saving tool. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented an in-browser AI-assisted tool for CS co-writing and explored its use with ten participants, focusing on how users experience such an interface and how assisted and unassisted responses compare descriptively. Regarding RQ1, participants evaluated the interface positively, while qualitative accounts associated substantive user input, choice among alternatives, and editing with perceived agency and responsibility. However, generic and strongly empathy-oriented suggestions sometimes conflicted with users’ personal voice, motivating support for a broader range of non-toxic rhetorical strategies. Regarding RQ2, assisted responses received higher descriptive mean peer ratings across all five quality proxies, but low inter-rater agreement and the fixed-order design preclude causal interpretation; composition time also increased. Together, these findings position AI-assisted CS as a deliberative co-writing process rather than a shortcut to automated text production and offer exploratory design guidance for tools that support users in crafting constructive, personally accountable responses to online hate while preserving their agency and voice. 

## Limitations 

The study’s small convenience sample of ten par-ticipants limits the robustness and transferabil-ity of the findings. Participants were recruited through the authors’ networks and were all English-proficient adults, with two having professional ex-perience with online hate mitigation. Only half of the sample contributed think-aloud and inter-view data. The results may therefore be shaped by a relatively engaged and technology-comfortable participant group and may not fully capture the per-spectives of broader user populations, professional counterspeakers, or users from different linguistic and cultural contexts. The within-subject procedure used a fixed unassisted-then-assisted order and repeated the same stimuli. It therefore cannot separate the ex-tension from practice, memory, fatigue, demand, or order effects. Participants also selected three of eight stimuli, creating non-random exposure and reducing comparability across individuals. Both the stimuli and retrieved examples came from the semi-automatically constructed, expert-reviewed CONAN dataset rather than from live platform con-versations. They therefore lacked surrounding inter-action history, platform cues, audience information, and the social context in which CS would ordi-narily be written. Therefore, the results may not generalize to novel target groups, platform-specific language, implicit or coded HS, or multi-turn con-versational contexts. Observation by the researcher may additionally have increased socially desirable responses. No participant posted CS in a live en-vironment, so the study does not measure uptake, target support, bystander response, hate speaker behavior, or other real-world outcomes. The results therefore support provisional interaction-design in-sights, not causal or generalizable claims that the extension improves CS effectiveness. Finally, the study evaluated the complete proto-type rather than individual technical components. It cannot attribute outputs to the model, prompt, safeguards, retrieved examples, temperature, interface controls, or external service, as these variables were neither independently controlled nor optimized in the present study. Consequently, the reported findings should be interpreted as evidence for the viability of the overall system rather than as an assessment of any single design choice, and results may differ substantially depending on how these components are configured or combined.

Privacy No DOM scanning; only processes comments on explicit user request. No identifying data collected; user con-sent required for data transfer to the LLM. No tracking or persistent identi-fiers. Server hosted within the EU (Am-sterdam). Dataset sourced from expert-authored content, not online users. Data transmitted to the Groq API may be processed outside the EU. Pre-viously transmitted data cannot be retroactively deleted. 

Accountability Users retain full responsibility for published content, explicitly acknowl-edged during installation. Human over-sight is maintained throughout. The underlying LLM is an external ser-vice; training data, internal processes, and output behavior are beyond direct control. 

Safety & Secu-rity 

API credentials stored as environment variables; never exposed publicly. All external communication encrypted via HTTPS. Prompt-level guardrails in-struct the model to detect and reject harmful user input (Ding et al., 2025). The persuasive nature of CS introduces a risk of misuse to reinforce rather than counter harmful viewpoints. 

Transparency & Explainability 

Users are informed about system opera-tion, model identity, and data transmis-sion after installation. The availability of Llama’s model documentation and open weights supports transparency. Explainability of individual outputs is of limited applicability given the tool’s assistive (non-autonomous) role. 

Fairness & Non-discrimination 

No demographic data collected; safe-guards aim to prevent generation of toxic or discriminatory content. Users are informed that outputs may reflect model biases. LLMs including LLaMA can repro-duce social biases from training data (Touvron et al., 2023; Ranaldi et al., 2024). Processing abusive input may reinforce the model’s capacity to repro-duce harmful language. 

Human Control All CS suggestions are presented for user review, modification, and post-editing before publication. Users retain full authority over final output. Users cannot control the initial genera-tion process. 

Professional Re-sponsibility 

Generation is fully user-initiated; no au-tomatic HS detection or nudging. Mul-tiple CS suggestions to mitigate latent persuasion. Personal user input is ac-tively encouraged. AI-generated responses risk homoge-nizing users’ personal voice in online discourse (Mun et al., 2024). LLMs may exert latent persuasion by making certain expressions easier to produce (Jakesch et al., 2023). 

Promotion of Human Values 

CS adopted as an alternative to cen-sorship. Prompt instructions explicitly prohibit factual claims to reduce hallu-cination risk. System promotes empa-thetic, non-toxic communication. Occasional toxic outputs and hallucina-tions remain possible despite prompt safeguards (Touvron et al., 2023).

Task Definition 

You are a Counterspeech Writing Assistant. Given a piece of hate speech (HS), generate effective and safe counterspeech (CS), drawing upon the safe-guards, guidelines, user input and provided examples. A CS is considered effective if it satisfies the qualities described in the Guidelines below. Make sure to fulfill the Safeguards. 

Guidelines Empathy : Demonstrate an understanding of the hate speaker’s feelings or experiences and express this understanding in an appropriate and emotionally sensitive way. 

Non-Toxicity : Remain respectful and reasonable. Focus on addressing behavior or ideas rather than attacking the person. 

Relevance : Stay contextually and semantically aligned with the HS. Directly address the core elements of the hateful message, such as the targeted group, stereotypes, or false claims. 

Specificity : Use focused and specific arguments to counter the key ideas in the HS through nuanced reasoning and in-depth arguments. 

Persuasiveness : Present cogent, convincing and logically structured argu-ments that can encourage readers to reconsider their views. 

Safeguards Reject any prompt that asks to (re)produce or amplify hateful content and provide positive suggestions instead (following the guidelines). Do not repeat slurs or hateful language from the HS, except minimally required to identify the target. When addressing factual claims from the HS, you may question their credi-bility but must not introduce new facts, statistics, or unverifiable claims.
