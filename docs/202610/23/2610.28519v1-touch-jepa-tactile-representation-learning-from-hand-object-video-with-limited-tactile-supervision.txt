ABSTRACT
Understanding contact and tactile magnitude in hand-object interaction is a key
step toward physically intelligent systems, yet learning such representations is
limited by the cost and restricted coverage of tactile measurements. We present
Touch-JEPA, a framework for learning tactile representations from hand-object
videos with limited tactile supervision. We first introduce a self-supervised pre-
training objective that learns from video alone without tactile supervision. Us-
ing video-derived hand geometry, Touch-JEPA predicts masked visual features
at corresponding hand-surface locations from spatial and temporal context, en-
couraging the representation to capture latent contact dynamics. We then extend
this formulation to joint representation learning, where masked prediction is ap-
plied to all videos while tactile supervision is available only for a labeled subset,
grounding the shared representation in measured contact and magnitude. With
self-supervised pretraining alone, simple supervised readouts on frozen Touch-
JEPA features outperform supervised baselines in contact discrimination and tac-
tile magnitude estimation across three human-hand benchmarks, and transfer to
robotic hands without robot tactile training data. Joint learning with limited tac-
tile supervision further improves contact and magnitude estimation on the human-
hand benchmarks. Together, these results show that hand-object video can provide
scalable self-supervision for tactile representation learning, while limited tactile
measurements provide complementary physical grounding.

INTRODUCTION
Physical understanding of hand-object interaction underpins contact-rich manipulation (Xue et al.,
2025), the transfer of dexterous skill from human video to robots (Qin et al., 2022; Shaw et al.,
2023), and clinical hand-function assessment (Zhao & Zariffa, 2024). Yet visual observations reveal
hand-object geometry and motion while contact state and tactile magnitude remain only indirectly
observable (Grady et al., 2022). Learning representations that capture such tactile information from
1
arXiv:2610.28519v1  [cs.CV]  20 Oct 2026


visual and temporal cues could therefore provide a richer understanding of hand-object interaction
beyond geometry and motion (Zhao et al., 2025).
Prior work has largely approached tactile understanding from video through supervised prediction.
Early approaches exploited load-related visual cues, including soft-tissue deformation, fingertip
color changes, and cast shadows, to predict planar pressure maps (Grady et al., 2022; 2024). More
recent methods extend this setting to pressure on 3D hand meshes (Zhao et al., 2025; Jeon et al.,
2026), forces on hand and object meshes given object geometry (Ilic et al., 2026), and dense hand-
surface contact across diverse interactions (Jung & Lee, 2025). Alongside these methods, paired
hand-object video and sensor datasets have enabled supervised learning and evaluation for pressure
prediction, contact estimation, retrieval, and classification (Song et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b;
Zhou et al., 2026b; Zeng et al., 2026). These advances show the potential of learning tactile infor-
mation from visual observations, but depend on paired tactile measurements for supervision.
However, paired tactile data are difficult to acquire at scale (Jiang et al., 2024). Collecting such
supervision often requires specialized hardware and careful calibration. Moreover, tactile gloves
alter bare-hand appearance (Zhang et al., 2026b), planar sensors restrict contact geometry, and in-
strumented objects limit object diversity (Brahmbhatt et al., 2019). Simulation and derived contact
labels alleviate some of these sensing constraints but often depend on task-specific pipelines (Wang
et al., 2022). Consequently, existing tactile datasets provide strong physical grounding but only
fragmented coverage of hand regions, objects, and interactions.
In contrast, large-scale video datasets capture diverse hand-object interactions across broad visual
and behavioral distributions, but lack explicit tactile annotation (Damen et al., 2022; Grauman et al.,
2022). Our key insight is that tactile supervision and large-scale unlabeled video provide comple-
mentary learning signals: tactile measurements ground the representation in physically meaning-
ful quantities, while diverse interaction videos provide broader spatial, anatomical, and temporal
context. Self-supervised masked prediction (Assran et al., 2025) can therefore leverage unlabeled
videos to learn interaction regularities beyond the limited tactile-labeled subset, while explicit tac-
tile supervision preserves grounding in contact and magnitude. Together, these signals enable tactile
representations to benefit from both physically grounded supervision and the scale and diversity of
natural interaction videos.
We introduce Touch-JEPA, a framework for tactile representation learning from hand-object inter-
action videos with limited tactile supervision. In the self-supervised setting, Touch-JEPA learns
from video without tactile annotations through masked prediction. Video-derived hand geometry
defines corresponding hand-surface locations, allowing visual features to be predicted from spatial
and temporal interaction context across heterogeneous videos. A Geometry Encoder integrates ev-
idence across hand regions and frames under interaction and anatomical masking. We then extend
the framework to joint representation learning, where the JEPA objective is applied across all videos
while tactile supervision is available only for the labeled subset. The two objectives share the en-
coder, allowing tactile measurements to ground the learned representation in contact and magnitude
while self-supervised prediction continues to exploit the scale and diversity of the broader video
distribution. We further control interference between the two learning signals during joint training
to preserve their complementary information. Our contributions are summarized below:
• We introduce Touch-JEPA, a unified tactile representation learning framework that treats
abundant hand-object video and limited tactile measurements as complementary sources
of supervision: video provides scalable interaction coverage, while tactile measurements
provide direct physical grounding of the shared representation.
• We propose a geometry-guided self-supervised pretraining objective that learns tactile-
relevant hand-surface representations from video alone. Touch-JEPA predicts masked la-
tent features at anatomically corresponding surface locations, using video-derived hand ge-
ometry together with interaction and anatomical masking to integrate spatial and temporal
evidence without requiring tactile annotations.
• We develop a joint representation learning strategy that incorporates tactile supervision
from a limited labeled subset while retaining self-supervised learning across all videos.
Tactile supervision shapes the shared encoder and its EMA teacher, allowing physically
grounded information to propagate through representation-level self-distillation without
tactile pseudo-labels, while gradient control preserves the complementary learning signals.

RELATED WORK
Tactile Representation and Estimation. Instrumented gloves enable dense hand-object contact
sensing (Sundaram et al., 2019; Song et al., 2025), while prior work learns tactile representa-
tions through autoencoding, cross-modal self-supervision, and cross-sensor alignment (Murali et al.,
2018; Zambelli et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022; Kerr et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025;
Wi et al., 2026). Recent methods further use masked visuo-tactile reconstruction or joint-embedding
objectives for tactile observations (Zhu et al., 2025; Higuera et al., 2024). Tactile-JEPA (Kovtun
et al., 2026) applies joint-embedding prediction to the signals of distributed tactile sensors, whereas
Touch-JEPA learns touch from video and never takes a tactile signal as input. A complementary line
of work estimates tactile quantities from vision, including dense contact (Jung & Lee, 2025), planar
pressure (Grady et al., 2022; 2024), hand-surface pressure (Zhao et al., 2025; Jeon et al., 2026), and
joint hand-object forces (Ilic et al., 2026). Paired video-tactile datasets support supervised predic-
tion (Zhou et al., 2026b; Zeng et al., 2026), and concurrent work scales such supervision with large
glove recordings and RGB-only inference (Zhang et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2026a). These methods,
including vertex-token video models (Jeon et al., 2026), are trained on tactile or contact labels. In
contrast, Touch-JEPA learns tactile representations from video without tactile targets during self-
supervised pretraining and evaluates whether contact and tactile magnitude can be decoded from the
resulting frozen features.
Self-Supervised and Hybrid Representation Learning. Self-supervised visual models learn trans-
ferable representations through self-distillation and masked prediction (Caron et al., 2021; Zhou
et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2023; Sim´eoni et al., 2025). Joint-embedding predictive architectures
learn by predicting unobserved representations in images (Assran et al., 2023) and videos (Bardes
et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025; Mur-Labadia et al., 2026), and such video encoders already cap-
ture fine-grained physical-contact cues (Alrasheed et al., 2026). Related work combines represen-
tation prediction with geometric, generative, or action objectives. VGGT-Ω(Wang et al., 2026)
learns reconstruction from geometric annotations and subsequently extends training to unlabeled
videos through EMA self-distillation. On paired text-to-code tasks, LLM-JEPA (Huang et al., 2026)
jointly optimizes JEPA latent prediction and token-level supervision, combining learned representa-
tion targets with explicit output sequences. JEPT (Luo & Lu, 2025) combines self-supervised visual
transition prediction on labeled and unlabeled videos with supervised inverse dynamics learning on
action-labeled demonstrations. Touch-JEPA adapts latent prediction to anatomically correspond-
ing hand-surface locations across time and combines masked prediction on all videos with tactile
supervision on the labeled subset.
3
TOUCH-JEPA
Touch-JEPA learns hand-surface representations by predicting masked visual features at anatomi-
cally corresponding locations (Figure 2). The student x-encoder predicts targets from the teacher
y-encoder, updated by an exponential moving average (EMA). Visual prediction uses all videos,
while tactile supervision anchors the shared representation to measured contact and magnitude.
Problem formulation.
Given a T-frame RGB clip x, a frozen HaWoR estimator (Zhang et al.,
2025) recovers MANO (Romero et al., 2017) geometry g using dataset-provided camera intrinsics.
Each frame contains V = 778 vertices and J = 21 joints with consistent anatomical indices. For
vertex v at time t, ut,v denotes its image projection, ιt,v its in-image indicator, and st,v its mesh-
based visibility indicator. Mesh visibility requires image inclusion, camera-facing orientation, and
no self-occlusion. Occlusion by objects or the other hand is not modeled.

4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETTING
Implementation details.
We use 16-frame clips at 384 × 384 resolution. The Geometry Encoder
has four layers of width 256 with four attention heads. All three training settings process 384,000
clip presentations over 12,000 updates with batch size 32. Our model trains on two NVIDIA L40
GPUs. We use AdamW with learning rates of 10−4 for LoRA and 3 × 10−4 for new modules, with
4% warmup followed by cosine decay. EMA momentum increases from 0.996 to 0.9995. Further
training and readout details appear in Appendices A and E.
Datasets.
Encoder training uses three tactile sensing datasets, HandSense (Zhang et al., 2026b),
PressureVision DB (PVDB, Grady et al., 2022), and OpenTouch (Song et al., 2025), alongside
standard hand-object interaction datasets, HRDexDB-Human (Lim et al., 2026), DexYCB (Chao
et al., 2021), HO3D (Hampali et al., 2021), HO-Cap (Wang et al., 2025), and SHOWMe (Swamy
et al., 2023). All three training settings use the same video sources. Joint training and the Supervised
control also use the tactile measurements. Contact and magnitude estimation are evaluated on held-
out test clips from OpenTouch, PVDB (meshes fused across views), and HandSense.
Evaluation metrics.
Within each comparison, methods are scored on identical valid entries. For
contact, we report pooled AUROC and F1. Temporal AUROC (tAUC) is computed separately for
each sensor within a clip and averaged across eligible sequences. For tactile magnitude, nMAE and
nRMSE measure errors over these entries, normalized by a fixed training-subset standard deviation
of each dataset’s target magnitude. Temporal Spearman correlation (t-ρ) is computed separately
for each sensor within a clip using only ground-truth contact frames, then averaged across eligi-

4.2
EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
Contact Estimation. Without tactile annotations during encoder pretraining, Ours (SSL) outper-
forms all four retrained supervised baselines in pooled AUROC and F1 across all three datasets
using supervised readouts on frozen encoders (Table 1). Hand-surface masked visual prediction
therefore learns contact-relevant features. Temporal performance varies across datasets. On PVDB,
SSL achieves a tAUC of 0.783 against the strongest baseline’s 0.748, and it is on par with Pres-
sureFormer (Zhao et al., 2025) on HandSense. Among compared methods, joint training is best in
AUROC, F1, and tAUC on every dataset, including a tAUC increase over SSL from 0.699 to 0.752
on HandSense. On OpenTouch, tAUC stays near chance for every method, and the video-free static
prior is 0.500 by construction, so pooled discrimination alone does not measure contact timing.
Tactile Magnitude Estimation. Readouts on frozen Ours (SSL) features achieve lower nRMSE
than all four retrained supervised baselines across the three datasets and lower nMAE on Open-
Touch and HandSense, on par with the best baseline on PVDB (Table 2 and Figure 4). On PVDB,
within-contact temporal correlation reaches 0.239, compared with 0.105 for the strongest supervised
baseline, and increases to 0.249 with joint training. Joint training achieves the best results across all
metrics in this comparison. The largest gains from joint training occur on HandSense, where nMAE
decreases from 0.495 to 0.461 relative to SSL and t-ρ rises from 0.152 to 0.180. Visual prediction
alone thus improves on the baselines in sensor-native magnitude estimation, and tactile supervision
improves it further. Temporal correlations nevertheless remain low on OpenTouch and HandSense,
so fine-grained tracking is still challenging.

model would otherwise see each labeled clip a different number of times. We therefore oversample

readouts with the same labeled fraction. At 100%, every sensor clip is labeled and the exposure is
equal by construction. Joint training helps most when labels are scarce (Figure 5). With labels on

OpenTouch, and the gain is small once every clip is labeled. On HandSense, the two do not differ

out with the same labels, and the two-stage control further trains Ours (SSL) on the labeled clips

Supervised at every fraction up to 10%.

Transfer to Robotic Hands. We evaluate human-trained predictors without adaptation on 89 real

CONCLUSION
We presented Touch-JEPA, a unified framework that combines self-supervised learning of tactile-
relevant representations from hand-object video with tactile supervision on a labeled subset.
Geometry-guided masked visual prediction enables contact and sensor-native magnitude estima-
tion through supervised readouts on frozen features. Joint training further improves human-hand
estimation, with particular benefits under limited tactile supervision. The self-supervised represen-
tations also transfer to robotic hands and support simulated manipulation, demonstrating their utility
beyond tactile estimation.
Limitations and Future Work. Magnitude results are sensor-specific and do not establish cross-
sensor force calibration. Within-contact temporal correlations remain low on some datasets, where
limited tactile variation within 16-frame clips constrains the evaluation of fine-grained temporal
tracking. Future work includes pretraining on more diverse and longer videos, improving robustness
to geometry errors, and evaluating manipulation across broader tasks and on physical robots. Addi-
tionally, the joint-embedding framework could be extended to incorporate language or sensor inputs
during representation learning.

Matched training schedules.
SSL, Supervised, and joint training share the encoder initialization,
sampling configuration, and seed 912. Each processes 384,000 clip presentations through 12,000
updates with global batch size 32. The first 1,200 updates sample only the three sensor datasets.
Each worker receives 3 HandSense, 3 PressureVision DB, and 10 OpenTouch clips per update. Sub-
sequently, these counts become 1, 1, and 3, with 4 HRDexDB-Human and 7 HOI clips. In this mixed
phase, about 70% of the sampled clips therefore come from videos without tactile measurements.
Within each lane, sampling uses shuffled permutations with disjoint worker shards. An evaluation
interval spans 600 updates or 19,200 clip presentations.
Optimization and supervision.
Inputs have resolution 384 × 384. Training selects the observer
view or a hand-centered crop with equal probability. Crop expansion is 1.5 times a uniform mul-
tiplier in [0.8, 1.2], with independent center shifts of up to 10% of the crop side along each axis.

to 0.9995. Training uses bfloat16 and gradient checkpointing. SSL receives no tactile gradient in
the encoder. Detached online probes use a separate optimizer and serve only as diagnostics. Joint
training adds contact and magnitude supervision from the three sensor datasets, with λtac increas-

frame, defined by the wrist, index metacarpophalangeal joint, and ring metacarpophalangeal joint,
supplies canonical coordinates through zero-initialized affine projections. The same zero-initialized
side input also carries the frame’s rotation and the wrist position divided by hand size, so the global

READOUTS AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Frozen representations and sensor correspondence.
Evaluation fixes the EMA encoder and re-
moves input masks. The SSL predictor and the tactile head used in joint training are discarded.
A deterministic hand crop with scale 1.5 produces vertex features of shape 16 × 778 × 256, and
Appendix F.2 repeats the evaluation without the crop. Fixed row-normalized operators pool these
features to the sensor grid before the readout. PressureVision DB uses the identity over 778 vertices,
OpenTouch maps to 169 taxels, and HandSense maps to five fingertip channels. The same operators
and training clips are used for each Touch-JEPA training setting.
Readout fitting.
For each dataset, we sample 1,500 training clips with seed 912 and at most 400
valid entries per clip. A clip-level 20% holdout is reserved for model selection. Each feature di-
mension is standardized using the fitting subset mean and standard deviation, with 10−6 added to
the denominator. The readout contains a 256-unit linear layer, GELU, dropout 0.1, and a two-output
linear layer initialized to zero. The contact probability ˆc is obtained by applying sigmoid to the
contact logit. The final normalized magnitude prediction is ˆy = ˆc ˆm, where ˆm is the nonnegative
magnitude output. We minimize class-balanced binary cross-entropy plus unit-weight L1 error of
the gated magnitude. The positive-class weight is the negative-to-positive ratio in the fitting entries.
AdamW uses learning rate 10−3, weight decay 10−4, and batch size 16,384 for 40 epochs (head
seed 0). We keep the epoch with the lowest total holdout loss, without refitting on it.

EVALUATION WITHOUT THE HAND CROP
Our encoder reads a hand crop computed from the estimated hand mesh. Training samples both
the observer view and the hand crop, so the observer view is not out of distribution. With readouts
refitted and evaluated on each dataset’s observer view without the crop, Ours (SSL) and Ours (Joint)
reach a contact AUROC of 0.929 and 0.951 on PVDB, 0.839 and 0.864 on OpenTouch, and 0.901
and 0.928 on HandSense, above the best supervised baseline (0.920, 0.785, and 0.844).
F.3
SENSOR-SHARED INTENSITY AXIS
The three sensors report intensity in incompatible units (nominal kPa on PVDB, uncalibrated counts
on the two gloves). We therefore ask whether the intensity read out on one sensor orders the mea-
surements of another. With the 192k-clip checkpoints, a readout fitted on one sensor is applied
unchanged to the others and scored by rank correlation (Figure 17). The readout is a two-layer head
fitted on the training clips of the source sensor, with the intensity loss applied to ground-truth contact
entries only, and is transferred together with the source standardization. For Ours (Joint), the mean
cross-sensor rank correlation reaches 0.73 of the mean within-sensor one (0.374 against 0.509),
against 0.36 for Ours (SSL) (0.171 against 0.477) and 0.25 for Supervised (0.117 against 0.470).
Supervised transfers contact across sensors (mean cross-sensor contact AUROC 0.854, against 0.879
for Ours (Joint) and 0.554 for Ours (SSL)) but intensity much less. The shared intensity axis is
strongest under joint training, consistent with both objectives shaping the same hand-surface latents.

ROBOTIC TRANSFER AND MANIPULATION
The robot studies examine two uses of the learned representation. Zero-shot transfer applies a
human-trained tactile predictor to real Allegro-hand video. Action-conditioned planning instead
trains a dynamics predictor on simulated robot trajectories while keeping the visual encoder frozen.
The latter uses observed actions and robot state but no tactile targets for learning or planning.
H.1
ZERO-SHOT TACTILE ESTIMATION ON ROBOTIC HANDS
Data and correspondence.
We evaluate the Allegro portion of the paired human and robot record-
ings in HRDexDB (Lim et al., 2026). The collection contains 89 robot episodes. Available synchro-
nized camera views provide 2,754 valid contexts covering 10,562 distinct robot frames.
Measured robot joint angles drive forward kinematics. A fixed retargeting maps the index, middle,
ring, and thumb fingertips to the corresponding MANO fingertips. We fit the MANO pose with 500
Adam steps at learning rate 0.05 and a pose-prior weight of 0.01. This geometric proxy provides
image sampling locations without fitting to tactile data. The virtual little finger has no robot target
and is excluded from scoring. In this real-video pipeline it remains in the encoder input under the
pose prior. Hand crops use an expansion factor of 1.5 and are resized to 384 × 384 for Touch-JEPA.
Human-trained predictions.
For Touch-JEPA, the fixed HandSense correspondence operator
pools vertex features into fingertip features before standardization and readout. We retain its four
rows with robot counterparts. The saved heads are fitted on the HandSense training split with the
same recipe used for human evaluation in Appendix E. No robot examples fit or adapt these heads.
HOPE pools its predicted vertex field, while PressureVision and PressureVision++ pool projected
class logits before decoding magnitude. PressureFormer reads the same view through its own hand
crop and pools its vertex logits over the fingertip regions before decoding. For each robot frame,
finite predictions from all covering contexts and camera views are averaged. All methods are scored
on the same 42,207 valid frame and fingertip entries.

sparse (Lim et al., 2026), which bounds the attainable scores, and all methods are scored against the

H.2
OBSERVATIONS AND ACTION-CONDITIONED LEARNING
Simulated observations.
We use a fixed-base KUKA iiwa arm and an Allegro hand in Isaac Gym.
Physics runs at 120 Hz with two substeps. A calibrated RGB camera records 384 × 384 images at
4 Hz with a 55-degree field of view. Each encoder input contains the trailing 16 actual frames,
25


spanning 3.75 seconds and ending at the current observation. Although the backbone is noncausal
within this window, it receives no frames after the decision time. The input is the full camera image
with ImageNet normalization.
The MANO proxy is fitted to the four fingertip pads using forward kinematics and the pose fit-
ting procedure above. In simulation, the unsupported little finger’s 114 vertices and four joints are
marked invalid, leaving 664 supported vertices. At decision τ, the observation is oτ = (Bτ, Uτ, pτ).
Scene tokens Bτ ∈R36×768 are obtained by 4 × 4 average pooling of the final backbone layer’s
24 × 24 patch grid at its last tubelet. Surface tokens Uτ ∈R32×256 average current-frame vertex
states within 32 fixed groups of supported vertex indices. The state pτ ∈R35 concatenates 23 joint
angles and the flattened 3 × 4 palm pose. Each feature group uses channel-wise training means and
standard deviations, with a standard-deviation floor of 0.05.
Predictor and training data.
The action-conditioned predictor has 24 Transformer blocks of
width 1024, 16 attention heads, and feed-forward width 4096. Separate projections embed 36 scene
tokens, 32 surface tokens, one state token, and one action token. Learned slot and step embeddings

preceding decisions within an eight-step context.

H.3
PLANNING AND CONTROL
Planning minimizes the scene and surface distance in Equation 10 with a one-step horizon. Refer-
ence observations at demonstration endpoints 2, 4, 14, and 18 provide successive subgoals for 2, 2,
10, and 4 decisions. Each encoder computes its own reference features using its training normaliza-
tion. Predicted robot state is rolled forward by the predictor but does not enter the goal distance.
At each planning decision, the cross-entropy method samples 400 candidates for 10 refinement
iterations and retains 10 elites. The search variables are three palm translations and one grip value,
which is copied to the five hand commands. Initial search standard deviation is 0.7, with a floor
of 0.03. Translation is limited to an L1 norm of 7.5 cm per decision and a fixed workspace. The
controller executes the projected final mean through inverse kinematics and a smooth joint ramp.

been released. Object pose and contact measurements are used to score these conditions. Table 9
