## Abstract 

Web agents increasingly serve the same user across repeated online tasks such as booking travel, shopping, and scheduling, so their long-term usefulness depends on how well they know the user’s preferences. Passive memory and personalization methods cannot obtain preferences the user never reveals, and clarification methods that value a question by its benefit to the current task overlook the benefit of reusing the answer in later tasks. We study Active Preference Acquisition (APA): which missing preferences are worth the cost of asking? We define the Prospective Personalization Value 

(PPV) of a missing preference as its expected cumulative utility gain over future tasks minus the effort and disclosure-risk cost of asking, and select questions under a per-interaction budget. We characterize when cumulative reuse justifies acquisition despite insufficient single-task benefit, and evaluate this regime with an initial-profile diagnostic. APA computes the counterfactual gain of each candidate preference on public task prototypes with the agent’s own task solver and aggregates it by the expected reuse of each task family. We evaluate it on ActiveWebPref, a controlled longitudinal benchmark with hidden but queryable user profiles and exact task utilities. At a horizon of 20 tasks, 34.4% of budget-feasible valuable missing preferences are amortization-dependent; APA acquires 80.3% of them, whereas current-task value of infor-mation acquires 5.1%. Under per-user asking-cost caps matched to APA’s realized expenditure, APA achieves higher net utility than random, frequency-based, impact-based and LLM-ranked selection. In our cost setting, asking about every unknown preference yields lower net utility than not asking, while APA recovers 84.2% of its utility gain with 27.6% of its questions. The value of acquiring a user preference should account for its reuse across future tasks, not only its benefit to the task at hand.

## 1 Introduction 

Web agents can now carry out realistic Web tasks such as online shopping and navigating real-world websites [9, 41, 52], and they are moving from one-shot assistance to persistent service. The same agent may book a user’s conference travel in March, order office supplies in April, and reserve a team dinner in May. Its usefulness over time then depends less on any single task than on how well it knows the user : that the user avoids red-eye flights, keeps hotels under a per-night budget, and needs vegetarian options. This paper studies that user-modeling problem for users of persistent Web agents: which missing preferences a persistent Web agent should acquire, and when doing so pays off. Valuable preferences, however, need not appear in the interaction history. Personalization methods condition on past behavior [33] or infer profiles from what users have said [48], and long-term memory systems decide how to extract, store, retrieve, and update observed information [7, 27, 32, 40, 51]. These methods work with evidence that exists; a preference the user has never had reason to mention or to reveal through behavior cannot be extracted or retrieved. Asking can fill such gaps, but asking is not free: each question costs the user effort, and each answer discloses personal informa-tion. An agent therefore cannot simply ask about everything; in our experiments, asking about every unknown preference, or about whatever an LLM deems most worth knowing, yields lower net utility than not asking at all (§6). Valuing each question by the current task does not resolve this either. Current-task clarification and value-of-information (VoI) methods ask when a question improves the current task enough to justify its cost [1, 11, 30, 43]. While booking a hotel, the agent can finish without the user’s seat preference, so current-task VoI does not ask (Figure 1). Why not wait and ask when a flight is booked? Because even then, a single booking’s gain may not cover the cost of asking; only reuse across many later bookings does. A criterion that values a question only by the task at hand then never acquires such a preference, however often it is reused (Proposition 1). An agent therefore needs to value acquisition across tasks: 

Which currently unknown user preferences are worth acquiring, because their benefit across future tasks outweighs the cost of asking? 

We call this problem Active Preference Acquisition (APA). At each interaction the agent holds a partial profile and can already complete the current task; it decides which missing preferences to ask about and stores the answers persistently. Unlike cold-start elicitation or within-session clarification, APA interleaves acquisi-tion with ongoing service and values persistent attributes by their reuse across heterogeneous future tasks. We define the Prospective Personalization Value (PPV) of a missing preference: its expected utility gain across future tasks, minus the effort and disclosure-risk cost of asking. PPV complements clarification rather than gener-alizing it, and the key distinction is in how a preference is valued, not when it is asked: a cross-task criterion may still ask at the first relevant task. We estimate PPV by counterfactual reuse valuation . ## 2 Related Work 

User modeling and personalization. Personalized systems condi-tion generation or ranking on user profiles, given as explicit persona statements [46] or drawn from behavioral history [33]. Recent LLM agents continuously infer and refine a unified user profile from dialogue turns [48], or update preference memories as user behav-ior evolves by fusing short- and long-term averages [35]. These methods infer preferences from evidence that already exists. They cannot recover a preference that has never been expressed or re-vealed through behavior, and they do not decide which unobserved preferences are worth eliciting for future tasks. 

Preference elicitation. Decision-theoretic preference elicitation frames question asking as a POMDP that trades question cost against the quality of a final decision [5]. Recommender systems ask new users to rate items in a cold-start interview [31], possibly adapting the next item to earlier answers with a decision tree [14], and use active learning in collaborative filtering more broadly [13]. Conversational recommenders decide within a session which item or attribute to ask about next [8, 10, 16, 21, 22, 36]. LLMs have also been used to elicit preferences through open-ended questions: GATE prompts models to elicit a user’s intended task behavior [23], and STaR-GATE fine-tunes a questioner to ask useful clarifying questions [2]. APA differs from this work in three ways. (i) When

and why to ask. Conversational elicitation asks in order to finish the current session. Cold-start interviews do ask for future benefit, but in a single onboarding step before any task is served. In APA, questions are interleaved with ongoing tasks that can already be completed, and each question is justified by its expected effect on future tasks. (ii) Scope. Elicitation targets item ratings or attributes within a single catalog. APA acquires persistent user attributes that are reused across heterogeneous task families, such as flights, hotels, dining, and scheduling. (iii) Valuation target. Like prior elicitation, APA weighs benefit against the cost of asking; the difference is that the benefit is counted across the heterogeneous future tasks that reuse the answer, rather than within one decision or session. APA builds on established value-of-information and preference-elicitation principles [5, 15], operationalizing them for persistent preferences reused across heterogeneous tasks, and adds a bench-mark in which the counterfactual value of an unasked preference can be measured. 

Long-term memory for agents. Surveys organize agent mem-ory by its sources, forms, and operations (writing, management, and reading) [49]. Memory architectures give LLM agents persis-tent state through hierarchical context management [27], memory streams with reflection [28], forgetting-aware stores [51], scalable extraction and retrieval [7], and temporal knowledge graphs [32]. ProMem improves memory extraction itself: it extracts details, events, and relations separately, checks completeness to recover missed events, and verifies atomic facts [40]. Benchmarks such as LongMemEval [37] and LoCoMo [25] test whether facts present in the history can be recalled. PrefEval tests whether models follow stated or implicit preferences [50], and PersonaMem whether they track preferences that evolve across sessions [17]; both find that preferences already expressed are often not followed. These sys-tems answer how to retain and use what is known . APA addresses 

which unknown information should be learned , and can run on top of any of them. 

Clarification and value of information. Asking clarifying ques-tions has been studied in information retrieval [1, 43] and in NLP, for example by ranking questions by expected value of perfect infor-mation [30]. For LLM agents, the IN3 benchmark annotates which details of a vague task are missing, and Mistral-Interact is trained to ask about them before executing [29], and Ask-before-Plan studies clarification before travel planning [47]. Proactive Agent instead anticipates and offers help that the user has not requested [24]. Dong et al. [11] formalize when an agent should ask rather than act by weighing the expected utility gain of a question on the current decision against the cost it imposes on the user. Two lines of work look beyond the current response: one labels response preferences by simulating future conversation turns, teaching models when to ask clarifying questions [45], and CollabLLM trains models with a multiturn-aware reward estimated from simulated future con-versations [38]. Howard’s formulation values information for a single decision problem [15], and the non-myopic VoI of Krause and Guestrin [20] selects a budget-constrained set of observations jointly, rather than greedily, to reduce uncertainty in one fixed model. These principles can accommodate long-term benefit; APA instantiates them as a

Web agents. Benchmarks for Web agents such as WebShop [41], Mind2Web [9], and WebArena [52] pose each episode as a sin-gle, self-contained instruction, with no user state carried across tasks. Personalized Web agents have recently been studied through PersonalWAB, which pairs simulated users’ profiles and behavior histories with personalized search, recommendation, and review tasks and whose multi-turn track lets the agent clarify a request with a simulated user [6], and Persona2Web, which requires agents on the open Web to resolve ambiguous queries from long user histories [19]. Both personalize mainly from histories, and any questions serve the current request. Neither studies which missing preferences an agent should acquire for future tasks.

## 4 The APA Framework 

APA estimates the terms of Eq. (1) with four modules (Figure 2). Its core is counterfactual reuse valuation (§4.3) combined with cost-sensitive budgeted selection (§4.4); gap discovery (§4.1) and future reuse modeling (§4.2) are implementation components that supply candidates and reuse estimates. To isolate the acquisition decision, all LLM roles share the same backbone, and all policies share the same deterministic task solver. Prompts are given in Appendix D. 

## 4.1 Preference Gap Discovery 

The agent knows a complete public attribute catalogue but not the user's missing values; it selects which unknown fields to evaluate for acquisition. Given (𝐻 𝑡 , 𝑃 𝑡 ) and the arrived task (null before the sequence), a proposer LLM selects min (𝐾, |A 𝑡 |) 

distinct IDs from the allowed unknown-field set A𝑡 , predicting stability and a natural-language question 𝑞 𝑖 . The catalogue supplies each description, so the resulting candidates retain the form 

(𝑚 𝑖 , description 𝑖 , stability 𝑖 , 𝑞 𝑖 ). The proposer must follow the following rules: 

• exclude attributes already in 𝑃 𝑡 or stated in 𝐻 𝑡 , their publicly declared exact aliases, known refusals, and IDs already pending; 

• prefer reusable preferences, including constraints valid within the planning window, over transient details; infer stability from public descriptions, without gold labels; 

• never guess a value; Candidate IDs are validated against the public catalogue; the evalu-ator computes gap recall against missing-field annotations (§7).

## 4.5 Profile Update and Reuse 

Answers are written as typed attribute–value pairs to a structured persistent profile; user-local permission is updated after success or refusal. Source, step and sensitivity are retained in the experiment logs. Every later task in any domain can read them. In the main comparison, all policies share the same simple profile store, so differences in results come from acquisition alone. 

## 5 ActiveWebPref and Experimental Setup 

Existing long-term memory benchmarks either place the infor-mation needed for each question in the history or ask models to abstain when it is absent [25, 37]; none rewards acquiring missing information. APA instead requires preferences that are useful but initially absent from the history . It also requires task utilities that can be computed exactly, so that the benefit of a question can be measured without an LLM judge. We therefore build ActiveWebPref as a controlled longitudinal testbed. The benchmarks discussed in §2 do not directly provide a protocol with hidden but queryable profiles and counterfactual utilities of unasked preferences. Ac-tiveWebPref is deliberately controlled so that the counterfactual utility of an unasked preference is known exactly, allowing us to measure whether each question was worth asking. This control comes at the cost of realism, which we discuss in §8. 

## 5.1 Benchmark Construction 

Domains and task families. ActiveWebPref covers five service domains: Travel (flight booking, hotel selection, ground transport, conference trips), Dining (restaurant choice, reservation, takeaway), 

Shopping (product selection, gifts, repeat purchases), Scheduling 

(meetings, recurring events, appointments), and Work/Conference 

(conference, coordination, work events): 16 task families in total. Task options are synthetic structured alternatives, rather than items taken from live Web catalogues. 

Hidden user profiles. Each synthetic user has 27 attributes. Each attribute has a ground-truth value and the following metadata: 

• stability : stable, semi-stable, or ephemeral; 

• sensitivity : low, medium, or high; 

• a normalized fixed asking cost 𝑐 𝑚 ∈ [ 0, 1];

• the set of relevant task families R ( 𝑚 ) and an impact level; 

• whether it is initially observed; 

• whether it is queryable. Between 40% and 60% of useful attributes are hidden at 𝑡 = 0. Profiles are stored separately from dialogues and are visible only to the user simulator and the evaluator. The privileged oracle also accesses the hidden profile; practical policies receive the public catalogue and visible profile/history only. 

Task sequences. For each user we save a separate initial context and 20 future tasks. Main CTU evaluates the first 𝑇 = 10 future tasks; horizon scaling uses nested prefixes 𝑇 ∈ { 1, 3, 5, 10 , 20 }. The generator varies reuse frequency, cross-domain relevance, acqui-sition cost, sensitivity, and attribute dependence, to evaluate their effects on acquisition decisions. It includes five kinds of attributes: 

• attributes with heterogeneous reuse: some are used once, some repeatedly, some never; 

• cross-domain attributes (e.g., meeting mode) as well as domain-specific ones (e.g., seat preference); 

• distractors : attributes that look personal but never affect utility; 

• attributes that are high-value but also high-cost or high-sensitivity; 

• correlated attributes, whose joint value may be less than the sum of their individual values. 

Exact utilities and user simulator. As in evaluations that separate user-side private information from the agent [4, 42, 44], each task has a candidate set, for example flight options with seat, layover, and price attributes. The solver picks the best candidate under the agent’s current profile, and utility is the score of that pick under

Splits. The two disjoint test splits contain IID users and users with unseen preference combinations. All splits were frozen before any test-set evaluation. ## 6.4 RQ4: Does the benefit grow with the horizon? 

Figure 5 varies 𝑇 . At 𝑇 = 1 their mean NU difference is −0.011 rather than zero. The implemented APA values only tasks that have not yet arrived (§4.3): with a single task, it can act only before the task arrives, whereas Current-task VoI (public) can still ask about that task once it is known. ## 8 Limitations and Ethical Considerations 

Simulated users. ActiveWebPref uses synthetic profiles and a simulated user, which makes exact utilities and controlled ablations possible but cannot fully reflect real user behavior. Real users may answer vaguely, change their minds, or find questions annoying in ways that our cost model only approximates; the validity require-ments and limitations of LLM-based user simulators are discussed in [4]. No completed blinded human evaluation, paraphrase/noise robustness study, or live-Web deployment is reported. The deter-ministic simulator binds structured attribute IDs to text; this does not validate how a real user understands a generated question. 

Single backbone and benchmark. All LLM roles use Qwen3-8B, and all results come from one controlled benchmark; whether the findings hold with stronger backbones, other solvers, or other task distributions remains to be tested. APA also relies on a public schema for each task family to generate prototypes, so extend-ing it to unseen task families would require inferring such schemas, for example with the LLM backbone.

Two pos-sible explanations remain untested. First, the benchmark's regular task-family design may make a uniform distribution a strong ap-proximation, leaving little room for the learned forecast to improve selection. Second, Qwen3-8B's limited capacity may constrain pre-diction quality. The current forecast uses prompting alone, without dedicated training, calibration, or a theoretical accuracy guaran-tee. Its reliability and the design of future-task-weight prediction therefore need further investigation. 

Per-interaction budget. Under 𝐵 = 0.5, attributes whose asking cost exceeds the per-interaction budget cannot be asked by a bud-geted policy, even when their cumulative value would justify the cost; at 𝑇 = 20 this excludes 26 otherwise amortization-dependent pairs. A budget that accumulates across interactions would admit them. 

Misspecified futures and drifting preferences. PPV depends on the estimated distribution of future tasks. If that estimate is wrong, the agent asks about preferences that are never used. Acquired prefer-ences may also become stale, and combining APA with preference-update methods [35] is a natural extension. 

Privacy and consent. An agent that asks questions proactively could be misused to collect more personal data than needed. LLMs and LLM agents already leak private information in contexts where disclosure is inappropriate [26, 34], and data minimization, giving an agent access only to the data a task needs, has been proposed as a defense [3]. APA is designed to limit such over-collection. Its goal is selective acquisition, not maximal profile completion. Privacy risk is a modeled cost term, not a calibrated privacy guarantee. The simulator refuses all high-sensitivity requests and charges asking cost even on refusal. Practical policies can still attempt intrusive questions before learning a refusal; the implemented rule does not enforce an exception for task-required sensitive disclosure. In de-ployment, acquired attributes should be visible to users, editable, and deletable, and asking should require consent. All data in Ac-tiveWebPref is synthetic and contains no real personal information. 

## 9 Conclusion 

The passive personalization and memory methods discussed here use only the preferences that users happen to reveal. We argued that long-term Web agents should selectively acquire missing user infor-mation when expected reuse justifies its cost. We formalized this as Active Preference Acquisition, defined Prospective Personalization Value over future tasks as a complement to current-task clarification, and characterized an amortization regime that current-task valua-tion systematically misses because no single use justifies the cost of asking. In controlled long-term personalization tasks, our experi-ments support the reuse-based motivation: at 𝑇 = 20, amortization-dependent preferences make up 34.4% of budget-feasible valuable initially missing preferences, and APA acquires 80.3% of them while Current-task VoI (public) acquires 10 of 198; under matched asking-cost caps, APA selects better questions than heuristic baselines; cross-task, cost-aware acquisition recovers most of exhaustive ask-ing’s utility gain with far fewer questions, and its mean advantage is larger at 𝑇 = 20 than at 𝑇 = 1. Future work should test the effect under richer task distributions and with real users. A personal-ized agent should learn not everything about a user, but the things whose reuse makes them worth asking about.

## B Implementation and Metric Details 

Prototypes and public belief. Each task family has 𝑁 proto = 3 pro-totypes (48 in total), produced once by the public task generator with fixed seeds and shared by all users; the generator reads no test user or future task. The belief 𝑞 𝑃 𝑡 keeps known applicable values fixed. An unknown categorical attribute takes every legal value; an unknown scalar takes its lower bound, integer midpoint and upper bound; an unknown price range within [minimum , ceiling ]

takes the proportions (0, 0.4), (0.3, 0.6) and (0.6, 0.9), and a known range raises the lower bound of an unknown ceiling. Preferred time windows take {9–12 , 13–17 , 18–21 } and blocked windows 

{12–13 , 17–18 , 19–20 } (hours, half-open). A ceiling and a range, and the two time windows, form coupled groups: illegal combina-tions are removed, duplicates merged, and the remaining states are weighted equally within a group and independently across groups. Rebuilding this finite belief after an answer can produce signed increments. 

Oracle Future-VoI.. The oracle solves a dynamic program over 

𝑇 + 1 decision nodes: the pre-sequence opportunity, which yields no utility, and the arrival of each task; the value after the last node is zero. The state is the node and the set 𝑀 of attributes ac-quired so far; an action asks any subset 𝑆 of unacquired attributes with Í𝑚 ∈𝑆 𝑐 𝑚 ≤ 𝐵 . Because the budget resets at every node, no remaining-budget dimension is needed. Values use hidden answers, the actual future tasks, the shared solver and NU at the evalua-tion prices. Candidates exclude known, non-queryable and high-sensitivity attributes, attributes with 𝑐 𝑚 > 𝐵 , and attributes no task in the sequence uses, leaving 4–12 attributes per user at 𝐵 = 0.5. All feasible subsets at every node are enumerated with memoization and no pruning, so the solution is exact for this candidate pool; the largest number of cached states is 36,404 over all users at 𝑇 = 10 and 77,364 over the amortization cohort at 𝑇 = 20 (bound (𝑇 + 2) 2𝑛 

for 𝑛 candidates). Values within 10 −12 are tied and broken by the smallest cumulative asking cost so far, then the fewest acquisitions, then the bitmask, which favors deferring an acquisition when de-ferral loses nothing. The 0% before-use rate in Table 3 therefore shows that an NU-optimal schedule within this candidate pool never needs to acquire these attributes before first use, not that waiting is strictly better. Asking earlier can be strictly better when the budget binds: the logs contain two such early acquisitions at 

𝑇 = 10 (all users) and two at 𝑇 = 20 (amortization cohort), none of a designated attribute. For example, a restaurant-radius question (cost 0.2) and a meal-budget question (cost 0.4) do not fit together within 𝐵 = 0.5 at the task that uses both, so the radius is acquired one task earlier. 

Cost-matched heuristics. Random-𝑘 shuffles the remaining can-didates with one of five fixed seeds at each opportunity; Frequency ranks by the estimated reuse Í𝑓 ˆ𝑛 𝑓 𝑅 (𝑚, 𝑓 ) of §4.2; Impact-only ranks by max 𝑓 𝐼 (𝑚, 𝑓 ), recomputed after each answer, with ties broken by attribute ID; LLM-Importance keeps the backbone’s rank-ing within an opportunity. None applies a positive-value threshold: each asks candidates in rank order, skipping any that does not fit and continuing with later ones, under the smaller of the remaining per-interaction budget and the user’s remaining total cap, which is set to APA’s realized total asking cost; refused questions also consume the cap. The caps bound expenditure but do not equalize it. 

Missed valuable rate. An initially missing preference 𝑚 of user 

𝑢 is valuable if adding its true answer alone to 𝑃 0 raises realized CTU over the user’s 𝑇 tasks by more than its weighted cost 𝜆𝑐 𝑚 +

𝛽𝐶 risk (𝑚 ) plus a fixed margin of 0.2, so that only preferences with a clear net gain, not any positive gain, count as valuable. The margin is a descriptive threshold that was never tuned; it affects only which preferences MVMR counts, not any acquisition decision. Unlike the diagnostic of §6.2, this uses realized rather than public-belief gains and does not filter by 𝐵 . MVMR 𝑢 is the fraction of 𝑢 ’s valuable preferences that the policy did not acquire; it is averaged over users with at least one valuable preference (49 at 𝑇 = 10 and 102 at 𝑇 = 20 in the amortization cohorts), and undefined for the others.
