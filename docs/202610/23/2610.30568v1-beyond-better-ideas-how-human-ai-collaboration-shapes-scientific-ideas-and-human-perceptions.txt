# ABSTRACT 

As AI increasingly becomes a collaborator in scientific research, its impact may extend beyond improving ideas to reshaping how they are evaluated, attributed, and perceived by humans. Yet existing research has largely examined these di-mensions in isolation. We present a unified empirical study of Human–AI scientific ideation , investigating two complementary perspectives: i) how AI collaboration shapes scientific ideas and their evaluation , and ii) how firsthand collaboration reshapes human perceptions of AI . Through a shared experimental setting involv-ing Human–AI and Human–Human research teams, human and LLM evaluators, collaboration-provenance analysis, and participant surveys, we uncover several counterintuitive findings. First, AI collaboration shows no significant advantage in human-evaluated idea quality, while iterative revision consistently improves quality. Second, AI judges can approximate human scores yet disagree on idea rankings; notably, an AI judge favors AI-assisted proposals without a correspond-ing advantage in human evaluations. Third, collaboration provenance remains only partially detectable, revealing substantial overlap between Human–AI and Human–Human scientific outputs. Finally, firsthand AI collaboration is associ-ated with greater perceived usefulness but smaller efficiency gains than expected, suggesting a nuanced recalibration of human expectations. Overall, our findings challenge the notion that AI’s value in scientific ideation lies primarily in pro-ducing better ideas, revealing instead its broader influence on scientific creation, evaluation, attribution, and human perceptions .

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly moving from tools that merely retrieve or summarize scientific knowledge to active participants in the production of new knowledge. Researchers increas-ingly use generative AI to brainstorm hypotheses, formulate research questions, design experiments, and refine scientific arguments, while LLMs are simultaneously being explored as evaluators of re-search ideas, proposals, and scientific outputs. This convergence creates a consequential new regime for science: AI may increasingly participate on both sides of the scientific process—helping to gen-erate ideas and helping to decide which ideas are valuable . Understanding this regime is urgent. If AI changes what researchers propose, how those proposals are evaluated, or even researchers’ own beliefs about what constitutes good scientific reasoning, its influence can propagate far beyond individual interactions to shape the allocation of attention, resources, and ultimately the direction of scientific discovery. The central question is therefore no longer simply whether AI can gener-ate plausible research ideas, but how scientific ideation changes when AI becomes simultaneously a collaborator, an evaluator, and an increasingly difficult-to-observe contributor .Despite growing interest in AI-assisted science, these roles have largely been studied in isola-tion. Prior work has examined whether LLMs can generate novel hypotheses or research ideas (Si

Yet these research threads leave several fundamental questions unanswered. Does collaborating with AI actually pro-duce research ideas that human experts consider better? If AI itself evaluates those ideas, does it preserve human preferences, or does it systematically value AI-assisted work differently? As AI-generated and human-generated reasoning become increasingly intertwined, does the resulting methodology retain detectable traces of human intellectual participation? And beyond the artifacts themselves, does firsthand collaboration recalibrate researchers’ beliefs about AI’s scientific capa-bilities? These questions cannot be answered by studying generation, collaboration, or evaluation independently. They require observing the entire pipeline—from how research ideas are produced, to how humans and AI evaluate them, to what their provenance reveals, and finally to how collabo-ration changes the humans involved. To enable such an investigation, we construct a controlled empirical testbed centered on scientific research proposals. Our real-world corpus contains 70 one-page NLP/ML research proposals pro-duced by 35 student teams under Human+AI and Human+Human collaboration conditions, with both initial and revised submissions spanning 20 research questions. Each proposal is evaluated by human reviewers and multiple state-of-the-art LLM judges using the same rubric, covering novelty, feasibility, detail and clarity, broad applicability, correctness and soundness, and overall quality, to-gether with within-question rankings. To study whether collaboration provenance can be inferred beyond surface stylistic cues, we additionally construct a synthetic training corpus derived from 1,000 human-authored research papers, creating paired methodologies that differ in the information available during their construction, and evaluate transfer exclusively on the real student proposals. Finally, pre- and post-study questionnaires allow us to examine how direct collaboration changes participants’ perceptions of GenAI. This design connects creation, evaluation, provenance, and human perception within a single empirical framework rather than treating them as separate phe-nomena. Our results reveal a considerably more nuanced picture than either “AI improves science” or “AI fails to match humans.” First, Human+AI and Human+Human teams achieve nearly indistinguish-able overall quality under human evaluation; both improve substantially through revision, suggesting that the dominant benefit in our setting comes from iterative refinement rather than AI participation itself. Second, AI judges can appear surprisingly human-like under absolute scoring while still disagreeing with humans about which proposal is better : GPT-4.1 achieves the strongest score cali-bration among the tested models, yet a substantial gap remains in preference ranking, revealing that 

score calibration and preference alignment are distinct properties of an evaluator . Third, and more strikingly, evaluation is not neutral to collaboration provenance. GPT-4.1 assigns systematically higher scores to Human+AI proposals even when the human panel shows essentially no correspond-ing advantage, with the largest divergence appearing in “broad applicability”. Thus, introducing AI on both sides of the pipeline can create an evaluator-side amplification effect : AI may make AI-assisted work appear better without an equivalent shift in human judgment. Fourth, collaboration leaves a detectable but weak provenance signal: synthetic supervision transfers to real proposals and enables above-chance identification of collaboration condition, but accuracy remains only about 66–67%, indicating substantial overlap between methodologies produced with and without the cor-responding human contribution. Finally, firsthand Human–AI collaboration does not simply make participants more optimistic about AI. Instead, it recalibrates their mental model: participants per-ceive greater practical helpfulness and capability while simultaneously reporting less speed-up and slightly lower confidence in integrating GenAI than they initially expected. Together, these find-ings suggest that AI’s impact on scientific ideation is not a single capability gain, but a coupled transformation of how ideas are produced, judged, attributed, and understood by humans .Our work makes the following contributions: • An end-to-end empirical framework for AI-mediated scientific ideation. We jointly study AI as a collaborator, AI as an evaluator, collaboration provenance, and researchers’ perception shifts, connecting stages of the scientific ideation pipeline that prior work has largely examined independently. • A controlled proposal corpus linking collaboration to human and AI evaluation. We construct a dataset of 70 real research proposals from Human+AI and Human+Human teams, including 2revision trajectories, multi-dimensional human judgments, within-question rankings, and evalua-tions from multiple frontier LLMs, complemented by a synthetic corpus for studying provenance transfer. • A broader account of AI evaluation beyond human-score approximation. We show that re-liable AI evaluation cannot be reduced to reproducing human scores: an AI judge may be well calibrated in absolute scores while diverging from human preference orderings and, more conse-quentially, may systematically favor AI-assisted work even when human reviewers do not. These findings reveal AI evaluation as an active component of the Human–AI scientific ecosystem, where 

how knowledge is produced and who evaluates it can interact to shape conclusions about scientific quality. • A broader account of what Human–AI collaboration changes. Beyond proposal quality, we show that collaboration provenance remains partially detectable and that direct experience with GenAI recalibrates researchers’ expectations in multiple directions, providing evidence that AI changes not only scientific artifacts but also the human judgments and beliefs surrounding them. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Our work connects several lines of research that have largely developed separately: Human–AI collaboration, AI-assisted scientific ideation, LLM-based evaluation, collaboration provenance, and human perceptions of AI. 

Human–AI Collaboration and Scientific Ideation. Prior work has established LLMs as active collaborators in creative and knowledge-intensive tasks. CoAuthor (Lee et al., 2022) studied human– LM interaction during writing, while controlled experiments show that generative AI can improve productivity and output quality (Noy & Zhang, 2023), albeit with important tradeoffs. AI assistance can increase individual creativity while reducing collective diversity (Doshi & Hauser, 2024), and its benefits can reverse when tasks fall outside the model’s capability frontier (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026). Together, these results suggest that Human–AI collaboration should be evaluated across multiple dimensions rather than through a single measure of quality. This question is particularly consequential in scientific ideation. Chen et al. (Chen et al., 2026) find that AI use in scientific publications is associated with higher novelty and disruption on av-erage, with differences across levels of creativity, fields, and types of AI tools. Systems such as SciMON (Wang et al., 2024b) explicitly optimize scientific ideas for novelty, while increasingly au-tonomous systems such as the AI Scientist (Lu et al., 2024) automate substantial parts of the research workflow. Most closely related, Si et al. (Si et al., 2025) find that LLM-generated research ideas can be judged more novel but less feasible than human-generated ones. Whereas this literature primarily contrasts human-generated and AI-generated research, we study the intermediate and increasingly common regime of Human–AI collaboration : humans remain responsible for research design while AI participates in developing and revising the ideas. 

LLMs as Evaluators and Scientific Judges. A parallel literature asks whether LLMs can ap-proximate human judgments as scalable evaluators. Studies such as G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023) and MT-Bench/Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023) demonstrate substantial alignment between LLM and human evaluations, motivating broader use of LLM-as-a-Judge methods (Chiang & Lee, 2023; Bavaresco et al., 2025). Yet agreement varies substantially across tasks, criteria, and evaluation protocols (Thakur et al., 2024), and LLM judges exhibit systematic biases even when aggregate human–AI agreement appears strong (Gu et al., 2024). Scientific evaluation makes this problem especially challenging: novelty, feasibility, and soundness are inherently multidimensional, and even expert reviewers exhibit substantial disagreement (Cortes & Lawrence, 2021; Beygelzimer et al., 2023). Indeed, GPT-generated scientific feedback can over-lap with human feedback at levels approaching human–human agreement (Liang et al., 2023b). We therefore use agreement among human reviewers as a benchmark for evaluating AI judges and, importantly, distinguish score calibration from preference alignment : assigning human-like scores does not necessarily imply ranking scientific ideas as humans do. 3Creator–Evaluator Interaction. Existing research has identified position, verbosity, and other biases in LLM judges (Zheng et al., 2023), including evidence that models can recognize and pref-erentially evaluate their own generations (Panickssery et al., 2024). We extend this question from properties of the generated text to the process that produced it . Specifically, we ask whether an AI judge evaluates work created through Human–AI collaboration differently from comparable Hu-man+Human work, even when human evaluators show no such preference. This motivates a broader creator–evaluator interaction : measured gains from AI-assisted science may depend not only on the quality of the resulting work, but also on who evaluates it. This interac-tion becomes increasingly important as AI participates on both the creation and evaluation sides of the scientific pipeline. 

Detecting Collaboration Provenance. AI-generated text detection has proven fragile: paraphras-ing can substantially degrade detectors (Sadasivan et al., 2024; Krishna et al., 2023), while detec-tors may capture unintended linguistic characteristics such as non-native writing style (Liang et al., 2023a). Related work has therefore also considered corpus-level estimation of AI-modified con-tent (Liang et al., 2024). Our provenance problem is fundamentally different from conventional human-vs.-AI detection. Both of our conditions involve human authors; we instead ask whether the collaboration pro-cess —Human+AI versus Human+Human—leaves detectable signatures in the resulting scientific methodology. This shifts provenance analysis from identifying who wrote a text to inferring how an intellectual artifact was produced. 

Human Perceptions of AI Collaboration. Human–AI teaming also depends on how people per-ceive their AI partners. Prior work shows that expectations and pre-existing attitudes shape will-ingness to collaborate with AI (Zhang et al., 2021), while educational studies highlight the role of norms and disclosure expectations in students’ use of generative AI (Adnin et al., 2025). We comple-ment these studies by examining perceptions around a concrete scientific collaboration experience, asking whether hands-on Human–AI ideation recalibrates participants’ views of AI’s usefulness, trustworthiness, efficiency, and role in research. 

# 3 DATA COLLECTION 

Our study integrates two complementary types of data to capture both the outputs and the human experience of Human–AI scientific collaboration. First, our scientific ideation data combine a large-scale synthetic corpus for model training with a controlled real-world corpus of research pro-posals for evaluation. The real-world proposals are further annotated by both human reviewers and AI judges using the same multidimensional scientific-quality rubric. Second, our participant ques-tionnaire data capture participants’ expectations and perceptions of generative AI before and after the scientific-ideation study. Jointly, these data allow us to study Human–AI scientific collaboration from three complementary perspectives: the artifacts it produces, how those artifacts are evaluated, and how the experience changes the humans involved. 3.1 SCIENTIFIC IDEATION DATA 

Synthetic Training Corpus. To obtain scalable supervision without using any real participant data for training, we construct a synthetic corpus from 1,000 human-authored research papers. Each paper provides a research question together with its original methodology, allowing us to construct two controlled methodology-generation conditions. For each paper, GPT-4.1 generates two methodology descriptions. In the human-grounded condi-tion, the model receives the research question ( RQ ) together with information from the source paper, grounding its methodology in an existing human-designed solution. In the RQ-only condition, GPT-4.1 receives only the research question and independently generates a methodology. We use these conditions as synthetic proxies for Human+Human and Human+AI methodological development, respectively. Importantly, GPT-4.1 verbalizes both conditions; the controlled distinction is therefore the source of methodological reasoning , rather than surface authorship. This procedure yields 2,000 methodology instances—1,000 per condition. 4Real-World Proposal Corpus as Testing Data. The real-world corpus comes from a controlled Human–AI scientific ideation study in which participants worked in teams to develop one-page research proposals addressing predefined NLP/ML research questions. Teams operated under either a Human+Human or Human+AI collaboration condition. The corpus contains 70 proposal identities—36 Human+AI and 34 Human+Human—covering 20 research questions, listed in Appendix Table 9. The study spans both undergraduate and graduate cohorts. Each team submitted an initial proposal (v1) and a revised proposal (v2) one week later, providing a longitudinal view of how scientific ideas evolve through revision. Then, those real-world proposals are evaluated independently by human reviewers and AI judges using an identical scientific-quality rubric. Evaluators assign integer scores from 1 ( very poor ) to 5 ( outstanding ) for five dimensions: novelty , feasibility , detail and clarity , broad applicability , and 

correctness and soundness , together with an overall quality score. Each assessment is accompanied by a short written justification. Evaluators additionally rank proposals addressing the same research question from strongest to weakest. The annotations therefore provide two complementary views of scientific judgment: absolute quality , captured by multidimensional scores, and relative preference ,captured by within-RQ rankings. This common protocol enables direct comparison of human and AI evaluation. No real-world proposal is used to train or fine-tune the provenance classifier. The real corpus there-fore provides a strict synthetic-to-real transfer evaluation. 3.2 PARTICIPANT QUESTIONNAIRE DATA

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We investigate five interconnected research questions to understand how scientific ideation changes when AI serves not only as a collaborator in developing research ideas but also as an evaluator of their quality. Rather than examining AI generation and evaluation in isolation, we study their inter-actions across the scientific ideation pipeline. We first ask whether Human+AI collaboration pro-duces research proposals that human experts consider better, and how other factors shape proposal quality ( RQ1 ). We then examine whether AI judges can faithfully reproduce human assessments, 5distinguishing absolute score agreement from relative preference alignment ( RQ2 ). Crucially, we in-vestigate whether AI judges systematically favor AI-assisted proposals even when human reviewers do not, revealing potential interactions between how ideas are produced and how they are evaluated (RQ3 ). Beyond evaluation, we ask whether collaboration leaves detectable traces in the resulting proposals ( RQ4 ), and whether firsthand Human+AI collaboration reshapes researchers’ perceptions of AI’s scientific capabilities ( RQ5 ). Jointly, these questions move beyond whether AI improves individual research tasks to examine how its simultaneous roles as collaborator, evaluator, and con-tributor may reshape the production, assessment, attribution, and human perception of GenAI. 4.1 RQ1: H OW DO DIFFERENT FACTORS (E.G., COLLABORATION STYLE , PROPOSAL REVISION , RESEARCHER EXPERIENCE , AND TASK DIFFICULTY ) AFFECT THE QUALITY OF SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH PROPOSALS ?

Motivation. As AI becomes increasingly involved in scientific ideation, a fundamental question is whether AI collaboration actually improves the quality of research ideas, and how its contribu-tion compares with other factors shaping scientific creativity. Beyond collaboration style, proposal quality may depend on iterative refinement, researchers’ experience, and the difficulty of the re-search problem. Understanding their relative roles is essential for distinguishing the benefits of AI assistance from those of established research practices. 

Method. We first compare Human+AI and Human+Human proposals across both submission rounds. This comparison tests whether reviewers assigned different overall scores or quality pro-files to the two collaboration conditions. Human+AI and Human+Human proposals addressed the same research question in only one of the 20 cases. We therefore interpret the condition estimates as associations in this sample rather than effects caused solely by AI collaboration. We next study how each team’s proposal changed from v1 to v2. Each team continued working on the same research problem, so the within-team comparison directly captures changes made dur-ing the one-week revision period. Finally, we compare the gains of the two conditions using the condition-by-round interaction: 

∆∆ = (v2 − v1) Human+AI − (v2 − v1) Human+Human .

A positive ∆∆ indicates a larger revision gain for Human+AI; a negative value indicates a larger gain for Human+Human. Each observation is one reviewer’s score for one proposal. Each team contributed both v1 and v2. For each research question, reviewers scored and ranked all proposals submitted by the different teams. We use a linear mixed model so that these repeated observations do not count as indepen-dent evidence. The model accounts for consistent differences among reviewers and teams when estimating the collaboration-condition and revision effects. We use overall quality and normalized rank as the primary outcomes and analyze the five rubric dimensions as exploratory outcomes. For each comparison, this produces five dimension-level tests. Testing several dimensions increases the chance that one will appear significant by chance. We therefore adjust each set of five p-values using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate pro-cedure; q denotes the adjusted p-value. For v1–v2 changes, we report dz , which expresses the mean within-team change in standard-deviation units. For collaboration-condition differences, we report Hedges’ g, which expresses the between-condition difference in standard-deviation units and includes a small-sample correction.

Averaged across v1 and v2, Hu-man+AI proposals scored 0.04 points higher than Human+Human proposals (95% CI [−0.31 , 0.40] ,Hedges’ g = 0 .08 ). The small effect size and the confidence interval spanning zero indicate similar overall ratings in the two conditions. Conclusions. Our findings reveal that proposal revision is more consistently associated with quality improvement than collaboration style . Human+AI collaboration does not significantly outperform Human+Human collaboration under human evaluation, although the two settings ex-hibit suggestive differences across quality dimensions. Undergraduate proposals outperform gradu-ate proposals on selected measures, while task difficulty shows no detectable association. Together, these results challenge the assumption that AI assistance or greater academic experience necessarily produces better scientific ideas, and highlight the importance of iterative refinement and multidi-mensional human evaluation. 4.2 RQ2: H OW WELL DOES AI-AS -J UDGE ALIGN WITH HUMAN JUDGMENT ?

Motivation. A key question for AI-assisted scientific evaluation is whether LLM judges can reli-ably evaluate research proposals at a level comparable to human reviewers, enabling them to assist or partially replace human evaluation. To answer this question, we compare AI judgments against the aggregated human panel and further benchmark them against the agreement between individual human reviewers and the panel. This reviewer–panel agreement serves as a reference for assessing whether AI judgments approach human-level consistency. 

Method & Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate four representative LLM judges on the 70 propos-als: GPT-4.1 (OpenAI, 2025), Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2025), Claude Sonnet 4.6 (An-thropic, 2026), and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct (Qwen Team, 2024). Each model follows the same two-stage evaluation pipeline. First, every proposal is independently scored on the six evaluation di-mensions using a 1–5 scale. Second, proposals belonging to the same research question are ranked according to their predicted quality without re-scoring. The following templates summarize the prompts used for proposal scoring and within-question ranking. The same templates were used for GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Qwen 2.5 72B Instruct.

Evaluation Metrics. For each proposal, we average human reviewers’ scores separately for each criterion and their ranks within the corresponding research question (Section 4.1). We compare AI scores and rankings with these human-panel averages. We also compare individual human reviewers with the same averages using the same metrics. Their agreement provides the human baseline for interpreting AI performance. For score assessment, we report Mean Absolute Error (MAE), bias, Spearman’s ρ, and quadratic weighted Cohen’s κ. MAE measures the average absolute difference between an AI score and the corresponding human-panel mean, with lower values indicating closer numerical agreement. Bias is the mean signed difference between AI and human-panel scores: positive values indicate over-scoring relative to humans, negative values indicate under-scoring, and values closer to zero indicate less systematic deviation. Spearman’s ρ measures whether proposals receiving higher human scores also tend to receive higher AI scores, with higher values indicating stronger agreement in ordering. Quadratic weighted Cohen’s κ measures agreement beyond chance between AI scores and rounded human-panel scores, penalizing larger score disagreements more heavily; higher values indicate stronger agreement. For ranking, we report Kendall’s τ , Spearman’s 

ρ, rank MAE, top-1 accuracy, bottom-1 accuracy, and pairwise concordance. Top-1 accuracy is the percentage of research-question groups in which the AI and human panel identify the same strongest proposal. Bottom-1 accuracy similarly measures agreement on the weakest proposal. When an extreme rank is tied, a match is counted if the AI and human sets share at least one proposal. Pairwise concordance is the percentage of within-question proposal pairs ordered in the same direction by the AI and human panel, excluding pairs tied by either evaluator. Higher values indicate stronger agreement for all three metrics.

# 5 CONCLUSION 

As AI increasingly participates in both creating and evaluating scientific ideas, understanding its influence on scientific discovery becomes essential. Our findings reveal that AI collaboration does not necessarily improve research quality beyond human collaboration, while iterative refinement remains a powerful driver of improvement. More importantly, AI judges can systematically favor AI-assisted proposals despite comparable human evaluations, raising concerns about feedback loops in AI-mediated science. Meanwhile, collaboration leaves only weakly detectable traces in scientific artifacts and reshapes researchers’ perceptions of AI’s capabilities and limitations. Together, these findings suggest that the future of AI-driven science depends not simply on making AI a more capable collaborator, but on understanding and governing the interactions among human creativity, AI assistance, and scientific evaluation. Ultimately, trustworthy AI for science must advance not only how knowledge is created, but also how its quality, provenance, and value are established.
