## Same Context, Different Agents: A Behavioral Study of Semantic Layers, Orchestration and Compaction in Data Agents 

Zhivar Sourati 1,2 Marianne Menglin Liu 2 Zheng Wang 2

Rongguang Wang 2 Sai Ashish Somayajula 2 Tao Sheng 2

Kyu J. Han 2 Sujith Ravi 2 Dan Roth 2,3 1University of Southern California 2Oracle AI 3University of Pennsylvania 

Abstract 

Answering questions over heterogeneous data means combining evidence from files, databases, documents and the web. Data agents automate this, but their success depends on context. Three context-engineering techniques (CETs) help by shaping it: semantic layers de-scribe the data, orchestration splits work among agents, and compaction condenses interaction history. Existing studies validate each CET alone, leaving open how agent behavior shapes its effect. We present a comprehensive anal-ysis of the three CETs, alone and combined, with seven large language models (LLMs) on 1,514 long-horizon tasks from five benchmarks, covering behavior, accuracy and cost. We find that data agents without CETs form three be-havior groups: investigators, premature answer-ers that answer without inspecting data, and early stoppers that give up. Each CET delivers its known benefit, from more investigation to smaller prompts, but only the behavior group it matches turns that benefit into accuracy, such as premature answerers under orchestration. For investigators, the same CETs can hurt: the se-mantic layer stops them from looking, orches-tration makes them overconfident, costing 5 percentage points, and compaction makes them repeat steps. Combining CETs yields smaller gains and losses than their sum. We derive guidance for choosing CETs by agent behavior, task demands and accuracy-cost trade-offs.

Each is usually studied alone, in a setting built for it, and judged by its average accuracy ef-fect, although all three edit the same finite prompt (Section 5). Such studies tell whether a CET helps, but not what it changes in how an agent works, for which models or when CETs meet. Practitioners need to know which failure their agent has, which CET repairs it and when it breaks. Asked the question in Figure 1, GPT-5.4 opens the file that answers it and replies São Paulo; given a semantic layer naming that file and saying to ver-ify values with tools, it repeatedly replies “Lima” without any tool, as if the layer gave it the answer. GPT-5.5 answers which of four exchanges had the highest average market value among mining firms in 5-7 calls; with compaction it records the same answer by call nine, then “verifies” it for about 300 more actions. Alone, GPT-6 Astra correctly answers in four calls whether any metro area had more identity-theft than fraud reports; with orches-tration, its verifier refuses a table labeled “Fraud and Other” as a stand-in for fraud and after 41 calls reports the question cannot be determined. Such cases raise four research questions (RQs; Figure 2B-C). RQ1 : How do LLMs behave and fail as data agents without CETs? RQ2 : Does each CET help every model, and at what cost? RQ3 :What does each CET change in how an agent works: does a semantic layer make it look at the data (in-spect it with tools) or stop looking, does orchestra-tion make it investigate or box a strong one in, and does compaction save context and money or lose what was found? RQ4 : Do CETs add up? We study these questions where CETs are pushed to their limits: long-horizon tasks whose evidence is spread over files, databases, documents and the web, where the agent must choose what to inspect. Each CET is switched on or off on top of a baseline agent , a single tool-using agent with a growing context, and we test all eight combina-tions with seven LLMs on five benchmarks (1,514 tasks), with model, tools, limits and prompt fixed. A CET’s effect is not a property of the CET but of its match with the agent’s behavior: the same CET can rescue one model and degrade another, and what it was designed to improve, such as tool use, calls and prompt size, can improve while answers get worse. Orchestration gives models that answer before looking 23 points at four times the cost; the layer gives models that give up a larger gain than orchestration at a fraction of its cost; compaction cuts their give-ups on the longest tasks; and no CET raises the accuracy of agents that already in-vestigate. Choosing a CET is thus a question about the agent, not a default. Our contributions are: (1) a controlled evaluation of data agents under every combination of seman-tic layers, orchestration and compaction, which prior work evaluates one at a time, measuring ac-curacy, behavior, cost and each benchmark’s pro-cess measures; (2) a behavioral analysis of every 2run’s trace that sorts the seven LLMs into three behavior groups and shows that each CET helps the groups whose habit it repairs while harming the investigators, and that combined CETs yield smaller gains and losses than their sum; (3) an analysis of wrong answers showing that selecting the wrong slice of data remains the most common cause after the agent inspects the data, under every combination of CETs; and (4) practical guidance for choosing and combining CETs by agent behav-ior, task demands and the accuracy-cost trade-off. 

2 Framework 

An agent answers a question by acting on files, databases, documents and, where allowed, the web. Switching each CET on or off on top of a baseline agent gives eight configurations that share the same model, tools, decoding settings and budget of steps and calls (Figure 2B; Appendix A). 

Baseline agent. A single tool-using agent (Yao et al., 2022). At each step it writes a thought and issues one action (Python, SQL, a file operation, or a web or document search), observes the result and decides what to do next, until it answers. It there-fore already plans, inspects any source, tests inter-mediate results with code and corrects its course when an action fails or a result looks wrong. Its prompt tells it that computed values must come from executed code over the data, not from the question’s wording, and to keep working until it has computed the answer. The CETs do not add functions the baseline agent lacks; they change its context and how its work is organized. 

Semantic layer. A description of the data, writ-ten offline once per benchmark by an LLM from the data, with no answers or task-specific hints. For every source it records the ontology (what the source, its tables and columns are), the data pro-file (value samples, units, missing values, parsing recipes) and the contextualization (join keys, re-lated sources, ambiguous names). The entries most relevant to the question go into the prompt before the first step, and the agent must verify them. 

Orchestration. A planner without tools splits the question into subtasks and delegates them to specialist agents for files, Python, SQL, or web and document search. Each uses the baseline agent’s tools in its own context and returns a condensed result. A verifier accepts each result or sends the work back. With the layer on, the planner passes the relevant entries to each specialist. 

Compaction. The agent carries a bounded work-ing memory instead of the full history, rewritten after every step: the goal, what is done, computed values with their evidence and open items. 

3 Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. We use five benchmarks with 1,514 tasks in total, counting FDABench’s report and choice tasks as two. KramaBench (Lai et al., 2026) (104 tasks) asks questions over data lakes of many files with irregular layouts. DataClaw 

(Zhang et al., 2026) (492 tasks) poses consulting questions over noisy enterprise tables and policy texts with entity names in two languages, scored by an LLM judge. FDABench (Wang et al., 2026) joins a database with documents and web evi-dence, as 218 report tasks scored by a rubric and 320 multiple-choice tasks. HybridDeepResearch 

(HDR; Wu et al. 2026; 380 tasks) gives the agent no schema (it must discover tables by querying) and chains clues across the database and the web. 

Models. GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5 and GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI, 2026a,b,c), Claude Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, 2026a,b), Gemini 2.5 Flash (Co-manici et al., 2025) and the open-weight GLM-4.7-Flash (Zeng et al., 2025), served locally, at provider defaults (Appendix C); GPT-5.5 wrote the layers. 

Metrics and analysis. Accuracy is each bench-mark’s own score (Appendix L). Cost is dollars per task at list prices (Appendix K). A CET’s effect is the change when it is switched on, averaged over the settings of the other two. Accuracy effects are in percentage points with 95% bootstrap intervals over tasks, and group effects average their models.

We answer the research questions in turn: how the models behave as baseline agents (RQ1, Sec-tion 4.1), whether each CET helps every model (RQ2, Section 4.2), what each changes in how an agent works (RQ3, Sections 4.3 to 4.5) and whether the three add up (RQ4, Section 4.6). 

4.1 Three Groups of Baseline Agents 

Under identical tools and instructions, the seven baseline agents fall into three behavior groups, in-vestigators , premature answerers and early stop-pers , which clustering recovers exactly (Figure 3). Investigators lead every benchmark (Table 1). For each group, our analyses rule out the obvious ex-planation. 

Investigators’ accuracy advantage is linked to grounding. Opus, Sonnet and Astra act in almost every run, ground most answers in tool outputs and rarely give up. Their edge is not knowledge: overall, answers without any tool are right in 7% of runs, against 44% after looking (Appendix B). 

Premature answering can persist despite rea-soning. GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.4 often reply without any tool (on 54% and 24% of tasks), although the prompt tells them to compute from the data. When GPT-5.5 does act, its accuracy is more than twice as high, so this is a habit, not a lack of ability or of thought: more reasoning does not reliably remove it, while orchestration, which demands evidence, does (Appendix C and Section 4.3). 

Early stoppers abandon solvable tasks despite substantial effort. Gemini and GLM act, but give up with “no relevant data” in about 15% of runs and repeat an identical action in 28-29%. They do not give up for lack of data or effort: when they do, they have usually touched every source type the task needs, after three times as many actions as an investigator’s typical run, and at least one investi-gator solves 80% of those tasks (Appendix D). After looking, all groups fail alike, mostly by selecting the wrong data slice or misreading docu-ments and web evidence, rarely by arithmetic, and no CET removes these causes (Figure 14).

Process scores can rise while accuracy falls. The benchmarks’ process scores, the share of expected steps a run takes, rise most with orchestration (Fig-ure 5), but do not tell whether answers improve: orchestration raises investigators’ process score by 11 points while their accuracy falls by 5. Neither shows how an agent works, so we measure behav-ior (Figures 6 and 17), in three blocks: engagement (Acts, Coverage, Persists), reliability (Grounded, No give-up, No repeats, Long loops, Recovers) and commitment (Answers, Flags uncertainty). 

Behavior shows what the scores hide. Orchestra-tion raises engagement, most for premature answer-ers, who act, cover sources and persist in 36-50 points more runs, and its cost falls on commitment, which neither score sees: investigators, whose pro-cess score rises while their accuracy falls, flag un-certainty and end with an answer less often, as do the early stoppers. The layer raises reliability at the expense of engagement: runs repeat and loop less in every group but persist less, so investiga-tors’ unchanged scores hide that they look less. Compaction barely changes either score but adds repeated actions in all groups. A CET helps when it changes the behavior the agent lacks; otherwise it changes the agent without improving it.

The gain is not a larger budget. A single GPT-5.5 agent forced to act before answering, or to spend as many calls as orchestration, closes at most 37% of the gap (Figure 19): it runs one action, then repeats the answer it gave before looking. What orchestration adds is structure: a planner that asks for evidence, and specialists that must return it. 

Orchestration also has a cost. For investigators, it lowers accuracy by 5 points at 1.7 times the dollars, mostly on tasks that chain a database with docu-ments and web search (6-12 points), and not for lack of data: it usually reaches every needed source (Appendix G). 

It changes how an answer is judged and stated. 

The verifier approves 87% of the investigators’ wrong answers, or, for Astra, rejects usable evi-dence until the planner abstains on 15.5% of HDR tasks, and investigators hedge far less. Orchestra-tion makes a careful agent more confident and less thorough. 

4.4 Semantic Layers Help Early Stoppers but Can Discourage Investigation For the weaker groups, the layer works. The semantic layer is built to help in three ways: the ontology says what each source is, the data pro-file how to read it, and the contextualization how sources relate. It names candidate sources, contains no answers and tells the agent to check every entry. It works as built (Figure 7): told that an answer exists and where it lives, the models that give up most halve their give-ups on DataClaw, and early stoppers ground more answers and loop less. Each part does what it is built for (Appendix H): with the ontology, agents open the source it lists first in 27-42% of runs, against 4-13%; with the data profile, 16-20 points fewer runs fail to read a file; and the contextualization’s join keys are used where joins are needed. 

Fewer calls do not mean lower cost. A layer is also expected to make an agent cheaper, and every group needs 22-30% fewer calls; but the layer is in every prompt, so dollars rise 1.1-1.9 times, and only for the early stoppers is the gain nearly free. 

The layer also has a side effect: told what the data contains, the investigators look less. Opus stops using tools in 73 runs where its baseline agent used them and starts in only 11, and on the investi-gators’ runs that stop looking, accuracy falls from 68% to 31%. The layer is rarely wrong: of 100 investigator answers that went from right to wrong with the layer, it misled the agent in 2, while in 25 the agent stopped checking. Nor does the writer matter much: a layer written by a 3B model gives nearly the same gain as one by GPT-5.5 (Figure 24). The layer is a guide, and the investigators treat it as the answer. If a layer correctly says a sales ta-ble lists a price per product, for instance, the agent reads that column and answers, where without the layer it would open the table and notice a second column of discounted prices. Although told to ver-ify every entry, they stop checking, the checking that sets them apart (Appendix H). 4.5 Compaction Bounds Context but Can Increase Cost and Repetition Compaction delivers the benefit it is known for. 

Each prompt stays near three times the size of the first prompt (Figure 8), whereas without com-paction the full transcript grows to fourteen times. GLM-4.7-Flash, with its short context window, no longer has to truncate its history when it orches-trates. The working memory keeps what matters: after every DataClaw rewrite, most models kept 92-100% of computed values. 

Its accuracy effect depends on task length. 

Where the history grows longest, compaction does most for the agents that lose track of it: on tasks that take other models longest, the early stoppers give up less and do 2.5 points better than on their other tasks, and GPT-5.4 gives up less. On short tasks, with little to compress, early stoppers lose 8 points, as each rewrite risks losing what was found. 

It also has costs: a smaller prompt is not a smaller bill. Every step adds a call that rewrites the memory, so total input barely changes (4-17% more tokens) while output, the costliest token, grows 2.3 to 3.5 times, and dollars rise 1.2 to 1.8 times. 

And some agents loop. A model that distrusts its result keeps checking it: 81% of premature an-swerers’ repeats re-check a value still in memory, and 42% of GPT-5.5’s DataClaw runs reach 100 or more calls (none without it) at eight times the cost. 

4.6 Combining CETs Offers Limited Added Gains but Can Mitigate Harms Combined effects are smaller than the sum of their parts. One might expect CETs to help each other, each fixing what others miss. For accuracy they mostly do not (Table 2): they fix the same behaviors, so each helps less when another is on, and their harms do not add up either (Appendix J). Each pair shows a different side of this. 

The layer adds little to orchestration. The layer and orchestration both help agents find data. One might expect the layer to make orchestration cheaper, since its specialists search less, and they make fewer calls; but every prompt carries the layer, so the cost rises while accuracy barely does. 

Orchestration and compaction combine worst.

5 Related Work 

Data agent benchmarks. LLM evaluation on data has moved from one query over one database (Lei et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024) to agents that find, read and combine evidence across data lakes of irregular files (Lai et al., 2026), enter-prise tables and policy texts (Zhang et al., 2026), databases joined with documents and the web (Wang et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026), and hetero-geneous workspaces (Ma et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). Even strong agents are far from reliable (Jing et al., 2025): the best published KramaBench agent scores 56%, humans 77% (Lai et al., 2026). They also trace part of the difficulty to how agents behave, not what they can reach: giving an agent only the gold files raises KramaBench accuracy by 0-7% (Lai et al., 2026), DataClaw finds mod-els stopping early on tasks they fail (Zhang et al., 2026), and reasoning models can cost more without answering better (Wang et al., 2026). 

Context-engineering techniques. A line of work adds techniques that manage what the model sees. A semantic layer describes the data, and such de-scriptions raise the accuracy of question answer-ing over databases (Sequeda et al., 2024; Rumi-antsau and Fokeev, 2026; Somayajula et al., 2026); schema selection helps weaker text-to-SQL models in particular (Maamari et al., 2024). Orchestration splits the work among agents with their own con-texts (Wu et al., 2023; Hadfield et al., 2025) and helps most when the single agent is weak (Kim et al., 2025). Compaction bounds the history and, with optimized or trained compression, keeps accu-racy at lower peak token use (Packer et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2025). The same studies report limits, mostly for strong models: schema selection and a static semantic layer can lower the accuracy of capable models (Maamari et al., 2024; Chong et al., 2026), a single agent given the same budget can match a multi-agent sys-tem (Tran and Kiela, 2026), and plain summaries are no better than dropping old observations (Lin-denbauer et al., 2025). The closest studies relate orchestration’s benefit to the single agent’s strength (Kim et al., 2025) and compare ways of delivering a semantic layer (Chong et al., 2026); both judge by accuracy. We study the CETs together across seven LLMs and describe each by the behavior it changes and the failures it repairs, relative to the agent’s habits. 

6 Practical Guidance and Conclusion 

Context-engineering techniques are usually judged by their average effect; our results show that what they do depends on the agent’s habits. The same CET can rescue one agent and hurt another, and it can hurt while the measures it was built to improve, tool use, calls, prompt size and the benchmarks’ process scores, look better. The agents most at risk are the strongest: given a semantic layer, investiga-tors treat it as the answer and stop checking, and under orchestration they hedge less and approve wrong answers. What helps weaker agents is not more compute but the CET that matches their habit: orchestration, which demands evidence, for agents that answer without looking, and a semantic layer for agents that give up. Compaction keeps prompts small but not the bill, and is safest with a layer, which keeps compacted runs from looping. None of the three fixes the most common remaining fail-ure, choosing the wrong data after looking, which is where work on data agents should turn next. 8Limitations 

Our study covers seven LLMs from four providers, from frontier models to a small open-weight model served locally, under all eight combinations of three CETs on five benchmarks. Models change quickly, and newer ones may show behaviors beyond the three groups we observe; the behavioral measures we use make it straightforward to place a new model among them, and extending the study to more models and model families is a natural next step. We study three widely used CETs, each in one representative design. Context engineering also includes other techniques, and other designs of the ones we study. Our aim is not to cover them all but to show that CETs interact with the agent’s behavior and with each other in ways their average effects do not reveal; applying the same behavioral lens to other techniques and designs is promising future work. Our tasks ask agents to answer questions over heterogeneous and messy data from files, databases, documents and the web. Other domains, such as mathematical reasoning or software engineering, place different demands on agents, and whether the same behavior groups and interactions appear there is an interesting question for future work. Finally, we evaluate CETs as fixed configurations; systems that choose or adapt CETs to an agent’s observed behavior, which our results suggest could pay off, are a direction for future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

Our study evaluates context-engineering tech-niques for data agents, not their readiness for au-tonomous deployment. We find that the same technique can improve one model while degrad-ing another, and that reduced cost or increased tool use does not necessarily imply more accurate answers. Semantic layers can encourage unsup-ported conclusions, compaction can omit relevant evidence, and orchestration can propagate errors or introduce unnecessary computation. These failures could have serious consequences when analytical outputs inform medical, legal, financial, or other high-stakes decisions. Such applications require task-specific validation, traceable evidence, and appropriate human oversight. Agents accessing files, databases, or web sources should also oper-ate with least-privilege permissions and safeguards against unintended disclosure or modification of data. Finally, our behavioral groupings describe patterns observed under the evaluated tasks and configurations, rather than fixed properties of mod-els. We encourage practitioners to validate context-engineering choices against answer correctness and evidence provenance, rather than relying on aggre-gate benchmark gains or efficiency measures alone.

All eight configurations run in one harness with the same tools and decoding settings, and a budget of up to 300 steps and 600 model calls per task. With orchestration, a finalizer writes the answer once the verifier has accepted the special-ists’ results. With compaction, each rewrite of the working memory is one extra model call, and the layer’s entries sit outside the memory, so a rewrite never drops them. We use the FDABench report and multiple-choice tasks that have reference an-swers. Appendix M gives the software, hardware and time per run. 

B Behavior and Task Features 

This section distinguishes the measurements used for clustering from those used to describe the re-sulting groups. Twelve trace features define the model groups and run types; eight behavioral indi-cators summarize their behavior in the radar charts. We also describe the annotation of uncertainty and stop reason and the 19 task-demand dimensions. Figure 9 shows each model’s baseline behavioral indicators, and Figure 11 shows each benchmark’s task-demand profile. 

Trace features used for clustering. We normal-ize every trace to model steps and executed actions. An executed action is a call whose action block (Python, SQL, schema linking, web, vector or file operation) has a saved tool observation. For or-chestrated runs, we count planner decisions and every specialist execution. We compute twelve features per run: whether the run acted, the share of steps with an executed action, the number of source families used, the number of distinct sources touched, the numbers of inspection actions, web searches and executions, the error rate of executed actions, the number of identical repeated actions, the number of actions after the last new informa-tion, whether the run ended by giving up, and whether it ended in another non-answer (budget, empty reply, refusal, abstention, plan). Counts are on a log scale. 

Clustering models and individual runs. We dis-tinguish model-level behavior groups from run-level behavior types . To identify model groups, we average the twelve standardized features over each model’s baseline runs. We apply Ward clustering to these seven model feature vectors and partition them into three groups. Complete linkage yields the same groups; average and single linkage merge GPT-5.4 with the investigators or isolate it. To identify individual run types, we apply k-means to the baseline runs using the same twelve features, standardized on baseline runs. We select the number of clusters by bootstrap stability mea-sured with the adjusted Rand index. We freeze this baseline fit and assign runs from every configura-tion to the nearest baseline centroid, making run types comparable across CETs. 

Behavioral indicators displayed in the radar charts. The radar charts use eight indicators to describe and interpret the behavior groups. Clus-tering uses the twelve trace features above; these indicators provide an interpretable summary of the resulting profiles. Acts : at least one executed action. 

Answer grounded : the submitted value appears in a tool output (KramaBench, DataClaw, HDR). Cov-ers sources : mean share of the needed source types touched. Persists : three or more executed actions. 

Recovers : mean share of failed calls followed by a successful one. No repeats : no identical repeated call. Flags uncertainty : an annotator labels the answer as hedged. No give-up : the run does not stop with “no data found”.

C Reasoning Effort 

All main runs use provider defaults, under which every model reasons except GPT-5.4 (the Claude models use adaptive thinking, which Claude Opus 5.5 cannot turn off; Anthropic, 2026a,b). To see whether answering before looking is a setting or a habit, we ran the baseline agent of both models on KramaBench (104 tasks) and HDR (380 tasks) at other reasoning efforts: low and high for GPT-5.5, medium and high for GPT-5.4 (Figure 12). Everything else is unchanged. In the figure, the orange bars fall and the blue bars rise for GPT-5.4 as effort grows, while both stay nearly flat for GPT-5.5. For GPT-5.4, reasoning removes the habit. Its share of tasks answered without a tool falls from 24% to 8% (medium) and 3% (high) on Kram-aBench, and from 32% to 7% and 3% on HDR. Its accuracy rises by 35 points on KramaBench (.37 to .72 and .70; +35.1 [+25.1, +44.6] at medium, 57 tasks better and 8 worse) and by 35-36 points on HDR (.08 to .43 and .45; 157-162 tasks better, 16-17 worse). Both values exceed the best of its eight configurations without reasoning (.63 and .23). The cost per task rises from $0.07 to $0.18-$0.21 on KramaBench and from $0.10 to $0.53-$0.66 on HDR, about what orchestration costs it. For GPT-5.5, more reasoning moves the habit only a little. At high effort it still answers without a tool on 78% of KramaBench and 87% of HDR tasks (89% and 94% at the default), and its accu-racy does not change clearly (+4.3 [ −2.1, +11.3] and +0.2 [ −2.4, +2.9] points). Low effort raises the share without a tool to 96% and 98%. When it does act at high effort, its accuracy is again more than twice as high (.49 against .20 on KramaBench). Answering before looking therefore has two forms, with two different remedies. In GPT-5.4 it comes with reasoning switched off, and switching rea-soning on gives a larger gain than orchestration at about the same cost. In GPT-5.5 it persists at ev-ery effort: more thinking does not make the model look, and only orchestration, which asks for evi-dence from outside the model, does (Section 4.3). Reasoning and orchestration are therefore not in-terchangeable ways of spending more compute; which one helps depends on why the agent does not look.

H Semantic Layer: Further Results 

This section backs the layer results of Section 4.4: how the layer changes the kind of run (Figure 22), what each of its three parts does (Figure 23), what the layer covers and what still fails, and how much its author matters and whether it makes the models alike (Figure 24). 

The layer moves every group toward quick lookups, for different reasons. The layer moves every group toward a quick lookup, for different reasons (Figure 22). For the early stoppers it re-places failure: their long repetitive runs fall from 21% to 13% of runs and their stuck runs from 24% to 20%. For the investigators it replaces work: 14% of their runs move from exploring then computing to a quick lookup, and quick lookups rise from 31% to 45% of their runs. Whether the layer makes an agent stop or start looking depends on the model rather than its group: Opus stops looking in 73 runs and starts in 11, and Gemini in 68 against 26, while Sonnet, GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.5 start more often than they stop. The same layer that makes GPT-5.4 look makes Opus stop. 

How we check each part. Entries are retrieved through two channels, a lexical one that matches exact identifiers, codes and units and a dense one that matches the meaning of the information need, and the agent can retrieve again by writing a new query for each channel. The layer has three parts (Section 2): the ontology helps the agent find the right source, the data profile helps it read values correctly, and the contextualization helps it com-bine sources. We check each part where it is meant to help, comparing runs with and without the layer on the same task rather than adding the parts one by one, and count a part as used only when its content shows up in the agent’s code, SQL or tool calls. To see which part decided a changed answer, an LLM judge (GPT-5.4) read 600 such run pairs with the layer split into its three parts; a second judge (GPT-5.5) agrees moderately ( κ = .41 ). 

Each part does its job, and the ontology decides the most answers. Each part does its job (Fig-ure 23). With the ontology, agents start from the right place more often: the first file they open is the one the layer lists first in 27-42% of runs, against 4-13% without it. With the data profile, fewer runs fail to read a file: on KramaBench the share falls from 28% to 12% for the investigators, from 28% to 8% for the premature answerers and from 59% to 43% for the early stoppers. With the contextual-ization, agents use join keys where a join is needed (14-27% of runs) and almost never elsewhere (1-5%). Of the three, the ontology decides the most answers: per 1,000 runs, 47 fixes and 17 breaks, against 6 and 2 for the data profile and at most 5 and 2 for the contextualization. It helps most the agents that search least: it brings the early stoppers to a source they had missed in 619 runs and away from one in 427, while for the investigators, who had usually found the source themselves, the bal-ance is 77 against 448. Where a run applies a unit or format the layer states, wrong answers become right four times as often as the reverse; Opus, for example, divides an HDR success rate by 100 be-cause the layer says it is a percentage, and answers right. Runs that use a join key from the layer score 20 points higher than runs that do not. Because the layer’s entries ride in every prompt, input tokens rise 1.7 to 2.4 times. 

Agents use the layer as a guide, not as an answer. 

The layer is built to prevent the baseline agent’s failures (Section 4.1). It names several candidate files, and the right one is among them in 92-96% of cases; it says what each column means, with units and example values; it says how to read each file and how files join; and it tells the agent to check everything with its tools. Agents use it as a guide, not as an answer. When the first file it names is the wrong one, they open that file first about as often as without the layer (9-23% of runs, against 5-31%), most go to other files instead, and only 0-3% answer from it alone. What it gives them is hard to find on their own: for 80 of the 107 answers the ontology fixed, the judge found that the deciding fact would have taken further searching without the layer. When an answer still breaks, the layer is rarely the cause: in a sample of 300 broken answers, it misled the agent in 24 and played no part in 198. In the remaining 78, the agent stopped checking because the layer looked complete, chose a similar file over the right one, or opened the right file and read the wrong sheet or column. These breaks were easy to avoid: in 39 of the 64 that the ontology decided, the right information was one obvious step away. The investigators are not fooled by the layer so much as tempted to skip the check: the investigators were misled in 2 of 100 sampled breaks and stopped checking in 25, while the early stoppers were misled in 13. In most of the investigators’ stop-checking breaks the layer was correct and described the common case: it named one file, column, key or grain, and the agent took that as the whole picture, where its baseline run had inspected the data and found the second column, key or source. On KramaBench’s beach water-quality tasks, for example, the layer correctly says each datasheet holds one Enterococcus value per row; with it, Opus and Sonnet read only that column, where without it they had inspected the headers and also used the per-site columns. The layer can say what each column is, but not which one a given question needs; that choice, and the choice to check, stay with the agent. Its fixes show the same: most come from saying what a column means (48 of 107). 

A small author suffices, and the layer makes models alike in how they work. Writing a layer costs one pass of a model over every source, so it matters whether a small model can write it. On DataClaw, a layer written by a 3B model gives a GPT-5.4 single agent nearly the same gain as one written by GPT-5.5 (+12.7 against +14.7 points; Figure 24, left), and every author’s gain excludes zero. Because the layer helps the weaker models most, one may also expect it to make the models alike. It does so in how they work far more than in what they score (Figure 24, right). Across the seven models, the spread in giving up halves (standard deviation 6.2 to 3.2 points), and the spread in long loops and in grounded answers falls by about a third (9.7 to 6.4 and 12.9 to 8.1); the spread in reaching a gold source falls only from 17 to 14 points, and in accuracy from 20 to 18. The layer removes the failure modes that separate the weaker models from the strong ones (giving up, looping and answering without grounding) without costing the strong ones much, but it does not close the gap in finding and using the right data, which is where accuracy is decided. 

I Compaction: Further Results 

This section backs the compaction results of Sec-tion 4.5: how it changes the kind of run, where its gains come from, why some agents loop, and what it does to long runs. 

Compaction pushes the weaker groups into loops. Compaction changes little for the inves-tigators: no move between behavior types reaches 2.5% of their runs (Figure 25). For the weaker groups it pushes runs into loops: 5% of the pre-mature answerers’ runs and 6% of the early stop-pers’ move from exploring then computing into long repetitive runs, so that the early stoppers’ long repetitive runs rise from 11% to 23%. 

Where compaction helps, it helps for differ-ent reasons. Its two clearest gains have different sources. GPT-5.4 gains on DataClaw (+6.4 points, +14.7 for the baseline agent) at every task length, and fewer “no relevant data” replies explain only a small part of it (+1.5). GLM gains on HDR mostly where its full transcript had overflowed its context window (+7.1 points there against +0.9 elsewhere, although the difference is not significant), which is the problem compaction is built for. Agents also use what the memory keeps: on DataClaw, when the right value had appeared in a tool out-put, the premature answerers submit it more often with compaction (wrong answers in those runs fall by 4 points). The cost comes from the rewrites: runs make 1.7 to 3.1 times the calls, and rewritten prompts no longer reuse the provider’s cache. 

Loops follow the task, not the agent’s habits. 

The loops have two causes. The premature an-swerers repeat to re-check a result that is still in their memory (81% of their repeated actions); the early stoppers repeat to fetch again what a rewrite dropped (only 14% of their repeats re-check a kept result). Loops grow with the length of the work (for GPT-5.5, 22% of runs on tasks of one to three steps against 70% on tasks of eleven or more), and whether a run loops is predicted by how long the baseline run on the same task was and how much computation the task needs (AUC .79 and .76), not by the baseline agent’s habits (.50). K Cost 

This section explains how cost is computed and gives the cost of every model and configuration (Figure 29). Cost is dollars per task at each provider’s list price, accessed on 6 October 2026; prices range from $0.06 to $10 per million input tokens and from $0.40 to $50 per million output tokens, and are printed beside each model in Fig-ure 29. By group, orchestration multiplies dollars per task by 1.7, 4.0 and 2.3 for investigators, pre-mature answerers and early stoppers, the seman-tic layer by 1.9, 1.4 and 1.1, and compaction by 1.2, 1.4 and 1.8; the baseline agent costs $1.97, $0.18 and $0.15 per task. Input and output tokens are billed at the model’s own rates, and reasoning tokens as output. Where the provider recorded prompt-cache use (GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5 and GPT-6 Astra), cached tokens are billed at the cache price, using cache shares measured in a strati-fied sample of 2,800 runs; Claude recorded no cache use, and the usage records of Gemini and GLM have no cache field, so their cost is an upper bound. Group ratios and baseline costs are geo-metric means over a group’s models, and prices are public list prices. GLM-4.7-Flash, which we served locally and which is free on its provider’s API, is priced at the cheapest paid host. Billing all input at list price, or every repeated prompt prefix at the cache price, changes no group’s direction except the layer for early stoppers, which becomes cheaper (0.93 times) under the second bound. Figure 29 shows how far the models are apart: a baseline Astra run costs $8.46 per task and a baseline GLM run $0.055. Within a model, a CET can change the cost several-fold: compaction raises GPT-5.5 from $0.34 to $2.67 through its re-verification loops, and orchestration with com-paction to $5.27, while the layer halves Gemini’s baseline cost ($0.39 to $0.19).

35 M Implementation and Compute 

Software. The harness is written in Python 3.10 and calls every API model through an OpenAI-compatible chat-completion interface. Tool code runs in a separate Python process with a time limit, SQL runs on read-only copies of the bench-mark databases (SQLite, and BigQuery for the FDABench tasks hosted there), and web search uses Perplexity Sonar 1. The semantic layer’s dense channel embeds entries with text-embedding-3-small 2, and its lexical channel scores the term over-lap of identifiers, codes and units. The main li-braries are vLLM 0.22.1, PyTorch 2.11, pandas 2.3 and NumPy 2.2. 

Hardware. All models except GLM-4.7-Flash are accessed through their providers’ APIs, which do not disclose their parameter counts (GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude

Opus 5.5, Gemini 2.5 Flash and text-embedding-3-small), so these agents run on CPU nodes (48 cores and 256 GB of memory per job), each job running many tasks in parallel. GLM-4.7-Flash (30B pa-rameters, 3B active) is served with vLLM in BF16 on NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, with tensor paral-lelism of 2 or 4 and a context of 131,072 tokens. The open-weight layer authors of Figure 24, Minis-tral 3B, 8B and 14B (Liu et al., 2026a) and Mistral Small 3.2 24B 3, are served the same way on two A100 GPUs. 

Artifacts. We use the benchmarks as their cre-ators intend, for evaluation only. DataClawBench and FDABench are released under the MIT license and HybridDeepResearch under Apache-2.0; the BIRD (Li et al., 2023) and Spider (Yu et al., 2018) databases that FDABench draws on are under CC BY-SA 4.0, and KramaBench’s dataset card lists a Creative Commons license. The tasks are in English; DataClaw’s data, which its creators de-identified, includes entity names in Chinese. We collect no data about people, and the hand checks of task-demand labels were done by the authors. The semantic layers, run traces and labels we derive are intended for research use. 

Time per task. Pooled over the tasks of all five benchmarks and all eight configurations, solving one task with an API model takes a median of 6.6 minutes and 12 model calls: 4.5 minutes and 7 calls for a single agent, and 9 minutes and 17 calls with orchestration. By benchmark, the median ranges from 2.1 minutes and 5 calls on KramaBench to 9.1 minutes and 15 calls on FDABench reports. Solving one task with GLM-4.7-Flash takes a me-dian of 25 model calls, from 15 on FDABench multiple-choice to 47 on DataClaw. A semantic layer is written once per benchmark, in one pass of the author over every source: the DataClaw layer took about 1,800 calls and 17 minutes with GPT-5.5, and 31 to 58 minutes with the smaller authors; the KramaBench and FDABench layers, with about 47,000 and 350,000 entries, took several hours each.
