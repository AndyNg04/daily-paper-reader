ABSTRACT 

Computer-use agents increasingly handle private information and act on users’ behalf across desktop and web applications, including email. Phishing therefore poses privacy and security risks that depend not only on whether agents recognize malicious messages, but also on how they respond. Existing phishing datasets were developed primarily for conventional detection, leaving agent susceptibility to persuasive malicious requests insufficiently characterized. To expose vulnerabilities that pre-existing datasets may miss, we introduce AgentPhishing , a benchmark for evaluating whether persuasive phishing attacks can evade detection and induce LLM email agents to comply with malicious requests. We develop PhishOpt ,which optimizes reusable email-generation skills against individual agents under fixed benchmark constraints and a realism constraint. We analyze the generated emails using Cialdini’s persuasion principles to identify strategies associated with agent failures and use these findings to construct a universal generation skill. Our evaluation shows that agents can handle pre-existing phishing emails safely yet remain susceptible to attacks adapted to agent behavior, while in an AgentPhishing evaluation of nine agents, four exhibit phishing-detection rates below 10% and malicious-request compliance rates above 45% . These results highlight the limits of inferring agent safety from conventional phishing evaluations and the need to assess how agents respond to persuasive malicious requests. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Computer-use agents powered by large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) offer a way to delegate everyday tasks across desktop and web applications (OpenAI, 2025; Anthropic, 2024; Google DeepMind, 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Kimi Team, 2026; Xie et al., 2024; Drouin et al., 2024). To carry out these tasks, agents interpret external content while accessing private information and taking actions on users’ behalf. Adversaries who influence this content may redirect those actions, creating privacy and security risks (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024). Agents managing email provide a concrete setting for studying these risks: unlike conventional phishing detectors that classify messages, they may also carry out requests within them, including requests to disclose sensitive information or perform other unauthorized actions (Wu et al., 2026; 1Password, 2026). Assessing their trustworthiness in this setting requires examining both whether they detect phishing and whether they comply with malicious requests, rather than inferring safe behavior from detection performance alone (Levy et al., 2026). Prior work shows that deceptive messages can evade phishing detectors, influence LLM judgments, and induce unsafe actions by agents carrying out user tasks (Qi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2026; 1Password, 2026). Existing phishing email datasets provide reference material for evaluating detection performance (Puyang, 2025; Ganiuly & Smaiyl, 2025), but as agents and their defenses evolve, continued risk assessment also requires methods that can generate and refine attacks against updated systems. Phishing research provides foundations for such methods through reusable generators with realism validation and persuasion analysis (Chen et al., 2024), and iterative email refinement using detector feedback (Qi et al., 2025). However, realism and detection evasion do not measure whether an agent carries out a malicious request, motivating evaluation that accounts for behavior. Feedback-driven red-teaming addresses this need by learning reusable attack strategies and optimizing attacks through target responses and execution outcomes (Liu et al., 2025; Wen et al., 1arXiv:2610.31404v1 [cs.CR] 22 Oct 2026 Preprint 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Syros et al., 2026). Yet success under prompt-injection attacks does not establish how effective social-engineering attacks remain when explicit instruction overrides and fabricated system directives are excluded. AgentBait directly evaluates social-engineering attacks that use deception and persuasion to induce unsafe actions by task-performing agents, including through email-based inducements (Wu et al., 2026). Its reported attack construction, however, follows predefined strategies rather than iteratively improving generation from the evaluated agent’s responses. These complementary advances motivate a more specific investigation: how do agents’ phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests change when the generation of social-engineering emails is optimized against their behavior under these attack-content constraints? To address this question, we introduce AgentPhishing, a benchmark for evaluating agents’ phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests under social-engineering attacks in email tasks. We construct its adaptive attacks using PhishOpt, which optimizes target-specific generation skills and uses persuasion analysis to derive a universal skill. Its optimization procedure builds on Skil-lOpt (Yang et al., 2026) to refine email-generation skills from target-agent feedback under fixed generation requirements and a realism constraint. We analyze emails generated by the agent-specific skills using Cialdini’s persuasion principles (Cialdini, 1984), both to characterize framings associated with detection and compliance outcomes and to guide the construction of a universal generation skill. Our experiments compare pre-existing phishing emails, unoptimized synthetic emails, and skill-generated attacks under a common evaluation protocol to assess how attack generation affects observed safety performance. We also evaluate agent-specific and universal skills across agents to examine their cross-agent effectiveness. Our contributions are: • We introduce benchmark AgentPhishing for agent safety evaluation under social-engineering attacks in email tasks, measuring phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests. • We present framework PhishOpt that evolves reusable, target-specific phishing-generation skills using agent feedback and derives a universal skill from persuasion analysis. • We show that strong performance on pre-existing phishing emails can coexist with detection failures and malicious-request compliance under PhishOpt-generated attacks, and character-ize cross-agent susceptibility and persuasion framings associated with these outcomes. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Phishing Detection and Susceptibility. Phishing research examines whether deceptive content can be identified and whether recipients act on it. Conventional studies develop email classifiers, investigate judgments of fraudulent websites, and characterize real-world SMS phishing campaigns (Fette et al., 2007; Dhamija et al., 2006; Nahapetyan et al., 2024). Complementary human-centered research examines how social relationships and contextual cues influence susceptibility across email, social media, and text messaging (Jagatic et al., 2007; Vishwanath, 2017; Tabassum et al., 2024; Zhuo et al., 2023). These studies assess behavior such as information disclosure, extending phishing evaluation beyond classification (Jagatic et al., 2007; Vishwanath, 2017). Recent research evaluates LLMs in several distinct roles. LLM-PEA studies models as phishing detectors, whereas Fraud-R1 examines their responses to staged fraud inducements in advice-giving and recipient role-play settings (Hasan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025a). SE-VSim studies adaptive social-engineering conversations with simulated human victims, and PhishSim verifies credential-submission outcomes within its simulation (Kumarage et al., 2025; Tewari et al., 2026). For agents carrying out delegated tasks, AgentDojo, InjecAgent, and WASP evaluate whether malicious external content redirects agent behavior (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024; Evtimov et al., 2025). AgentBait and the industry benchmark SCAM further examine social-engineering threats within agent workflows, including email-based inducements and phishing scenarios (Wu et al., 2026; 1Password, 2026). AgentPhishing studies this delegated-task setting through email, measuring both phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests. 

Feedback-Driven Attack Generation. Phishing-generation methods can use feedback to improve realism or detection evasion. PEEK trains a reusable generator with real-vs-synthetic discrimination and incorporates extracted persuasion patterns (Chen et al., 2024). SpearBot revises emails using detector critiques (Qi et al., 2025). 3 METHODOLOGY 

3.1 OVERVIEW 

To study agents’ phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests in email tasks, we synthesize evaluation emails using PhishOpt, which develops reusable, target-specific skills and an analysis-informed universal skill. Each skill specifies natural-language email-generation instructions. PhishOpt comprises two stages: target-specific skill optimization and analysis-informed universal skill construction. The first (Section 3.2, 3.3) evolves a shared base skill into target-specific skill using agent feedback. Built on SkillOpt’s bounded-edit procedure (Yang et al., 2026), it adds a phishing-specific objective and generation constraints (Section 3.3). The second analyzes emails generated by the evolved skills using Cialdini’s persuasion principles to relate framing to detection and compliance outcomes and construct a universal skill without further optimization (Section 3.4). 3Preprint 3.2 TARGET -S PECIFIC SKILL OPTIMIZATION 

In stage 1 of PhishOpt (Figure 1(a)), optimization updates the generation skill rather than individual emails or generator-model parameters. The skill is supplied as the generator’s system prompt. 

Candidate proposal. For each target agent, the shared base skill is evaluated to initialize the best skill found so far and its recorded metrics. At each iteration, the optimizer receives this skill and its detection, compliance, and classification metrics, then proposes a candidate through one bounded edit. Edits must preserve a locked rules block specifying fictional entities, placeholder secrets, benign controls, JSON output, and an approximately balanced benign–phishing label split; candidate skills that alter it are rejected. 

Constraint checking and objective evaluation. The generator produces a probe batch from the candidate skill. The realism judge assesses this batch to determine whether the skill satisfies the realism constraint. If the skill passes, the generator produces a separate, fresh evaluation batch containing phishing emails and benign controls. The target agent processes this batch and selects actions and, where applicable, drafts replies. The evaluator computes detection, compliance, and classification metrics from these outputs to obtain the objective value defined in Section 3.3. 

Skill update. The candidate and its metrics replace the best skill and its recorded metrics only if the candidate’s objective value exceeds the recorded score; otherwise, both remain unchanged. The 

optimizer receives the best skill and its recorded metrics at the next iteration. After the prescribed number of iterations, optimization returns the target-specific best skill. 3.3 OPTIMIZATION OBJECTIVE AND GENERATION CONSTRAINTS 

The optimization objective measures the failures exposed by a candidate skill, while generation constraints maintain the intended synthetic phishing setting. The objective supplies the acceptance criterion for the update procedure in Section 3.2. 

Optimization objective. PhishOpt maximizes a weighted score for candidate skill selection: 

J = 0 .5(1 − D) + 0 .3C + 0 .2(1 − F1), (1) where D and C are the target agent’s detection and malicious-request compliance rates on phishing emails, and F1 is its classification score over both phishing emails and benign controls. Detection failure and compliance may overlap; F1 combines recall, equal to D under our action-based defi-nitions, with precision, which accounts for benign emails classified as phishing. The operational definitions of agent outcomes and metrics are specified in Section 4. 

Realism constraint. The intended attack space consists of social-engineering content, excluding explicit instruction overrides and fabricated system directives. The realism judge scores a probe batch on plausibility, coherence, naturalness, and the absence of unrealistic exploitation artifacts, each on a [0 , 1] scale. A candidate passes if the mean score across all four criteria and sampled emails is at least τ ; otherwise, it is rejected before target-agent evaluation. 3.4 PERSUASION ANALYSIS AND UNIVERSAL SKILL CONSTRUCTION 

The second stage of PhishOpt (Figure 1(b)) constructs a universal generation skill from persuasion patterns observed in emails generated by target-specific evolved skills. The analysis characterizes how these patterns relate to agent outcomes and informs the universal skill’s generation instructions. 

Persuasion analysis. The LLM-based Cialdini annotator scores generated phishing emails according to Cialdini’s persuasion principles (Cialdini, 1984). Pooling observations across target agents, we use ϕ correlations to quantify associations between persuasion-principle presence and detection and compliance outcomes separately. 

Universal skill construction. The LLM-based skill synthesizer constructs the universal skill from these associations, favoring principles associated with unsafe responses over those associated with safer handling. 4 BENCHMARK 

AgentPhishing evaluates safety risk of agents under phishing email attacks, measuring both phishing detection and compliance with malicious requests across pre-existing and adaptively generated emails. Figure 6 in Appendix A provides an overview of the evaluation flow, comparison setups, and diagnostic capabilities. 

Task setting and agent interface. The target agent receives a shared inbox-assistant instruction and an email’s sender, subject, body, and available links. It returns a structured response specifying an action, whether a reply is needed, an optional draft reply, and its reasoning. Actions comprise reporting, archiving, ignoring, unsubscribing, replying, following up, clicking a link, and providing information. Evaluation scores action selection and drafted content, not external execution. Table 1 lists model usage and configurations by role. 

Evaluation emails. Agents are evaluated on pre-existing emails from public corpora (Puyang, 2025) and synthetic emails generated using base, target-specific evolved, and universal skills. Figure 2 shows an annotated example. 

Outcome definitions and metrics. Detection rate D is the proportion of phishing emails assigned one of four designated safe actions, namely report, archive, ignore, or unsubscribe. Compliance rate C is the proportion of phishing emails for which the agent chooses to click a link or provide information, or produces a draft reply judged unsafe. Precision and F1 are computed over phishing emails and benign controls, treating the four detection actions as positive predictions. 

Evaluation protocol. The target-agent role in Table 1 appears in both PhishOpt and AgentPhishing. The PhishOpt target agent provides the feedback used to evolve a target-specific skill, whereas the 

AgentPhishing target agent processes evaluation emails generated using that skill. Target-specific evaluation uses the same model in both roles. Cross-agent evaluation varies these identities: diagonal conditions use the same model as the PhishOpt and AgentPhishing target agent, whereas off-diagonal conditions evaluate a skill optimized against one model on another. The fixed generator remains separate from both target-agent roles. The universal skill has no PhishOpt target agent and is evaluated directly across AgentPhishing target agents with the skill and generator model held fixed. The experimental setup specifies model versions, sample sizes, repetitions, and whether agents receive the same email batches. Post-generation diversity and repetition diagnostics include Self-BLEU (Zhu et al., 2018), Distinct-1/2 (Li et al., 2016), gzip compression ratio, and repeated 4-gram rate. 5Preprint

Emails generated using the universal skill are evaluated across the same AgentPhishing target agents. Results. Figure 4A shows stronger variation across AgentPhishing target agents than across PhishOpt target agents. As AgentPhishing target agents, Kimi and Qwen have the highest mean Susceptibility scores across skills evolved against different PhishOpt target agents, 0.4305 and 0.4238 , whereas Sonnet has the lowest, 0.0374 . Table 2 further shows that Sonnet detects every phishing email and never complies across skills evolved against all six PhishOpt target agents, while Qwen and Kimi exhibit both detection failures and compliance across multiple rows. Although the skill evolved against GPT-5.4 has the highest off-diagonal mean score, 0.3581 , no evolved skill is uniformly effective across the six targets. 7Preprint Figure 4B shows a similar target-dependent pattern among the nine additional agents: five have mean Susceptibility scores at or below 0.1036 , whereas four score at or above 0.5062 . Together, these results show greater variation in Susceptibility across AgentPhishing target agents than across target-specific skills, with vulnerabilities also observed among agents not used as PhishOpt targets. 5.3 PERSUASION -P RINCIPLE ANALYSIS AND UNIVERSAL SKILL 

Experimental setup. To address Q3 , we analyze 180 phishing-email evaluations from the six target-specific evolved-skill corpora. The analysis includes all phishing emails, covering both successful and unsuccessful attacks rather than conditioning on agent failures. We annotate the emails for six persuasion principles: Reciprocity, Commitment and Consistency, Social Proof, Authority, Liking, and Scarcity/Urgency. We compute ϕ coefficients relating each principle’s presence to detection failure ( ↓) and malicious-request compliance ( ↓). Positive coefficients indicate an association with the corresponding unsafe outcome, whereas negative coefficients indicate an association with its absence. We then provide the annotations, agent outcomes, and generation constraints to Opus 4.8 to synthesize a universal skill without additional target-specific optimization. 

Persuasion-principle analysis. Across the 180 evaluations, we observe 51 detection failures and 21 compliance cases. Figure 5 shows that Commitment and Consistency is positively associated with both detection failure ( ϕ = 0 .2407 ) and compliance ( ϕ = 0 .1391 ). This principle captures requests framed as continuing an existing task or honoring a prior commitment, suggesting that apparent consistency with an ongoing workflow can accompany unsafe agent responses. Liking, which captures friendly or relationship-building framing, is positively associated with detection failure ( ϕ = 0 .1914 ) but has almost no association with compliance ( ϕ = 0 .0018 ). This difference illustrates that persuasion features associated with missed detection need not also be associated with malicious-request compliance. In contrast, Authority and Scarcity/Urgency are negatively associated with detection failure ( ϕ = −0.1502 and −0.1976 , respectively), and Scarcity/Urgency is also negatively associated with compliance ( ϕ = −0.1460 ). Together, these observations suggest that overt authority and urgency cues may be easier for agents to recognize than workflow-continuation framing. These are associations within the evaluated corpora, not evidence that individual principles independently cause the observed outcomes. 

Universal skill results. In Figure 4B, emails generated using the universal skill yield detection rates below 10% and compliance rates above 45% for four of the nine additional target agents. These results show that the analysis-informed skill generates effective attacks against multiple agents without further target-specific optimization. The universal skill also achieves the highest mean PhishOpt objective score across these nine targets, although its advantage over the GPT-5.4-derived skill is not statistically significant in the paired comparison. The results therefore support cross-agent effectiveness, rather than consistent superiority over target-specific skills or causal effects of individual persuasion principles. −0.3 −0.2 −0.1 0.0 0.1 0.2 0.3

5.4 ABLATION STUDY AND DIAGNOSTICS 

Experimental setup. We assess scoring sensitivity by removing the F1 term and renormalizing the detection-failure and compliance weights, rescoring existing base- and evolved-skill results without rerunning optimization or making new model calls. We also evaluate two universal-skill variants on Gemini-3.5-Flash and GPT-5.4: removing Commitment and Consistency framing and replacing it with Authority plus Urgency. 

Results. Without the F1 term, evolved-skill scores exceed base-skill scores by 0.8542 , 0.5521 ,

0.5104 , and 0.3750 for Qwen, Kimi, Grok, and GPT, respectively, while Sonnet and Gemini show no increase. Thus, the score increases for four agents are not solely attributable to F1. Table 3 shows that both framing variants reduce Susceptibility scores and increase detection on both targets. Removing Commitment and Consistency lowers compliance on GPT but leaves it unchanged on Gemini; replacing it with Authority plus Urgency yields perfect detection and zero compliance on both targets. 

Diagnostics. Diversity and variance diagnostics check whether the results are caused by duplicate generations or unstable batches. Diversity metrics are reported in Appendix E, using Self-BLEU Zhu et al. (2018), Distinct-1/2 Li et al. (2016), gzip compression ratio, and repeated 4-gram rate. These metrics test whether accepted skills generate varied email batches rather than relying on repeated or near-duplicate templates. 0.7626 ± 0.0832 , while five universal-skill passes over seven held-out agents give 0.2820 ± 0.0348 .Together, these diagnostics suggest that the reported patterns are not simply artifacts of duplicate generations or a single unstable batch. 

6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced AgentPhishing to evaluate phishing detection and malicious-request compliance by LLM email agents, and PhishOpt to optimize reusable target-specific generation skills and construct an analysis-informed universal skill. Our experiments show that safe handling of pre-existing phishing emails can coexist with failures under adaptive persuasive attacks. Cross-agent and held-out evaluations reveal substantial differences in susceptibility across evaluated agents, while detection failure and compliance remain distinct outcomes. Cialdini-principle analysis informs a universal skill that exposes vulnerabilities across multiple held-out agents without further target-specific optimization, although it does not establish consistent superiority over evolved skills. Together, these findings motivate adaptive, behavior-based evaluation alongside conventional phishing tests. 

Limitations. Our evaluation covers synthetic, single-message social-engineering attacks under fixed generation constraints and selected agent configurations. It scores action selection and drafted replies rather than external execution, so measured compliance does not establish completed real-world harm. Finite evaluation batches and model-version dependence limit generalization, while rule-based outcome scoring and LLM-based realism judgments and persuasion annotations may introduce measurement error. Pooled persuasion associations do not establish independent causal effects; the framing ablations provide evidence on two targets but do not isolate every principle or generalize to all agents. Future work should extend evaluation to multi-turn interactions, richer account and tool environments, broader model configurations, and controlled studies of defenses and persuasion mechanisms. 9Preprint 

7 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS 

This work studies methods for generating phishing emails that can evade LLM email agents, creating a clear dual-use risk. The same techniques used to evaluate agent susceptibility could be misused to craft more effective phishing attacks. We believe publication is justified because the basic capability is already accessible to anyone who can run a scoring loop against commercial APIs, while the contribution of this paper is a controlled characterization of which agent behaviors and persuasion patterns are associated with failures (§5.3). This characterization provides defensive value for researchers, model providers, and deployers who need to evaluate and harden email agents before they are used in settings involving private information or user-authorized actions. The benchmark is constructed in a controlled synthetic setting. All generated emails use fictional organizations, people, sender addresses, domains, and accounts, and all credentials, links, or secrets are nonfunctional placeholders enforced by the locked benchmark constraints in §3.3. No generated email was sent to a real mailbox, no real organization or individual was impersonated or targeted, and no human participants were recruited, deceived, or exposed to generated emails. These constraints al-low AgentPhishing to measure detection failure and compliance without creating real-world phishing victims or handling real private data. 

REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT 

Our release policy is designed to support reproducibility while limiting direct misuse. We release the evaluation pipeline, evolved skill files, metric definitions, analysis code, and aggregate results, but withhold the raw generated email corpora. Withholding the corpora avoids publishing a ready-made set of phishing emails and reduces the risk of casual reuse. This mitigation is limited: because the released skills are generator prompts, a determined user could regenerate similar examples. We therefore treat withholding as a friction-raising measure rather than a complete safeguard, preserving reproducibility while avoiding the easiest path to direct misuse. We did not pursue coordinated disclosure with individual model providers because the behavior studied here is not a single patchable vulnerability in one deployed system; it arises from interactions among model behavior, agent prompts, task interfaces, and persuasive email content. We treat public characterization as the appropriate disclosure mechanism because it informs model providers, agent developers, and deployers about a risk channel that may not be exposed by static phishing tests or prompt-injection benchmarks alone.

E DIVERSITY METRICS : F ULL DETAIL 

A skill that raised the adversary score by collapsing onto one high-performing email template would be an artefact of the optimizer rather than a finding about persuasion. We therefore measured lexical diversity on the scam-labelled emails ( label = 1 ) produced by each best skill, against a corpus generated from the unoptimized skill v1.md as baseline. Self-BLEU Zhu et al. (2018) scores each email against all others in its own corpus, so higher values mean a more self-similar — less diverse — corpus; distinct-2 Li et al. (2016) is the complementary measure, the ratio of unique bigrams to total bigrams. We fixed the flag criteria before looking at the results: a corpus is flagged for mode collapse if its Self-BLEU reaches 1.30 × the baseline ( ≥ 0.3171 ) or its distinct-2 falls to 0.70 × the baseline (≤ 0.5072 , against a baseline of 0.7246 ). No corpus was flagged: zero of seven on Self-BLEU and zero of seven on distinct-2. Two evolved cor-pora — gemini-3.5-flash and grok-4.20 — were in fact more diverse than the unoptimized baseline. The optimization did not proceed by narrowing the generator’s output distribution. The margin is not uniform, and we report it rather than rounding it away. qwen3.7-plus — the model driven furthest, and the one whose corpus would be the most natural candidate for collapse — clears the threshold by 0.0063 , about two percent of the threshold value. Its Self-BLEU of 0.3108 

against a threshold of 0.3171 is a near-miss, not comfortable headroom. The honest reading is that the strongest attack sits at the edge of the diversity criterion we set, while still on the right side of it; a stricter threshold, or one more epoch of optimization, could plausibly have crossed it. One methodological point matters for interpreting Table 7. Self-BLEU and distinct-n both scale with sample size, so corpora of unequal size cannot be compared directly. Our batches yield 34 –36 

scam emails rather than a uniform count ( claude-sonnet-5 34 ; gpt-5.4 and the baseline 36 ;the rest 35 ), and the comparison is size-matched to n ≈ 35 for this reason. The correction is not cosmetic: on unmatched samples qwen3.7-plus scored 0.232 and appeared to be the second most 

diverse corpus, the opposite of its size-matched position as the least diverse.
