# Learning Rewards in Strategic Games

Abstract 

Understanding the objectives that drive agents’ behavior is crucial in strategic environments, where multiple agents interact and adapt to one another. This challenge arises in domains such as cybersecurity, autonomous driving, and online markets, where agents’ underlying incentives are typically unknown and must be inferred from their observed behavior. Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) provides a natural framework for this problem, but extending IRL to strategic multi-agent settings introduces a fundamental challenge: a reward that explains observed behavior does not necessarily induce optimal strategies when used for decision-making. In this work, we study this problem through the lens of strategic identifiability , and show that learning from a fixed policy pair can be insufficient to strategically identify rewards, independent of the number of expert samples K. Motivated by this, we introduce an active IRL algorithm for two-player repeated Stackelberg games, in which the leader adaptively selects policies to elicit informative responses about the follower’s reward. Combining maximum-likelihood estimation with experimental design, we derive finite-sample guarantees on the Stackelberg sub-optimality induced by the learned reward, matching up to polylogarithmic factors a new minimax learning rate of K−1/2. Finally, we evaluate our approach in a realistic anti-poaching setting, demonstrating the benefits of active exploration for reward recovery and downstream strategic performance. 1

# 1 Introduction 

Strategic decision-making often requires reasoning about agents whose objectives are un-known. A defender may need to anticipate the incentives of an attacker, an autonomous vehicle the preferences of other drivers, or a wildlife-protection agency the objectives of poachers. In such settings, agents’ behavior provides an indirect signal about their underlying incentives. Recovering these objectives is valuable not only for explaining observed behavior, but, crucially, for predicting how agents may respond to new policies or interventions. Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) (Ng et al., 2000; Abbeel & Ng, 2004) provides a natural framework for inferring latent reward functions from the behavior of approximately optimal agents. While IRL has been extensively studied in single-agent decision-making, strategic interactions fundamentally change the problem. An agent’s observed behavior depends not only on its own reward, but also on the behavior of the agents it interacts with. Consequently, a reward that perfectly explains behavior under one interaction may fail to predict how

At deployment, a small error in reward estimation can lead to highly sub-optimal behavior from a rational patrol. A key difficulty is that passive observations may provide insufficient information for this objective. Observing an agent repeatedly against a fixed policy only reveals its preferences under that particular interaction. Distinct reward functions may therefore generate indistin-guishable behavior while inducing substantially different responses to alternative policies. As we show in this work, this can prevent low strategic sub-optimality even with infinitely many observations. This limitation suggests a different approach: rather than passively observing strategic behavior, can we actively choose interactions that reveal the reward information needed for downstream decision-making? We answer this question in repeated Stackelberg games, where a leader can adaptively select policies and observe the responses of a bounded-rational follower. This setting naturally allows the leader to trade off exploiting its current estimate of the follower’s reward with selecting interactions that are informative about the follower’s underlying objectives. Related work Our work connects inverse reinforcement learning and learning in games. In single-agent settings, IRL has long studied the ambiguity inherent in recovering rewards from demonstrations (Ng et al., 2000; Abbeel & Ng, 2004). In multi-agent settings, recent work has focused on whether policies learned from demonstrations remain robust when interacting with strategic agents, typically through notions of exploitability (Tang et al., 2024; Freihaut et al., 2026a; Bergerault et al., 2026a). Emerging work on IRL in games has instead characterized sets of rewards compatible with observed strategic behavior (Freihaut & Ramponi, 2026; Barbara et al., 2026). We take a complementary perspective: rather than only characterizing which rewards are consistent with observed behavior, we ask which interactions provide the information needed to identify a strategically useful reward, and how such interactions can be actively selected with finite-sample guarantees. A more extensive discussion on previous work can be found in Appendix A.

# 4 Active Stackelberg IRL in normal-form games 

In many applications, indeed, one can actively interact with the environment over time. Examples include cybersecurity settings where defenders adapt to strategic attackers and online platforms interacting with self-interested users. In those cases, the learner is not restricted to passively observing a fixed behavior, but can deliberately choose policies inducing diverse environments for the ν-player. This allows the learner to elicit informative responses from the follower, related in spirit to the strategy of Rolland et al. (2022), who modify the dynamics of a single-agent setting to make the reward exactly identifiable from expert demonstrations. # 6 Conclusion 

We study IRL for interactive agents in strategic games as the problem of learning reward functions that (approximately) preserve optimality of agents when deployed among strategic players. In two-player normal-form games under linearity and entropy-regularization assump-tions, we develop an active algorithm that provably minimizes Stackelberg sub-optimality at a minimax optimal rate, ignoring polylogarithmic factors. We then empirically validate our algorithm on a realistic anti-poaching game and a set of synthetic environments. 

Future directions A current open problem is principled learning in dynamic games. Specifically, open challenges include the derivation of exploration strategies over states and the computation of reward estimators from trajectories (beyond strong myopic assumptions of previous works).

We review the literature most relevant to our problem, with a particular focus on reward identifiability and inverse reinforcement learning. 

Reward identifiability Strictly convex regularization of the objective, such as entropy, ensures the uniqueness of the optimal policies to any given set of rewards. Nevertheless, inverse RL remains ill-defined as rewards are invariant to potential shaping (Ng et al., 1999). This identifiability issue was studied in the seminal work of Cao et al. (2021), later refined by Rolland et al. (2022) and Schlaginhaufen & Kamgarpour (2024). More recently, Freihaut & Ramponi (2026) extends those results to games, showing that even the nature (e.g. collaborative or competitive) of a game cannot be determined from the feasible reward sets of an expert. This line of work has motivated inverse RL works like Metelli et al. (2021); Lindner et al. (2022), that minimize the distance between the true and estimated feasible reward sets. Metelli et al. (2021) assumes access to a generative model, an assumption later removed by Lindner et al. (2022). However, these works do not provide a method for extracting a good reward or task representation from the interactions. This is the target of more traditional methods, inspired by the single-agent case. 

Inverse reinforcement learning in games When considering multiple interacting agents, inverse RL aims to recover the individual rewards explaining their behaviors. To this end, Mehr et al. (2023) empirically extend the classical MaxEntIRL framework of Ziebart et al. (2008) to games, learning from demonstrations of a Nash equilibrium. Similarly, Liao et al. (2025) study reward recovery from samples of a fixed expert in two-player zero-sum game expert under coverability assumptions. Later, Barbara et al. (2026) derives sample complexity bounds for recovering the feasible reward set by sampling a fixed expert, evaluated in the Hausdorff distance. Under the strong assumption of oracle access to the loss gradient, Goktas et al. (2025) perform principled inverse multi-agent RL with parameter-dependent payoffs. All these settings assume access to reliable equilibrium demonstrations, i.e. that the game has been solved a-priori by a set of players we can learn from (e.g. humans). This is restrictive in many applications, particularly in non-cooperative games where a player must learn to act against an adversary with unknown incentives. This motivates our setting, in which a player learns to behave strategically in arbitrary downstream tasks, playing against a best-responding agent. Closer to this setup, Chen et al. (2023) extract the reward function from observing the follower in a two-player Stackelberg scenario. By controlling a prescription announced by the leader, they are able to bound the suboptimality of the optimal leader of the estimated environment. In contrast, we assume that the leader directly commits to a policy (as opposed to a prescription), and actively select leader strategies via optimal design to bound the offline suboptimality objective. This has been studied by Chen et al. (2025), where the convergence of their algorithm is studied in the asymptotic regime. Contrary to their work, we derive an algorithm with sample complexity analysis and a matching minimax lower bound. Previously, Wu et al. (2022) also studied bounded rationality in the context of 18 Stackelberg Games. In particular, the authors show that observations are more informative from a QRE than a Nash equilibrium. This means that bounded rationality helps to be more robust against an irrational opponent. Under finite action spaces, this makes it possible to construct algorithms with exponential dependencies on neither the number of leader actions nor the number of follower actions. 

Learning against unknown or adversarial opponents. Several works study learning in multi-agent environments with unknown or adaptive opponents, where non-stationarity and strategic interactions make learning particularly challenging. In particular, Sessa et al. (2020) consider learning effective strategies against unknown opponents, while more recent approaches use demonstrations to improve learning in strategic settings, as in DIML (An & Du, 2026). Other works study robustness against adversaries and worst-case behaviors during training (Liu et al., 2022). In contrast, we focus on reward learning under strategic interactions rather than solely on policy optimization or robustness. Additionally, we expand below on the recent developments around adjacent tasks, such as multi-agent imitation learning, and contrast with the scope and goals of the present paper. 

Imitation learning in games Recent imitation learning works in games measure sub-optimality of the learned behavior by the notion of Nash gap, initially introduced by Ramponi et al. (2023). In general Markov games, Tang et al. (2024) showed how harder this objective is compared to the common value gap extension from single-agent problems. Later, these remarks have been extended to more precise characterizations for imitation learning by Freihaut et al. (2026a;b); Bergerault et al. (2026a). In the non-interactive case, these works show that the hardness of equilibria extraction can be reduced to notions of concentrability and continuity at equilibrium. Further, Freihaut et al. (2026a;b) show how to optimize such Nash gap regrets in the interactive setting by forcing the exploration of the state space to cover uncertain deviations. This is done by controlling all the agents sequentially. We argue that such control over the agents is not realistic in many scenarios, where agents are not collaborative in learning, e.g. in real world environments when learning from interactions with (partially) adversarial players. To this end, we adopt a Stackelberg objective to measure the optimality of extracted policies, evaluating a leader when deployed with other strategic players. 

On reward identifiability Furthermore, imitation learning extracts a policy from (ap-proximately) optimal agents corresponding to fixed incentives. Modeling agents’ behavior for a fixed task without describing their intents prevents transferring to new environments or learner tasks, limiting adaptation of the learner at deployment. Fortunately, observing (approximately) optimal actions from strategic players allows to extract signals about their individual rewards. However, the complete rewards can in general not be recovered, even with infinite data. This ambiguity on the reward function has first been explored in the single-agent case, with the analysis of potential-based transformations or potential shaping originating from Ng et al. (1999). In the normal-form game setting, these transformations reduce to constant shifts, whose invariance to optimal policies carry over to the entropy-regularized setting (i.e. 19 softmax policies remain invariant to constant shifts of the rewards). The picture extends to games, where each individual reward is contained in an individual feasible reward set. In this scenario, even the nature of the game (e.g. cooperative, zero-sum) can sometimes not be identifiable from the feasible sets alone (Freihaut & Ramponi, 2026). To mitigate this issue, one could consider learning in different environments, as pointed out by Rolland et al. (2022); Freihaut & Ramponi (2026). A second possibility is to weaken the exact identifiability objective, which aims to exactly learn the reward function, in particular by minimizing the mismatch with the ground truth in the Euclidean norm (Liao et al., 2025). Our work applies both propositions, where the learner agent induces different environments to the soft-optimal follower, and where only the strategic identifiability of its reward is considered. 

Empirical inverse reinforcement learning In the single-agent case, the seminal work of Ziebart et al. (2008) develops MaxEntIRL, an algorithm recovering the reward matching the maximum-entropy solution given by the demonstrations. The proposed method operates under linear rewards. Their method essentially boils down to gradient descent on the reward parameters, where gradients, given by expected features, are approximated from rollouts of the policy in the environment. MaxEntIRL has later been empirically extended to games by Mehr et al. (2023), and the theoretical guarantees of such extensions in terms of strategic exploitability are yet to be understood. Furthermore, one caveat of this method is the requirement that all players are controllable, which can be unreasonable in various applications. In contrast, we develop in this work a principled algorithm with PAC guarantees under a finite number of samples. In our two-player setting, only one agent is assumed controllable, while the non-controllable agent (the follower) is best-responding to the learner (the leader policy).

ii) Minimizing uncertainty in the Fisher information geometry and the elliptical potential lemma We bound Fisher uncertainty by a greedy selection rule maximizing Fisher information gain. iii) Self-concordance The instantaneous log-likelihood as a function of the logits is self-concordant-like in the span semi-norm. This allows to control the reparameterization of Fisher information matrices. Precisely, an optimal schedule for the leader policies depends on knowing the true coefficient ω. Crucially, this last tool introduces exponential dependency in the induced distance between the proxy ˜ ω and ω. This is the reason for the warm-up phase, which removes this dependency in the final bound by using a burn-in budget. As mentioned, in this offline part we use an arbitrary proxy ˜ ω = 0 and rely on global self-concordance. Hence, our offline warm-up would always produce an exponential sample complexity guarantee. We solve this problem by using a subsequent online phase in the improved self-concordance regime induced by the warm-up certificate (Lemma 19).

These methods are implemented with and without an initial warm-up phase (using Algorithm 2), and are operating under the assumption of a follower best-response oracle, given a leader policy that is actively selected. 51 • Active IRL (ours): An implementation of Algorithm 1. • Fixed random : An MLE-based reward extraction similar to Algorithm 1, where the leader policy is randomly sampled then kept fixed throughout all online iterations. • Online random : A similar MLE-based algorithm using a new randomly sampled leader policy at each online iteration. 

Fixed-expert algorithms. These methods assume access to a fixed expert, from which they can collect samples. The expert is defined with respect to a fixed (but unknown) leader reward, which is also in contrast to active algorithms. • Fixed-expert MLE : Uses the same constrained MLE as the active methods, but fixing the policy as a known Stackelberg optimal leader. • Follower-only OLS : Recovering the ordinary least-square solution of the empirical version of the linear system of Equation 2. This assumes access to an entropy-regularized joint-QRE expert oracle. • Zero-sum OLS : Similar to Follower-only OLS and following the analysis of Liao et al. (2025), leverages the fact that the game is known to be zero-sum and taking the ordinary least squares solution of the stacked system formed by the linear equality constraint of Equation 2 and its leader counter-part. For each algorithm, we term target SubOpt (respectively target Fisher) the Stackelberg sub-optimality of the policy obtained by optimizing the leader on the learned reward (respectively the norm in the Fisher information geometry described by the reward transfer lemma, Lemma 3), for the true leader reward used during training. Similarly, the averaged transfer SubOpt and averaged transfer Fisher describe the same quantities, but averaged over ten selected leader rewards. These ten downstream tasks have been chosen from a pool of 500 randomly chosen reward candidates, by greedily maximizing diversity of payoff matrices. The pool is disjoint from the tasks used for hyperparameter selection. Averaged transfer SubOpt and averaged transfer Fisher are used as proxies for measuring the strategic-transferability of rewards.

We provide additional experiments using the Lobéké National Park environment from the GUARD benchmark (Krever et al., 2026), in the same setup as in the main text. We describe it below. 

Task The environment models an anti-poaching scenario in the Lobéké National Park (Cameroon), designed from a dataset of recorded elephant movements. This classical green security game involves patrols (leader player) to protect wildlife against poachers (follower player). Both players distribute in the park and the followers are rewarded based on the area they occupy (as they can poach more animals) and the presence of patrols (as patrols are limiting their capacity to poach). On the other hand, the patrols can be incentivized in minimizing the rewards for the poachers, but also in trading-off this objective with the covering of prioritized areas. 52 Model (illustrated in Figure 4) The game is model as a 5 × 5 grid comprised of 23 accessible locations. At a given time step t, the leader first chooses a distribution over patrol schedules, to which the follower reacts by choosing a location distribution, modeled as the entropy-regularized best-response to the patrol policy.
