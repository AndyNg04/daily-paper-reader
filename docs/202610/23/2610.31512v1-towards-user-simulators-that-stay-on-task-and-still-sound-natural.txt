ABSTRACT 

Users increasingly rely on AI systems for extended, multi-turn tasks, yet evidence indicates that LLM-based system performance degrades over prolonged interac-tion. To evaluate and improve AI assistants in such settings, we need user simu-lators that faithfully follow complex user intents while exhibiting realistic conver-sational behavior. Existing approaches manifest a trade-off: prompted assistants can follow complex user intents but lack behavioral fidelity, while specialized user simulators better approximate conversational behavior but struggle to remain faithful to complex intents. In this paper, we propose a reinforcement learning (RL) recipe that jointly rewards task intent adherence and behavioral natural-ness. A crucial contribution of the method is operationalizing naturalness with a lightweight discriminator trained in an alternating, adversarial loop with the user simulator. We release the resulting model, UserLM-2.0 , which produces valid simulated conversations (i.e., meeting both task-faithfulness and naturalness criteria) 46.2% of the time, over three times the rate of the strongest prior user simulator (14.1%).

1arXiv:2610.31512v1 [cs.CL] 22 Oct 2026 1 INTRODUCTION 

Users increasingly expect AI assistants and agents to help them accomplish complex tasks through sustained, multi-turn interaction, rather than provide isolated answers to individual queries (Handa et al., 2025; Chatterji et al., 2025). Yet prior work has shown that current LLM-based systems severely degrade in performance in such interactive settings (Laban et al., 2025; Tack et al., 2026). Understanding and addressing this gap requires evaluations that capture how real users communicate their goals and respond to assistants over multiple turns (Chang et al., 2025a). A crucial component of interactive benchmarks is a user simulator , which takes as input a high-level task intent (such as the one shown in Figure 1) and generates plausible user utterances through iterative interaction with an assistant (Yao et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025). We focus on two essential properties of user simulators: task fidelity and behavioral naturalness. Task fidelity requires the simulator to convey the information specified in the task intent over the course of the conversation, without omitting or contradicting the specification. Behavioral natural-ness requires the simulator to resemble real users in both how it phrases individual messages and how it conducts the interaction across turns. A simulator that omits task requirements can make as-sistant models appear to fail when the simulator is at fault, making benchmarking unreliable (Mehri et al., 2026c; Zhou et al., 2026), whereas a simulator that does not adequately capture the interactive behavior of real users may yield conclusions that do not transfer to real interactions (Zhou et al., 2026; Seshadri et al., 2026). We show experimentally that current approaches favor one requirement at the expense of the other. Prompting LLM assistants to act as users is the most common method for simulating users (Yao et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025). While prompted assistants can stay on task, they lack behavioral naturalness (Naous et al., 2026; Mehri et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2026). This is illustrated in the example in Figure 1, where GPT-5.6-Sol (a strong assistant LLM) accurately enacts the task intent but expresses it through unusually polished and exhaustive messages, unlike the conversational patterns in real user-AI exchanges. An alternative is to train user simulators by “flipping the dialogue”: supervised finetuning an LLM on user utterances from a corpus of user-assistant interactions (Naous et al., 2026; Suh et al., 2026). As reported in prior work, such finetuned user simulators exhibit realistic interaction behavior but often struggle to precisely enact the task intent they are given (Naous et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). In the example in Figure 1, the SFT-trained UserLM-8B (Naous et al., 2026) ends the conversation prematurely, before conveying all of the specified task requirements to the assistant. We propose a post-training recipe that improves task fidelity while preserving behavioral natural-ness in user simulators. We first finetune an LLM on user turns from a corpus of user-assistant interactions, then post-train it using multi-turn reinforcement learning. Our RL training objective jointly rewards task fidelity and behavioral naturalness. Rewards related to task fidelity use LLM-based judges (Zheng et al., 2023), for example by evaluating the extent to which a simulated conversation has conveyed the information specified in the task intent. We find that LLM-as-a-judge for behavioral naturalness fails to reliably encourage realistic user behavior. Instead, drawing on adversarial training (Goodfellow et al., 2014a) and its application to dialogue generation (Li et al., 2017), we propose a discriminator-based reward for behavioral naturalness. Specifically, we continually retrain a discriminator to distinguish real user conversations from con-versations generated by the user simulator being trained. This choice defines the naturalness reward in terms of how indistinguishable a given simulated conversation is from real user conversations. We update the discriminator as the simulator evolves to keep the naturalness reward responsive to its changing outputs and reduce opportunities to exploit a fixed evaluator (Gao et al., 2022). We train, evaluate, and release the resulting artifact, UserLM-2.0. Among the simulators we evalu-ated, UserLM-2.0 lies on the Pareto frontier for task fidelity and behavioral naturalness. We count a simulated conversation as valid if it adheres to our quality and termination criteria, achieves at least 80% coverage of the task intent and passes our naturalness criterion, assessed using three dis-criminators including Pangram (Glickenhaus et al., 2026). By this definition, UserLM-2.0 yields valid conversations for 46.2% of task intents, more than three times the 14.1% rate of the strongest prior user simulator (SFTUser-14B). This gap grows even further to a factor of 12 at higher task complexities, highlighting the effectiveness of our method. 22 METHOD 

Our goal is to train a user simulator that takes a high-level task intent as input, and simulates realistic user utterances in a multi-turn user-assistant conversation. As previously discussed, a good user simulator must (1) generate natural utterances, and (2) obey the task intents closely. 2.1 PROBLEM DEFINITION 

Each conversation is a multi-turn interaction where a user simulator U interacts with an assis-tant model A to satisfy an input intent c. This intent c captures the high-level goal of the user without detailing behavior on a turn-by-turn basis. Given c, we sample conversations τ =(c, u 1, a 1, . . . , u n−1, a n−1, u n), where ui and ai represent the sampled i-th user and assistant utter-ances respectively. A conversation terminates either when the maximum number of user turns N is reached or when the user outputs an <|endconversation|> tag. Prior work (Wang et al., 2025; Naous et al., 2026) trains user simulators via SFT on a large corpus of user-assistant interactions. These exhibit the desired naturalness behavior, but are extremely poor at faithfully enacting task intents. We propose treating such SFT models as initializations rather than the final user simulator. We train our own SFT model, UserLM-2.0-SFT, and design an RL-based post-training pipeline that improves its task fidelity without sacrificing naturalness. We perform multi-turn reinforcement learning using the GRPO algorithm (Shao et al., 2024). Our reward balances the above two goals of user simulation, task fidelity and behavioral naturalness. Although these are orthogonal, models can easily reward hack one at the cost of the other, such as by unnaturally including all information in the first utterance for perfect task coverage. The combined reward composition safeguards against such behaviors. 2.2 REWARD DESIGN FOR REINFORCEMENT LEARNING 

We first describe the individual reward components and then our formula for combining them: 

Naturalness: The naturalness reward should score how closely the user utterances of simulated conversation resemble the way real users interact with AI assistants in a target corpus. Let Pdisc 

be a discriminator that can distinguish between real and simulated user utterances. Our naturalness reward measures how well the user simulator fools such a discriminator based on Pdisc (y = real |

ˆu1... ˆuk, c ), where ˆu1... ˆuk is the multi-turn output from the user simulator and c is the associated task intent. For training, we set our naturalness reward to be binary; it is 1 if this discriminator score is above a threshold ( 0.5 in our experiments) and 0 otherwise. Broadly, we have two options for discriminators: LLM judges or specially trained discriminators. LLM judges are ill-suited for this task. Naturalness captures multiple implicit dimensions such as the surface-form of the utterance, pace of the conversation, coherence of follow-up utterances, etc., all of which might be impossible to extract from the target corpus and enumerate as instructions. In fact, prior work (Russell et al., 2025) has shown that prompted LLM judges are ineffective at distinguishing between real user and AI text. 1 In our work, we train lightweight discriminators on top of ModernBert-Large (Warner et al., 2025) to distinguish between real and simulated user utterances. This allows us to both limit the costs and latency during RL and specialize to the user behaviors in the target corpus. We include ablations to empirically demonstrate the issues with using LLM judges and static discriminators in Section 4.4. 

Coverage: This reward component quantifies how completely the user simulator follows a task intent. We define coverage as the proportion of the sub-goals of the task intent that are included in the user utterances generated by the simulator. This is instantiated with an LLM judge. We highlight that coverage can be an easy metric to reward hack for user simulators. For example, given a detailed task intent with fully specified sub-goals, a user simulator can maximize coverage

Therefore, we introduce an information asymmetry between the task intent provided to the user simulator as input ( c) and the one used to compute the coverage reward by the LLM judge ( c∗), where c is a more abstract version of the fully-specified task intent c∗. This ensures the model receives poor coverage when simply paraphrasing, and encourages exploring more diverse behaviors. In addition to coverage, we include two other reward components for task fidelity. These target proper formatting of the user utterances and elimination of trivial failure modes of the SFT models. 

Quality: Our quality reward is a binary score that assesses the baseline quality of a simulated conversation. It targets three common error modes of the SFT models: (1) verbatim repetition of task intent, (2) verbatim repetition of past user turns, and (3) switching roles from user to assistant mid-output. We first check the initial two conditions programmatically and otherwise use an LLM judge. A simulated conversation is given a reward 1 if all three conditions are met, and 0 otherwise. 

Termination: The goal of the termination reward is to ensure that user simulators properly ter-minate conversations when all sub-goals in c∗ are met and penalize early termination. We give a positive reward λ for including the termination tag <|endconversation|> in the last utterance if all sub-goals have been met in the earlier utterances and a negative reward −λ if the conversation is terminated without satisfying all sub-goals. Note that this ties the termination reward to coverage. In some cases, the user simulator fails to terminate the conversation at all, i.e. does not generate the 

<|endconversation|> tag; in these cases, we give it a 0 termination reward. 

Final Reward: We combine all four reward components into a overall reward score for a simulated conversation using the following formula: roverall = rquality · (rcoverage + rnaturalness + rtermination ). Note that the overall reward is 0 if the quality reward is 0. Therefore, the basic quality checks serve as a necessary condition for any successful simulation. 2.3 GAN-S TYLE TRAINING WITH DYNAMIC DISCRIMINATORS 

One limitation of trained naturalness discriminators, especially lightweight ones with fewer parame-ters, is that a larger user simulator trained against it will quickly learn to reward hack. It will quickly converge on a narrow set of behaviors that reliably fools the specific discriminator but does not learn broader behavioral naturalness. Our initial experiments validated this intuition – user simulators trained against a static discriminator maximized the naturalness reward within 75 steps by greatly increasing average utterance length while reducing conversation turns. To address this, we propose a GAN-style training recipe that periodically re-trains the discriminator every k steps. This improves discriminator quality by exposing it to synthetic conversations with distinct behavioral patterns and further helps detect and discourage reward hacking behavior. 

3 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

3.1 DATASET CONSTRUCTION 

Dataset Processing. We run our experiments on the non-toxic subset of WildChat-4.8M (Zhao et al., 2024). We build on top of the dataset processing scheme of Naous et al. (2026), additionally removing interactions with only one user turn as our goal is to simulate multi-turn interactions. This reduces the WildChat-4.8M dataset size from 3,199,860 to 199,249 interactions. We also follow Naous et al. (2026) in how we construct train, validation and test sets. We hold out 10,000 and 1,000 conversations from the train and validation set for RL training, 3,200 and 1,000 conversations from the train and validation set for discriminator training, and use the rest for SFT. Refer to Appendix A for more details on dataset construction. 

Intent and Subgoal Extraction. We need to extract both intents (given as input for simulation) and fine-grained sub-goals (used for LLM-based coverage reward). We use Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025) to extract intents for all 199,249 conversations. For sub-goals, we few-shot prompt the best available GPT models without reasoning. Details are in Appendix A. 4Test dataset. We report all results on a 2000 intent test set, constructed such that the number of intents per sub-goal count (ranging from 1 to 7) is balanced. For each user simulator, we sample four conversations per intent with Qwen3.5-9B as the assistant. 3.2 TRAINING DETAILS 

User Simulator Training. We use Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as the backbone for UserLM-2.0. We replicate the SFT training of Naous et al. (2026) and select the checkpoint with the best validation loss for subsequent RL. This checkpoint is UserLM-2.0-SFT. For RL, we simulate conversations with a maximum turn count of N = 5 , and use Qwen3.5-9B without reasoning (Team, 2026) as the assistant model. We set λ = 0 .2 for the termination reward, batch size to 64 , group size to 8 and KL hyperparameter β = 0 .001 . We measure model performance on the validation set every 

25 steps for checkpoint selection. See Appendix B for further training details. 

Naturalness Discriminator Training. We use the 395M ModernBERT-Large (Warner et al., 2025) as the discriminator. For each task intent, we sample one simulated conversation (using the user and assistant models) and one real conversation (from the ground truth dataset). 2 This ensures that our dataset is always balanced. We re-train our discriminator every 25 steps of RL training. At each iteration, half of our training dataset consists of conversations based on task intents from the most recent 25 RL steps, while the other half is sampled uniformly from the remaining training history. We train for five epochs and pick the checkpoint achieving the best validation accuracy. Refer to Appendix B.3 for exact hyperparameters and training details. 3.3 BASELINES 

We compare UserLM-2.0 against two categories of models: assistant LLMs prompted to behave like users, and specialized finetuned user simulators, as well as held out reference users. 

Finetuned Models. We compare against USP (Wang et al., 2025), UserLM-8B (Naous et al., 2026), UserLM-2.0-SFT (our SFT initialization), SFTUser-14B (Suh et al., 2026) and HumanLM-Opinion (Wu et al., 2026). UserLM-8B and SFTUser-14B are trained on WildChat-1M (Zhao et al., 2024) via SFT but differ in their backbone initialization. USP is also SFT-trained but on a subset of 94,874 LMSys-Chat conversations (Zheng et al., 2024). HumanLM is trained via RL to align with la-tent states such as user goals, opinions or emotional valence. We use the HumanLM-Opinion check-point as other HumanLM checkpoints have not been released. For USP and HumanLM-Opinion, we include the input intent and termination instructions in system prompts (details in Appendix C.1). 

Prompted assistant LLMs. We use a range of small and large, open- and closed-source mod-els. Specifically, we evaluate: Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) (Llama-3.1-8B in the tables), Qwen3.5-9B (Team, 2026), GPT-5.6-Luna, GPT-5.6-Sol (OpenAI, 2026), DeepSeek-V4-Pro (Xu et al., 2026) and Kimi-K2.6 (Moonshot AI, 2026). We follow the prompting strategy of Naous et al. (2026), with prompts and inference details in Appendix C.2. 3.4 EVALUATION METRICS 

We evaluate all simulators on a common set of 2,000 test task intents. For each task intent, we sim-ulate four independent conversations and compute three evaluation metrics that assess task fidelity and three metrics that assess naturalness. Finally, we define a compound metric that assesses the overall validity of each simulated conversation. 

Task Fidelity Metrics. We assess each generated conversation by reusing the Terminated, Quality, and Coverage as defined in Section 2.2. In short, Terminated is the percentage of conversations with an explicit <|endconversation|> present. The Quality metric is a binary check for three undesirable behaviors (e.g., verbatim copying of the task intent in a single user utterance). For the evaluation, we used Qwen3.5-9B as the LLM judge for Quality, and the stronger GPT-5.6-Luna

2. Naturalness-General: We train a general discriminator on data from all simulators, which includes our models, baselines from §3, and experiments with reward design alternatives in §4.4. This discriminator is generally less strict than the previous one, as it must distinguish between simulations from multiple distributions. 

3. Pangram: As a dataset-agnostic discriminator, we use Pangram-4 (Glickenhaus et al., 2026), a state-of-the-art AI detection model trained to distinguish human and AI assistant text. We re-port Pangram’s recorded fraction of human-written text. For each intent, we randomly select one sampled conversation. To take into account’s Pangram’s limitations around short texts, we remove termination tags and concatenate a conversation’s user turns through the first whole turn that exceeds a cumulative 100 words, or use all user turns if the conversation is shorter. 

Naturalness Pass Rate (%). A conversation passes the overall naturalness criterion if at least one of the individual naturalness scores (specific, general, Pangram) is at or above 0.5. This pass rate represents the proportion of simulated conversations where at least one discriminator assigns majority probability to the conversation being classified as real (i.e., the discriminator is “fooled”). 

Conversation Validity Metric. We define an aggregate metric that measures whether a conversa-tion passes both task fidelity and naturalness expectations. This single, over-arching metric captures the proportion of conversations produced by a simulator that are usable for downstream purposes. Under our definition, a simulated conversation is valid if it meets four conditions: (1) a Quality score of 1, (2) a Terminated score of 1,4 (3) a Coverage score of at least 0.8, and (4) passing the naturalness criterion. We also report estimated cost in USD per 100 valid conversations (lower is better), with base-model pricing proxies for fine-tuned models.

5 DISCUSSION & I MPLICATIONS 

The remaining gap between UserLM-2.0 and real user conversations. We first note that our existing metrics are imperfect, as human reference conversations do not achieve perfect scores on Quality or Coverage. Nonetheless, the human references still strongly outperform all simulators, including UserLM-2.0, on these metrics, despite our training directly optimizing these metrics. Ac-cording to our framework, more than 80% of reference conversations are valid in terms of task fidelity and naturalness. This is more than 30% above UserLM-2.0, a gap roughly as large as the gain between the previously best simulator and UserLM-2.0 (14.1% to 46.2%). We believe that further innovation and scaling in the RL recipes could yield further gains on the metrics within our framework, delivering state-of-the-art user simulators. In other words, we believe the gap in metrics that remains in our framework is significant and invite the community to close the gap to develop improved user simulators. 

Poorly chosen naturalness rewards can be worse than none at all. One surprising finding from our alternative reward design experiments (Section 4.4) is that several formulations of naturalness re-wards lead to worse naturalness performance than no naturalness reward at all. More specifically, we found that evaluating naturalness either with an LLM-as-a-judge prompt or using a fixed discrim-10 inator trained only once can harm simulator performance by leading to simulations that are more easily distinguishable from real conversations (i.e., less natural). This phenomenon is understand-able through the lens of reward hacking: the simulator optimizes for a flawed notion of naturalness, making simulated conversations more easily identifiable and less natural. This finding highlights the importance of the dynamically retrained discriminator of our training framework: we demonstrated empirically that this enables the trained simulators to minimize the gap with a reference corpus of real interactions while preventing reward hacking. We hope this experiment encourages the com-munity to limit the use of LLM-as-a-judge for naturalness evaluation, as we show it is not effective. 

UserLM-2.0 is trained and evaluated only on chatbot interactions. For UserLM-2.0, our train-ing and evaluation intend to simulate conversations from the WildChat-4.8M (Zhao et al., 2024) dataset. WildChat conversations are pure chatbot interactions, with no environment present (such as a file system or access to a computing environment). As agentic interaction with AI-based systems become more prevalent, being able to simulate users not only in pure chat settings but also in agen-tic settings that involve tools and environments become more important. This can help evaluate not only how users dynamically reveal their intents, but also how they access files, verify task comple-tions from the agent, and how they choose to delegate tasks within a shared environment. Creating such agentic user simulators faces several key challenges, including a lack of diverse data of agentic user-AI interactions. Initial resources such as SWE-chat (Baumann et al., 2026) show a promising path forward that can enable the training of user simulators for agentic settings. 

Alternative designs for the discriminator. During training, we made several decisions on the information the naturalness discriminator is conditioned on. Specifically, the discriminator we use sees all user utterances, none of the assistant utterances, and the input task intent for a given simu-lated conversation. This causes the discriminator to not only assess whether the user utterances are natural in terms of the surface form, but also whether the conversation is a naturalistic enactment of the entire task intent (as real conversations are always full enactments of the task intent). We believe that alternative inputs to the discriminator can define subtly distinct naturalness rewards, which can be combined to produce more robust and well-rounded naturalness rewards for training. For example, one could formulate turn-level or conversation-level discriminators, with or without task intents provided, with or without assistant utterances included. We hope that the community will explore diverse formulations of discriminator conditions, and find effective ways to combine them into robust methods to train natural user simulators. 

User simulators for real-world use-cases must take privacy into account. Naturalness is a question of how well a given user simulator aligns with behavior in a target corpus. UserLM-2.0, for instance, is trained by first performing SFT on WildChat-4.8M to align its initial behavior with that of users in the dataset. In realistic settings, simply doing SFT on user data runs the risk of having models regurgitate private information. The development of user simulators should work in tandem with the design of privacy-aware training strategies or methods for generating synthetic data for training that remains naturalistic. 

User simulators should be robust to a variety of assistants. Our training and evaluation both used Qwen3.5-9B (Team, 2026) as the assistant model that the simulated user interacts with. For a user simulator to be robust, however, it should provide naturalistic responses to both state-of-the-art assistant models, as well as those that perform poorly or inconsistently in a given task. Devising training and evaluation environments that expose user simulators to a broader range of assistant behaviors is a valuable next step. 

User simulators should be evaluated holistically. Research on user simulators is still nascent, and we currently lack a standardized evaluation methodology. One line of work evaluates simulators by comparing corpus-level statistics of behaviors like turn-counts, turn-lengths, etc. against a corpus of human-AI conversations. These metrics are intuitive and useful for characterizing aggregate behaviors, as shown in our behavioral analysis in Section 4.3. However, a corpus-level alignment on multiple behaviors independently does not guarantee that individual simulations are usable. For example, consider a simulator that, on aggregate, improves both coverage and naturalness but each generated sample maximizes one at the cost of the other. In this case, no individual sample is usable for downstream use-cases of evaluating or training assistant models. Therefore, in addition 11 to corpus-level metrics, we believe that a simulation-level metric is crucial to establishing simulation readiness. Our validity metric captures this new evaluation principle. In our work, we instantiated it to combine task fidelity and naturalness dimensions, although this exact combination can be altered depending on domain specifications. We hope that future work will evaluate user simulators using both simulation- and corpus-level tests for holistic evaluation. 

6 RELATED WORK 

User Simulation: Early work focused on user simulation as a means to evaluate or train task-oriented dialogue policies (Eckert et al., 1997; Shi et al., 2019), with methods ranging from rule-based systems (Schatzmann et al., 2007) to neural architectures (Asri et al., 2016; Kreyssig et al., 2018). Currently, user simulation is predominantly done using LLM assistants prompted zero-shot to act as users on settings ranging from chatbot interactions (Li et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025) to agentic tasks (Barres et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). While such simulations benefit from LLMs’ ability to enact specified task intents, they also suffer from poor alignment with human behavior (Yoon et al., 2024; Ivey et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Prior work has separately explored training models to generate user turns in dialogues. This has most commonly been done through finetuning on human-assistant interactions (Sekulic et al., 2024; Naous et al., 2026; Suh et al., 2026) on datasets such as MultiWOZ and WildChat (Eric et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2024). The resulting models are naturalistic, but also brittle and poor at task-fidelity (Naous et al., 2026). As a result, recent work has placed emphasis on alignment and consistency with user intents via RL (Abdulhai et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Mehri et al., 2026b). We propose training an SFT initialization using RL to maximize both task fidelity and naturalness, operationalized as fooling a dynamically trained discriminator. 

Evaluation for User Simulators: There is currently no consensus on how to evaluate user simu-lators (Pietquin & Hastie, 2013; Deriu et al., 2021). One common approach is evaluating similarity to human interactions, which can involve comparisons of user simulator surface-form statistics and stylistic features (Schatzmann et al., 2005; Pietquin & Hastie, 2013; Ivey et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026) or behavioral patterns (Zhou et al., 2026; Mehri et al., 2026a) against human data. Alterna-tively, similarity can be assessed by how well user simulators approximate assistant performance with real humans (Williams, 2008; Dou et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025b). Other work relies on hu-man judgement as a measure of simulator naturalness (Shi et al., 2019; Sekuli´ c et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2026). However, this can be expensive and depends on annotators’ ability to detect LLM-generated text (Russell et al., 2025). In our work, we use state-of-the-art AI-detection models (Glickenhaus et al., 2026) for a generic measure of naturalness. We complement this by also reporting dataset-specific performance with discriminators trained on our target corpus (Li et al., 2017). 

GAN-Style Training for Language Models: Past work has explored GAN-style adversarial train-ing (Goodfellow et al., 2014b) within language modeling (Fedus et al., 2018; de Masson d’Autume et al., 2019). Most relevant work uses adversarial training for response generation in dialogues or for assistant dialogue agents (Li et al., 2017; Liu & Lane, 2018), where Li et al. (2017) also con-duct evaluation with trained discriminators. Since the inception of LLMs, however, this GAN-style training has largely been abandoned. While trained discriminators exist for AI detection (Thai et al., 2026; Glickenhaus et al., 2026), they are not used for model training. When the notion of discrim-inability is introduced to training, it is through prompted LLM judges (Wang et al., 2026), which we show harms naturalness. Our work uses GAN-style adversarial training for user simulation instead. 

7 CONCLUSION 

We introduce UserLM-2.0, a user simulator for User-AI interaction trained via RL to optimize both task-fidelity and naturalness. We operationalize naturalness as fooling a lightweight discriminator that is periodically retrained throughout RL on real and model-generated conversations. We compare our model against existing trained user simulators and state-of-the-art assistant models prompted to act as users, and find that UserLM-2.0 produces valid conversations meeting both task-fidelity and naturalness criteria 46.2% of the time, more than three times the rate of the next-best user simulator. A DATASET CONSTRUCTION 

A.1 DATASET FILTERING 

We build on top of the filtering strategy of Naous et al. (2026) for WildChat-1M (Zhao et al., 2024) to filter conversations in WildChat-4.8M. We restrict our attention to the already filtered non-toxic com-ponent of the overall WildChat-4.8M dataset. We firstly filter out conversations that were marked as non-English by WildChat. We then filter out conversations featuring heavily templated language. Specifically, we filter out any conversation whose opening 7-gram occurs at least 50 times in the dataset and whose opening 12-gram similarly occurs at least 15 times. We also filter out conversa-tions whose opening turn is repeated verbatim as the first turn at least 50 times in the dataset. Going beyond Naous et al. (2026), we separately observe many interactions attempting to circum-vent the restrictions of the WildChat API by placing interaction histories or few-shot prompts inside the first user turn in an unconventional manner. We heuristically remove such interactions by check-ing for “User:”, “Assistant:”, “System:” and “Bot:” strings in the first turn. As our goal is to model multi-turn interactions, we also filter out conversations that feature only one user turn. Finally, we filter out conversations containing toxic strings, including any conversation featuring mention of the Doki Doki Literature Club characters Natsuki, Sayori, Monika and Yuri to remove interactions from one particular, prolific user generating. A.2 DATASET SPLITS 

In order to construct the train/val/test split, we similarly follow Naous et al. (2026). We use WildChat metadata to group users by country and split the users of each country into train, validation and test splits with a 90/5/5 ratio. Each split then contains interactions associated with users from the respective split. For training and validation sets, we allocate 167625 and 6816 intents for SFT; 10,000 and 1,000 intents for RL; and 3,200 and 1,000 for intents Discriminator training. The 2,000 datapoint test set used for evaluation is then subsampled from the remaining 9,608 remaining test set conversations. It is constructed to feature intents containing 1 to 7 sub-goals, with balance in the number of intents per sub-goals count. In practice, the test dataset contains a small number of intents with 1 and 7 sub-goals. As a result, the test set features 202 intents featuring 1 sub-goals, 336 intents featuring 2-5 sub-goals each, 335 intents featuring 6 sub-goals and 119 intents featuring 7 sub-goals. We use the recommended temperature of 0.7, top-p of 0.8, top-k of 20 and generate at most 512 tokens. We then extract fully-specified intents c∗ for the RL dataset interactions and the full test set. Prompt Construction In order to construct the few-shot examples for the quality prompt, we sam-pled a set of conversations from an SFT-initialized model and then manually labeled each individual example according to what error mode it represents or whether there is no error mode. We then constructed a dataset of 15 examples for each error mode, and for the valid category. We randomly constructed 10 few-shot prompts from this set of examples and evaluated model performance on the remainder. We picked the best performing set, which had an accuracy of 85% with no errors for valid conversations. For coverage, we likewise sampled a set of 100 conversations and annotated the 22 coverage score of each conversation, using GPT-5.3 with reasoning. We then constructed 10 three-shot prompts and evaluated each against the remainder of the 100 conversation pool. We then picked the best-performing selection in terms of MAE against the number of sub-goals in a conversation, at 0.773. We perform evaluation with GPT-5.6-Luna to improve the robustness and reliability of the LLM judge. 

Checkpoint Selection. For checkpoint selection, we select the checkpoint leading to the most nat-ural interactions above a set coverage threshold. Firstly, we find that the Human Reference coverage score on the validation set, as computed by Qwen3.5-9B, is 85.6%. Given this, we set a validation coverage score of 85% as the lower threshold for valid checkpoints. We use the similarity between utterance-length and turn-count histogram distributions of generated conversations and Human Ref-erence conversations as a cheap proxy for naturalness. We use the Jensen-Shannon Divergence as the distance metric and choose the checkpoint receiving the lowest JSD above the 85% coverage threshold. For the No Coverage experiment, there were no such checkpoints. As a result, we chose the checkpoint with the lowest JSD among the two checkpoints that had the highest coverage score. B.3 DISCRIMINATOR TRAINING DETAILS 

We use the 395M ModernBERT-Large (Warner et al., 2025) as the discriminator. For each task intent, we sample one simulated conversation (using the user and assistant models) and one real conversation (from the ground truth dataset). We use only the user turns for discrimination, as we found including assistant turns made simulated conversations perfectly discriminable due to stylistic differences between assistants. This ensures that our dataset is always balanced. For the initial discriminator, we sample interactions on the dedicated Discriminator training and validation set with UserLM-2.0-SFT, using the same inference procedure as in RL training. For discriminator re-training during RL, on the other hand, half of our training dataset consists of con-versations based on task intents from the most recent 25 RL steps. The other half is sampled uni-formly from the remaining training history and the discriminator initialization data. We train for five epochs and pick the checkpoint achieving the best validation accuracy. We use AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) as the optimizer. We use a learning rate of 2e-5 with linear decay and a warm-up ratio of 0.05, a weight decay of 1e-4 and gradient clipping norm of 1.0. The batch-size is 64. B.4 LLM J UDGE PROMPT CONSTRUCTION
