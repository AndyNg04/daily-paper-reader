## Abstract 

Personal agents need to draw on long-term interaction history to retrieve and apply external knowledge suited to a user’s current request. Even an answer grounded in relevant documents can be unsuitable when it overlooks the user’s circumstances. This creates two connected challenges: using historical conditions to identify the knowledge needed for a new request, and applying that knowl-edge to the user’s situation. Existing benchmarks advance memory recall, personalized retrieval, and memory-guided action, while the joint evaluation of these two challenges remains underexplored. We introduce ColaBench, a synthetic benchmark in health and daily living, with 349 tasks across 10 Profiles and a knowledge base of 13,033 documents. Its Evidence Graph connects personal condi-tions to their disclosures across public interaction sessions, records prior document exposure, and links answer requirements to per-sonal and newly acquired document evidence. We also introduce MemKB, which uses state summaries to inform queries and rule application, and memory–document links to retrieve knowledge associated with the user’s history. MemKB achieves 86.25% Accu-racy with an average of 4.07 interaction rounds per task. Task-level analysis finds larger gains on tasks integrating information across multiple historical Facts; error analysis further identifies unsup-ported personal assumptions and rule misapplication as obstacles to using retrieved evidence. ## 1 Introduction 

Personal agents must draw on long-term interaction history to handle users’ evolving requests [10, 19]. Users often leave prior experiences, preferences, and constraints unstated [6], while the current task may require new external knowledge [8]. Consider a user asking whether they qualify for a caregiver respite grant after mentioning, in separate conversations, that they care for a relative with Alzheimer’s disease and live alone. The agent must find the grant’s eligibility rules and connect the co-residence requirement to the earlier disclosure of living alone (Table 1). Overlooking that connection can turn relevant information into unsuitable advice. Reliable personalization therefore depends on how history guides knowledge acquisition and how the acquired knowledge is applied to the user. These tasks pose two connected challenges. Knowledge acqui-sition requires recovering the historical conditions that determine arXiv:2610.30989v1 [cs.AI] 22 Oct 2026 WWW ’27, May 10–14, 2027, Dublin, Ireland Zhang et al. 

what to search for, even when the current request leaves them im-plicit. Rule application requires matching retrieved prerequisites, exceptions, and procedures to known personal circumstances while preserving what remains unknown. The process can move in both directions: history shapes the search, and a newly retrieved rule can make an earlier disclosure relevant. When memory and knowledge are retrieved separately, the agent must establish these connections through its queries and reasoning. Existing benchmarks address important parts of this process. LoCoMo and LongMemEval provide a foundation for evaluating memory through cross-session question answering, temporal rea-soning, and state updates [9, 16]. TREC iKAT brings user context, query reformulation, and external retrieval into the answering pro-cess [1]. Mem2ActBench and MemoryArena examine how memory guides subsequent tool use and interdependent actions [5, 15]. A remaining gap concerns the joint evaluation of how disclosed per-sonal conditions guide the acquisition and application of knowledge not yet encountered in the interaction. This requires tasks that trace what the user has disclosed, what documents the agent has already encountered, and which personal and knowledge premises support each required conclusion. These dependencies make it possible to examine where the connection between history and knowledge succeeds or breaks down. We introduce ColaBench , a benchmark built around these de-pendencies. Its Evidence Graph connects personal Facts to the public messages that disclose them, records document exposure during interaction, and links subsequent answer requirements to their personal and knowledge sources. Its document links guide knowledge exploration, and its disclosure and exposure records con-strain staged, multi-agent task construction to disclosed personal conditions and supporting documents not previously exposed in the history. The resulting benchmark comprises 349 enhanced and validated Items across 10 Profiles and a knowledge base of 13,033 documents. The graph makes each task’s evidence dependencies explicit, supporting evaluation of knowledge acquisition and rule application and diagnosis of whether errors arise from missing personal conditions, missing knowledge, or their use in the answer. We introduce MemKB, a memory-guided knowledge retrieval method that connects historical context to knowledge candidates. A user state summary brings dispersed personal conditions into query formulation and subsequent judgment. MemKB encodes memory text, establishes memory–document links, and fuses documents associated with retrieved memories with direct semantic and key-word retrieval results for the current query. The answering agent combines retrieved memories and document evidence to assess which rules apply to the user’s circumstances. MemKB achieves 86.25% Accuracy, compared with 79.94% for Mem0+Hybrid using the same underlying memories, a gain of 6.30 percentage points, while reducing average interaction rounds from 4.44 to 4.07. The gain is larger on tasks requiring multiple historical Facts: 9.35 percentage points, compared with 4.96 on single-Fact tasks. Case and error analyses show how personal assumptions and rule misapplication can affect answers even when relevant evidence is available. These findings motivate evaluating memory through the knowledge it helps agents acquire and the user-specific judgments it supports. Our contributions are as follows: 

• Evidence Graph–driven benchmark synthesis. We de-velop a staged, multi-agent synthesis framework in which accumulated Fact disclosure and knowledge exposure con-strain subsequent Item construction, producing the traceable ColaBench benchmark and its evaluation protocol. 

• Memory-guided knowledge retrieval. We introduce MemKB, which combines state summaries and memory– document links to guide knowledge retrieval and support user-specific rule application. 

• Empirical analysis of memory utility. Through task-structure groupings, component ablations, and evidence traces, we characterize memory utility in knowledge acquisi-tion and rule application: gains on tasks integrating multiple historical Facts and failures involving omitted personal con-ditions, unsupported assumptions, and rule misapplication after knowledge access. 

## 2 Related Work 

Long-Term Memory and Task Execution. Memory architectures organize interaction history for subsequent decisions. Generative Agents combine experience retrieval and reflection to support plan-ning [11]; MemoryBank updates user memories over time [19], and MemGPT manages information across memory tiers [10]. Mem0 supports memory extraction, consolidation, and retrieval [2]. A-MEM builds evolving links between memory notes [17], while Graphiti, the graph engine underlying Zep, maintains temporal entity and fact relations [12]. LoCoMo generates long-term conversations from profiles and temporal event graphs, with human review, to evaluate question answering, event summarization, and multimodal dialogue genera-tion [9]. LongMemEval covers cross-session reasoning, temporal information, and state updates, and analyzes memory systems at the indexing, retrieval, and reading stages [16]. Mem2ActBench exam-ines how memory supports subsequent tool selection and argument specification [15], and MemoryArena evaluates experience reuse across interdependent multi-session tasks [5]. ColaBench studies subsequent actions through knowledge search, evidence reading, and rule application, tracing how recovered user state supports each required conclusion. 

Personalized Conversational Search. LaMP studies retrieval of user-specific records for personalized classification and genera-tion [14]. PersonaMem evaluates profile tracking and response se-lection as users’ circumstances evolve [6]. TREC iKAT is a closely re-lated benchmark for personalized knowledge acquisition. Personal textual knowledge bases specify user backgrounds, and conversa-tional tasks require systems to use this personal context. Evaluation covers passage relevance as well as the relevance, completeness, grounding, and naturalness of generated answers [1]. ColaBench records personal-state disclosure and knowledge exposure across simulated interactions. Its tasks trace evidence from public user statements through rule premises to required judgments, enabling evaluation of history-guided knowledge retrieval and rule applica-tion.

## 3 Evidence-Graph-Based Construction 

ColaBench uses a Plan–Create–Review multi-agent framework with stage-specific roles and information access to ground synthetic personal backgrounds, interaction histories, and tasks in retrieved documents. An Evidence Graph G = (𝑉 , 𝐸 ) begins with docu-ments and their relationships and expands throughout construc-tion. The graph incorporates Document, Profile, Fact, Topic, Turn, and Item nodes. Document links guide knowledge exploration, while Fact–Topic relations organize user states and interaction tasks. Records of fact disclosure and document exposure determine which personal conditions and new knowledge are available for later Items. Figure 1 shows the roles and information boundaries across document preparation, the three generation stages, and Item enhancement. 

## 3.1 Task Definition 

The evaluation tasks examine how an agent uses personal condi-tions from history to acquire and apply external knowledge. We denote the task setting by 𝑥 = (𝑞, H, K; M) , where 𝑞 is the cur-rent request, H is the preceding public interaction history, K is the frozen knowledge base, and M denotes the evaluation metrics defined in Section 5.2. Personal conditions can guide knowledge search, and newly retrieved rules can prompt the agent to revisit history. Table 1 illus-trates this interaction through a grant’s co-residence requirement. 

## 3.2 Stage 0: Building the Document Layer 

We collect public health information, drug labels, and open text-books from screened public sources through APIs, XML files, and web pages. After text cleaning, the base corpus contains 11,069 documents. Each document forms an initial Document node in the Evidence Graph, retaining its text and source information. Appen-dix A.1 details the corpus sources and processing. Starting from seed documents, an LLM retrieves and reads re-lated documents to establish edges grounded in specific knowledge relationships, storing a description and weight for each relation. We further search, fetch, and filter web pages on relevant topics, incorporating the resulting documents into the corpus and graph. This document layer supplies knowledge and exploration links for Profile generation and Item construction. Web pages discovered during these stages are also added as Document nodes. 

## 3.3 Stage I: Grounding Profiles and Facts 

To construct internally consistent users with varied life circum-stances, a Profile construction agent, the Director, plans new sce-narios from the existing profile and consults retrieved documents to create Facts describing personal conditions and Topics specifying interaction tasks. An independent review agent checks consistency, relevance, and task completeness against the existing state and source material. Accepted content is added to the Evidence Graph. Documents ground the synthetic scenarios in relevant contexts and knowledge, while links among Profiles, Facts, Topics, and Docu-ments support continued profile development across life domains and provide evidence for subsequent interactions. 

## 3.4 Stage II: Growing Public Interaction Traces 

Interactions between a user Simulator and an Assistant gradually disclose personal conditions as tasks unfold. The Simulator has access to the frozen Profile and Topic, whereas the Assistant re-sponds using the public conversation and retrieved knowledge. The Simulator follows up or switches Topics in response to the Assistant’s actual replies, producing a continuous Trace driven by task progress. Accepted interactions enter the Evidence Graph as Turn nodes, recording Fact disclosures and document exposure to establish the history available for subsequent construction. 

## 3.5 Stage III: Constructing Items 

To construct an initial Item, the Builder starts from disclosed per-sonal conditions and seeks new knowledge whose application re-quires those conditions, establishing the task and its answer ev-idence. An independent question-writing role then formulates a natural request from the public background and task intent, leaving the necessary personal context in history. A history-removal test checks whether the task can be completed without the public inter-action history and whether an unmet answer requirement depends on missing historical conditions. Subsequent review assesses source support, knowledge novelty, and continuity with the preceding in-teraction. Accepted Items enter the Evidence Graph with links to the required Facts, historical Turns, and supporting Documents, providing a traceable account of how personal conditions affect the application of new knowledge. Construction details and evidence annotations appear in Appendix A.

## 4.2 Memory-Linked Knowledge Retrieval 

Hybrid retrieves documents directly from the KB through semantic and keyword channels. At retrieval step 𝑡 , the agent uses the current request 𝑞 and information acquired so far to generate a semantic query 𝑞 𝑡 and a keyword set 𝑤 𝑡 . Vector similarity and term matching produce candidate lists 𝐿 sem and 𝐿 key , respectively, whose rankings are then fused. In baselines with separate memory and knowledge retrieval, a dedicated tool returns personal memory, which influ-ences knowledge retrieval through the queries formulated by the agent. MemKB adds a third retrieval channel that transfers query rel-evance from memory cards to linked documents. The same 𝑞 𝑡 re-trieves cards by normalized inner product. Following card rank order, a round-robin procedure takes one document from each card per pass, skipping documents already in the candidate list, until the candidate budget is reached or all cards are exhausted. This produces 𝐿 mem . Distributing candidate slots across relevant cards allows knowledge associated with different historical conditions to enter the candidate set. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) [3] combines the memory-linked candidates with Hybrid’s two direct retrieval channels, incorporat-ing both associations with personal history and direct matches to the current query:

## 4.3 From User State to Rule Application 

The same answering agent compares the conditions specified by retrieved rules with the user’s state and memory records, continu-ing to search or call Read as needed. Its answering prompt includes a source-attribution constraint: distinguish the speaker, actor, and event status in memory records, and ground personal communi-cations and decisions in the records that support them. When the source or event status is unspecified, the agent is instructed to pre-serve that uncertainty. This constraint guides the agent to retain the provenance of personal conditions when applying rules. 

## 5 Experimental Setup 5.1 Methods and Experimental Settings 

We pair Mem0, Graphiti, and A-MEM with a general-purpose retrieval-augmented generation component, Hybrid, and compare them with No Memory+Hybrid and MemKB. Hybrid combines semantic and keyword retrieval over the KB through RRF [3]. The Mem0+Hybrid baseline uses Mem0’s non-graph memory [2]. Graphiti+Hybrid retrieves facts about entities and their relations ex-tracted from conversations [12], while A-MEM+Hybrid uses mem-ory evolution and neighbor expansion [17]. These three methods build memory from the same public user and assistant messages. MemKB additionally uses public records of historical document access to build a memory-linked retrieval channel and supplies a state summary before the first query. The main comparison uses all 349 tasks from 10 Profiles and the same frozen KB across methods. All methods can make tool calls for up to four rounds per task, followed by a final answering round. Knowledge search returns five documents by default, with a maximum of 20. Read returns at most 100 lines and 3,000 tokens per call. All methods use DeepSeek v4.1 Flash for answering. GLM-5.3 Flash evaluates each answer in a separate call with low reasoning effort. Model parameters appear in Appendix B.2, and the shared evaluation protocol appears in Appendix D.1. 

## 5.2 Evaluation Metrics 

Accuracy measures whether an answer correctly completes the current task. A passing answer must address all explicitly requested subquestions and provide correct core conclusions and key actions, including the relevant personal conditions, applicability require-ments, and limitations. The evaluator checks factual errors and omissions by asking whether correcting them would change the conclusion, action, timing, applicability, or safety. Errors and omis-sions that affect task completion lead to failure; incidental differ-ences are recorded separately. Grounded equivalent wording and implicit use of personal conditions are accepted, while unknown facts require the necessary conditional branches. Evaluation inputs combine the full public history, reference answer, requirements, historical and task-supporting documents, and each method’s ac-tual Search and Read returns. Metric and input details appear in Appendix D.1.

## 6.2 Combining Personal Information 

Personal Conditions and Their History Sources. We group tasks by the number of annotated Facts and cited user–assistant interaction pairs to examine tasks that combine more personal information. In Figure 3(a,b), the Accuracy gain is 4.96 percentage points on single-Fact tasks and 9.35 points on multi-Fact tasks. The gain is 5.33 points on tasks citing one or two interaction pairs, and 8.57 points on tasks citing at least three pairs. These groupings overlap: 91 multi-Fact tasks also cite at least three interaction pairs. MemKB gains more when tasks combine personal information. 

Sensitivity to Task Composition. Stratifying by Profile and answer-requirement count and applying common weights to strata con-taining both Fact groups yields gains of 4.87 and 14.41 percentage points on 316 tasks (Figure 3(d)). Adding construction cohort yields gains of 5.86 and 12.24 points for single-Fact and multi-Fact tasks on 275 tasks. Appendix C.2 details the strata and bootstrap intervals. Larger observed gains across these analyses highlight the value of organizing multiple historical conditions for knowledge selection and rule application. 

## 6.3 Shared Access and Answer Quality 

We examine answer quality on subsets defined by both methods’ observed reference-document access (Figure 3(c)). The 136 tasks satisfying Both exposed are those where each method receives the reference document through search or a nonempty Read response. Accuracy is 86.03% for Mem0+Hybrid and 90.44% for MemKB. On the 108 tasks satisfying Both read , where each method receives docu-ment text through Read, Accuracy is 87.96% and 90.74%, respectively. Even with access to the same document, the visible passages, avail-able personal conditions, and their application can differ. Together with the following cases, these answer gaps highlight the role of personal conditions in applying retrieved knowledge. 

## 6.4 Case Studies: Rule Application 

Table 5 contrasts recovery of personal history with a shared failure to preserve an unknown condition; Appendix C.4 provides source evidence. 

Personal History Beyond the Query Topic. Despite reading the same contraindication document, Mem0+Hybrid’s two memory searches emphasize teaching content, while its answer claims no record of cancer treatment. ## 7 Discussion and Limitations 

Method development and comparison use ten Profiles, with com-ponent ablations on three selected Profiles. Generalization to new users and the reliability of automated evaluation require indepen-dent data and human calibration. The frozen health and everyday-life corpus makes document versions, sources, and reading scope reproducible; generalization to other domains and live Web envi-ronments remains to be evaluated. Knowledge comes from real documents, while interactions encode synthetic personal states. Task answers have not been clinically validated. 

## 8 Conclusion 

ColaBench evaluates memory-guided knowledge acquisition and application through an Evidence Graph linking answer require-ments to historical and document evidence. MemKB improves task accuracy with fewer interaction rounds, with ablations supporting the contributions of state summaries and document links. Together, they connect memory evaluation to the knowledge agents acquire and the user-specific judgments they make.

## A.4 Acceptance and Evidence Mapping 

The history-removal test gives a blind solver the current question and new supporting text. A comparator checks its answer against the reference answer and necessary requirements. A task is labeled answerable without history when all requirements are satisfied, and history-dependent when a requirement is unmet because the historical context is missing. Remaining cases are labeled as hav-ing insufficient evidence. Final validation checks whether the task naturally extends the interaction, whether personal conditions are grounded in their sources, whether historical knowledge is insuf-ficient, whether new evidence supports the answer, and whether the answer adds information and satisfies safety requirements. It retains the history-dependence label from the preceding test. We represent each answer requirement as 𝑒 𝑗 = (𝑟 𝑗 , 𝑈 𝑗 , 𝐹 𝑗 , 𝐾 𝑗 , 𝑎 𝑗 ),linking the requirement 𝑟 𝑗 to verbatim user statements 𝑈 𝑗 , personal conditions 𝐹 𝑗 , knowledge rules 𝐾 𝑗 , and the required judgment or action 𝑎 𝑗 . Personal evidence traces back to user messages, and knowledge evidence traces back to documents. Unknown premises are handled through conditional branches. Programmatic checks validate identifiers and reference integrity, while a language model assesses semantic support against the source text. 

Item Refinement and Enhancement. Refinement jointly updates the question, reference answer, requirements, and evidence map-ping while preserving public history and personal conditions. Re-wording retains the supporting document. Multi-condition enhance-ment combines at least two disclosed conditions around the initial task, identifying the judgment affected by each, and may replace the document with a retrieved candidate from the same Topic. Failed drafts are excluded, and revisions are re-evaluated under the same criteria.

## D Evaluation Protocol D.1 Metrics and Scoring Inputs 

Accuracy follows Section 5.2: one call checks personal facts, knowledge claims, and task requirements, returning pass , fail ,or needs_adjudication . All main-comparison and ablation judgments resolve to pass or fail ; Accuracy divides passes by all tasks in the comparison. The 48 MemKB errors in Section 6.6 are failed answers. Read Recall follows Section 5.2, using unique reference docu-ments with nonempty Read returns; search snippets, failed reads, and empty reads do not count. First-search and overall search hit rates measure reference-document presence in the first or any suc-cessful knowledge-search response, respectively. Their denomina-tor includes all cohort tasks, counting tasks without successful searches as misses. Memory-only searches, failed calls, and internal candidates are excluded. All methods share the task’s complete public history, reference answer, requirements, and fixed historical and new-support docu-ments as evaluation references. The 1,015 requirements specify ap-plicability conditions and unknown premises. Candidate inputs add the answer and actual Search snippets and Read text. Agent-visible evidence includes supplied states, memories, and Search/Read re-turns; internal candidates and evaluator-only material are excluded. Full construction, memory, and evaluation prompts and input tem-plates will be provided on GitHub. Model parameters appear in Appendix B.2. 

## D.2 Answer-Quality Dimensions 

We evaluate the 222 Mem0+Hybrid and MemKB answers on the 111-task ablation cohort. State Fidelity checks whether represented personal conditions preserve the historical speaker, actor, timing, and uncertainty. Condition Coverage measures whether the per-sonal premises needed for the current task are substantively re-flected. Evidence Grounding assesses support for knowledge claims in the supplied documents and actual retrieved passages. Rule Ap-plication evaluates whether prerequisites, restrictions, exceptions, and timing are correctly applied to the user’s situation. Answer Completeness measures coverage of requested subquestions and necessary actions. Each call returns a score with candidate excerpts, source evidence, and a rationale. 

## D.3 Error Categories and Evidence Visibility 

The primary category identifies each failed answer’s decisive er-ror; its Accuracy verdict is unchanged. Personal Assumptions covers treating unknown states, plans, advice, or another person’s informa-tion as established personal facts. Context Underuse covers failing to apply established user conditions to the current judgment. Rule Mis-application covers incorrect knowledge claims, rule interpretations, or applicability judgments. Answer Omissions covers unanswered necessary subquestions, actions, restrictions, or steps when no more direct personal-context error explains the omission. Other Issues covers errors outside these categories or unresolved attribu-tion and source conflicts. Up to two secondary categories record other independently supported errors; Figure 4 reports primary categories. Visibility concerns the personal premises and rules needed to resolve the errors, assessed against the agent’s question, states, memories, and Search and Read returns. Visible indicates that all required evidence was available; Partly visible , that some evidence was available but key content was missing; and Not visible , that the key evidence was absent. Evaluator-only material and unreturned candidates are excluded; for unsupported personal assertions, visi-bility concerns the correct condition or uncertainty. Uncertain is used when the records or source conflicts prevent a reliable judg-ment; no answer received this label.
