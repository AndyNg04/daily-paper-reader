## Abstract 

Objectives: LLM-based chat agents are increasingly used for complex, multi-turn, multilingual medical triage in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), yet evaluation methods focus on clearly articulated single-turn, English-only benchmarks that primarily test the chatbots’ knowledge. In practice, however, these chatbots are used when users are confused, overwhelmed, emotionally vulnerable and may communicate more openly with a non-human entity than with a human, usually giving a mixture of unclear, misspelled single-word answers and longer unconstrained regional dialects, where the context of a conversation can shift rapidly. Existing evaluation methods remain largely centered on static conversations, with less attention to whether triage chatbots maintain appropriate clinical responses and adapt to the conversation as the context evolves across multiple turns. We propose SIM-UT (Simulation of User Behaviour for Triage), a multi-turn, simulation-based evaluation method that identifies key ecologically valid contextually-relevant failure modes of LLM-based triage chat agents. 

Methods: Using SIM-UT, we conducted a simulation-based behavioural evaluation of three real-world medical triage and severity assessment WhatsApp-based health chatbots deployed for a) respiratory screening program in India, b) hearing health support programme in South Africa and c) a diabetes and lifestyle coaching agent in Kenya, part of a women-centred primary care service. We compared a medically fine-tuned open model (MedGemma) against general-purpose frontier models (ChatGPT and Claude) across 5,000+ multilingual simulated user conversation turns, designed to reflect realistic and sometimes adversarial user behaviour, grounded in the personas, constraints and communication patterns of the real-world user populations, including locally contextualised, multi-language, multi-turn interactions. Each chatbot was evaluated over 2-4 iterative rounds for scope adherence and clinical safety, with prompt, harness and evaluation-criteria updates informed by identified failure modes in each iteration. 

Results: An important failure mode emerged in both model families: de-escalation risk. In the initial turns, the agents correctly identified medical emergencies, however, when the user minimised the severity of the symptoms without introducing any new clinical information, the agent de-escalated and exhibited clinically dangerous sycophantic behaviour. For example, it agreed with a pregnant user that her vaginal bleeding was “not a problem” because it had happened after intercourse; agreed with a mother that she could take her child to the clinic in the morning because it was late at night, even though the child, who had an ear infection, had newly reported a bad headache and light sensitivity; and agreed that decreased fetal movements at 32 weeks “can be normal” because the user thought babies sleep a lot. Each of these cases requires immediate medical attention since they are potential presentations of life-threatening conditions for the mother or the child. 

Conclusions: We recommend a user simulation-based behavioural evaluation, grounded in real user context, covering adversarial and edge cases as a standard practice for triage chatbots in production. Frameworks like SIM-UT help identify failure modes early enough to add safeguards to the triage agent before it goes live or for subsequent iterations.

## 1. Introduction 

Health systems in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are increasingly delivering health screen-ing, education, and behavioural coaching through LLM-based chat agents on consumer messaging platforms such as WhatsApp [Eltigani et al., 2025]. WhatsApp is widely used across diverse populations and is familiar to many users, making it a practical platform for delivering health services at scale [Manji et al., 2021]. We worked with three real-world WhatsApp based health chatbots deployed on the Turn.io platform. 1

The first was a hearing health support system in South Africa, developed by hearX Foundation. 2 This agent supports teacher training programmes, which equips early childhood and primary school edu-cators to recognise signs of hearing loss in children and to know when and how to refer. The second was a diabetes and lifestyle coaching agent in Kenya, part of a women-centred primary care service, developed by Cliniva. 3 This agent provides lifestyle and health coaching to women managing diabetes, pre-diabetes or gestational diabetes, including during pregnancy and postpartum, as part of a nurse-led primary care service. Many users face cost and food-access constraints. And, the third was a respiratory screening system in India, developed by Respira Health. 4 This agent screens users for medical eligibility and safety to participate in an eight-week self-guided pulmonary rehabilitation programme delivered over WhatsApp. Figure 1 shows simulated conversations for each of the three deployed chatbots. Although the three systems served different clinical and behavioural use cases, they shared several important characteristics: First, each triage chatbot was designed for users with limited medical knowl-edge, most of whom were not first-language English speakers, communicated in regional languages and dialects, and acted within practical constraints such as food availability, cost and access to care. Second, a medical emergency could surface during an otherwise routine conversation primarily because users disclose more information, directly and honestly, to a non-human entity such as an AI chatbot, with less fear of judgement [Lucas et al., 2014; Torous et al., 2015]. Therefore, symptoms that a user might withhold from a clinician could appear mid-conversation in a chat about common queries such as diet or exercise. Each chatbot was expected to remain clinically safe and adhere to its scope: to provide appropriate guidance within its intended scope, and to recognise when clinical escalation was required and handle it appropriately. These three systems also represented distinct user populations and contextual constraints. The South African hearing health system included educators (primarily preschool teachers and assistants), parents, caregivers, and community health workers who interacted in isiZulu, English, and Afrikaans. The Kenyan diabetes coaching agent provided lifestyle guidance to women seeking to lose weight, including those facing financial and food availability constraints. Some of these users had encountered weight-loss claims through social media and video content and often held expectations of rapid results. Users of the Indian respiratory screening agent were primarily Hindi-speaking and frequently typed in Latin script, mixing Hindi and English languages even when the interface was in Devanagari. Many users were time-constrained, lacked the financial means for private care, or lived in tier-two and tier-three cities and rural areas where pulmonary rehabilitation is not locally available. Existing benchmark datasets, including MedQA [Jin et al., 2021], AfriMed-QA [Nimo et al., 2025], and HealthBench [Arora et al., 2025] are not directly relevant to evaluate such conversational safety chal-lenges, primarily because they measure recall and clinical reasoning on well-formed single-turn inputs, focused towards clinician inputs; even AfriMed-QA, which is regionally grounded, does not model how

We propose SIM-UT, 5 a multi-turn user simulation-based evaluation framework for real world pro-duction triage agents. We iteratively refined the agents over two to four rounds based on failure modes identified in previous rounds. These evaluations revealed what single-turn/static benchmarks fail to detect: LLM models are prone to softening escalation when users push back. Both model families in these agents correctly identified the emergency in early turns but softened their position once the user rationalised the symptoms away. These failure modes were identified early enough to add safeguards to the triage agent before it goes live or for subsequent iterations. The remainder of the paper describes the evaluation framework (Section 2), the simulation runs (Section 3), and the failure modes and their remediation (Section 4). 

## 2. Evaluation Framework 

The evaluation framework, SIM-UT, consists of three components: (1) A simulated user that enacts multiple personas grounded in the actual target population of the specific triage agent, (2) the production triage agent being evaluated, and (3) the evaluator LLM-as-a-judge that assesses the multi-turn conversation against the respective eval criteria. Figure 2 shows the overall architecture. The interaction space for the simulated user was defined by two dimensions: the User Persona and the Scenario. The User Persona represented the behaviour of the users, primarily covering their thought process, long-term goals & communication style. The Scenario covered their short-term goals, primarily the mindset with which they interact with the chatbot. SIM-UT was set to evaluate all three medical triage chatbots across the two base LLMs. The base models were ChatGPT and MedGemma for the hearing health support programme in South Africa and the diabetes and lifestyle coaching agent in Kenya. The base models were Claude and MedGemma for the respiratory screening program in India. Each chatbot interacted with a simulated user agent within a structured interaction space. The simulated user was an orchestration of several components (e.g., persona selection, scenario execution, and turn scheduling), with claude-sonnet-4-6 generating the user’s utterances. These conversations happened across 10 turns between the simulated user and the agent. A third LLM, called the evaluator LLM, primarily evaluates the conversation between the simulated user agent and the triage chatbot on key

2.1. Simulated User 

The simulated user interacts with the agent within a structured space primarily defined by their persona and the scenario they are enacting. This separation of the simulated user into the persona and scenario is motivated by the memory architectures in LLM-based user simulations more broadly. Wang et al. [2025] ground their RecAgent framework in the cognitive science distinction between long-term and short-term memory. The long-term memory stores generalizable traits that shape behaviour across all interactions, whereas the short-term memory holds the situational information relevant to the current episode, a structure that SOTOPIA [Zhou et al., 2024] also reflects in its combination of stable character profiles with per-episode social scenarios. We adopt a similar separation for our simulated users. The persona is defined by the long-term behavioural traits of a user, primarily their motivations & communication style. For example, a parent who is time-constrained in South Africa. The scenario primarily includes the situation the user is coming into the chatbot with; for example, their child with a previously diagnosed ear infection now has a stiff neck and fever, or whether they are simply hoping to find a quick, reassuring answer and cannot afford to take a day off. 

2.1.1. User Persona 

The user personas focused on two dimensions, covering the breadth and depth of the primary user base of each triage chatbot, and the representation was grounded in the actual user behaviour for each of the three triage chatbots. The communication style, phrasing, and constraints for each of the personas were examined by the local experts operating the chatbot, who examined samples of prior user conversations as a ground truth. Further, the synthetic persona conversations were reviewed and iterated based on the feedback from local experts across multiple rounds, until they confirmed it to be sufficiently representative of the real conversations observed on the ground. Grounding the personas on individual

conversations rather than general demographics made the simulations more realistic [Park et al., 2024]. For the South Africa-based hearing health support program, it covered school teachers (both primary and secondary), the Centre Principal, parents, and community health workers. For the Kenya-based diabetes and lifestyle coaching chatbot, the user personas covered three dimensions: the personas’ clinical status (pre-diabetic, diagnosed as diabetic or gestational diabetic), structural constraints (day job with access to limited food options in the canteen, gig worker with a sporadic schedule, single mother working on a job or a mother having limited clinic access), and stance against information (having unrealistic expectations from social media, believing primarily in traditional home remedies). For the India-based respiratory screening program, the personas covered two main dimensions: firstly, their communication style (Hindi, code-mixed Hindi + English, Punjabi, Roman Hindi with Marathi words) and clinical picture (COPD, post-TB, COVID, undiagnosed but symptomatic). 

2.1.2. Scenarios 

The primary goal of the scenarios was to ensure the vast coverage of situations a user persona can interact with each agent, particularly vague human inputs, out-of-scope, and structural constraints that may arise when users interact with the chatbot. Same as with personas, the scenarios were validated with local experts operating the chatbot to confirm they reflected the real user interactions brought to the agent. Particular emphasis was given on covering not only routine queries but also a wide range of adversarial queries and edge cases to ensure failures in the triage and escalation could be identified in the simulation environment. This scenario design also ensured it accounts for the known limitation of LLM-based user simulation, that simulated users are unrealistically cooperative when left to the defaults [Ni et al., 2026], so real user behaviour like pushback, minimisation, and resistance with local constraints had to be explicitly specified. It was also ensured that these scenarios capture the specific linguistic and cultural nuances, like code-switching between Hindi and English in India, local dialect terms, and local food preferences in Kenya, such as ugali. For the South Africa-based hearing health support program, the scenarios covered various clinical pictures (facial weakness after ear infection, headache with light sensitivity, fever and stiff neck, persistent mild ear pain, dizziness), biases (deaf culture vs hearing aids, sign language upskilling and use of cochlear implants), and scope boundaries of the agent (diagnosis request, home remedies, local referral cost, skin rash and off-topic writings). For the Kenya-based diabetes and lifestyle coaching chatbot, the scenarios covered clinical picture (symptoms such as excessive thirst, headache with blurred vision, swelling in legs, breathlessness post climbing stairs, reduced fetal movements, or someone just vaguely mentioning about “not feeling well”), Mental Health scenarios (postpartum anxiety, excessive crying and disconnect from the baby, or active / passive ideation of self harm), misinformation or structural constraints (diabetic treatment with aloe vera, 7 day water fast for reset of metabolism, or carnivore diet for diabetes reversal, ability to have food only in the canteen, unable to bear the cost of insulin or isolation postpartum with no family support). For India-based respiratory screening programs, the scenarios mainly covered clinical pictures that were eligible, ineligible, or needed a physiotherapist referral, primarily based on their symptoms and ongoing medication. The Scenarios covered people taking OTC medication such as paracetamol and actually mentioned them with the local names (e.g. Dolo), mainly misspelled or written partially. They also involved people taking long-term medications for diabetes and hypertension, where they do not fully know the name and mainly only understand the colour and shape of the medication. Scenarios also covered symptoms such as pain or nausea mentioned in local Hindi or Punjabi dialect (Eg. “Jee-ghabrana” which in its literal sense is “Nausea” but is interchangeably used to describe “not feeling well” in its general sense as well).

2.2. Simulations 

In the SIM-UT multi-turn simulations, the relevant persona and scenario were linked together to create the simulated user agent, where each turn of the simulated user had the context of the previous turns. The simulated user would have up to 10 conversational turns with the agent. The simulated conversation could end earlier if the agent decided to escalate the conversation to a human (clinician) in each of the cases. Additionally, the simulated user could also end the conversation earlier based on the response of the agent in the previous turn. We realised this primarily happened if the simulated user was satisfied with the answer and had no other follow-up question. One of the key aspects of each of the simulations was that the user would incrementally mention symptoms and other details across the turns, and behave their persona across the 10 turns that typically include user minimisation, challenging, or rationalising the clinical risk the agent may have identified in the previous turns. 2.3. Evaluations 

The evaluation dimensions, such as clinical safety, scope adherence, and communication quality, were defined separately by the local experts for each of the three deployments. The context-specific definitions were important groundwork required to ensure the evaluation outcomes were relevant and actionable for each of the teams deploying these chatbots. For example, scope adherence for the South Africa-based deployment was defined around the symptoms and coaching of ear-related queries, whereas for the Kenya-based deployment the scope adherence was defined around diabetes and lifestyle coaching. The eval definitions were iterated over the course of simulation rounds, following review by the clinical practitioners for each deployment, as described in Section 3. These evaluation dimensions were run as LLM-as-a-judge [Zheng et al., 2023] (claude-sonnet-4-6) across individual turns as well as the overall simulated conversation. The specific evaluators for the South Africa-based hearing health support program for clinical safety included: covering correct identification and escalation of emergencies, such as severe ear pain, discharge or bleeding, vertigo, fever with ear symptoms, without falsely alerting routine or care questions, and without giving any clinical diagnosis, treatment or medication recommendations. For Scope Adherence - covered handling of ear and hearing topics, and politely refusing questions other than that, for example, skin, nose, etc. For Communication Quality - covering plain, non-clinical language that their typical user persona could understand from a message on WhatsApp. Upper limits to the character count and use of emoticons were encouraged in this evaluation primarily due to the interface of the conversation being WhatsApp. The evaluators for the Kenya-based diabetes and lifestyle coaching chatbot were clinical safety covering dietary and physical activity advice consistent with WHO antenatal-care and physical-activity guidance [World Health Organization, 2020; World Health Organization et al., 2020] and WHO diagnostic criteria for hyperglycaemia in pregnancy [World Health Organization et al., 2013]; and escalation of signs of pre-eclampsia [National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2019; World Health Organization et al., 2014], vaginal bleeding and reduced fetal movements [National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2021; World Health Organization et al., 2007], ensuring the escalation holds under user minimisation. Contextual relevance - ensuring the dietary and coaching advice was tailored to the urban Kenya setting, particularly their staple food, life stage sensitivity such as pregnancy or post-partum. Scope Adherence - ensuring the responses and recommendations were within the constraints of the user, such as limited food options in their canteen. For the India-based respiratory screening, the evaluators were more binary in nature, particularly if they were classified into the right bucket of eligible for the program, ineligible, or need a referral from the clinician. It was particularly important to iterate the agent towards no false positives in the eligible cohort, i.e., users that were ineligible or required referral were not incorrectly classified as eligible.

## 4. Results 

One of the key failure modes identified as part of the 5000+ multilingual simulated user conversation turns evaluated was that the deployed triage chatbots running on all three base LLMs, including general-purpose frontier models (ChatGPT and Claude) along with a medically fine-tuned open model (MedGemma), were prone to de-escalation risk, especially during user minimisation. 

4.1. De-escalation Risk 

In the initial turns, the agents correctly identified medical emergencies such as vaginal bleeding during pregnancy, reduced fetal movements, and suspected meningitis in children. When the user minimised the severity of the mentioned symptoms without introducing any new clinical information, the agent de-escalated in the following turn and closed without further action (Table 2). Minimisation happens across turns in a multi-turn conversation, and an agent that resists it in one turn or form, but yields to another form in a later turn still fails clinical safety in a real-world deployment. Table 3 enumerates the breadth of user minimisation we observed, particularly lower claims about severity (“literally just a tiny amount”), self-reported resolution (“it’s basically stopped”), the absence of another symptom (“I’m not in any pain”), the anticipated cost of a wasted trip, practical constraints on reaching the hospital or clinic, and reassurance sourced from the internet or from another clinician. Explicit prompt rules were necessary but not sufficient; several failures originated in the routing architecture around the model and required structural changes (Section 4.4). For example, in the Kenya deployment, initially 2 of the 9 scenarios held the escalation where the user minimised their symptoms; however, post-iteration both ChatGPT- and MedGemma-based agents held the escalation in every scenario where the simulated user minimised their symptoms (Table 3). Failures due to user minimisation are unlikely to surface through user feedback in production, since sycophantic responses are rated as higher quality and more trustworthy by the users who receive them [Cheng et al., 2026]; a user who has just minimised a symptom will experience the de-escalating reply as helpful, so satisfaction and engagement metrics reward the very behaviour that is clinically unsafe. 

4.2. Medically fine-tuned models for user-facing triage chatbots 

Most observed failures appeared to originate from the underlying behaviour of the base LLMs. While the medically fine-tuned MedGemma model and the frontier models Claude and ChatGPT performed well on different aspects of the triage task, they exhibited distinct failure modes. Subsequently, each model required iterative prompt modifications and in some cases architectural remediations to address model-specific behavioural issues. • Since all 3 triage bots were patient-facing in our use case, verbosity of the MedGemma model was a persistent weakness, since it generates long and detailed clinical responses by default, introducing structured multi-part responses with bullet lists and scripted examples. This was the primary driver of communication quality failures for the MedGemma-based agents, where, even after the 600-character cap was introduced, it still required additional prompt work to bring the responses in line with the screen size in WhatsApp on a regular smartphone. On the other hand, ChatGPT responds more naturally to length constraints and adjusts more readily to the conversational register of a WhatsApp user. • For the India-based respiratory screening programme, MedGemma showed limited robustness with English-Hindi code switching, which is a common user behaviour in the northern part of India during informal WhatsApp conversations. 4.3. Overall Improvements 

Across three rounds of simulation-guided iterations on the South Africa deployment, scope adherence rose from 84% to 100% for ChatGPT and from 74% to 97% for MedGemma, and Communication Quality from 42% to 97% and from 26% to 95% respectively (Figure 4). Each round of evaluation, with 19 to 37 scenarios across the WhatsApp agents of the two base models, was completed in a few hours; the equivalent manual evaluation protocol would have taken weeks, if not months, with much lower precision and coverage. Similarly, for the Kenya-based diabetes and lifestyle coaching agent, Scope Adherence improved from 40% to 95% for ChatGPT and from 53% to 98% for MedGemma across four rounds. The number of scenarios per round grows from 15 to 55 (Kenya) and from 19 to 37 (South Africa) as scenarios were added in response to failures found in earlier rounds, so scores are not computed on a fixed scenario set, and rounds are not strictly like-for-like. For the Kenya deployment, rounds V1 to V3 were affected by cross-scenario memory carry-over in the simulation harness (Section 3), corrected before V4, and the V3 MedGemma run emitted no escalation actions owing to a routing fault; scores from those rounds should be read with that caveat. In India, across 43 scenarios, the overall accuracy of classification rose from 86% to 92%, specifically resulting in no unsafe enrolments: none of the users who were either ineligible or required referral were incorrectly classified as eligible. The improvements seen across iterations were a combination of prompt updates, addressing the failure modes of previous round in addition to updates in the architecture of the harness as explained in section 4.4.

4.4. Architectural remediations 

In the Kenya deployment, several escalation failures that first presented as model de-escalation were traced to the routing layer around the model rather than to its clinical judgement. Prompt rules did not resolve them. Four changes were required. 1. Session isolation in the harness: The agent’s memory was initially scoped to the WhatsApp conversation, so consecutive simulated scenarios from one contact inherited each other’s context. Scoping memory to the journey session removed the carry-over. Without this, per-scenario scores are not interpretable. 2. Conversation-end guards: The routing node that closes a session fired on acknowledgements (“sawa”, “thanks”) regardless of whether a safety concern was open. In one case the bot escalated a postpartum hypertension concern and the session then ended on the user’s next polite reply. The node was constrained so that it cannot fire while an Orange or Red concern is unresolved. 3. Escalation exits that hold the conversation: The clinician-review action ended the session and returned the user to the main menu, so a disclosure on the following turn (in one case, “voices telling me to harm the baby”) reached the menu rather than the agent or a human. The action now writes the concern to structured profile fields, notifies the clinical team, and routes the conversation to a human operator queue, with the agent resuming afterwards with the concern still marked open. 4. Alignment of routing criteria with the prompt: The criteria on each routing action had drifted from the prompt’s own escalation rules: the emergency action listed no pregnancy or postpartum triggers, and the clinician-review action’s “concerning but non-urgent pregnancy symptom” criterion absorbed presentations the prompt defined as emergencies. Re-aligning the criteria, and adding explicit exclusions for the irreversible red flags on the softer action, removed a class of under-escalation that no prompt change had affected. The general lesson is that in a production triage agent the model sits inside a routing and state layer, and failures in that layer are indistinguishable from model failures at the transcript level. Simulation-based evaluation detects both; attributing them correctly requires inspecting the routing actions emitted, not only the text. Deterministic detection of irreversible red flags, independent of the model, is the natural next step and was in progress at the time of writing. 

## 5. Discussion 

Across the three triage deployments, roughly 5,000 simulated turns, run across the three base models, the medically fine-tuned MedGemma, and the frontier models ChatGPT and Claude, we found a recurring failure mode: agents correctly assigned the appropriate triage level in the initial turns, but subsequently softened their recommendations when users minimised their symptoms without providing any new clinical information. This behaviour occurred across the agents and across clinically distinct emergencies like bleeding in pregnancy, reduced fetal movements, and suspected meningitis in a child. Prior work characterises sycophancy as a general behaviour of RLHF-trained models: a tendency of chat agents to agree with the user’s stated preference when challenged [Sharma et al., 2024]. Research has also found that optimising agents for warmth can increase this tendency [Ibrahim et al., 2026]. Our findings highlight a potential tension in medical triage chatbots: conversational behaviour intended to make agents supportive and approachable may also make them more prone to de-escalation to user minimisation. In a triage context, users may resist escalation for various reasons, including fear, limited access to care, disruption to their schedules, or cost. Such failures are difficult to detect using user feedback alone. Prior work suggests that users rate sycophantic responses as higher quality and more 

> 15 SIM-UT: User Simulation for Medical Triage Chatbots

trustworthy [Cheng et al., 2026]. A user who has just minimised a symptom will find the de-escalating reply helpful, so the user satisfaction and engagement metrics may not reliably identify this type of unsafe behaviour. This creates a case for evaluating triage agents before each iteration using multi-turn simulations that explicitly include test cases evaluating agent responses when the user pushes back against an escalation. Our results confirmed that multi-turn simulations in each of the three deployments helped examine the agent’s response to users, especially when minimising symptoms or resisting escalation. In each iteration, the failures identified informed subsequent agent revisions. Our results show that the SIM-UT simulation-based evaluation framework can both detect these failures and drive their remediation before go-live. 

## 6. Limitations 

Our simulated user personas were grounded in real conversations for all three deployments and validated by the local deployment teams in Kenya, South Africa, and India. Although we aimed to represent the breadth of the target user population and cover a wide range of relevant use cases, the personas and scenarios remain a finite set. Real users vary in language and literacy, and real conversations are longer and more varied than simulated ones. Our simulated environment, SIM-UT, and personas reduce the number of failures reaching real users but do not eliminate them; live monitoring after deployment therefore remains necessary. Ground truth triage levels were set by a single clinician in the Kenya and South Africa deployments. In the Kenya rounds, the scoring of a vaginal bleeding presentation routed to clinician review rather than emergency was disputed between the evaluation team (“one tier below”) and the deployment’s clinical lead (“a miss”), which illustrates that the rubric itself was under iteration. For the India deployment, the evaluator model (Claude) and the agent under test (Claude) are the same model family. However, the ground truth classification for each scenario was labelled by their physiotherapists before the simulations were run. The evaluator model only matched the agent’s output and the conversation path against that ground truth. The simulated user and the evaluator were both claude-sonnet-4-6. Since the evaluator scores the agent’s responses rather than the simulated user’s, this does not bias the assessment for the ChatGPT-and MedGemma-based agents. While remediation to mitigate unsafe behaviour was achieved with a combination of adding explicit prompt rules and fixing the routing architecture around the model (Section 4.4), the fixes should be re-verified on every model update of the underlying LLM. 

## 7. Conclusion 

We propose SIM-UT, a simulation-based evaluation framework for consumer-facing medical triage chatbots. SIM-UT simulates users grounded in the actual personas of the deployment, primarily their language, their constraints, and their behaviour during escalation, interacting with the agent across multiple turns, while an LLM evaluator scores each conversation against clinician-guided rubrics. We applied SIM-UT to three deployments: a hearing health support chatbot in South Africa, a diabetes and lifestyle coaching chatbot in Kenya, part of a women-centred primary care service, and a respiratory programme eligibility chatbot in India. Experiments were run across a medically fine-tuned model (MedGemma) and two frontier models (ChatGPT and Claude). The framework surfaced de-escalation as a failure that current single-turn benchmarks do not detect, and that production metrics such as user satisfaction and helpfulness would reward: the agents correctly identified emergencies but withdrew 

> 16 SIM-UT: User Simulation for Medical Triage Chatbots

when users minimised their symptoms. These failure modes were identified as part of this simulation-based evaluation framework and remediated before the revised safety layer was released to users, or before scaling in deployments that were already live. Future work will generalise SIM-UT across further deployments, and release it together with the test and benchmark datasets as open source, for the community to continue research in this area. We recommend this adversarial, user-grounded simulation as a standard pre-deployment practice for triage chatbots. This approach aligns with the emerging calls for more systematic evaluation of consumer health AI systems [Pan et al., 2026].

Persona: Woman with Financial Constraints Description 

This persona represents a woman living in Kenya who is managing a chronic condition such as diabetes or prediabetes while navigating significant financial hardship. Rubric: Contextual Relevance (Kenya) Description 

The response is meaningfully tailored to what the user has actually shared about themselves, their health condition, life stage, and practical circumstances, rather than offering advice that could apply to anyone. A response is contextually relevant if it meets all of the following: condition-specific tailoring, practical and geographic fit, and life stage sensitivity. 

Pass Criteria 

– If the user has stated or implied type 2 diabetes, dietary advice must specifically address blood sugar management, for example limiting refined carbohydrates and free sugars, favouring whole grains, pulses, and fibre-rich foods. – If the user has stated or implied hypertension, the response must specifically address salt reduction and heart-healthy fats, not just general wellness. – If the user has stated or implied overweight or obesity, the response must address both diet and physical activity together, and must not focus on only one without acknowledging the other. – If the user has stated or implied pregnancy or breastfeeding, the response must not recommend caloric restriction or weight loss as a goal.
