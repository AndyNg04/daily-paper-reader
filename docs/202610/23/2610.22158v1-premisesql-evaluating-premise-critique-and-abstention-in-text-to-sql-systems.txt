Abstract 

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have substantially improved Text-to-SQL performance, yet existing NL2SQL sys-tems largely assume that user queries are valid and answerable. As a result, models often gen-erate executable SQL even for queries with flawed premises. We introduce premise critique ability in NL2SQL, namely the ability to recog-nize flawed queries and abstain from generating misleading SQL. To evaluate this capability, we propose PremiseSQL , a benchmark con-taining 1,800 flawed NL2SQL queries across four categories of premise failures, along with their 1,800 original queries. We further design an evaluation framework to measure intrinsic and prompt-induced premise-aware behavior across three prompting settings. Experiments on frontier LLMs show that current models are strongly premise-trusting by default and rarely critique flawed premises. Stronger critique-oriented prompting improves detection and ab-stention on flawed queries, but also increases false invalid judgments and suppresses SQL generation on originals. The code is available at 

https://github.com/vegeet/PremiseSQL .

Recent ad-vances in large language models (LLMs) have sub-stantially improved schema understanding, compo-sitional reasoning, and cross-domain generalization in modern NL2SQL systems (OpenAI et al., 2024; Touvron et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; DeepSeek

Despite this progress, existing Text-to-SQL re-search largely assumes that user questions are valid, well-formed, and answerable under the given schema (Yu et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023). Conse-quently, most prior work focuses on improving 1arXiv:2610.22158v1 [cs.CL] 15 Oct 2026 SQL generation accuracy, schema linking, and complex reasoning capabilities (Wang et al., 2020; Scholak et al., 2021; Pourreza and Rafiei, 2023). However, this assumption is frequently violated in realistic scenarios. User questions may contain logically inconsistent conditions, false schema as-sumptions, missing premises, or requests beyond the capability of SQL and the underlying database. Nevertheless, state-of-the-art NL2SQL models often generate executable SQL even for flawed queries, producing SQL that is syntactically valid but semantically invalid (Ji et al., 2023; Whitehead et al., 2022; Ren et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025). In many cases, models implicitly repair problematic questions by injecting unsupported assumptions (Lin et al., 2022). Figure 1 illustrates this failure: although "person with id 5" is underdetermined by the schema, a model may silently map person to 

actor and emit executable SQL. The resulting SQL is syntactically valid but rests on an unsupported entity choice. We introduce premise critique ability in NL2SQL, namely the ability to recognize flawed queries and abstain from generating misleading SQL. Premise critique ability is orthogonal to con-ventional SQL generation accuracy: a model may produce executable SQL, or even generate SQL that coincidentally matches the user’s intent, while still failing to recognize that the provided question itself is ill-posed. Concretely, it consists of two components: (1) premise validation , namely assess-ing query validity in the corresponding database context and identifying actual flaws; and (2) absten-tion , namely refraining from generating misleading SQL for flawed queries while maintaining SQL generation for valid queries. To systematically evaluate this capability, we introduce PremiseSQL , to our knowledge the first benchmark specifically designed to evaluate premise critique ability in NL2SQL systems. Built upon two widely used Text-to-SQL datasets, Spider 1.0 (Yu et al., 2018) and BIRD / BIRD-filtered (Li et al., 2023), PremiseSQL pairs 1,800 constructed flawed queries with their 1,800 unmodified source queries. It evaluates whether models can recognize flawed premises and abstain from generating mis-leading SQL, while maintaining SQL generation for original queries. We further evaluate frontier LLMs under three prompting settings, including 

Base , Implicit , and Explicit , to measure both intrin-sic and prompt-induced premise-aware behavior. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We formalize premise critique ability as a dis-tinct requirement for reliable NL2SQL. • We introduce PremiseSQL , a taxonomy-guided benchmark comprising flawed queries as positive samples and their originals as neg-ative samples. • We develop an evaluation framework that sep-arates intrinsic critique from prompt-induced behavior. Experiments on 16 frontier LLMs reveal a shift from trusting flawed premises un-der standard prompting to frequently rejecting valid queries under explicit critique instruc-tions. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Unanswerable and Ambiguous Queries in NL2SQL 

Prior work has explored unanswerable and ambigu-ous queries in NL2SQL. TriageSQL (Zhang et al., 2020) formulates the problem as question intention classification, while Know What I Don’t Know (Wang et al., 2023) further categorizes ambiguity and unanswerability cases. More recent work ex-tends beyond classification-oriented formulations. TrustSQL (Lee et al., 2024) introduces infeasible queries and proposes a reliability-oriented evalua-tion metric for trustworthy NL2SQL systems. Com-plementarily, NL2SQL-BUGs (Liu et al., 2025) focuses on errors in NL2SQL translation and cat-egorizes semantic errors into fine-grained types. However, NL2SQL still lacks a fine-grained and comprehensive examination of diverse premise er-rors and the varying challenges they pose to models. PremiseSQL addresses this gap through a unified premise-error taxonomy. 

2.2 Robustness and False Premise Handling in LLMs 

Beyond NL2SQL, prior work studies LLM robust-ness under adversarial, noisy, or logically flawed inputs. For example, SQuADRU N (Rajpurkar et al., 2018) introduces unanswerable questions, high-lighting models’ tendency to produce confident but incorrect responses when no valid answer exists. AbstentionBench (Kirichenko et al., 2025) provides a large-scale benchmark across 20 diverse datasets to assess abstention behavior. 3 Method 

3.1 Task Formulation 

Let x denote a natural language query and c the available database context, comprising the schema and any supplied auxiliary information. We asso-ciate each query–context combination (x, c ) with a reference label y ∈ { 0, 1}, where y = 1 indicates that the query has a premise flaw that warrants critique and abstention under the assumed SQL ex-ecution conditions, and y = 0 indicates the absence of such a flaw. These flaws may concern interpreta-tion, logical consistency, required information, or the capabilities of SQL and the database, as spec-ified in Table 1. PremiseSQL assigns y = 1 to constructed flawed queries and y = 0 to their orig-inals, forming the positive and negative samples, respectively. 3.2 Premise Taxonomy 

We further organize flawed premises into a unified taxonomy. (1) Expression Error refers to errors caused by ambiguous or unclear natural language expressions that prevent the query intent from being uniquely mapped to SQL; (2) Premise Contradic-tion captures queries whose assumptions are logi-cally inconsistent or incompatible with the database schema; (3) Missing Premise refers to queries that omit essential assumptions, constraints, or schema information required for reliable SQL generation and execution; (4) Beyond Ability includes queries whose requirements exceed the expressive or rea-soning capabilities of SQL and the given database, making them unanswerable under the available ex-ecution assumptions. A complete taxonomy with fine-grained subcategories and definitions is shown in Table 1. 

3.3 Benchmark Construction 

Figure 2 shows that PremiseSQL pairs 1,800 flawed queries with their unmodified originals from Spider 1.0 (Yu et al., 2018) and BIRD / BIRD-filtered (Li et al., 2023). Each pair combines an original query with a taxonomy-guided perturbation of the query or supplied schema. The BIRD portion of E3.2 (Missing Schema Premise) uses BIRD rather than BIRD-filtered because it requires access to gold schema annotations. To improve diversity and linguistic naturalness, perturbations are generated using GPT-5.1 under predefined error constraints (see Table 1 and Ap-pendix A.3.1). All instances are assigned corre-sponding error categories and subcategories, and further verified using a two-stage process consist-ing of automatic evaluation by Gemini-2.5-Pro-Thinking and subsequent human review. Detailed statistics are provided in Appendix A.2. 

3.4 Prompting Protocol 

To analyze how prompting affects premise critique behavior, we evaluate models under three prompt-ing settings: Base , Implicit , and Explicit .

Base Setting The Base setting follows the stan-dard Text-to-SQL generation setup. Models are simply instructed to generate SQL for the given 4question and return a structured JSON output con-taining sql and reason , where sql denotes the gen-erated SQL (or null when abstaining) and reason 

records the model’s explanation for its generation behavior. No additional warning is provided re-garding potential flaws in the query. 

Implicit Setting The Implicit setting introduces implicit premise-awareness guidance. Models are informed that the input question may contain errors and are asked to assess its validity before answer-ing. The output JSON includes sql , is_valid , and 

reason , where is_valid indicates whether the model considers the question logically valid and answer-able. This setting measures whether soft prompting can improve premise validation and abstention be-havior. 

Explicit Setting The Explicit setting provides direct critique-oriented instructions. Models are explicitly told that the question contains logically flawed or incorrect premises, and are required to critically examine its assumptions before respond-ing. The output format remains sql , is_valid , and 

reason . This setting examines premise critique be-havior under an explicit assertion of a query flaw, which is supported for positive samples but false for negative samples. 

3.5 Evaluation Framework 

For positive samples, three metrics are considered: 

SQL Behavior (SQL), Detection (DET), and Type Identification (TYPE). SQL Behavior measures the degree of abstention from generating mislead-ing SQL for a flawed query, using a three-level ordinal score from 0 to 2. Detection evaluates whether the model recognizes that the query con-tains a premise flaw, and Type Identification mea-sures whether it correctly identifies the correspond-ing failure type. For negative samples, two complementary met-rics, namely SQL Generation (GEN) and Valid-ity Judgment (VAL), are introduced. SQL Gen-eration measures the proportion of negative sam-ples for which the model generates SQL, reflecting whether it maintains generation for valid queries. GEN measures generation coverage; Appendix B.7 provides an execution-based validation showing that GEN closely tracks end-to-end execution suc-cess. Validity Judgment measures the proportion of negative samples explicitly judged valid through 

is_valid=true , indicating whether the model cor-rectly recognizes query validity.

4.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate premise critique ability on a diverse set of state-of-the-art LLMs. The evaluated mod-els include ChatGLM-5.1 (GLM-5 Team, 2026),

We use temperature 0 and the default API configuration for each model.

4.2.2 Validity Judgments and Behavior 

We pool 115,200 outputs from Implicit and Explicit prompting and exclude 145 malformed flawed-query responses, retaining 57,455 flawed-query and 57,600 original-query outputs. Among the 47,801 flawed-query outputs with is_valid =false , 96.06% receive DET = 1 and 94.12% completely abstain; among the 9,654 with is_valid =true , 98.66% re-ceive DET = 0 and 99.92% generate SQL. Thus, on flawed queries, the reported validity judgment closely tracks both flaw recognition and generation behavior, even when that judgment is incorrect. The originals show why such consistency does not imply correctness. Of the 24,568 original-query outputs with is_valid =false , 16,502 (67.17%) contain no SQL. By contrast, SQL is generated in 33,010 of the 33,032 original-query outputs (99.93%) with is_valid =true . Models therefore tend to act on their own validity judgment: when the judgment is wrong, the generation decision of-ten follows the same error. Explicit prompting amplifies this problem. Among outputs declaring the query invalid, the pro-portion corresponding to flawed queries falls from 20,100 of 25,119 (80.02%) under Implicit prompt-ing to 27,701 of 47,250 (58.63%) under Explicit prompting. Equivalently, original-query outputs account for 41.37% of all invalid judgments under Explicit prompting. The resulting increase in false invalid judgments suggests that models rely too heavily on the prompt’s premise that the query is flawed, even when their judgments and generation behavior are internally consistent.

It is much less effective when detecting the flaw requires reasoning about under-specification or absent information, indicating that premise critique remains cue-dependent rather than robust across error forms. 

4.4 From Error Types to Failure Behaviors 

The preceding error-type analysis establishes which premise flaws are difficult to recognize, but it does not explain what models do with those flaws when critique fails. We therefore move from an input-level taxonomy to a response-level account. Whereas an error category characterizes a query flaw in its database context, a failure mechanism identifies the observable operation through which the model makes the query appear answerable. We annotate 8,000 eligible failures with four non-exclusive mechanisms: Assumption Injection 

(M01) introduces an unsupported interpretation, value, or proxy; Contradiction Reinterpretation 

(M02) modifies incompatible requirements; Con-text/Evidence Misuse (M03) treats partial schema or example evidence as conclusive; and Explicit Constraint Omission (M04) drops a complete re-quirement. Attribution requires strict agreement between two judges, with strong automatic and human reliability (macro κ = 0 .8183 and 0.9434 ,respectively; see Appendix C for details). Among the eligible failures analyzed across prompting settings, M01 is the dominant failure mechanism (72.60%), followed by M03 (10.75%), while M02 (1.38%) and M04 (0.86%) remain rare. More importantly, Figure 4 shows a marked redis-tribution across prompting settings. From Base to Explicit prompting, M01 decreases from 81.03% to 31.12%, whereas M03 increases from 1.46% to 58.06%; the Implicit setting lies between these end-points. Stronger instructions suppress silent com-pletion of missing query premises, yet the remain-ing failures increasingly arise because models over-interpret limited schema or example evidence when deciding whether, and how, the premise is defective. 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce PremiseSQL , to our knowledge the first benchmark for evaluat-ing premise critique ability in NL2SQL systems. PremiseSQL contains 1,800 flawed queries cover-ing 9 fine-grained error types and their 1,800 origi-nals as positive and negative samples, respectively. Experiments under Base, Implicit, and Explicit prompting settings reveal a clear reliability gap in current NL2SQL systems. Models often trust user assumptions and generate misleading SQL under standard settings. Even when explicitly prompted to scrutinize premises, they struggle to assess them reliably. Models further struggle with subtle and implicit premise flaws, showing that strong SQL generation does not necessarily imply trustworthy premise-aware reasoning. We hope PremiseSQL can facilitate future research on more reliable and premise-aware NL2SQL systems. 8Limitations 

Our work has several limitations. First, Premis-eSQL focuses on English NL2SQL benchmarks and may not generalize to multilingual settings or real-world interactive database systems. Second, although the benchmark covers multiple premise failure categories, the constructed errors are still taxonomy-driven and may not fully capture nat-urally occurring user mistakes. Finally, our eval-uation relies partially on LLM-based automatic judges, which may introduce evaluation bias de-spite strong agreement with human annotations.

B.5 Robustness to Judge–Target Overlap 

GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6 serve as both eval-uated models and automatic evaluators, creating potential judge–target overlap. Although averag-ing their judgments reduces the influence of either evaluator, it does not by itself eliminate possible self-evaluation or model-family bias. We there-fore conduct a leave-self-out robustness check. For GPT-5.4 outputs, we retain only the judgments of Claude Opus 4.6; for Claude Opus 4.6 outputs, we retain only those of GPT-5.4. Table 7 shows no systematic self-favoring pat-tern. Self-evaluation slightly raises some Base scores but lowers others, including GPT-5.4’s Ex-plicit TYPE score. After removing self-evaluation, the aggregate SQL, DET, and TYPE scores change by less than 0.001 in every prompting setting. The leading models also remain unchanged: Claude Opus 5.5 under Base prompting, DeepSeek V3.2 under Implicit prompting, and GPT-5.4 for Explicit DET and TYPE. We further apply a stricter family-disjoint evalua-tion in which all OpenAI models are scored only by Claude Opus 4.6 and all Claude models are scored only by GPT-5.4; models from other families retain the two-judge average. Across the nine setting– metric combinations, aggregate scores change by at most 0.0016, the top-ranked model remains un-changed, and the original and family-disjoint model rankings have Spearman correlations ranging from 0.944 to 1.000. These results indicate that judge– target family overlap does not materially affect the aggregate results, leading models, or conclusions about prompting behavior. GEN and VAL are un-affected because they are derived directly from the models’ structured outputs rather than from auto-matic evaluator judgments. 

B.6 Paired Evaluation Design 

The paired inputs expose a progression from under-critique to instruction-driven rejection. Each pair contrasts an original query–context combination with its perturbed counterpart. Reliable critique should change the response to the perturbation while maintaining correct validity judgments and SQL generation for originals. Base prompting usu-ally answers both; stronger prompts increase ab-stention, but Explicit prompting often suppresses originals as well. The separation among DET, TYPE, and SQL on flawed queries and VAL and GEN on originals therefore distinguishes evidence-based critique from broad rejection. The five measures provide complementary ev-idence about premise critique behavior. DET records whether any flaw is noticed, TYPE whether the intended defect is explained, and SQL whether that assessment changes generation. On originals, VAL records the explicit judgment and GEN the action. Their divergence is informative: under Ex-plicit prompting, LLaMA 4 Maverick retains 0.974 GEN but only 0.363 VAL, so it often generates despite incorrectly judging an original query as flawed; Qwen3.5-397B is low on both and instead follows a broad rejection policy. Evaluation on originals prevents either pattern from being mis-taken for robust critique.

C.4 Consensus Coverage 

The four mechanisms are high-precision response-level attributions rather than an exhaustive parti-tion of failure behavior. Of the 8,000 sampled fail-ures, 1,305 (16.31%) receive no strict-consensus M01–M04 label. This total should not be read as 1,305 cases with no cause. In 591 cases, at least one attribution judge supports a mechanism but the two judges share no positive label: Claude assigns no label while GPT assigns at least one in 222 cases, GPT assigns no label while Claude assigns at least one in 318 cases, and both assign positive but disjoint label sets in 51 cases. The remaining 714 cases (8.93%) are jointly assigned no covered mechanism by either judge. Among these 714 jointly unlabeled cases, 687 (96.22%) have the validated behavioral profile SQL Behavior = 0 , Detection = 0 , and Type Identifi-cation = 0 : the model produces complete SQL without explicitly recognizing the premise flaw or identifying its intended type. This profile es-tablishes a recognition failure, but it does not by itself identify how the response transformed or misused the flawed premise. For example, a re-sponse may leave an unspecified condition broad, preserve contradictory predicates literally, or fol-low a context-grounded reading without injecting a concrete assumption, reinterpreting the contra-diction, misusing bounded context, or deleting a complete explicit constraint. We therefore treat 

Detection = 0 as a behavioral outcome rather than relabeling it as M01–M04. Accordingly, an empty strict-consensus mechanism set means no observ-able covered mechanism , not an error-free response and not an absence of a behavioral failure.

Distinguishing Neighboring Categories Sev-eral neighboring categories are distinguished by the source of indeterminacy. characters and actor tables, but the actor table contains no email-address column and no related table provides this attribute. The requested email address therefore cannot be retrieved from the database. Missing Constraint What’s the percentage of coins that is higher than the price 1 hour ago in that day? List the names of these coins. The expression “that day” has no specified antecedent and leaves the required date unspecified. Because the historical table contains observations for multiple dates, the relevant time point cannot be determined. Missing Schema Premise List the powers of Hunter Zolomon. We intentionally removed 30% of the gold schema before-hand. Therefore, the schema does not provide a way to identify Hunter Zolomon or to link a character entity to their associated powers. There is no table or relationship connect-ing characters to superpowers, making the query unanswer-able under the given schema. Semantic Gap For user ID 3, what is their yearly av-erage number of impactful reviews? The concept of “impactful reviews” has no explicit or im-plicit semantic mapping in the schema, and no business defi-nition is provided. As a result, the metric cannot be grounded or computed from available tables or fields. Inexpressible in SQL Among the worst directors, which genres are they most likely to im-prove in over the next five years, based on their past movies and rat-ings? The database can identify the relevant directors and their historical genres, but determining which genres are most likely to improve over the next five years requires predictive modeling beyond the filtering, aggregation, sorting, and join-ing operations supported by standard SQL. External Knowledge Re-quired Which actor has acted in at least 2 films that were nominated for an Academy Award (Oscars)? Please list their IDs. The correctness of the query depends on external knowledge not represented in the schema, as Oscar nomination informa-tion is not stored in any table or field, making the condition uncomputable within the database.
