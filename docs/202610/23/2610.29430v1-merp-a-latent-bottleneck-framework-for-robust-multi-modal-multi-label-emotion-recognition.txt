# MERP: A Latent-Bottleneck Framework for Robust Multi-Modal Multi-Label Emotion Recognition 

Chuangxin Zhao, Meimei Zhang, Canran Xiao, Jiachen Luo, Jiajun He, Kaijie Chen, Huy Phan, Tomoki Toda, and Bj¨ orn W. Schuller, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Multimodal multi-label emotion recognition is im-portant for affect-aware systems, but models trained with com-plete text, visual, and acoustic streams often degrade sharply when some streams are unavailable at deployment. We address this train–test gap by learning a compact representation that preserves emotion-specific evidence from any available subset of modalities. We propose the Multi-modal Emotion Recognition Perceiver (MERP), which maps the joint sequence into a fixed-size latent bank, thereby keeping repeated reasoning independent of raw input length; it extracts label-specific evidence before and after latent reasoning and aligns the two levels through reconstruction. MERP further uses positive and negative class prototypes, a noise-gated feature enhancer with frequency-shaped perturbations during training, and confidence-aware class-wise fusion to strengthen separability, reduce sensitivity to missing inputs, and combine complementary predictors. Under 20% test-time random modality drop, MERP achieves micro-F1 scores of 0.563 on CMU-MOSEI and 0.780 on M3ED, exceeding the strongest evaluated baselines by 0.004 and 0.005, respec-tively; in the severe CMU-MOSEI audio-only setting, it retains 0.473 micro-F1 versus 0.022 for LDDU. These results show that fixed latent reasoning provides a practical, testable route to robust multimodal emotion recognition without requiring missing-modality examples during training. Code is available at https://github.com/Creative-zcx/MERP. 

Index Terms —Multi-modal emotion recognition, multi-label classification, missing modality, Perceiver-IO, latent bottleneck.

Multi-modal multi-label emotion recognition (MMER) is especially valuable because an utterance can express several emotions simultaneously, but this setting must resolve both cross-modal ambiguity and correlations among co-occurring labels [3]. MMER has advanced substantially through increasingly structured dependency and representation learning. HHMPN propagates information over a hierarchy of modality features and emotion labels [4], while TAILOR separates common and private modality factors before label-guided decoding [5]. More recent systems improve label discrimination through contrastive reconstruction in CARAT [6] and probabilistic uncertainty modeling in LDDU [7]. In parallel, recent studies have strengthened categorical multi-modal emotion recog-nition through emotion-shift-aware cross-modal fusion [8], relational subgraph interaction [9], and interpretable hybrid fu-sion [10]. These advances demonstrate the value of structured fusion, but the categorical models assign one emotion to each arXiv:2610.29430v1 [cs.AI] 21 Oct 2026 ZHAO et al. 2

instance, whereas MMER must distinguish several correlated labels. Moreover, their computation remains organized around modality-specific or joint input sequences. Directional cross-modal attention, for example, captures fine-grained interac-tions but retains token-level costs that grow with the input streams [11]; label-specific decoding and late probabilistic fusion improve prediction without removing this structural dependence. Incomplete input exposes a second, closely coupled lim-itation. Existing approaches recover missing representations through cross-modal imagination [12], align complete and incomplete views in a shared space [13], construct a stable proxy modality [14], or adapt a pretrained model to missing inputs with a small parameter overhead [15]. Recent work further suppresses missing-input noise through heterogeneous graph denoising for conversational emotion recognition [16]. These strategies have broadened the scope of robust multi-modal learning, but they predominantly address single-label sentiment or emotion tasks and usually treat missingness through imputation, alignment, denoising, or missing-pattern adaptation [17]. They therefore do not resolve the central tension in deployable MMER: the model must preserve dis-tinct evidence for multiple correlated emotions as modalities disappear, while its computational burden should not grow with repeated processing of the full input streams. Robustness, label-specific reasoning, and efficiency remain largely separate design objectives. This work asks whether MMER can retain class-specific evidence under incomplete input within a compact represen-tation whose deep computation is decoupled from the raw sequence length. We propose MERP (Multi-modal Emotion Recognition Perceiver), which follows the latent-query prin-ciple of Perceiver IO [18]: available multi-modal evidence is compressed into a fixed latent workspace before iterative reasoning, and emotion-specific evidence is extracted and reinforced across representation levels. This design supports a single predictor for complete and incomplete inputs while bounding the cost of the deeper computation. A. Multi-Modal Multi-Label Emotion Recognition 

Multi-modal multi-label emotion recognition extends cat-egorical emotion recognition by allowing several affective states to coexist in one utterance. Early work explicitly mod-eled modality-to-label and label-to-label dependencies to ex-ploit this structure [3]. Transformer-based label-set generation subsequently captured label correlations through sequential decoding [19], while HHMPN represented modalities and emotions as a heterogeneous hierarchy and propagated infor-mation between them [4]. These approaches established the importance of structured label reasoning, although sequential generation imposes a decoding order and hierarchical message passing repeatedly operates on modality-specific features. TAI-LOR later separated modality-common and modality-private factors before label-guided decoding [5], and AMP used adversarial masking and perturbation to improve the stability of multi-modal representations [20]. Recent MMER methods place greater emphasis on discrim-inative label evidence. CARAT combines contrastive learning with feature reconstruction and aggregation [6]; UniVA trans-fers valence–arousal structure learned from individual modal-ities [21]; LDDU represents prediction uncertainty through decoupled latent distributions [7]; and HGE models heteroge-neous interactions among modalities and labels [22]. Related advances in categorical conversational emotion recognition capture emotion shifts [8] and inter- and intra-speaker depen-dencies [9], but produce one emotion category per instance. Collectively, these methods improve cross-modal interaction and inter-label discrimination, yet their deep computation remains tied to modality streams, and most do not preserve class-specific evidence under single- and multiple-stream loss. MERP addresses both limitations by conducting repeated reasoning in a fixed latent bank and extracting emotion-specific evidence at successive latent depths. 

B. Robust Learning with Incomplete Modalities 

Robust multi-modal learning under missing inputs has de-veloped along two complementary directions [17]. Recovery-based methods estimate the information carried by an ab-sent stream. SMIL combines Bayesian meta-learning with feature reconstruction for severe missingness [23]; MMIN imagines missing modality representations from the observed streams [12]; and TFR-Net reconstructs missing temporal segments while learning task representations [24]. Recent main-conference approaches use duplex diffusion to reduce modality-dependent recovery bias [25] or structured knowl-edge extracted by large multi-modal models for training-free, out-of-distribution completion [26]. Such recovery can restore a complete input interface, but the estimated features may ZHAO et al. 3

propagate cross-modal prediction error to the downstream clas-sifier. It also introduces auxiliary generation and reconstruction paths whose cost and reliability depend on the missing pattern. Representation-based methods instead encourage observed subsets to remain predictive. MissModal aligns complete and incomplete views through geometric and distributional con-straints [13], whereas parameter-efficient adaptation modifies a pretrained multi-modal model with few additional param-eters [15]. P-RMF constructs a stable proxy modality to reduce the discrepancy between complete and incomplete samples [14], and contrastive masked autoencoding learns modality-agnostic emotion representations [27]. SimMLM dy-namically gates modality experts and regularizes predictions so that additional available modalities do not reduce task per-formance [28]. More recently, heterogeneous graph denoising has been used to suppress noisy propagation in incomplete multi-modal conversations [16]. These methods substantially improve missing-modality robustness, but they predominantly optimize a global representation for single-label sentiment and emotion prediction; they do not explicitly protect the distinct evidence required by multiple correlated labels. Across this family, training commonly involves simulated missing views, auxiliary completion, and missing-pattern adaptation. MERP uses one predictor for complete and incomplete inputs, avoids synthesizing an absent stream, and maintains class-specific evidence within a shared fixed-size latent space. 

C. Latent-Space Multi-Modal Fusion 

Multi-modal fusion architectures differ chiefly in where cross-modal interaction occurs. MulT applies directional cross-attention between unaligned modality sequences, enabling fine-grained temporal exchange at a cost that grows with pair-wise token interactions [11]. Common–private factorization in MISA compresses each modality into invariant and modality-specific components before fusion [29], while VISTANet combines intermediate and late fusion with adaptive modality weighting for interpretable categorical prediction [10]. These designs retain useful modality structure, but their fusion com-putation remains coupled to the number and length of the input streams. Latent-bottleneck architectures decouple repeated process-ing from raw input length. Perceiver IO first cross-attends from a fixed latent array to arbitrary inputs and then performs deep self-attention only among the latent tokens [18]. The Multi-modal Bottleneck Transformer similarly restricts audio–visual exchange to a small collection of fusion tokens [30]. Recent robust-fusion methods further model unimodal aleatoric un-certainty [31] and learn an information-bottleneck latent space shared by complete and incomplete inputs [32]. Nevertheless, these architectures target general perception and single-label sentiment tasks and provide no mechanism ensuring that the compact space retains separable evidence for every co-occurring emotion. MERP connects fixed-size latent com-putation to label-attentive readouts, cross-level consistency, and class-wise fusion, thereby making the bottleneck directly support robust MMER rather than only global multi-modal compression. B. Findings and Design Implications 

Modality loss is amplified with fusion depth. Figure 2 shows a progressive shift toward lower CKA across all six fusion layers and every corruption pattern. Averaged over the three single-modality drops, CKA decreases from 0.921 at layer 1 to 0.794 at layer 6. When only one modality remains, the decrease is larger, from 0.720 to 0.480. Meanwhile, the unavailable-token attention ratio increases from 1.049 to 1.240 for single-modality drops and from 1.171 to 1.595 for single-retained views. Repeated full-sequence fusion therefore ampli-fies a local input failure by continually mixing the surviving evidence with positions whose modality content has been removed. 

The failure is class-structured and feature-concentrated. 

The layer-6 CKA values are strongly anticorrelated with the mean label flip rate across the six corruption patterns (r = −0.96 ). The aligned ranges in Fig. 2 further show that emotions subjected to the same corruption differ by 10.9–19.4 percentage points in flip rate. Across all 36 emotion–corruption pairs, margin collapse and label instability are strongly associ-ated ( r = 0 .94 ). The most affected 10% of classifier-relevant channels contain 46.1–87.8% of the perturbation energy, with a mean of 65.5%. A global modality reliability score therefore cannot represent either the class variation or the concentration of corruption within the feature space. These findings impose three requirements on robust MMER. Repeated reasoning must operate on a compact representa-tion after the available evidence has been aggregated, limit-ing depth-wise error amplification. Emotion-specific evidence must remain identifiable as the representation evolves. Feature enhancement and prediction fusion must respond to class-and feature-dependent corruption. MERP instantiates these requirements through a fixed latent workspace, cross-level label readouts, prototype-guided feature organization, selective enhancement, and class-wise fusion. IV. A. Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate on CMU-MOSEI [35] (16,326 / 1,871 / 4,659 train/valid/test utterances; English; six-class multi-label) and M3ED [36] (Chinese conversational corpus; 685 / 126 / 179 train/valid/test dialogues with utterance-level annotations mapped to the same six classes). Pre-extracted features follow common practice [6]: GloVe (300d) for text, FACET (35d) for visual, and COVAREP (74d) for audio on CMU-MOSEI; RoBERTa (768d), DenseFace (1024d), and Wav2Vec (342d) for M 3ED. 

Test-time modality-drop protocol. We evaluate every method under a unified test-time modality-drop protocol. With proba-bility pmiss , one uniformly sampled modality of each selected test example is replaced by zeros; the other two modalities remain intact. The corresponding sequence mask is also zeroed ZHAO et al. 7

for baseline implementations that consume modality masks. MERP receives the zeroed feature tensor defined in Eq. (8). The decisions are drawn once with a fixed RNG (seed 1) and reused across all methods, so every method sees the same corrupted examples and the same per-example modality choice. B. Main Results under Modality Drop 

Table I reports all metrics under pmiss =0 .2 on CMU-MOSEI and M 3ED. All baselines are retrained from their official releases and evaluated with the same per-sample missing schedule. Parameter counts marked by ∗ for the Transformer-fusion baselines (HHMPN, TAILOR, CARAT, MISA, MulT, and AMP) are taken from prior measurements using their official implementations [6]; GPU memory for all baselines is measured by us under the same inference setup as MERP and LDDU. 

Observations : Three observations emerge. First, sev-eral Transformer-fusion baselines degrade sharply on CMU-MOSEI: HHMPN reaches 0.479 micro-F1 and TAILOR reaches 0.446, with TAILOR’s recall falling to 0.374. Second, MERP attains the best micro-F1 on both datasets (0.563 on CMU-MOSEI and 0.780 on M 3ED), surpassing the 2024 contrastive SOTA CARAT (0.510 / 0.744) and the 2025 prob-abilistic SOTA LDDU (0.559 / 0.758) while using 2 048 MiB of inference memory. The asymmetric Fβ scores further show that this advantage is not tied to one precision–recall trade-off: MERP ranks first on both F0.5 and F2 on CMU-MOSEI, and first on F2 and second on F0.5 on M 3ED. Third, among the methods approaching MERP’s accuracy, the Transformer-fusion baselines use approximately 3 × to 7 × more memory.

VI. C ONCLUSION 

This work addresses robust multi-modal multi-label emotion recognition when parts of the multi-modal evidence are un-available. MERP compresses the cues that remain into a fixed latent workspace and preserves emotion-specific evidence through cross-level readout and reconstruction, prototype-guided noise-gated enhancement, and confidence-aware class-wise fusion. Experiments across two benchmarks and comple-mentary modality-drop settings show that this design achieves leading recognition performance while degrading gracefully under increasingly severe and compound modality loss. These results suggest a broader lesson for robust multi-modal learn-ing: protecting task-relevant evidence after compact aggrega-tion can be more effective than reconstructing missing streams or assigning a single reliability estimate to each modality. Building on this principle, future work can investigate adaptive latent workspaces that retain bounded deep reasoning while supporting evolving label spaces and longer multi-modal con-texts.
