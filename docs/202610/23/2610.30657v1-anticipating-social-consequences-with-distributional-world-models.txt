Abstract 

Social world models predict how interactions develop after an action, helping agents nego-tiate, cooperate and mediate. In these situa-tions, one action can lead to several plausible futures, hence predicting only one future can overlook consequential alternatives, while ex-ploring them through repeated simulations in-creases inference cost. We propose DSWM, a Distributional Social World Model that predicts alternative responses, their probabilities and im-mediate and final outcome values in one output per candidate action. We train a 4B model on repeated simulations using supervised fine-tuning and GRPO. At inference time, the actor uses these possible consequences to choose an action. Across three seen and two unseen en-vironments, with interaction backbones absent from training collection, DSWM achieves the best average performance on 13 of 15 metrics. Ablations show that retaining multiple futures and their predicted probabilities improves out-comes in all five environments. 

1 Introduction 

A social world model describes how a social in-teraction may change when an agent takes an ac-tion. For example, a customer may accept an offer, counter it or leave, with different consequences for the agent. The beliefs and goals of the other parties help determine which response occurs. Zhou et al. (2025) describe social world models through the evolving environment, agent observations and ac-tions, while mental world modeling couples physi-cal changes with changes in beliefs and other men-tal states (Fei and Zhao, 2026). By anticipating these changes before an action is taken, social world models can support negotiation, coopera-tion and mediation (Zhou et al., 2024; Mou et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). Herein, outcome measures such as revenue, goal completion and cost make

the consequences of different actions comparable (Filzmoser and Gettinger, 2019; Hale et al., 2025). Unlike certain physical environments that can be modeled with deterministic transitions to a single next state (Assran et al., 2025), social environments can produce several plausible future states from the same visible situation and action. Participants may have different private goals and choose differ-ent responses. A distribution over future states is therefore a natural representation: an agent should consider both what may happen and how likely it is. Figure 1 illustrates how this changes a deci-sion. Predicting acceptance for either offer leads the seller to choose $90, but the buyer leaves. When the prediction also represents rejection and its prob-ability, the seller chooses $80 for its higher accep-tance probability and expected revenue, and com-pletes the sale. However, these alternatives are not always explicit in prior approaches. S3AP Foresee-and-Act uses one predicted next state at each simu-arXiv:2610.30657v1 [cs.AI] 22 Oct 2026 lation step (Zhou et al., 2025), leaving alternative responses to that action implicit. GDP-Zero consid-ers multiple future states through repeated simula-tions during search (Yu et al., 2023), which requires additional inference-time computation. These ap-proaches motivate a compact output that presents alternative responses, their probabilities and their consequences together to guide decisions. To address this limitation, we propose DSWM, a Distributional Social World Model . Given the visible interaction and a candidate action from an agent, DSWM predicts multiple future states, each represented as a response cluster with a probability, a representative reply, and immediate and final out-come values. We construct the training data from repeated simulations, resulting in a 4B world model trained with supervised fine-tuning and GRPO. At inference time, DSWM provides these alternatives in one prediction per candidate action, allowing the actor to consider different responses and their likelihoods before choosing an action. To demon-strate the effects of applying DSWM, we perform extensive evaluation on both seen and unseen envi-ronments, showing that DSWM outperforms other world modeling methods in 13 of 15 metrics. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • An explicit distributional representation that con-nects alternative social responses with their like-lihoods and consequences for action evaluation. • A training procedure that constructs targets from repeated interactions and learns them through supervised fine-tuning and GRPO. • An evaluation across three seen and two un-seen environments with unseen agent backbones. DSWM achieves the best mean on 13 of 15 met-rics; ablations examine the importance of prob-ability assignments, response information and diverse outcome supervision. 

2 Methodology 

We first define the social world model and explain how its future-state representation supports action evaluation (§2.1). We then discuss how we train DSWM (§2.2) and apply it at inference time (§2.3), as illustrated in Figure 2. 

2.1 Social World Models 

A social world model predicts how an interaction develops after an action, taking the responses of other agents into account. DSWM is an action-conditioned model of this kind: for one candidate action, it summarizes the possible responses of the other parties and their consequences. 

Outcome measures. We use outcome measures 

to describe distinct consequences, such as revenue, goal completion and privacy violations, following negotiation and dispute-resolution research (Filz-moser and Gettinger, 2019; Hale et al., 2025). Their observed or predicted numbers are outcome val-ues . In reinforcement learning, a reward defines the optimization objective, while a Q-value is the expected cumulative reward after an action under a continuation policy (Sutton and Barto, 2018). An outcome measure can supply a reward, but we keep measures separate so that the actor can weigh ob-jectives and enforce constraints at inference. For example, revenue and privacy costs remain visi-ble before they are combined into utility. Final expected outcome values coincide with Q-values when those outcomes define the return; individual cluster values additionally condition on a response. 

Input. We call the agent whose actions are evalu-ated the focal agent . Its actor proposes and selects actions, while the world model (WM) evaluates their possible consequences. At a decision point, the WM receives visible context x, one candidate action a and a query q. The context contains the setting, the focal agent profile and objectives, and the interaction history visible to that agent. Private goals of other parties appear only if disclosed. The query specifies the requested outcome measures and horizons (Appendix A). 

Output future states. For each candidate action, DSWM outputs a distribution over up to K re-sponse clusters. A response segment contains the messages and actions of the other parties until the focal agent next acts or the interaction ends. Each cluster represents a set of similar response seg-ments; Section 2.2 explains how we construct them. The output describes cluster k by a short response text rk, its probability pk and, for each requested measure j and horizon h, the mean outcome value 

μk,j,h given a response in that cluster. The im-mediate horizon evaluates the interaction after the response segment. The final horizon evaluates it at termination under ordinary continuation, in which the actor and the other parties keep interacting un-der the unguided policy stated in the query. Thus, the responses and their outcome values summarize possible future states, with probabilities assigned to the response clusters. Relation to prior definitions. Like earlier social and mental world models (Zhou et al., 2025; Fei and Zhao, 2026), DSWM predicts changes follow-ing an action. It focuses on a distributional view of response alternatives and their consequences for decisions, rather than reconstructing the complete hidden environment or private mental states. Its output is a distribution over response clusters with conditional mean outcome values, not a full joint distribution of those values.

This is the law of total expectation: the overall expected value is the probability-weighted average of the conditional values. In the lower panel of Figure 1, the $90 offer has expected rev-enue 0.6 × 90 + 0 .4 × 0 = 54 , while the $80 offer yields 0.9×80+0 .1×0 = 72 . The seller therefore chooses $80: accounting for rejection reverses the preference based on acceptance alone. The representation therefore connects to expected-utility theory, which evaluates an action by weighting the utility of possible outcomes by their probabilities (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944). For one measure or a fixed weighted sum, utility is linear in the outcome values, so accurate expected values suffice to compare actions. When an objective combines measures nonlinearly, utility computed from cluster means only approximates expected utility, because the representation omits within-cluster variation and covariance (§2.3). Pre-dicted values are also approximate. The response descriptions show which responses produce each average, and our analyses test whether this informa-tion improves decisions beyond the numerical val-ues. To estimate these training targets, we repeat-edly take each action from the same saved state. 

2.2 Training DSWM Collecting future states. We save the complete environment state at a focal decision point, execute the same candidate action from that state N =50 

times, and sample the responses of the other par-ties. In batches selected for final-outcome collec-tion, every sampled interaction continues to ter-mination under the ordinary continuation policy; other batches supply immediate labels only (Ap-pendix I.1). Repeating an action and averaging the resulting outcomes follows Monte Carlo action evaluation (Sutton and Barto, 2018, Ch. 5). Col-lection can use the saved full state, while the WM learns to predict from the visible context alone. We then measure the consequences of each sam-pled response and, when available, its completed interaction. Deterministic environments compute measures such as negotiation utility and revenue directly. Otherwise, an LLM judge applies bench-mark rubrics or additional measures such as partner trust and secret leakage (Appendix B).

All measures share one response parti-tion and are averaged separately within each cluster, so binary measures can have fractional conditional means. We request a measure and horizon only when labels exist for all N samples, which for fi-nal values means batches continued to termination. Consequently, Eq. 1 recovers the sample mean be-fore rounding: clustering changes the representa-tion while preserving expected outcome values. 

Supervised fine-tuning. We serialize these tar-gets as JSON containing response texts, probabil-ities and conditional mean outcome values, and train the WM to generate them with SFT. Inputs contain the visible context, candidate action and query, excluding reference continuations, observed labels and hidden answer keys. Query views vary the requested measures, horizons and cluster count (Appendix B). This teaches the WM to express al-ternative future states in a common output format. 

Reinforcement learning. SFT can penalize a pre-diction that describes the same samples with dif-ferent wording or grouping. We therefore continue training with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), evaluating generated distributions against the raw reference continuations. Each reference response is matched to its most similar predicted response when their representative actions agree and semantic similarity reaches a threshold; otherwise, it counts as missed. The reward is 

R = g (0 .35 Rdist + 0 .20 Rcov + 0 .10 Rprec 

+ 0 .30 Rscore + 0 .05 Rdiv ). (3) The gate g is zero for an output that cannot be parsed, omits a requested measure or has the wrong cluster count. Rdist is one minus the total-variation distance between predicted probabilities and matched reference frequencies. Rcov mea-sures reference mass matched to explicit responses, while Rprec measures predicted mass receiving at least one reference. Rscore is one minus the mean squared normalized error between each reference outcome and the conditional mean of its assigned cluster. Finally, Rdiv penalizes near-duplicate re-sponses. SFT and GRPO draw on the same train-ing pool (Appendix I.3). Together, these objectives train the probabilities, response coverage and out-come values used at inference time. 

2.3 Applying DSWM at Inference Time 

At each decision point, the actor proposes up to four actions, and invalid or duplicate proposals are removed. The trained WM then predicts re-sponse clusters for each candidate once, request-ing four clusters, or one for an action that ends the interaction. From these predictions, we com-pute final expected outcome values using Eq. 1 and a combined utility for each candidate. For SOTOPIA-ToM, whose objective combines mea-sures nonlinearly, we compute utility from the con-ditional means within each cluster and then average it by probability; the other environments use linear combinations. We then filter candidates in two steps. First, we remove candidates whose predicted constraint costs exceed the lowest predicted cost by more than a small margin. Then, we remove a candidate if another is at least as good on every active objective and constraint and better on at least one (Figure 7, appendix). The actor receives the candidate actions with numerical and response guidance. Numerical guid-ance consists of immediate and final expected outcome values, plus combined utilities for eli-gible candidates. Response guidance consists of the two most probable response descriptions with their probabilities and the residual probability mass. Given this guidance and the eligible action IDs, the actor chooses an eligible action, which is executed unchanged. If all predictions are invalid, the actor selects without model guidance. Selection repeats at later turns, while the predicted final values as-sume ordinary continuation (Appendix E). Thus, the WM supplies possible future states and their consequences, while the actor remains responsible for the decision. 3 Experiments 

In the below sections, we examine how distribu-tional future states support social decisions. We evaluate action selection on held-out scenario fam-ilies in three training environments and two un-seen environments. We then examine the contri-butions of alternative responses, their probabilities and their consequences, and whether learning these together reduces the cost of evaluating multiple futures. 

3.1 Benchmarks 

Five benchmark adaptations cover negotiation, so-cial interaction, mediation and information sharing: 

Magentic-NL is a two-party negotiation adapted from the restaurant and home-contractor scenarios in Magentic Marketplace (Microsoft, 2025). Cus-tomers seek useful purchases at favorable prices, while businesses seek revenue. 

SOTOPIA tests open-ended social role-play be-tween two characters with individual goals (Zhou et al., 2024). Evaluation covers goal achievement and social consequences, including relationships and adherence to social rules. 

AgentSense tests goal completion and informa-tion reasoning in scenes derived from film and tele-vision scripts, spanning personal, workplace and public interactions (Mou et al., 2025). 

ProMediate tests multi-party mediation across healthcare, environmental policy and business cases (Liu et al., 2026). Here, the focal agent helps parties with competing preferences reach consen-sus. 

SOTOPIA-ToM tests coordination and privacy-aware information sharing among three to five agents with different private knowledge and pub-lic or private communication channels (YS et al., 2026). Together with ProMediate, it is unseen to the trained WM. 

3.2 Training Data 

From the three training environments, we ob-tain 12,247 training query rows: 6,041 from Magentic-NL, 4,810 from SOTOPIA and 1,396 from AgentSense. Different query rows can reuse the same context, candidate action and sampled continuations. We follow official splits where avail-able and otherwise hash scenario families into approximately 70/10/20 train/validation/test par-titions. We also apply filtering to remove non-finite or out-of-range labels. The validation and test files contain 2,137 and 2,731 rows. Appendix B provides the exact pool composition and held-out outcome-measure splits. 

3.3 Experimental Setup Models and evaluation. For these experiments, the WM is Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Qwen Team, 2025). Rollout agents used to create training data use Ornith-1.5-9B (Ornith Team, 2026), Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) and Gemma 4 12B (Gemma Team, 2026), whereas eval-uation pools the disjoint set of Ministral 3 14B, MiniMax H3 and Claude Sonnet 5. Evaluation outcome values come from the environment for de-terministic metrics or from GPT-5.6-Sol for metrics evaluated by an LLM judge (Appendix D).

Importance of probability assignments. Be-yond representing alternatives, the model must in-dicate how much each future should influence a decision. Uniform probabilities lower all five pri-mary metrics, although the SOTOPIA change is small (8.21 to 8.16). We further perturb probabil-ities while holding response texts and conditional outcome values fixed. At pooled average JS diver-gence 0.28, performance falls to 77–90% of the original values (Figure 4a). Reweighting changes how possible consequences contribute to expected utility and eligibility. These results show that the learned probability assignments carry useful de-cision information; calibration against observed frequencies remains a separate question. 

Importance of explaining how outcomes arise. 

Response descriptions further help the actor inter-pret the predicted consequences. With numerical guidance and filters fixed, full guidance outper-forms expected values only in four environments, with paired intervals above zero (Table 15). Adding response text benefits all four, while Magentic-NL remains unchanged under payoff-only filtering. Thus, even when expected values are available, the actor can benefit from descriptions of the responses behind them. Figure 3 illustrates such a choice. Re-sponse clusters also support nonlinear objectives: computing utility within clusters before averaging raises InfoMgmt from 0.2371 to 0.2462 in the sep-arate aggregation comparison (Table 12). This re-mains an approximation and operates within the eligible set (Appendix J.1). Together, these findings support retaining response information alongside numerical summaries for action selection. 

Learning diverse social consequences. Learn-ing varied consequences improves generalization to unseen environments. As shown in Figure 4b, training DSWM on 25 rather than 5 measures raises consensus change from 0.0506 to 0.0628 and In-foMgmt from 0.1851 to 0.2508, with diminishing gains across intermediate subsets. Shared rollouts and matched supervision volume support attribut-ing these gains to broader outcome supervision. Thus, learning different objectives and costs helps the same model support decisions in unseen envi-ronments and settings. 

Making multiple-future evaluation practical. 

Compact distributions reduce the cost of evaluat-ing alternative futures. With matched SFT budgets, DSWM (SFT only) exceeds sampled futures on all 15 metrics, though the InfoMgmt gain is only 0.0013 and target aggregation differs. GRPO im-proves 13 metrics and reduces prediction error, but worsens CPV and leaves information accuracy un-changed (Table 4; Appendix J.1). On SOTOPIA, prediction uses 16.2 × fewer tokens and 4.8 × lower latency than 30 sampled futures, including retries; direct expectation remains cheapest (Table 14). Thus, repeated training simulations support one distributional prediction per action at inference. 

4 Related Work 

Social world models and dialogue planning. 

Benchmarks cover goal-driven interaction (Zhou et al., 2024; Mou et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; YS et al., 2026) and cultural pressure (Pham et al., 2026b). Zhou et al. (2025) define probabilistic tran-sitions but use one next-state prediction in Foresee-and-Act. Fei and Zhao (2026) factor physical and mental updates without training, while Yu et al. (2026) learn collective-belief transitions. Dialogue planners simulate negotiations (Lewis et al., 2017) or search with prompted user simulators (Yu et al., 2023). DSWM instead learns response probabili-ties and conditional outcomes from rollouts for a separate actor. 

Decision making with predicted outcomes. 

Expected-utility theory weights utilities by proba-bility (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944), and Monte Carlo evaluation averages rollout returns (Sutton and Barto, 2018). DSWM estimates con-ditional mean outcomes within response clusters, whereas probabilistic ensembles model dynamics uncertainty (Chua et al., 2018) and distributional reinforcement learning models full return distribu-tions (Bellemare et al., 2017). Distributional align-ment predicts population answers to fixed survey options (Pham et al., 2026a); our alternatives are action-conditioned and expressed as generated text. Video-world-model evaluation also distinguishes outcome plausibility from probability accuracy (Pu et al., 2026), a distinction relevant here. 

5 Conclusions 

We presented DSWM, a distributional social world model that predicts future states as response alter-natives, probabilities and outcome values to guide a separate actor. Across five environments and three unseen backbones, it achieved the best per-formance on 13 of 15 metrics, outperforming other world modeling baselines. Ablations support retain-ing multiple futures and their probability weights, while richer actor-facing information improves out-comes in four environments. Limitations 

Simulated partners. All agents are language models. Training rollouts use Ornith-1.5-9B, Qwen3.5-9B and Gemma 4 12B, while evalu-ation uses Ministral 3 14B, MiniMax H3 and Claude Sonnet 5. DSWM models the responses of these language-model policies. Transfer to peo-ple remains untested, and per-backbone analysis is needed to establish which models benefit. 

Coarse targets. Fifty continuations per action give reference probabilities in increments of 0.02, so rare responses can be missed. Per-cluster means also omit within-cluster variance, so nonlinear util-ities remain approximate. In addition, judge noise enters the labels. Final-horizon targets come from the ordinary actor policy rather than from repeated model-guided selection. 

Ethical Statements 

Dual use. A model that predicts how others will respond to an utterance could help an agent pres-sure or manipulate people in negotiation or per-suasion. This work studies interactions among language-model agents in research environments. Any use with people should therefore involve dis-closure that an AI agent is participating, consent, and objectives that account for the interests of the counterpart. Furthermore, the eligibility filters in DSWM act on predicted expected values and are not safety mechanisms. 

Data and participants. This implementation uses scenarios and character profiles from exist-ing research benchmarks and from Magentic Mar-ketplace data. Some scenarios are derived from existing scripts and stories. Interaction partners are simulated. For the judge-validation audit, one study author manually annotated all 400 interaction transcripts, 100 per judged environment. Human involvement was limited to this author annotation, which provides an internal check and may share the assumptions of the study. Appendix H identifies the code and data licences, our adaptations, and the attribution and redistribution terms retained in the planned release. 

Judges and social outcome measures. Values of social outcome measures such as goal completion, trust or consensus are produced by LLM judges. They are therefore proxies that can carry cultural and stylistic biases of the judges. Their interpreta-tion is limited to the evaluated simulations. 

AI assistance. We used Codex and Claude to as-sist with language editing. The authors reviewed and revised the resulting edits and take responsibil-ity for the final content.

The world model uses the generative interface below with a Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 backbone adapted by LoRA. The stored prompts and JSON examples retain the implementation term “scores” for the outcome measures and their numerical out-come values defined in Section 2.1. Literal prompt strings, including “Forecast unavailable or invalid,” are preserved; the surrounding prose uses the termi-nology of the main text. Figure 5 shows the prompt template. 

Input. The chat template of the base model con-tains the setting, the profile of the focal agent, vis-ible history, candidate action, outcome-measure definitions, horizons, continuation policy and out-put schema. Outcome-measure definitions specify name, range, type and direction. Reference continu-ations, labels and hidden answer keys are excluded. 

Output future states. The output represents pos-sible future states as response clusters, each with representative response text, a probability and con-ditional mean outcome values. The optional resid-ual bucket, serialized as other , has probability mass and conditional mean outcome values but no response text. A response segment contains the messages and actions of the other parties until the focal agent next acts or the interaction ends. Out-come values summarize the continuations assigned to a cluster, rather than only its representative text. At inference, the predictor tests the candidate action in a copy of the environment to determine whether it ends the interaction. If so, it requests 

K=1 and a single (no response) outcome with probability 1. 

B Training Data 

B.1 Collecting Future States 

For each candidate action, we repeatedly restore the same complete environment state and sample response segments from the other parties until the focal agent next acts or the interaction ends. Each restored state retains the same private goals and secrets. Candidate generation and the other parties use temperature 1.0; ordinary focal-agent continu-ation uses 0.7. Rollout collection uses Ornith-1.5-9B, Qwen3.5-9B and Gemma 4 12B as interaction backbones. Each candidate receives 50 sampled response segments. The original collection distinguishes sampling a response segment from continuing the interaction to obtain final labels. A single rollout decision at each saved state is shared across all candidate actions and their samples. For a selected state, every sampled branch continues to termina-tion; otherwise, the batch supplies immediate labels only. The supplementary collection continues ev-ery branch to termination. Immediate-only batches contribute immediate targets; final targets require complete labels for the requested measure across all 50 samples. This collection rule concerns train-ing labels, independently of whether evaluation episodes complete. 

Outcome measurement. Outcome values come from deterministic calculations where the environ-ment supports them. For example, Magentic-NL deal, revenue and utility follow the Magentic Mar-ketplace analytics (Microsoft, 2025). All other outcome values come from an LLM judge, using official benchmark rubrics (the seven SOTOPIA-EVAL dimensions, AgentSense goal questions) or additional rubrics (for example partner trust, group cohesion and secret leakage). In the supplementary AgentSense collection, information-question accu-racy is computed from the actual multiple-choice answers given by the actor when questions exist. Questions and answer keys are never inserted into WM inputs. The original-collection labels were produced by an Azure-hosted judge whose raw responses were not retained in auditable form. Their structure can be validated, but a full semantic re-check of every legacy judgment is not possible. The supplemen-tary labels were judged by Azure gpt-6-luna at low reasoning effort. Training labels are never re-graded with the evaluation judge.

B.3 Query Views 

A candidate batch can support several requests. With seed 0, a random view requests one to four outcome measures, available paraphrases and feasi-ble horizons, adding an unselected official outcome measure with probability 0.6. The cluster count is sampled from {1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6} and capped by available clusters. Deployment-shaped views re-quest decision-time outcome measures, possibly more than four. Supplementary views cover every available outcome measure at both horizons with up to four clusters; recovered views request one previously unqueried final outcome value. A re-quested measure–horizon pair must have a finite label on every reference sample in the candidate batch. Deployment-shaped views prefer both hori-zons, then final only, then immediate only, provided all required measures are complete; otherwise, no such view is emitted. Consequently, final condi-tional means use all members of their clusters, not a subset of completed branches, and probabilities retain the full batch denominator N . Missing labels are never imputed. D Benchmark Implementations 

Domains and goals. Magentic-NL tests whether anticipating acceptance, counteroffers and rejec-tion helps customers and businesses negotiate use-ful agreements. SOTOPIA tests coordination, col-laboration, information exchange and competition between characters with private goals. Its evalua-tion includes believability and relationships as well as goal achievement. AgentSense spans family and friendship scenes, schools, workplaces and public interactions; characters pursue individual goals and may hold private information. ProMediate asks whether predictions learned from direct participation transfer to helping other parties resolve multi-topic disagreements. The fo-cal agent is the mediator. SOTOPIA-ToM spans sectors including healthcare, technology, finance and education. Agents must share useful facts with the appropriate recipients, seek missing informa-tion and respect disclosure restrictions. We use it as a transfer environment without adding a theory-of-mind component to DSWM. Because our collection procedure requires re-peated continuations from the same interaction state, we implement all five benchmarks in a shared framework that can save and restore complete en-vironment states. We port scenario data, agent prompts and outcome-measure calculations where they are available. Table 8 lists the deviations. Be-cause of these deviations, outcome values are not comparable with official leaderboard numbers. Us-ing SOTOPIA-ToM as a benchmark does not mean that DSWM contains a theory-of-mind or belief model.

Outcome measures at inference. SOTOPIA re-quests all seven native dimensions with equal weights. AgentSense requests goal completion, estimated information reasoning and secret leak-age (minimized, weight 0.25). ProMediate requests final consensus, consensus change, topic-level ef-ficiency, mediator intelligence and tension (mini-mized, weight 0.25). Magentic-NL requests role-specific payoff, deal reached and needs met; only payoff has positive weight. The reasoning estimate for AgentSense is a planning proxy; reported information accuracy comes from actual question answering. 

3. Expected outcome values. For each candi-date, the policy computes the probability-weighted mean of every outcome measure at both horizons, including the other bucket. 

4. Filters. Two eligibility filters run on the nor-malized final expected outcome values. • Constraints. For each outcome measure marked as a constraint, a candidate stays eligible only if its expected constraint cost is within 0.02 of the lowest constraint cost in the current eligible set. Constraints are applied sequentially in measure order. This is a prediction-based safeguard, not a safety guarantee. • Dominance. A candidate is removed if another candidate is at least as good on every objective and constraint measure and strictly better on at least one. Only positive-weight outcome measures and ac-tive constraints enter the dominance comparison; in Magentic-NL, this is payoff alone. Candidates with invalid predictions are not eligible. If every predic-tion is invalid, all candidates become allowed, no utility line is shown, and the fallback is logged. 

5. Combined utility. For each cluster, every final outcome value is mapped to [0 , 1] , flipped if it is minimized, clipped, and combined with the mea-sure weights. SOTOPIA-ToM uses a weighted geo-metric mean; all other environments use a weighted arithmetic mean. The result is averaged over clus-ters using their probabilities. For a nonlinear combi-nation, this is an approximation, because variation inside a cluster is not represented. The actor sees utilities for eligible candidates on an additional line, but is not required to maximize them. 

6. Selection. The same actor backbone receives the selection prompt in Figure 6. For each candi-date it shows: • the action; • its immediate and final expected outcome values; • its two most probable response descriptions with their probabilities, cut to 60 words; • the probability mass of the other bucket. After the candidates, the prompt lists the eligible action IDs and the combined utility line. A JSON schema restricts the output to an allowed ID. De-coding uses temperature 0, at most 64 tokens and a fixed seed. If two attempts fail, the episode fails explicitly. The policy never silently substitutes an-other action. For the separate qualitative case in Figure 3, we also retain the reasoning produced by the actor separately from its final candidate ID. The selection prompt is unchanged; the final ID deter-mines the executed action, while the reasoning is shown for interpretation. 

7. Execution. The environment executes a copy of the selected candidate exactly. The actor does not rewrite it. 

Logging and the candidates-only baseline. 

Each decision logs the candidates, predictions, eli-gible set, raw actor selection output, finish reason, fallback flag and outcome-measure specifications. The candidates-only baseline runs steps 1, 6 and 7 with the candidate list alone and all IDs allowed. 

Continuation mismatch. Final predictions are values under ordinary continuation. Online, how-ever, model-guided selection is applied again at every later focal agent turn. The targets do not model this repeated use, and the supplementary collection does not remove the mismatch.

I Implementation Settings 

Section 2 defines the task, targets and reward. This appendix records implementation settings; Appen-dices A, B and E give the interface, data and inference-time action-selection protocol. 

I.1 Collection Settings 

The actor proposes candidate actions at temperature 1.0, and the other parties respond at temperature 1.0. We sample 50 continuations per candidate in both collections, using Ornith-1.5-9B, Qwen3.5-9B and Gemma 4 12B as the interaction backbones. For final labels, the ordinary continuation policy uses actor temperature 0.7 and other-party temper-ature 1.0. Each training prompt states this policy. In the original collection, one rollout decision de-termines whether all candidates and samples at a saved state continue to termination or supply im-mediate labels only. The supplementary collection always continues every branch to termination. Ap-pendix B describes the complete-label requirement for requesting final values. 

I.2 Clustering Settings 

Within each partition defined by representative ac-tions, texts are embedded with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and clustered by average-linkage agglomera-tive clustering on cosine similarity with a threshold of 0.75. The K largest clusters are retained explic-itly. Each is represented by its sampled medoid, or its first member for clusters of one or two. Re-maining clusters form the residual bucket. Cluster probabilities and outcome-value targets are defined in Eq. 2; rounding details appear in Appendix B. 

I.3 Model and Training Settings 

We adapt Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025) with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), using rank 64 and α=128 on attention and MLP projections. SFT masks prompt and padding tokens. Rows longer than 8,192 tokens are excluded, and rows from smaller environments are repeated and sampled to match Magentic-NL. We train for two epochs and keep the final checkpoint; Tables 1–4 average over five training/evaluation seeds. The GRPO matcher requires cosine similarity of at least 0.75 and agreement of representative actions. The GRPO reward and its components are defined in Eq. 3. GRPO samples eight predic-tion completions per prompt, which are distinct from the 50 environment continuations used as references. It standardizes rewards within each group and uses neither a KL penalty nor a refer-ence model. SFT and GRPO draw on the same 12,247-row pool under their respective token limits, and GRPO lists rows with reference entropy above one nat twice. J.1 Mechanism Analyses Variation across seeds. The main tables and pre-diction diagnostics report mean ± SD. Within each seed, we average the three evaluation backbones us-ing the same weights as the reported mean, and then compute the sample SD across the five seed-level results. This spread describes variation across the runs and is neither a standard error nor a confidence interval for a method difference. In particular, the three backbone evaluations of a trained checkpoint are not three independent training seeds. The sepa-rate reliability audit retains its three-seed protocol and scenario-cluster confidence intervals. 

Paired comparisons. Table 15 reports differ-ences for eight comparisons on the five-seed main evaluation grid. We compute paired method differ-ences using the same aggregation weights as the main tables and average over the three evaluation backbones. To obtain 95% confidence intervals, we use 10,000 bootstrap resamples over both scenarios and the five training/evaluation seeds, preserving pairing between methods. The intervals therefore account for variation across scenarios and seeds, conditional on the three evaluated backbones. The number of independent training seeds remains five, so increasing the resample count improves the nu-merical stability of the bootstrap without adding independent runs. We interpret each interval sep-arately rather than as a simultaneous guarantee across all comparisons. 

World modeling and post-training. The paired comparisons support gains from distributional world modeling in several environments, with the strongest evidence depending on the baseline. Future representations. The expected-values-only representation uses the same final DSWM checkpoint and world modeling procedure as the full representation. It keeps visible context, candi-date actions, immediate and final expected outcome values, combined utility, eligibility and fallback rules fixed at a given decision point. It removes re-sponse descriptions, their probabilities and residual-mass annotations, but it does not recompute utilities from expected values. The without-response-text variant instead retains the probability information. Table 3 reports online outcomes, so histories can diverge after different choices. These comparisons vary what the actor reads rather than how the WM is trained.

Nonlinear selection. Dominance filtering based on expected outcome values is a heuristic: an ac-tion with a better vector of expected outcome val-ues need not have higher expected nonlinear utility. In particular, it can remove an action whose cluster-averaged geometric utility exceeds that of the re-tained actions. We do not quantify the frequency or utility cost of these conflicts. Such an audit would require the candidate predictions before and after filtering, with constraint eligibility held fixed.

q of its entries, using pα = (1 − α)p + αq . Re-sponse texts and conditional mean outcome values stay fixed, while expected values, utilities and eli-gibility are recomputed. This experiment measures sensitivity to probability assignments rather than calibration against an empirical reference distribu-tion.
