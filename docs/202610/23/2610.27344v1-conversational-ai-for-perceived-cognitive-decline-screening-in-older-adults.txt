Abstract. Early identification of cognitive decline in older adults remains chal-lenging because traditional neuropsychological assessments often require trained personnel, structured administration, and clinical access. Chatbot-based Intelligent Cognitive Assistants (ICAs) offer a scalable approach for collecting open-ended responses that may contain linguistic, semantic, and autobiographical cues related to cognitive health. This study evaluates whether ICA question-answer (Q/A) re-sponses from older adults can support screening for participant-reported perceived cognitive decline and related cognitive, functional, and personality outcomes. We compare traditional machine learning models, frozen transformer embeddings, fine-tuned transformer models, and large language model (LLM)-based reasoning strategies, including Global Q/A analysis, Single Q/A confidence averaging, and Aggregated Q/A analysis. Among text-based methods, the Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite 1-example-per-class Aggregated Q/A configuration achieved the highest held-out test performance (0.742 accuracy, 0.739 balanced accuracy, 0.728 macro-F1). Given the small test set ( n = 31 ) and the multiple configurations evaluated, these results are interpreted descriptively rather than as definitive model rank-ings. Frozen clinical transformer embeddings with a random forest head were the strongest non-LLM text-based approach, while fine-tuned transformers showed limited generalization. Models using structured questionnaires, cognitive tasks, and personality measures achieved higher observed classification performance than models using ICA interview text. Regression analyses showed a modest pre-dictive signal for various structured cognitive, functional, and personality-related measures of cognitive decline, including AD8, FAQ, IQCODE, neuroticism, and conscientiousness. Exploratory question-level analyses identified several direct symptom-related and indirect autobiographical prompts with comparatively high test-set performance; however, effective sample sizes varied substantially across questions, and the question-level models were selected and ranked on the same held-out set. These findings suggest that an ICA chatbot interview designed to measure cognitive functioning elicited measurable signals associated with per-ceived cognitive decline and may support scalable perceived cognitive decline screening research. Future research using larger, more clinically diverse samples and clinically validated outcomes is needed to support the use of these approaches in clinical settings. arXiv:2610.27344v1 [cs.IR] 19 Oct 2026 2 N. ## 1 Introduction 

Early identification of cognitive change in older adults remains an important challenge for cognitive-health research and clinical decision support [12]. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) and other forms of early cognitive decline can be difficult to detect before they substantially affect everyday functioning, particularly because subtle changes in memory, attention, language, and functional ability often emerge gradually. Standard neuropsychological assessments and clinical screening instruments provide valuable information, but they typically require trained personnel, structured administration, and access to clinical resources [3]. These requirements can limit scalability, particularly in rural and other underserved areas where the necessary clinical expertise and resources may not be readily available. As a result, there is growing interest in low-cost, accessible tools that can support early cognitive-risk screening while complementing, rather than replacing, formal clinical evaluation. Conversational AI offers a promising direction for scalable cognitive health screen-ing. Intelligent Cognitive Assistants (ICAs) and chatbot-based interview systems can administer semi-structured conversations and collect open-ended responses from older adults. Unlike fixed-response questionnaires, these interviews elicit naturalistic language that may contain linguistic, semantic, autobiographical, and behavioral cues related to memory, attention, verbal fluency, narrative organization, and functional self-reflection [6,11,13]. Prior ICA-based work has shown that older adults can engage meaningfully with chatbot-guided interviews and that these interactions can generate analyzable text for downstream modeling [21]. However, it remains unclear how much cognitive-health signal is present in these responses and which computational methods are best suited to extract it. The central question of this study is: to what extent can machine learning, transformer-based models, and LLMs extract perceived cognitive decline signals from chatbot-based question-answer responses? Although ICA systems offer a promising platform for scal-able cognitive-health screening, their practical value depends on whether computational models can recover reliable indicators from unstructured interview data. This question is especially important because several modeling strategies are now available, including traditional machine learning, frozen transformer embeddings, end-to-end transformer fine-tuning, LLM reasoning, structured-score-based baselines, and question-level analy-sis. A systematic comparison of these approaches is therefore needed to understand both the predictive potential and the limitations of ICA interview data. To address this gap, we organize the study around three complementary evaluation tasks: 1) perceived cognitive decline classification and comparison with structured-score baselines, 2) continuous-outcome prediction from ICA interview text, and 3) question-level analysis of predictive signals. We compare multiple families of models under a consistent participant-level evaluation framework. Traditional linear and tree-based models serve as interpretable baselines, while frozen transformer encoders test whether Conversational AI for Perceived Cognitive Decline Screening in Older Adults 3

pretrained language representations contain useful signal without task-specific fine-tuning. Fine-tuned transformer models evaluate whether end-to-end adaptation improves prediction from ICA text. We also evaluate LLM-based reasoning pipelines at three levels of textual granularity: 1) Global Q/A analysis of the full aggregated interview, 2) independent analysis of individual question-answer pairs, and 3) an aggregated reasoning strategy that combines intermediate outputs from question-level analysis. This design allows us to examine whether perceived cognitive decline signals are better captured by the full interview context, by specific high-value questions, or by structured aggregation across responses. Rather than treating the ICA as a clinical MCI detector, we frame this study as an evaluation of whether open-ended chatbot interviews contain measurable signals associated with perceived cognitive decline, and whether LLM reasoning can extract these signals more effectively than conventional supervised models in a small-data setting. More specifically, we address the following four research questions:     

> – RQ1: How effectively can traditional machine learning, transformer-based, and LLM-based methods predict perceived cognitive decline from ICA chatbot interviews with older adults?
> – RQ2: How does prediction from open-ended ICA interview text compare with prediction from structured questionnaire, cognitive-task, and personality measures?
> – RQ3: To what extent can ICA interview text predict continuous cognitive, functional, and personality-related outcomes?
> – RQ4: How are perceived cognitive decline signals distributed across full-interview and individual question responses, and which questions provide the strongest predictive evidence?

## 2 Background and Related Work 

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is commonly described as an intermediate stage between normal cognitive aging and dementia, in which individuals exhibit measurable cognitive decline while often maintaining basic daily functioning [17]. Early detection is important because individuals with MCI are at increased risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease or related dementia, and earlier identification may support monitoring, plan-ning, and timely intervention [12,16]. However, traditional cognitive screening tools such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assess-ment (MoCA), while relatively brief to administer, can be influenced by educational and cultural background and may have limited sensitivity to the subtle functional and linguistic changes associated with early mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s dis-ease [15,3,8]. These limitations motivate scalable, accessible, and remotely deployable screening approaches that can complement formal clinical assessment. Language-based assessment has become an important direction in cognitive health research because cognitive decline often affects memory retrieval, semantic organization, verbal fluency, coherence, and connected speech [22,14]. Prior studies have shown that linguistic features extracted from speech or text can help distinguish cognitively healthy adults from individuals with MCI or dementia, especially when tasks require autobio-graphical recall, picture description, or open-ended narration [4,10,21]. Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP), transformer representations, and LLMs offer new opportunities to model these subtle linguistic and semantic cues from conversational data 4 N. Gautam et al. 

[7,18,5]. At the same time, language-based signals should be interpreted cautiously when outcomes reflect perceived cognitive change rather than clinically confirmed impairment. Digital and AI-supported cognitive assessment tools, including ICAs, complement traditional screening by using chatbot-based interviews to collect rich, semi-structured narrative responses. Prior ICA research has shown that chatbot interviews can elicit meaningful text and support assessment of psychological attributes such as personality [9,20]. Recent work with older adults further suggests that they can meaningfully engage with ICA narrative interviews and generally perceive them as usable and accessible, although cognitive predictions from narrative text remain limited and depend on the design of cognitively relevant prompts [21]. Despite this progress, important gaps remain in the use of ICA interviews and LLMs for perceived cognitive decline screening. Recent work on LLMs suggests that models such as GPT, Gemini, and Claude can process unstructured natural language and support reasoning over complex clinical or psychological information [5,18,2]. However, it remains unclear whether LLM-based reasoning provides advantages over traditional machine learning, frozen transformer embeddings, or fine-tuned transformer models in the context of chatbot-based cognitive-health screening. It is also unclear whether the strongest predictive signal comes from full-interview representations, individual question-level responses, structured cognitive and question-naire scores, or LLM-based aggregation across questions. This study addresses these gaps by evaluating traditional machine learning models, frozen transformer embeddings, fine-tuned transformer models, and LLM-based methods for predicting participant-reported perceived cognitive decline and related cognitive, functional, and personality outcomes from ICA chatbot interviews with older adults. In doing so, we examine whether LLMs can serve as a scalable reasoning layer for interpreting naturalistic ICA interview responses, while positioning the task as screening for perceived cognitive decline rather than clinical MCI diagnosis. 

## 3 ICA Design and Dataset 

A narrative interview study with older adults using an ICA chatbot was used to collect data for this study. The ICA conducts open-ended, text-based interviews and collects narrative responses that may contain linguistic, autobiographical, and cognitive signals relevant to cognitive functioning and aging. 

3.1 Participants and Data Source 

Participants were recruited from Prolific 5, with eligibility restricted to adults aged 55 years or older, fluent in English, and based in the United States. The original study recruited 170 participants; after excluding those who failed attention checks or had in-complete data, the final analytic sample for our experiments comprised 153 participants.

3.2 ICA Chatbot Interview Responses 

The ICA interview was administered through the Juji platform 6 and followed a semi-structured narrative protocol. Participants responded to open-ended prompts about life history, school, work, family, friendships, hobbies, health motivations, and personal reflections. The ICA was not designed as a clinical diagnostic instrument. However, its initial interview questions were informed by items from established cognitive-decline and autobiographical or episodic-memory measures, including the CDR, MMSE, MoCA, ACE-III, and AMI. These items were adapted into open-ended conversational prompts requiring participants to retrieve autobiographical memories, organize their thoughts, maintain conversational context, and produce coherent written responses. The interview contains both indirect autobiographical or narrative prompts and ques-tions that more directly ask participants about perceived cognitive or functional changes. For example, some questions ask about conversational attention, spatial orientation, so-cial engagement, driving difficulties, concentration, and everyday functioning, whereas others ask participants to recall occupational experiences, weddings, life events, or construct an open-ended story. This distinction is important because the primary classifi-cation outcome reflects participant-reported perceived cognitive decline. Consequently, performance on direct symptom-related questions may partly reflect semantic overlap with the target, whereas performance on indirect questions may reflect a broader mixture of linguistic, autobiographical, and cognitive information. The present analyses do not formally separate these sources of predictive signal. 

3.3 Outcome Variables 

We define both binary classification and continuous regression targets across three primary operational categories. The primary classification target, perceived_decline , was derived from a single-item subjective cognitive decline measure included in the intake questionnaire prior to the ICA interview. Participants were asked, “ Do you believe you’re experiencing cognitive decline? ” with binary response options: 1 (Yes) and 2 (No). Responses of 1 (Yes) were coded as perceived-decline (PD), whereas responses of 2 (No) were coded as no-perceived-decline (NPD). This measure was collected independently prior to the ICA conversation and was not derived from interview transcripts. No participants in the final analytic sample had missing values for this measure. The final analytic sample contained 100 participants in the no-perceived-decline class and 53 in the perceived-decline class. The held-out test set contained 20 participants labeled NPD and 11 labeled PD .This output binary variable, as well as several continuous variables, are summarized in Table 1.

4.1 Prediction Tasks 

We define three primary evaluation tasks to answer our four research questions: 

– Task 1: Perceived cognitive decline classification and input comparison : Predicts participant-reported perceived cognitive decline from ICA interview text and compares the best text-based model with models trained on structured questionnaire, cognitive-task, and personality mea-sures. 

– Task 2: Cognitive, functional, and behavioral outcome regression using Q/A responses :Evaluates whether ICA interview responses can predict continuous participant-level outcomes collected through separate questionnaire and cognitive-task measures, including AD8, FAQ, IQCODE, Letter Comparison proportion correct, and Big Five Inventory traits. 

– Task 3: Per-question analysis for interpretable evidence : Evaluates each ICA question independently to identify which prompts provide the strongest evidence for participant-reported perceived decline. 

4.2 Data Splitting and Validation Strategy 

The dataset contains 153 participants with matched ICA interviews and outcome mea-sures. All splits are strictly partitioned at the participant level to prevent data leakage. We use a fixed 80/20 stratified split, yielding 122 participants for development and 31 for held-out testing. Because the final test set contains only 31 participants, held-out performance should be interpreted as an independent but sample-limited estimate of generalization. A difference of only one or two correctly classified participants can noticeably change accuracy and related metrics, so small differences among the strongest models should not be interpreted as precise rankings. Within the development set, model selection and hyperparameter tuning (via Optuna [1]) are performed using nested stratified cross-validation (5-fold outer loop for gener-alization; 4-fold inner loop for tuning). For classification, the optimization objective is macro-F1. A fixed random seed of 42 was used to enable reproducibility. 4.3 Modeling Approaches 

We evaluate four distinct modeling families: 

Traditional ML Baselines Linear and tree-based models, e.g., Logistic/Linear Regres-sion, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Random Forests (RF), trained on inputs or standard features. 

Frozen Transformer Embeddings Pretrained transformer encoders are utilized as fixed feature extractors. Lightweight linear and Random Forest heads are trained on the resulting participant-level embeddings. 

Fine-Tuned Transformers End-to-end fine-tuning of domain-general and clinical lan-guage models (BERT-base/large, BioClinicalBERT, ClinicalBERT, Clinical-Longformer, and BioClinical ModernBERT). Short-context models use a hierarchical architecture with masked mean pooling across Q/A pairs, while long-context models ingest full concatenated interviews. For short-context models, including BERT and ClinicalBERT variants, each Q/A pair is encoded separately with a maximum length of 128 tokens and participant-level representations are obtained by masked mean pooling over available question embeddings. A task-specific prediction head is then trained on the pooled representation. Long-context models, including Clinical-Longformer and BioClinical ModernBERT, ingest the full concatenated interview. 8 N. Gautam et al. 

LLM-based Classification We evaluate LLMs for participant-reported perceived cog-nitive decline classification using a prompt-optimized reasoning pipeline. Unlike the supervised models, the LLM setting frames the task as a research classification problem rather than a clinical diagnostic task. The models are explicitly instructed not to provide a clinical diagnosis, but to classify participants based only on the provided ICA Q/A responses. The LLM experiments use the same development and held-out test split as the supervised models. Development data are used for prompt optimization and few-shot example selection, whereas the held-out test set is reserved exclusively for final evaluation. Prompt optimization was performed entirely within the 122-participant development partition. For the LLM experiments, this partition was further divided using seed 123 into a prompt-optimization training pool ( n = 79 ) and validation pool ( n = 43 ); the held-out 31-participant test set was not used during prompt development. During each optimization iteration, up to 24 participants were sampled from each development pool. Optimization was performed under the zero-shot Global Q/A condition. Misclassified training examples, up to eight per iteration, were supplied to an optimizer LLM to propose revisions to five prompt modules: task definition, reasoning policy, decision rules, few-shot guidance, and output format. Two optimization iterations were attempted for each task model. Candidate prompts were evaluated on a sample of development-validation participants, and the prompt with the highest validation macro-F1 was retained, using balanced accuracy and standard accuracy as successive tie-breakers. The selected prompt was fixed before the held-out evaluation. For the final evaluation, we compare zero-shot prompting with one-example-per-class prompting. The zero-shot condition contains no labeled demonstrations. In the one-example-per-class condition, one PD and one NPD participant from the development data are included as demonstrations. For the one-example-per-class condition, one participant from each outcome class was selected from the development partition using a fixed few-shot sampling seed of 42, after excluding all held-out test participant identifiers. This seeded procedure produced the same two development examples, which were then reused across GPT-4o, Claude, and Gemini for all held-out evaluations. Each LLM response is constrained to a structured JSON output containing the pre-dicted class, class probabilities, evidence strength, and a brief explanation supporting the prediction. We evaluate three inference strategies. The Global Q/A strategy provides all available Q/A pairs for a participant in a single prompt and requests one participant-level prediction. The Single Q/A Confidence Average strategy prompts the LLM separately for each available Q/A pair and averages the LLM-reported class confidence scores across questions to obtain the participant-level prediction. Finally, the LLM-based Aggregated Q/A strategy first obtains the individual Single Q/A analyses and then uses a second aggregation prompt to combine the question-level predictions, confidence scores, ev-idence strengths, and brief explanations into a final participant-level decision. These three strategies allow us to compare reasoning across the complete interview context, independent question-level reasoning, and an additional LLM-based meta-aggregation step. 4.4 Evaluation Metrics 

Classification performance is measured using accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro-F1,and weighted-F1, with priority given to balanced accuracy and macro-F1 due to class imbalance. Regression models are assessed via the coefficient of determination ( R2). Beyond predictive performance, models are evaluated qualitatively for their ability to surface interpretable, question-level cognitive health signals. To quantify test-set performance uncertainty ( n = 31 ), we conducted a participant-level bootstrap (10,000 resamples with replacement, seed 42) to derive empirical 95% percentile confidence intervals for balanced accuracy and macro-F1. For accuracy, we report analytical Wilson 95% score intervals. 

4.5 Hyperparameter Selection and Final Testing 

Hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, regularization strength, tree depth) are tuned exclu-sively in the inner cross-validation folds. Selection relies on macro-F1 for classification. Final evaluation metrics are reported on the independent, 31-participant held-out test set.

5.5 Question-level Q/A Analysis for Perceived Cognitive Decline Classification 

Table 6 presents an exploratory Single Q/A analysis. Several higher-ranked prompts directly ask about perceived cognitive or functional changes, including conversational attention (Q41), orientation in familiar areas (Q33), changes in social engagement (Q24), and driving difficulties (Q15). Because the primary target reflects participant-reported perceived cognitive decline, performance on such questions may partly reflect semantic overlap with the outcome. ## 6 Limitations 

This study has several limitations. First, the modest sample ( N = 153 ; test n = 31 )yields wide participant-level bootstrap confidence intervals, limiting fine-grained com-parisons among models. Second, the target reflects self-reported perceived cognitive decline rather than a clinically adjudicated diagnosis, and some ICA questions directly query cognitive or functional difficulties, creating potential semantic overlap with the outcome. Structured measures such as AD8 and IQCODE are also conceptually related to perceived cognitive change and should therefore be viewed as contextual reference mea-sures rather than fully independent predictors. Third, the highest LLM configuration was identified descriptively among 18 test-set evaluations, while question-level analyses used 14 N. Gautam et al. 

variable sample sizes ( n = 10 –31) and test-set-based model selection; these findings should therefore be considered exploratory. Finally, participants were recruited through Prolific, and proprietary LLMs may change over time. Larger, clinically characterized, and longitudinal cohorts are needed to assess generalizability and clinical utility. 

## 7 Conclusions and Future Work 

This study examined whether open-ended ICA chatbot interviews contain measurable signals associated with participant-reported perceived cognitive decline and related outcomes. LLM reasoning produced the numerically highest observed text-based clas-sification results, although participant-level bootstrap confidence intervals overlapped substantially across the leading text-based models, and the highest configuration was selected descriptively among multiple test-set evaluations. Structured questionnaire, cognitive-task, and personality measures produced higher observed classification per-formance, while ICA text showed a modest predictive signal for several continuous outcomes. Exploratory question-level analyses further suggest that both direct symptom-related and indirect autobiographical or narrative prompts may contain useful informa-tion; however, variable sample sizes and test-set-based selection prevent confirmatory conclusions about individual questions. Overall, ICA interviews show promise for scal-able research on perceived cognitive decline but do not establish clinical MCI detection or diagnosis. Future work should evaluate fixed models and question sets in larger, clinically characterized, longitudinal cohorts and investigate whether combining conver-sational text with speech, timing, structured assessments, and other multimodal signals improves generalizability.
