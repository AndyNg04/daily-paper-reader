ABSTRACT 

Multi-task generalization is an important yet underexplored problem in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). Many real-world applications require multi-agent systems to generalize across complex, temporally extended tasks with diverse inter-agent coordination requirements. We introduce a specification lan-guage that combines temporal-logic task specifications with operators for express-ing such requirements. We propose SpecMAGIC, a hierarchical framework that leverages the automaton structure of task specifications for subtask decomposi-tion. A centralized high-level planner identifies and allocates compatible sub-task sequences across agents, while decentralized low-level policies execute in-dividual subtasks. This enables zero-shot generalization to new tasks by com-posing reusable subtask policies according to new specifications, without retrain-ing. We establish soundness and probabilistic completeness of the high-level plan-ner. Experiments on multi-agent navigation tasks show that SpecMAGIC substan-tially outperforms existing approaches on unseen task specifications and maintains strong performance as the number of agents increases. The code is available at https://github.com/BU-DEPEND-Lab/SpecMAGIC. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Multi-task multi-agent reinforcement learning (MTMARL) aims to learn multi-agent policies that generalize across diverse tasks (Omidshafiei et al., 2017; Chai et al., 2025). Recent methods have explored this setting in applications including multi-robot systems (Wang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024a; Zhu et al., 2025) and autonomous driving (Berahman et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). However, zero-shot generalization to complex, temporally extended tasks with diverse inter-agent coordination requirements, as arise in many real-world applications, remains largely underexplored. To capture this setting, we consider multi-agent systems where each agent is assigned individual tasks, while the team must additionally satisfy coordination requirements such as dependency and count constraints. Moreover, at test time, both the individual tasks and the coordination structure among agents can differ from those encountered during training. For example, warehouse robots that have learned to deliver packages need to follow different user-specified delivery sequences, where some deliveries must precede others and certain deliveries require a specified number of robots. Temporal logic provides a principled framework for specifying multi-agent behaviors over time. In particular, linear temporal logic (LTL) has been widely used to express complex temporally ex-tended tasks and to guide the learning of policies that satisfy them (Le´ on & Belardinelli, 2020; Liu et al., 2025; Yalcinkaya et al., 2025). Recent approaches exploit the structure of automata corre-sponding to LTL specifications (Vardi & Wolper, 1986) to decompose them into subtasks or subtask sequences (Vaezipoor et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2023; Jackermeier & Abate, 2025), enabling gener-alization to unseen specifications by composing previously learned subtask policies according to the structure of a new specification. This paradigm can, in principle, be extended to multi-agent specifications by combining individual tasks and inter-agent coordination requirements into a global specification and applying the same decomposition procedure. However, the automaton correspond-ing to a global specification can be exponential in the size of the specification, and composing 1arXiv:2610.30443v1 [cs.AI] 21 Oct 2026 Preprint agent-specific requirements can further induce a combinatorial blow-up in the joint state space. Consequently, explicit automaton construction and planning become increasingly intractable as the number of agents and specification complexity grow. In this work, we propose SpecMAGIC, a hierarchical framework for zero-shot Spec ification-guided 

Multi-Agent task Generalization with Inter-agent Coordination requirements. We introduce spec-ification operators for dependency and count constraints that can be translated into standard LTL formulas. allowing individual tasks and coordination requirements to be represented within a unified specification framework. To avoid constructing a global automaton over all agents and coordination requirements, SpecMAGIC decomposes the specifications into reach-avoid subtask sequences and plans directly over the resulting subtask space. A centralized sampling-based high-level planner then identifies, allocates and sequences compatible subtasks across agents. The resulting plan is executed by decentralized subtask-conditioned RL policies, compatible with a variety of goal-conditioned RL policies with subtask embeddings (Vaezipoor et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2023; Jackermeier & Abate, 2025; Guo et al., 2026a). This hierarchical approach enables learned subtask policies to be recom-bined at test time to satisfy unseen individual tasks and coordination constraints, including both finite- and infinite-horizon specifications. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We study zero-shot generalization to complex multi-agent LTL specifications that combine individual tasks with inter-agent coordination requirements, and introduce new specifica-tion operators to conveniently express these coordination constraints. • We develop a scalable task-allocation approach that avoids the exponential complexity as-sociated with explicit global product-automaton construction, and establish soundness and probabilistic completeness of the proposed planner. • We conduct comprehensive experiments on diverse multi-agent task specifications with varying numbers of agents in a navigation environment, demonstrating consistent improve-ments over state-of-the-art baselines in zero-shot task generalization and scalability. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Specification-driven RL. There has been growing interest in learning RL policies that satisfy tasks expressed through formal specifications. Early work primarily focuses on a single fixed specifi-cation (Hasanbeig et al., 2018; Shao & Kwiatkowska, 2023; Shah et al., 2024a). More recently, zero-shot generalization to unseen specifications has been studied through direct specification or automaton encoding (Vaezipoor et al., 2021; Yalcinkaya et al., 2024) and through subtask decom-position (Qiu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a; Jackermeier & Abate, 2025; Guo et al., 2026a). In multi-agent settings, formal specifications have been used to coordinate multiple agents toward a global/system specification through hand-designed (Neary et al., 2020), heuristic (Smith et al., 2023), or learned (Shah et al., 2025) decompositions. Recent work extends formal specifications to MTMARL over a given collection of tasks (Hammond et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025). We instead fo-cus on zero-shot generalization to unseen specifications; the closest work, ACC-MARL (Yalcinkaya et al., 2025), also considers unseen specifications but is limited to finite-horizon single-agent tasks, whereas we consider both finite- and infinite-horizon specifications with explicit inter-agent coordi-nation requirements. 

Multi-agent task decomposition and allocation. In multi-agent systems with temporal-logic spec-ifications, task decomposition and allocation are widely used to synthesize coordinated plans, rang-ing from independently assigned local specifications to global tasks with temporal dependencies, collaborative execution, and capability constraints (Guo & Dimarogonas, 2015; Liu et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2025; Luo & Liu, 2025). These methods primarily address planning for a given specification and typically assume known system dynamics. To improve planning efficiency, sampling-based ap-proaches incrementally explore the product-automaton state space rather than exhaustively searching it (Kantaros & Zavlanos, 2018; 2020). Hierarchical methods have also been developed for task de-composition and allocation in MTMARL (Wang et al., 2021; Iqbal et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024b), but do not consider temporal-logic specifications. In contrast, our high-level planner samples directly over reach-avoid subtask sequences derived from the component specifications, avoiding construc-tion of the global product automaton, while decentralized policies learn to execute reusable subtasks under unknown dynamics. 2Preprint 

3 PRELIMINARIES 

Markov Games. In this work, we consider partially observable Markov games (POMGs) (Littman, 1994). A POMG with N agents is defined as the tuple M := ( S, A, P, γ, d 0), where S = S1 ×· · · × S N denotes the joint state space, A = A1 × · · · × A N denotes the joint action space, O =

O1 × · · · × O N denotes the joint observation space. The transition dynamics are given by P :

S × A × S 7 → [0 , 1] , γ ∈ [0 , 1) is the discount factor, and d0 ∈ ∆( S) is the initial state distribution. Each agent i receives its local observation correlated with the state oi : S 7 → O i and selects an action according to a policy πi : Oi × A i 7 → [0 , 1] . The joint policy is denoted by π := ( π1, π 2, . . . , π N ).Unlike standard formulations, we do not explicitly define a reward function as part of the POMG since the reward is induced by the task specification provided at test time. 

Linear Temporal Logic. LTL (Pnueli, 1977) provides a formalism for expressing temporal proper-ties over system behaviors. Given a finite set of atomic propositions AP , an LTL formula is recur-sively constructed using Boolean operators negation ¬, conjunction ∧, and disjunction ∨, together with the temporal operators “until” ( U), “eventually” ( F), and “always” ( G): 

φ := a | ¬ φ | ϕ ∧ φ | ϕ ∨ φ | F φ | G φ | ϕ U φ

Intuitively, the formula φ1 U φ2 holds if φ1 is satisfied at all time steps prior to the first occurrence of φ2. The operator F φ holds if φ is satisfied at some future time, while G φ holds if φ is satisfied from the current step onward. For each agent i, let AP i denote its set of atomic propositions, and let AP = SNi=1 AP i denote the overall proposition set. Each agent has access to a known labeling function Li : Si 7 → 2AP i that maps its local state to the set of atomic propositions. We write 

L(s) = SNi=1 Li(si) for the propositions satisfied at a joint state s = ( s1, . . . , s N ). For a trajectory 

τ = s0s1 . . . , its trace is given by Tr( τ ) = L(s0)L(s1) . . . , and τ |= φ iff this trace satisfies φ.Accordingly, the probability of satisfying φ under policy π is Pr( π |= φ) = Eτ ∼π [1[τ |= φ]] . We are interested in learning policies that maximize this satisfaction probability for arbitrary φ.

B ¨ uchi automata and subtasks. We represent an LTL specification φ using a (non-deterministic) B¨ uchi automaton (B¨ uchi, 1966) Bφ = ( Q, Σ, δ, F, q 0), where Q is a finite set of automaton states, 

Σ = 2 AP is the alphabet, δ ⊆ Q × Σ × Q is the transition relation, F ⊆ Q is the set of accepting states, and q0 is the initial state. For an infinite word ω = α0α1 . . . ∈ Σω , a run r = q0q1 . . . 

satisfies (qi, α i, q i+1 ) ∈ δ for every i ≥ 0 and is accepting iff it visits an accepting state infinitely often. To keep track of progress toward specification satisfaction, reach-avoid subtasks (Jackermeier & Abate, 2025), denoted by σ = ( α+, α −), are proposed to represent transitions over the automaton. The reach part α+ ∈ Σ corresponds to a transition (q, α +, q ′) ∈ δ, where q and q′ are consecutive states along an accepting run and q′̸ = q when q / ∈ F . The avoid part α− ∈ Σ corresponds to a transition (q, α −, q ′) ∈ δ, where q and q′ are not consecutive states along any accepting run. Intuitively, the reach part specifies progress toward satisfying the specification, whereas the avoid part captures transitions that can lead to a violation.

We now introduce the specification language for the components of φglobal . We use standard LTL to express individual-agent specifications, allowing each φi, i ∈ I, to be an arbitrary LTL formula over the agent-specific atomic propositions AP i. To express inter-agent coordination, we augment these temporal-logic task specifications with operators for dependency and count requirements. 

Dependency requirements. To capture dependency relations among agents, we define the operators 

Req and EachReq , and specify dependency requirements by the following syntax: 

φdep ::= ( α Req β) | (α EachReq β) | ϕdep ∧ φdep | ϕdep ∨ φdep 

where α and β are Boolean formulas over atomic propositions. We say τ |= α Req β iff there exists a time t ≥ 0 such that τ, t |= β and ∀t′ ∈ [0 , t ), τ, t ′̸ |= α and τ |= α EachReq β iff for every t ≥ 0

such that τ, t |= α and either t = 0 or τ, t − 1̸ |= α, there exists t′ ∈ [0 , t ) such that τ, t ′ |= β and 

τ, t ′′ ̸ |= α for all t′′ ∈ [t′, t ). Intuitively, Req requires the prerequisite β before the first occurrence of α, whereas EachReq requires β before every non-consecutive occurrence of α. In contrast, the negation of a dependency relation, e.g., ¬(α Req β) or ¬(α EachReq β), does not correspond to a meaningful dependency requirement and is therefore excluded from the syntax. 

Count requirements. We also introduce operators Sync and Async to express count require-ments among agents, such as how many agents must satisfy a given condition. The corresponding specifications are defined by the following syntax: 

φcnt ::= Sync [I, α, m ] | Async [I, α, m ] | ¬ φcnt | ϕcnt ∧ φcnt | ϕcnt ∨ φcnt 

where α is a Boolean formulas over atomic propositions, I is the set of agents I ⊆ [N ], and 1 ≤ m ≤|I|. Satisfaction of φ by τ is defined as τ |= Sync [I, α, m ] ⇔ ∃ t : |{ i ∈ I | Li(si,t ) |= α}| ≥ m

and τ |= Async [I, α, m ] ⇔ |{ i ∈ I | ∃ t : Li(si,t ) |= α}| ≥ m. Intuitively, Sync requires at least 

m agents to satisfy α at the same (discrete) timestep, and Async only requires at least m agents to satisfy α, possibly at different timesteps. 

Connections to real-world applications. These operators provide a convenient way to express coordination requirements among agents that naturally arise in a wide range of multi-agent applica-tions. For example, in surveillance and security, a guard robot may need to inspect an area before another robot enters, which can be expressed using Req or EachReq , while Async can specify that a certain number of areas are visited for monitoring coverage. In warehouse automation, Req 

or EachReq can specify that inventory availability must be confirmed before items are retrieved, while ¬Async can prevent a certain number of robots from retrieving out-of-stock items. In multi-robot manufacturing, Req or EachReq can require an object to be stabilized before manipulation, while ¬Sync can limit the number of robots operating on the same object simultaneously. These operators also serve as a convenient specification interface and can be translated into standard LTL formulas, allowing standard automata-theoretic constructions and operations for LTL to be directly applied; the corresponding translations are provided in Appendix B.2. 4Preprint 4.3 TASK DECOMPOSITION AND ALLOCATION 

To obtain a feasible multi-agent task plan, a straightforward approach is to construct the automa-ton of the global specification φglobal , or equivalently the product automaton of its component specifications, and search for feasible subtask sequences. However, the resulting state space grows exponentially with the number of agents and specification complexity (Kantaros & Zavlanos, 2018; 2020; Abate et al., 2026), making explicit construction and search intractable at scale. Previous works (Qiu et al., 2023; Jackermeier & Abate, 2025; Guo et al., 2026a) have shown that leverag-ing equivalent subtask sequences derived from specifications can improve learning efficiency and generalization. Motivated by this, we develop a sampling-based task-allocation method that oper-ates on the reach-avoid subtask sequences of the component specifications, identifies compatible combinations of subtasks across components while respecting their coordination requirements, and incrementally constructs a feasible multi-agent plan without explicitly constructing the product au-tomaton. Under unknown dynamics and partial observability, we focus on finding feasible solutions and establish the soundness and probabilistic completeness of the planner.

Soundness and (probabilistic) completeness. Many automata-based search methods (Guo et al., 2013; Guo & Dimarogonas, 2015; Qiu et al., 2023; Shah et al., 2024b; Jackermeier & Abate, 2025) have been developed to find accepting paths that satisfy a given LTL specification. These methods search for a finite prefix reaching an accepting state together with a cyclic suffix, and the resulting prefix-suffix paths, i.e., subtask sequences, are guaranteed to satisfy the given specification. We leverage these methods to obtain candidate subtask sequences for each specification component over which our planner operates. Given this property, we have the following soundness guarantee: 

Theorem 1 (Soundness) . Let τ be a path returned by the proposed sampling-based planner. Then 

τ is an accepting path satisfying the global specification φglobal , i.e., τ |= φglobal .

Soundness guarantees that any path returned by the proposed method satisfies the global specifi-cation. We next show probabilistic completeness, i.e., if a feasible path exists, the probability of finding one converges to one given sufficient iterations. The proofs are provided in Appendix A. 

Theorem 2 (Probabilistic completeness) . Suppose that there exists a feasible subtask sequence that satisfies φglobal . Then, the probability that Algorithm 1 finds a feasible subtask sequence converges to one as the number of sampling iterations approaches infinity. 

4.4 POLICY LEARNING 

The goal is to learn a decentralized goal-conditioned policy πi(ai | oi, σ i) for each agent i, which takes as input its local observation oi ∈ O i and current reach-avoid subtask σi. We assume that all agents are homogeneous. The challenge is that the same set of local subtasks can correspond to dif-ferent coordination requirements under different specifications. For example, two agents may have the same local subtasks of reaching their respective target regions, while one specification allows them to complete these subtasks independently and another requires them to do so simultaneously. Thus, the global coordination requirement cannot be determined from the local subtasks alone. Con-sequently, commonly adopted cooperative MARL schemes based on shared team rewards and cen-tralized critics (Sunehag et al., 2017; Foerster et al., 2018; Rashid et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022) are not well suited to our setting. Instead, we use a subtask-dependent reward and cost function evaluated with respect to each agent’s own subtask, and follow an IPPO-style approach (De Witt et al., 2020) to learn decentralized policies and critics. During training, the high-level planner described in Section 4.3 is not required. We independently sample a reach-avoid subtask for each agent and train the low-level policies to complete their re-spective subtasks. Although the subtasks are sampled independently, agents still interact and must account for one another, allowing the policies to learn interaction-aware behaviors; for example, multiple agents may sample subtasks that require reaching the same region and must avoid colli-sions with other agents. Each agent learns to satisfy its subtask by receiving positive rewards for subtask satisfaction while being penalized for collisions and subtask violations. High-level coordi-nation requirements, such as synchronized or ordered subtask execution, are instead handled at test time by the centralized planner, which controls each agent’s current subtask and its progression. The learned subtask-conditioned policies are then reused to execute these planned subtasks across dif-ferent specifications, enabling zero-shot generalization to unseen specifications without retraining. We instantiate the low-level policies by adapting GenZ-LTL (Guo et al., 2026a) because it explicitly models safety constraints, which are common in our specifications, while our framework is also compatible with other goal-conditioned RL methods capable of learning reach-avoid policies (Qiu et al., 2023; Yalcinkaya et al., 2024). Further implementation details are provided in Appendix C.

Environments. We consider a multi-agent Zone environment based on prior work (Jackermeier & Abate, 2025; Guo et al., 2026a;b), where multiple agents with continuous action spaces and high-dimensional lidar observations navigate among randomly positioned colored regions to satisfy the given task specifications. The Zone environment is similar to the commonly used Multi-Agent Particle Environment (MPE) for MARL (Lowe et al., 2017), but involves complex long-horizon tasks, including reaching sequences of regions with varying lengths, satisfying diverse coordination constraints among agents, and avoiding inter-agent collisions at all times. An illustration of the envi-ronment with an example trajectory is shown in Figure 2; more details are provided in Appendix B.1. 

Baselines. We compare our method with ACC-MARL (Yalcinkaya et al., 2025), the only existing method that, to our knowledge, targets generalization across temporal-logic specifications in MARL. ACC-MARL first learns embeddings of compositions of deterministic finite automata (DFAs). It then learns decentralized policies, with each agent’s policy conditioned on the global state informa-tion together with the DFA embeddings of the specifications assigned to all agents. Finite-horizon tasks. We first evaluate finite-horizon tasks, i.e., specifications that can be satis-fied by finite-length trajectories. We consider 3 agents, each assigned an individual task, together with additional inter-agent dependency Φdep and count requirements Φcnt . We evaluate both in-distribution (IND) and out-of-distribution (OOD) specifications, following the specification distri-bution used to train ACC-MARL. ACC-MARL is trained on specifications represented as DFAs and sampled from a fixed distribution; IND specifications are drawn from this distribution, whereas OOD specifications fall outside it and require longer subtask sequences to satisfy. The same IND/OOD specification sets used to evaluate ACC-MARL are used to evaluate SpecMAGIC. 7Preprint As shown in Table 1, our method consistently outperforms the baseline across all evaluated spec-ifications, achieving higher success rates, lower violation rates, and better efficiency, as indicated by fewer steps required for satisfaction. ACC-MARL supports only Φind , where each agent has an individual task and access to the task automata of the other agents. Extending it to Φdep and Φcnt 

would require additional training distributions over these specifications, potentially biasing zero-shot evaluation. ACC-MARL also assumes access to agent state information si, whereas agents in our environment receive only partial observations oi through LiDAR sensing. Moreover, ACC-MARL pre-trains automaton embeddings using automata sampled from a fixed distribution, which limits its generalization when encountering unseen automata. In contrast, our method decomposes specifications into reach-avoid subtasks and learns reusable subtask-conditioned policies, enabling unseen specifications to be handled by composing previously learned subtask behaviors and thereby improving OOD generalization. Finally, performance generally decreases with increasing specifi-cation complexity, such as from Φind to Φind/dep/cnt . Inter-agent dependency and count require-ments introduce additional coordination constraints, while OOD specifications further increase the difficulty by requiring each agent to complete more subtasks and imposing more constraints in Φdep 

and Φcnt . These factors make successful coordination more challenging and can increase the like-lihood of inter-agent collisions in practice. Nevertheless, our method maintains high success rates and low violation rates across these more complex and unseen specifications.

6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This work develops a hierarchical framework for zero-shot generalization across diverse LTL spec-ifications in multi-agent systems. The framework consists of a novel centralized sampling-based high-level planner with soundness and probabilistic completeness guarantees for planning over the reach-avoid subtask space, and decentralized subtask-conditioned policies for low-level execution. Empirical results demonstrate that our method achieves strong generalization across diverse finite-and infinite-horizon specifications and remains effective as the number of agents increases. A limita-tion of our framework is that the high-level planner remains centralized, which may limit scalability as the number of agents further increases. Future work will investigate fully decentralized planning mechanisms to further improve scalability and integrate alternative safe RL methods (Kushwaha et al., 2025) into the low-level policies that may better handle safety constraints. 9Preprint

C IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

We use the official codebase of the baseline ACC-MARL 1. The low-level policy used in our frame-work is adapted from GenZ-LTL 2 by extending it to the multi-agent setting, while we implement the proposed high-level sampling-based planner. For a fair comparison, all methods use the same network architectures: the actor and critic are fully connected networks with three hidden layers of size [64 , 64 , 64] , and the value function network has two hidden layers of size [64 , 64] . We retain the original state/observation preprocessing of each method. All methods use the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 3 × 10 −4 and are trained for 15M environment interactions. The discount factor is set to γ = 0 .998 for ZoneEnv . Additional hyperparameter details are provided in the code included in the supplementary materials. All methods are trained on a 16-core AMD CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, with our model taking approximately 6 hours to train.

Replanning and value-guided sampling. During execution, we evaluate the current active propo-sitions L(s) and use them to update the automaton state of each specification component. If none of the automaton states changes, the agents continue following the current plan. Whenever the au-tomaton state of at least one component changes, we recompute the feasible reach-avoid subtask sequences from the updated automaton states and invoke the planner again to generate a new plan from the current specification progress. For infinite-horizon specifications, we construct each can-didate subtask sequence from its finite prefix followed by ncyc repetitions of its cyclic suffix, and apply the same planning and replanning procedure to these finite sequences. On the other hand, at test time, learned value functions can in principle be used to guide subtask selection, e.g., by choos-ing the candidate subtask σ with the largest value V (s, σ ) at the current state s. However, evaluating a multi-step plan also requires estimating the values of subsequent subtasks at future states that have not yet been observed. One possible approach is to train an additional model to predict a fu-ture state ˜s and then evaluate V (˜ s, σ ′) for the next subtask σ′. However, the planning horizon can be long, and prediction errors can accumulate over multiple subtasks, making accurate future-state estimates difficult to obtain. Therefore, value-function-based lookahead becomes challenging for long-horizon task allocation considered in our setting. Moreover, under partial observability, opti-mizing the planned subtask sequence is challenging, and value-guided sampling does not offer an obvious advantage. We leave a systematic investigation of this direction to future work.

Coordination-specification complexity. We also evaluate how performance changes as the com-plexity of the inter-agent coordination specifications increases in the 3-agent setting. To isolate the 18 Preprint effects of φdep and φcnt , each agent is assigned a simple individual task of the form F α, while the coordination-specification complexity is varied separately. For dependency requirements, we construct φdep as a conjunction of Req clauses and increase n = |Req |, introducing more inter-agent dependency relations. Similarly, for count requirements, we construct φcnt as a conjunction of Sync and Async clauses and increase n = |Sync | + |Async |, introducing more count re-quirements. Although dependency and count requirements can be combined within the same global specification, we vary them separately here to isolate the effect of each type of coordination com-plexity. As shown in Figure 6, increasing either type of specification complexity generally decreases the success rate ηs and increases the violation rate ηv and the number of steps μ required to satisfy the specification, as agents must satisfy a larger number of inter-agent coordination requirements. Meanwhile, the planning time tpath remains tractable as the number of coordination operators in-creases, since the sampling-based planner operates directly on subtask sequences without explicitly constructing the product automaton.
