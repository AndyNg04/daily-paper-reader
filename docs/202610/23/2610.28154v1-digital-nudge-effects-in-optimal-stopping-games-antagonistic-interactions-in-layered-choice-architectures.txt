# Digital Nudge Effects in Optimal Stopping Games: Antagonistic Interactions in Layered Choice Architectures 

# Batisse Manhardt and Ja-Nae Duane 

# Abstract 

Digital nudges have become a prominent area of research in human-centered interaction, yet little is known about how they interact when deployed together. This study examines the individual and combined effects of anchoring, salience, and social proof digital nudges on stopping behavior and outcome quality in a sequential decision task. Using a 2 3 between-subjects factorial design grounded in the secretary problem ( 𝑁 = 181), we find that (1) an anchor nudge significantly increased exploration rather than constraining it ( 𝑏 = 1.207, 𝑝 = .017; (2) the salience nudge produced the smallest effect of any condition; and (3) combining the anchor and social proof nudges produced the worst outcome quality of any condition ($181.83 vs. $192.21 for anchor-only), with outcome quality declining monotonically as nudge count increased. These findings provide the first controlled evidence for a digital nudge crowding-out effect with direct implications for the ethical design of digital interfaces. 

Keywords: digital nudges, secretary problem, stopping games, search-stop behavior, dark patterns 

# 1. Introduction 

When a consumer searches for an item on Amazon, they encounter an engineered decision environment. For example, an item may appear to be marked as “Amazon’s Choice” alongside a counter showing only five of the items left in stock. No option is removed, yet the consumer’s decision has already been guided before they consciously engage with it. 

This is digital nudging in practice. Digital nudges combine data, information, interactions, or incentives with design elements to leverage cognitive biases (Duane et al., 2024). A subset of digital nudges is dark patterns, characterized by their intent to guide users and to act against users' best interests (Duane et al., 2024; Gray et al., 2023). In 2024, the $2.5 billion FTC settlement against Amazon brought these tactics into regulatory focus, finding that the company used deceptive tactics to lead consumers to unintentionally purchase Amazon Prime subscriptions while making cancellation exceedingly difficult (Federal Trade Commission, 2025). Yet the underlying mechanics of these digital nudges and their subcategory of dark patterns, how they interact, amplify, or undermine one another, remain poorly understood. Digital nudges and dark patterns are now pervasive in all environments. Whether it is a fitness app or a supermarket’s website, nearly all corporations are aware of and actively utilize these. As Duane et al. (2024) argue, digital nudges are best understood as instances in which data, information, interactions, or incentives are combined with design elements within a digital environment to leverage cognitive biases or heuristics to influence a choice. Caraban et al. (2019) identify numerous variables that affect nudging design and effectiveness, further demonstrating the nuanced nature of the digital nudging space (Schneider et al., 2018; Weinmann et al., 2016). Despite this, nudges have largely been studied in isolation, examining single nudge types within single contexts, leaving open the question of how nudges interact when deployed together. In practice, consumers rarely encounter a single nudge on its own (Duane, 2023). Instead, they encounter layered combinations of defaults, social proof signals, anchors, and loss-framed cues. Whether these combinations amplify, neutralize, or antagonize one another remains an open empirical question. This paper addresses that gap with a controlled experiment grounded in the secretary problem (Gilbert & Mosteller, 1966), a well-validated sequential search-stop paradigm that isolates stopping behavior and outcome quality across varying choice architectures. Using a 23 between-subjects factorial design ( 𝑁 = 181), we tested three digital nudges (anchoring, salience, and social proof) and their seven possible combinations, comparing each against a no-nudge control. Our findings have direct implications for the future of human-computer interaction research and ethical interface design. As regulatory scrutiny following the Federal Trade Commission (Federal Trade Commission, 2025) makes clear, understanding how combinations of nudges work and fail is no longer merely an academic question. 

Search-stop studies have previously been conducted, but their main objective has been to test ideas such as exploration-exploitation trade-offs, in which optimal stopping theory is explored (Bouhlel, 2022; Goldstein et al., 2019; Rapoport et al., 2022). Such research has evolved to examine various environments, including competitive ones (Rios & Ghosh, 2024), as well as how heuristics influence individuals’ decisions (Gurney et al., 2025). However, although this research examines the cognitive domain of stop-search behavior, there is no literature on the effectiveness of digital nudges in these environments. Digital nudges occur when data, information, interactions, or incentives are combined with design elements in a digital environment to leverage cognitive biases or heuristics to influence user choices (Duane et al., 2024). This design logic can be pervasive and may utilize dark patterns that work against users’ interests (Gray, 2023; Schneider et al., 2018) through the same cognitive mechanisms identified in digital nudge research: exploiting defaults, social proof, and loss aversion to drive spending and engagement. One of the most common digital nudges is the use of defaults. Previous studies have examined the effectiveness of salience in relation to cookie-setting preferences on websites (Laine, 2021), whether visual cues can lead to early quitting in visual searches (Moher et al., 2024), and status quo bias in decision-making (Krieger & Felder, 2013). While several of the above studies may have implications for the digital nudging domain, they do not effectively examine a user’s interactions with a salience heuristic in a search-stop environment; rather, they focus on a specific use case, whether that be cookie-setting acceptance or health insurance policies. Caplin and Martin (2012) found that the default effect is strong when individuals are inattentive but erodes when they become attentive. Similarly, others have found that the strength of the default effect diminishes significantly as the duration increases (White et al., 2021). Salience nudges operate by directing System 1 attention toward a preselected option (Kahneman, 2011), though their effectiveness diminishes when users are attentive and engaged (Caplin & Martin, 2012; White et al., 2021), suggesting that motivated players may be less susceptible than passive users. Anchoring nudges leverage users’ tendency to over-rely on salient reference information when making sequential judgments (Mochon & Frederick, 2013; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Tversky and Kahneman (1974) first introduced the concept, studying how selecting a random number affected an individual’s response to a question they may not be knowledgeable about. More recent literature has examined individuals’ overall search effort (Gurney et al., 2025) and anchoring in sequential decision-making (Mochon & Frederick, 2013). Specifically, Mochon and Frederick (2013) measured how judgments serve as anchors for subsequent judgments, not how anchoring through demonstration may affect an individual’s search-stop actions. Social proof uses consensus information to guide behavior under uncertainty (Cialdini, 2007) and is especially potent when the quality of options is unknown. This is similar to the condition that sequential search creates (Venema et al., 2020). When combined with loss-framed anchoring, social uncertainty should amplify sensitivity to negative framing, producing larger deviations from optimal stopping than either nudge alone. Finally, while individual nudge effects are reasonably documented, combination effects are not. Howley and Ocean (2021) found non-linear interactions between nudge types, and Hummel and Maedche (2019) identified crowding as a mechanism by which additional nudges reduce effectiveness rather than compounding it. In digital interfaces that routinely layer multiple nudge types simultaneously, cognitive overload may cause combinations to antagonize rather than reinforce one another (Schneider et al., 2018). Based on the literature above, we propose the following hypotheses for this study: 

H1: The salience nudge will produce a smaller shift in stopping behavior and outcome quality than anchoring or social proof nudges, as attentive participants in a high engagement task are less susceptible to default-style cues (Caplin & Martin, 2012; White et al., 2021). 

H2: The anchor nudge will cause participants to stop earlier in the sequence, as visible progress anchors operationalize loss aversion by making skipped options increasingly salient (Mochon & Frederick, 2013; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). 

H3: The combination of social proof and loss-framed anchoring will produce greater deviation from the control than either nudge alone, as social uncertainty amplifies sensitivity to loss-framed information (Howley & Ocean, 2021; Venema et al., 2020). H4: Combining multiple nudges will not yield additive improvements in outcome quality; instead, combinations will exhibit antagonistic effects as the number of nudges increases, reflecting cognitive overload in interactive decision environments (Hummel & Maedche, 2019; Weinmann et al., 2016). 

# 3. Study Design 

A between-subjects design crossed three binary nudges: an anchor nudge, a social nudge, and a salience nudge, yielding eight experimental conditions for this game. The game was conducted through Qualtrics, and participants were randomly assigned to one condition and one of three box sets (stimulus sets containing ten monetary values each). All three box sets were counterbalanced across conditions. 

3.1. Participants and Recruitment 

Participants were recruited via an anonymous Qualtrics survey link through a convenience sample. Eligibility required participants to be between 18 and 25 years old, targeting young adults. No compensation was provided for this pilot of the game. This study had 267 participants. Of 267 participants, 47 did not complete the game. Thirty-three did not pass the attention check, where they were asked how many total boxes there were (10), whether they could return to a previously selected box (No), and what the objective of the game was (select the box with the highest monetary value). Finally, 6 participants were excluded for outlier time to complete the survey (greater than 900 seconds). The final sample for this study was 181. 

3.2. Variables 

Two primary dependent variables were used to capture participants’ behavior. The first was a stopping box (accepted box), which was the box number at which the participant accepted a value (range 1–10). This variable operationalizes stopping behavior relative to the search sequence. The second dependent variable was the accepted amount ($), representing the dollar value of the box the participant accepted (range $160 to $201). This operationalizes outcome quality. Finally, we had a robustness measure, the within-set percentile rank, which was constructed by ranking each participant’s accepted amount within their box set (on a 0–1 scale). This controls for stimulus-difficulty confounds more completely than box-set dummy variables.

Two patterns arise: 41.4% of participants stopped at boxes 1–3 (very early termination), and 50.3% stopped at boxes 6, 9, or 10 (late termination). Notably, only 3.3% stopped in the “optimal” range derived from the secretary problem. The dip between the two peaks of the distribution is consistent with many participants being unfamiliar with the secretary problem. From these two patterns, two potential strategies may explain the distribution: stopping quite early or having a higher risk tolerance and waiting until the absolute end, referred to as “Satisficers” and “Exhaustive searchers” respectively. Risk tolerance had no significant interaction with any nudge, despite a borderline main effect on stopping behavior ( 𝑟 = +0.15, 𝑝 = 0.040), indicating that higher risk tolerance is associated with stopping later in the sequence. However, there is no clear relationship between risk tolerance and the accepted box, further indicating that nudge effects are independent of one’s risk tolerance towards the accepted box number.

4.2. Interaction Effects: Antagonistic Nudges 

The anchor-only condition (100) produced the highest mean accepted amount in the study ($192.21; 𝑑 = +0.67) compared with the control. However, the anchor-social proof condition (110) interestingly reversed this effect: the mean accepted amount fell to $181.83, the lowest of any condition ( 𝑑 = 0.33 vs. control). Utilizing within-set percentile rank DV, condition 110 yields the lowest mean rank of any condition (0.366, vs. 0.600 for anchor-only and 0.500 for control), and the anchor × social interaction term is statistically significant ( 𝛽 = 0.256, 𝑝 = .048). H3 is partially supported by a significant anchor-x-social-proof interaction, but the interaction is antagonistic rather than amplifying, ultimately reversing the expected direction.

# 5. Discussion 

This study set out to examine how digital nudges, individually and in combination, shape stopping behavior and outcome quality in a sequential decision environment. Three findings stand out. The most striking result is the anchor reversal. The box counter nudge, designed to operationalize loss aversion by making skipped options increasingly salient, instead significantly increased exploration ( 𝑏 = 1.207, 𝑝 = .017). We attribute this to the Goal-Gradient Effect (Kivetz et al., 2006): rather than perceiving opened boxes as foregone opportunities, players appear to have read the counter as progress toward a goal, motivating continued search. This has direct implications for interface design. Progress indicators ubiquitous in e-commerce environments, such as rewards trackers or bundling options, may systematically encourage exploration rather than commitment, depending on how they are framed. Critically, reversing the anchor’s direction, showing remaining rather than opened boxes, may produce the loss-framing effect originally hypothesized and represents a clear target for future work. The salience nudge produced the smallest effect of any condition, consistent with H1 and with prior evidence that default-style cues lose power when users are attentive and engaged (Caplin & Martin, 2012; White et al., 2021). The search-stop task, which allowed deliberate System 2 thinking without time pressure, appears to have reduced the System 1 susceptibility that salience nudges rely on. The antagonistic interaction between the anchor and social proof nudges is perhaps the most consequential finding for practice. Anchor-only participants achieved the highest outcome quality in the study ($192.21, 𝑑 = +0.67 vs. control), while the anchor-social proof combination produced the lowest ($181.83, 𝑑 = 0.33 vs. control). The dose-response analysis reinforces this: outcome quality peaked with one active nudge and declined monotonically with each additional nudge. This crowding-out pattern, consistent with predictions of cognitive overload (Hummel & Maedche, 2019; Schneider et al., 2018), suggests that layering nudges may actively harm individual outcomes rather than compound positive effects. However, the three-way interaction term (Anchor × Social × Salience)’s partial reversal of this antagonistic effect created by the anchor-social proof combination suggests that through careful design, potential harm to player outcomes may be mitigated. 

# 6. Conclusion and Future Work 

There are several limitations to this study. While this study establishes a framework for investigating digital nudges and their antagonistic interactions, several limitations offer opportunities for future research within digital environments. First, this study had large effect sizes. Subsequent studies should utilize larger sample sizes to adequately powered comparisons and verify our findings. Second, digital environments should more deeply operationalize core behavioral constructs such as anchoring, scarcity, and loss aversion, thereby solidifying the theoretical link between digital nudging and the cognitive systems that drive user decision-making. This study investigated the effects of digital nudges on individuals in a sequential search-stop game environment. We find that a box counter anchor nudge significantly increases the number of boxes opened, overshadowing a weaker salience default nudge and, conversely, producing the worst outcome when combined with a social proof nudge. We find that, consistent with prior research, System 2 cues have the potential to override System 1 cues through digital nudging but have the potential to elicit overcrowding effects. By providing a rare direct comparison of competing digital nudges within a shared framework, this work establishes a critical foundation for future research on the boundary conditions and interaction effects of behavioral heuristics in digital environments.
