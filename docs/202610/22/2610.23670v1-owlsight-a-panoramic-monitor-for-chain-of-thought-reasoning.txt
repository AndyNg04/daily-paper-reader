## ABSTRACT 

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) monitoring offers actionable evidence for oversight AI systems, with the potential to enable early detection and intervention. However, existing approaches are focused on specific tasks, whereas the regulation of AI, particularly reasoning models, requires monitoring a broader range of tasks. We introduce Factor for CoT monitoring, a strategy that supports customizing mon-itoring requirements in the prompt, which enables a single monitor to cover dif-ferent tasks by relying on different factors. Furthermore, we introduce two types of factors, fine-grained and coarse-grained ( e.g. , monitoring whether a specific email address has been leaked versus monitoring privacy leakage), to accom-modate diverse user monitoring needs. Based on this, we develop a CoT mon-itor named OwlSight that relies solely on factor to monitor behaviors related to multiple dimensions, including capability, safety, and trustworthiness. Experi-ments across 15,971 reasoning trajectories show that OwlSight surpasses general-purpose LLMs by 1.32–30.57 and 11.71–27.81 points at fine and coarse granu-larity, respectively, and achieves state-of-the-art results on OpenAI’s CoT moni-torability benchmark. It also generalizes to unseen models’ CoTs and remains ro-bust to Factor rephrasings, CoT compression, and obfuscation, offering a promis-ing paradigm for CoT monitoring that can contribute to the oversight of fron-tier AI. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

As frontier AI systems become increasingly capable and are deployed across a broader range of sce-narios, oversight must account for an expanding range of capabilities, behaviors, and risks (McCain et al., 2026). One promising oversight path is chain-of-thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022), which can provide actionable evidence for reward hacking, deception and others in frontier reasoning models (Baker et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025). In the recent incident in which OpenAI agents compromised Hugging Face systems, OpenAI’s retrospective analysis indicated that a CoT monitoring system would have detected the initial activity and alerted the security team more than a day before the breach, highlighting its potential for early detection and intervention (OpenAI, 2026b). The challenge in such incidents is that frontier AI systems can exhibit a wide range of behaviors requiring oversight, including reward hacking, intent concealment, unauthorized access, and data leakage. Yet existing CoT monitoring approaches are developed for specific tasks, such as detecting hidden objectives in code generation (Diwan et al., 2026) and identifying covert malicious behavior in agent trajectories (Ahmed and Nafisi, 2026). These specific monitors cannot be applied to real-world oversight settings, where monitoring targets typically comprise multiple types of behavior. To address this challenge, monitoring objectives must be adapted flexibly at inference time rather than by retraining a separate classifier for each task (OpenAI, 2025). We introduce Factor for CoT monitoring, a strategy that allows users to customize monitoring requirements in the prompt, so that a single monitor can cover different tasks by relying on different factors. We further distinguish fine-grained and coarse-grained Factors, which support monitoring either broad task types such as privacy leakage or specific behaviors such as email-password leakage, thereby accommodating different levels of monitoring granularity, as shown in Figure 2. These findings suggest that a single Factor-conditioned monitor can enable flexible CoT monitoring across various objectives, contributing to the oversight of frontier AI. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Chain-of-Thought Monitorability. CoT monitoring is a promising oversight mechanism, as reasoning traces expose behaviorally relevant information beyond final outputs, including reward-hacking and other alignment-critical behaviors (Baker et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2025). Prior work has characterized when such signals are recoverable from CoT, the extent to which decision-relevant factors are exposed, and the overall quality of monitoring evidence available in reasoning traces (Guan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Anwar et al., 2026). 

Improving CoT Monitorability. There are two complementary approaches for improving moni-torability: increasing the informativeness of traces produced by the monitored model, or enhancing the monitor’s ability to extract relevant signals from existing traces. Most prior work prioritizes the former, investigating how training objectives, optimization pressure, and reasoning pipelines shape CoT monitorability (MacDermott et al., 2025; Xiong et al., 2026; Kaufmann et al., 2026; Zolkowski et al., 2025; Drori et al., 2025; Yueh-Han et al., 2026). Comparatively less work focuses on improv-ing the monitor itself, via stronger inference procedures (Guan et al., 2025), improved monitoring scaffolds (Kale et al., 2026), or filtering strategies for long trajectories (Arike et al., 2026). Our work belongs to this under-explored monitor-centered direction: we strengthen monitoring performance without modifying the model being supervised. 

Target-Specific CoT Monitors. Within this monitor-centered line of research, existing CoT mon-itors are typically trained or prompted for fixed, predefined behavioral targets such as deceptive alignment, hidden objectives, and covert malicious behavior (Baker et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025; Di-wan et al., 2026; Ahmed and Nafisi, 2026). These systems are optimized for individual targets. By contrast, we condition a single shared monitor on natural-language Factors, enabling evaluation across heterogeneous monitoring targets within one unified interface. 

## 3 METHOD 

Our approach allows users to specify CoT monitoring requirements directly in the prompt through natural-language Factors . Each Factor describes the behavior or relation that the monitor should track. We obtain a monitor named OwlSight by fine-tuning a small-scale LLM on diverse monitoring tasks. By training on CoT traces paired with different Factors and relevant task context, the model learns to make judgments according to the monitoring requirement specified by each Factor. We adopt multiple-instance learning (MIL) to handle long reasoning traces, where relevant oversight signals may appear at arbitrary positions across extended sequences. 3.1 FACTOR -C ONDITIONED COT M ONITORING 

Our monitor is conditioned on a user-specified Factor , allowing the monitoring requirement to be changed directly in the prompt. Rather than training a separate monitor for each task, the same model adapts its judgment according to the Factor provided at inference time. Each Factor describes the behavior or relation that the monitor should track. 

Factor specification. A Factor specifies what the monitor should track and how the correspond-ing Applied and Not Applied judgments are defined. Given a Factor, the observable CoT trace, and relevant task context, the monitor predicts which judgment applies. A Factor is Applied when the specified target substantively influences or shapes the model’s reasoning or behavior, and Not Applied otherwise, including cases where the target is only mentioned or recognized in the trace.

3.2.1 DATA CONSTRUCTION 

To obtain a monitor across diverse monitoring requirements, we construct each training example by pairing a reasoning trajectory with a Factor. This allows the same trajectory to be associated with different monitoring requirements depending on the Factor provided. A single reasoning trace can therefore be paired with different Factors and receive different judgments depending on the monitoring requirement. For example, the same modular-arithmetic trace is Applied for a Factor targeting Euler exponent reduction, but Not Applied for a Factor targeting a binomial-expansion method that is not used in the reasoning. Each training example is represented as 

(O, C, R, y ), y ∈ { 0, 1},

where O denotes the Factor, C the task context, R the reasoning trace, and y the corresponding judgment, with y = 1 for Applied and y = 0 for Not Applied , as shown in Figure 3. OwlSight uses only (O, C, R ) as input, while final answers, tool outputs, and environmental outcomes are kept separately for supervision and diagnostics. 

Training coverage. The training set covers three domains: Capability (Reasoning Method, Skill Use, Instruction Following), Safety (Deception, Sycophancy, Reward Hacking, Sabotage, Sandbag-ging), and Trustworthiness (Hallucination, Privacy, Jailbreak). We further categorize these Factors by the type of monitoring relation they encode: External Factors assess how external context influ-ences reasoning or behavior, while Internal Factors evaluate the deployment of reasoning methods, strategies, or decision criteria. Our Factors also fall into two object classes: fine-grained Factors and coarse-grained Factors. Where applicable, reasoning traces are paired with Factors from both classes. Source-specific trajectory origins and generators are documented in Appendix A.1. 

Factor construction. Each Factor specifies a target description q, a relation ρ, and criteria for 

Applied and Not Applied :

O = ( q, ρ, a +, a −).

As illustrated in the left portion of Figure 3, we build Factors via two construction routes: • Context-anchored construction. We use this route when the monitoring target can be directly specified from task context or metadata. We build the Factor from task descriptions, structured metadata, or predefined skill catalogs, without inspecting the reasoning trace or its label. For instance, a Skill Use Factor is built directly from the task objective and target skill identity, requiring no inspection of the reasoning trace. 4Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 • Trajectory-induced construction. We use this route when task context alone cannot fully define the monitoring target. First, we extract candidate monitoring targets from unlabeled reasoning traces. Next, we ground these candidates using the task context. Then, we remove wording tied to the specific trace. This yields a Factor that describes the general monitoring target, not evidence unique to a single reasoning trace. Each Factor and its application criteria are frozen before label assignment. This keeps Factor con-struction separate from the subsequent labeling process. 

Label assignment. As illustrated in the right portion of Figure 3, for every frozen Factor, we select a labeling route based on whether its application criteria map directly to verifiable properties of the model’s final output. • Outcome-based labeling. Under this route, we rely on observed final outputs for labeling. This means the Factor’s application criteria can be objectively checked from the model’s final response, without inspecting intermediate reasoning steps. This strict requirement rules out scenarios where one outcome can stem from distinct underlying causes. For instance, sycophancy requires carefully designed experiments to disentangle limited ability from deliberate deference before outcome-based labeling can apply. • Reasoning-trace-based labeling. We adopt this route when final outputs cannot directly verify the Factor’s application criteria. We draw on the model’s full chain-of-thought rea-soning trace for evaluation. We examine this full chain-of-thought reasoning trace to judge whether the Factor’s target behavior shapes or advances the model’s reasoning. For in-stance, in Sabotage settings, we flag concrete steps toward an unauthorized objective, even when the final attempt fails. Labeling rules and audit procedures are detailed in Appendices A.3 and A.4. Further details on data sources, training composition, hard negatives, leakage controls, and an ex-ample illustrating Factor construction are provided in Appendix A. 3.2.2 MODEL TRAINING 

Monitor implementation. We use supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to train OwlSight. The monitor is built upon a pretrained causal language model and performs Factor-conditioned binary classification. Let E denote this serialized input. The backbone Backbone θ encodes E into hidden states 

H = Backbone θ (E). We extract the final non-padding hidden state HT and feed it into a lin-ear classification head: 

p(E) = σ(w⊤HT + b),

where p(E) denotes the model’s predicted probability that the Factor condition is Applied . Full input formatting and optimization settings are provided in Appendix B. 

Long-CoT training. Long CoTs pose a challenge for monitor training because behavior-relevant evidence may appear anywhere in the reasoning trace, while processing the entire sequence can be prohibitively expensive. To avoid hard truncation while keeping each model forward within a fixed token budget, we apply multiple-instance learning (MIL)(Dietterich et al., 1997; Ilse et al., 2018) to overlength inputs. As shown in Figure 4, the CoT is split into overlapping windows covering the full trace, with the Factor and task context repeated for every window. Because supervision is available only at the trajectory level, we treat each Factor–trajectory pair as a bag of windows. For each long trajectory, training samples at most four windows. Early epochs sample windows near the start, one-third, two-thirds, and end of the trace; later epochs use two high-scoring windows alongside two under-visited exploration windows. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Monitor setup. We train three Factor-conditioned CoT monitors with Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026), Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026), and Llama-3.2-3B (Llama Team, 2024) backbones. Un-less otherwise specified, OwlSight refers to our primary Qwen3.5-4B monitor, trained on 17,812 monitoring queries over 8,796 reasoning trajectories. We train for three epochs and select the final checkpoint based on validation performance. Training details and backbone-specific settings are provided in Appendices B and D.1. 

Evaluation benchmark. We construct an evaluation benchmark covering 11 monitoring cate-gories, with each category evaluated at both fine and coarse granularity. In total, the benchmark contains 32,222 Factor-conditioned queries over 15,971 reasoning trajectories. Detailed benchmark composition, supervision, and trajectory provenance are provided in Appendix C.1. 

Evaluation protocol. Following Guan et al. (Guan et al., 2025), we use G-Mean 2 = TPR × TNR 

for standard binary monitoring, where TPR and TNR denote the true-positive and true-negative rates. For each monitor, we select a single decision threshold using validation predictions and keep it fixed across evaluation categories. Further metric and protocol details are provided in Appendix C.3. 6Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 4.2 EVALUATION ACROSS MONITORING FACTORS 

We start by evaluating OwlSight across all 11 monitoring categories. We compare our monitor with prompted general-purpose LLMs and dedicated guard models on the same evaluation benchmark. Comparison with general-purpose LLMs. OwlSight outperforms all evaluated general-purpose LLM baselines in macro-average G-Mean 2 at both fine and coarse granularities. It achieves macro G-Mean 2 of 69.57 (fine) and 66.80 (coarse), leading on 7 of 11 categories at fine granularity and 6 of 11 at coarse granularity. It ranks first at both granularities across five categories spanning all three monitoring domains: Instruction Following, Sycophancy, Hallucination, Privacy, and Jail-break. At fine granularity, its macro-average is only marginally higher than that of the strongest general-purpose baseline, Qwen3.8-Flash (69.57 vs. 68.25). This is expected: when the monitor-ing condition is fully specified, the task more closely resembles pattern matching, which general-purpose LLMs handle well. Even so, our 4B model still outperforms this much larger baseline. At coarse granularity, the margin is substantially larger (66.80 vs. 55.09), further demonstrating the effectiveness of OwlSight for CoT monitoring. These results demonstrate that OwlSight combines strong monitoring performance with a compact 4B model size, making it a lightweight alternative to substantially larger general-purpose LLM monitors. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduce OwlSight, a shared objective-conditioned CoT monitor that uses natural-language Fac-tors to specify diverse monitoring targets at fine and coarse granularity. Across 11 monitoring cat-egories, OwlSight achieves strong aggregate performance against general-purpose LLM and guard-model baselines, while tracking intervention-induced behavioral shifts and transferring to reasoning traces from unseen models. It responds consistently to changes in monitoring objectives, remains stable under minor Factor rephrasings, and retains monitoring capability under CoT compression and obfuscation. For long reasoning trajectories, windowed MIL preserves full-trace coverage with-out hard truncation while substantially reducing memory use. Overall, these results show that a single Factor-conditioned monitor can support flexible reasoning oversight across heterogeneous monitoring objectives. 

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work studies chain-of-thought monitoring for AI oversight. Several benchmarks used in our experiments contain safety-relevant or potentially harmful behaviors, including deception, reward hacking, sabotage, jailbreaks, and privacy-related behavior. These examples are used only for offline monitoring research; our experiments do not deploy harmful actions against real users or third-party systems. We recognize that analyses of monitoring failures or obfuscated reasoning may have dual-use implications by informing attempts to evade monitoring. We therefore reproduce only minimal excerpts needed for qualitative analysis and will respect the licenses and redistribution restrictions of all third-party datasets in any data release. Human review was performed by members of the research team as part of dataset quality control; no external human subjects were recruited and reviewer responses were not treated as research out-comes. The monitor is intended as one component of an oversight system rather than a standalone safety mechanism or substitute for human judgment. All authors have read and agree to adhere to the ICLR Code of Ethics.

Applied and Not Applied . The data pipeline maintains both fine-grained and coarse-grained Factors, corresponding to the two levels of monitoring granularity used throughout the paper. Reasoning-trace supervision uses a shared semantic-judge profile based on 

deepseek-v4-flash . The judge runs deterministically ( temperature = 0, top p = 1)with thinking disabled and a maximum output length of 2,048 tokens. The production configuration uses eight workers, a 60-second timeout, and one retry. Annotation is fail-closed: outputs without valid visible content or with invalid reasoning fields are rejected. Judge outputs are structured as JSON and include the binary Applied /Not Applied judgment, a con-fidence level, and supporting evidence identifiers referring to numbered CoT lines. Evidence must come from the reasoning trace rather than the task context or final response. Most trace-supervised categories retain only high-confidence annotations; unresolved cases are rejected or escalated. A second adjudication profile based on GPT-5.4 is used for selected difficult boundaries rather than as the default labeler. It is used for targeted Sabotage adjudication, selected Reward Hacking bound-ary cases, and HARP attribution review. Human and owner review is applied selectively according to the source: • Deception: a full authoritative overlay is applied to the reviewed queue, with explicit re-view of hard-negative boundaries. • Sabotage: difficult boundary cases receive secondary adjudication, with owner review used for exceptional disagreements. • Reasoning Method: composite-path examples are audited against the source attribution contract. • Skill Use: dual-view consensus labels are supplemented by stratified audit of constructed negatives. • Instruction Following: CoT-based judge annotations are retained only as diagnostic meta-data for the active MIL-11 artifact; the authoritative training label is the final-outcome verifier. 20 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 • Hallucination, Privacy, and Jailbreak: imported CoT application labels are supple-mented by targeted manual semantic review. • Sycophancy and Sandbagging: controlled behavioral outcomes, rather than a CoT judge, provide the authoritative training labels. This separation between Factor construction, supervision, and audit ensures that model-generated annotations do not implicitly define the Factor they are later asked to label. A.6 LEAKAGE CONTROL AND DATA SPLITS 

We separate Factor construction, labeling, and monitor input through explicit visibility contracts. Factor construction does not receive application labels, final responses, rewards, or environmen-tal outcomes. It also does not receive evidence spans from the trajectory. The trajectory-induced Reward Hacking discovery stage is the sole exception to direct CoT visibility: it may inspect un-labeled reasoning traces to discover candidate mechanisms, but the subsequent grounding stage is context-only and the final Factor may not copy trace-specific evidence. Reasoning-trace-based judges do not receive upstream labels, success buckets, rewards, or verifier outputs as label hints. OwlSight receives only the task context, Factor, and complete reasoning trace: Monitor Input = C + O + R. 

Final responses, tool results, and environmental outcomes are retained only in their declared super-vision or diagnostic channels. Related examples are grouped using a deterministic leakage group id before splitting. The final split has zero leakage-group overlap between train and validation, train and holdout, and vali-dation and holdout. Group definitions are source-specific: examples from the same HARP problem, skill instance, IF base task, deception scenario, sandbagging problem, and analogous source-level units remain in the same partition. Matched intervention conditions also preserve a shared monitoring target where required. For exam-ple, different Sandbagging pressure conditions associated with the same task share the same Factor definition, and Deception pressure variants from the same scene share the same task-specific Factor. Training and evaluation primarily rely on source-level separation: HARP is separate from the Ope-nAI Process evaluation, SRA-Bench from SkillEval, Aries DeceptionBench from MASK, Con-trolArena from SHADE-Arena, and the training Sycophancy sources from SycoBench-600. B.5 LONG -C OT W INDOW CONSTRUCTION 

The training-time endpoint budget is 33,024 tokens. A trajectory enters the MIL path whenever any associated monitoring query exceeds this budget. Window capacity depends on the non-CoT portion of the serialized input. For each query, the available capacity for the windowed reasoning trace is 

C = 33 ,024 − | serialized input with the reasoning trace removed | .

The task context, Factor, relation, criteria, and decision query are therefore repeated across windows, while the reasoning trace is partitioned. Adjacent windows use a target overlap of 6,144 tokens and jointly cover the complete source trace without gaps. No source tokens are truncated. A long trajectory may produce an arbitrary number of covering windows, but training forwards at most four windows per trajectory. At the beginning of training, these windows are selected at approximately the start, one-third, two-thirds, and end of the trace. Once cached window scores become available, later epochs select two high-scoring windows together with two exploration win-dows chosen from under-visited positions. Training therefore evaluates at most four windows per long trajectory, whereas evaluation scores all covering windows. For standard binary monitoring evaluations, the positive class corresponds to Applied . Given a decision threshold τ , we compute 

TPR( τ ) = TP TP + FN , TNR( τ ) = TN TN + FP ,

and report 

G-Mean 2(τ ) = TPR( τ )TNR( τ ).

The predicted class is Applied whenever the monitor score is at least τ . Evaluation units containing only one class are not assigned a G-Mean 2 value. 

Threshold selection. Each trained monitor uses a single model-specific threshold selected from its validation predictions. We enumerate all distinct validation scores, together with endpoints imme-diately below the minimum and above the maximum score, and select the threshold that maximizes balanced accuracy: 

BA( τ ) = TPR( τ ) + TNR( τ )2 .

Ties are resolved first in favor of the threshold closest to 0.5 and then in favor of the larger threshold. The threshold is selected from pooled validation queries across all 11 training categories. Fine-grained and coarse-grained Factors share the same threshold, and no category-specific threshold tuning is performed. Once selected, the threshold is fixed for evaluation. Long-context window aggregation is not separately recalibrated and uses the same threshold selected from the validation predictions. 27 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 

Main-table aggregation. The main paper reports macro averages over the 11 monitoring cate-gories, giving each category equal weight. The stress test in Section 4.7 is motivated by a broader limitation of CoT-based oversight: moni-torability depends on relevant evidence remaining exposed in the observable reasoning trace. Prior work has shown that CoTs do not necessarily verbalize all factors influencing a model’s behavior (Chen et al., 2025), that optimization pressure can induce obfuscated reasoning (Baker et al., 2025; 34 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 Hadida et al., 2026), and that shorter CoTs may preserve task performance while altering properties relevant to oversight (Zeng et al., 2026). We operationalize two corresponding stress conditions. Semantic compression tests robustness when evidence becomes more sparse, whereas semantic obfuscation tests robustness when behavior-relevant evidence remains present but becomes less explicit. D.4.1 TRANSFORMATION PROTOCOL 

We consider two post-hoc transformations: 

Semantic compression. Compression evaluates the effect of reducing reasoning length while pre-serving the original solution process. Unlike truncation, the transformation uses abstractive rewriting to remove redundant explanations and non-essential transitions while preserving the final answer, factual relations, numerical constraints, and load-bearing reasoning dependencies. The transformed CoT is generated by DeepSeek-v4-flash using a constrained rewriting procedure. The compression ratio is sampled independently for each trajectory, and the transformed output is validated to ensure that the answer and key reasoning dependencies are preserved. 

Semantic obfuscation. Obfuscation evaluates whether the monitor relies on explicit surface cues in the reasoning trace. We use semantic camouflage, which rewrites the reasoning presentation while preserving the original task meaning, answer, and causal dependencies. The transformation removes explicit factor-related cues and uses more neutral wording without introducing new information or changing the reasoning outcome. For both transformations, the same context, monitoring objective, and labels are retained. Threshold selection is unchanged, and no transformed examples are used for calibration. D.4.2 EVALUATION CONDITIONS 

Each evaluation sample is evaluated under three conditions: • Original : the canonical reasoning trace. • Compression-only : the reasoning trace after semantic compression. • Obfuscation-only : the reasoning trace after semantic camouflage. The same monitor and scoring protocol are used across all conditions. Binary evaluations use the standard frozen-threshold G-Mean 2 metric. Intervention and Process evaluations continue to use their corresponding specialized scoring adapters. D.4.3 DETAILED RESULTS 

Table 23 reports category-level results. Each category is evaluated separately for fine-grained and coarse-grained objectives. The results reveal heterogeneous robustness patterns across monitoring objectives. Compression generally preserves monitoring performance, although larger degradation appears for objectives such as Privacy and Sabotage, where relevant evidence may depend on detailed reasoning structure. Obfuscation produces more variable effects. While many categories remain stable or slightly im-prove after transformation, sycophancy detection shows a substantial degradation, suggesting that semantic camouflage can remove cues used by the monitor for certain behavior types. These results indicate that CoT transformation robustness depends on the relationship between the transformation and the evidence required for a specific monitoring objective.
