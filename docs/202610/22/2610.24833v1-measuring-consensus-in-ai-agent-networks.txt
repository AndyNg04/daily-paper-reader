# Measuring Consensus in AI Agent Networks 

Jin Gao 

Independent Researcher, USA gaojin@alum.mit.edu 

Abstract —As AI agents become increasingly connected through networks and take on tasks that require negotiation and joint decision-making, a systematic approach is needed to assess how their views converge and what information supports their deci-sions. We present an evaluation framework that treats consensus formation as the evolution of publicly stated positions toward a shared region, using accumulated communication history as context. The framework tracks position trajectories, group dis-persion, and information expressed in public messages. Two case studies examine staffing decisions based on distributed facts and decisions requiring agents to reconcile duplicate information and a cancelled contract. Results from 1,872 completed runs associate sharing explanations with more accurate group decisions, while the second task reveals persistent disagreement despite accurate aggregate answers. Membership-change experiments provide a complementary evaluation. By separating convergence among agents from progress toward a task’s full-information answer, the framework relates decision accuracy and bounded disagreement to the information available during the exchange. 

Index Terms —agent networks, consensus, evaluation, commu-nication history, membership changes 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Networked AI agents exchange information and proposals to address shared problems, such as planning staffing from facts held by different participants. As the discussion develops, their stated positions may move closer together, remain divided, or change direction when new information is shared. Evaluating this process calls for tracking both the evolution of group positions and the information expressed during the exchange. Research on agreement protocols [1], [2], [3], LLM-agent coordination [4], and distributed information use [5] provides complementary perspectives on these questions. We bring these perspectives together in an evaluation framework that follows consensus formation through the accumulated history of public communication. Classical agreement protocols coordinate distributed pro-cesses through rule-based updates to scalar or vector val-ues [1], [3]; applications of approximate agreement include clock synchronization and stabilization of sensor readings [1]. We study networks of autonomous LLM agents that generate proposals and revise their stated positions through language-based interaction. Our focus is on consensus states and the process through which they form. A consensus state describes publicly stated positions occupying a shared region within a task-defined tolerance. Consensus formation describes how these positions evolve through successive exchanges, inter-preted in the context of accumulated public communication. The framework tracks both the distribution of positions and their evolution alongside the information expressed in the ex-change. In our case studies, a full-information answer provides a separate benchmark for evaluating the group’s outcome. This paper presents an evaluation framework for describing consensus states and tracing their formation through pub-licly expressed positions and information. We examine the framework through two case studies of distributed-information decisions, complemented by membership-change experiments with LLM agents deployed on a Docker network. Together, these studies connect the measurement of group convergence with the information exchanged during deliberation. II. R ELATED WORK 

(1) Distributed information. Studies examine how groups use information held by different participants, including Hid-denBench [5], SILO-BENCH [6], and InfoDelphi [7]. (2) Opinion dynamics. Studies examine how positions and claims evolve during interaction, including Hao et al. [8] on stance convergence, DEBATE [9] on opinion dynamics, and TIDE [10] on misinformation propagation. (3) Numerical agreement. Studies formalize agreement and validity condi-tions for scalar or vector values, including Dolev et al. [1] on approximate agreement, Mendes and Herlihy [2] on multi-dimensional agreement, and Vaidya and Garg [3] on vector consensus. Our evaluation focuses on consensus states and their formation in networked LLM agents, relating publicly stated positions to accumulated communication history. III. E VALUATION FRAMEWORK 

The framework takes agents’ communication histories as in-put and produces measures of consensus states and trajectories of position changes (fig. 1). Its inputs include agents’ stated positions and explanations, delivery records, and membership updates. An observer follows one decision scope through these records; the accumulated exchange supplies the context for interpreting changes in the group’s positions. At each stage of an exchange, the observer represents agents’ publicly stated positions in a common, task-defined decision space. For vector-valued tasks, each position lies in Rd, where the task specifies the dimensions and their meaning. The positions observed at that stage form the group’s distribution. We measure its spread as the mean distance of these positions to their centroid, describing how closely the group is clustered. For vector-valued positions, we compare trajectories with two regions defined by the initial positions: (1) the coordinate-wise box, formed by the minimum and maximum on each dimension; and (2) the convex hull, containing all convex combinations of the initial positions [2], [3], [11]. C ASE STUDIES AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We use two staffing tasks to examine information pooling and the interpretation of related facts. 

Information pooling. We examine the framework through a staffing task with distributed information. A shared brief specifies the workload and per-worker capacity for three roles, from which agents compute a baseline staffing vector. Each of four agents receives one private fact about the following week and adjusts its initial estimate accordingly. Combining all task facts yields the full-information answer x∗, used by the evaluator as a scoring benchmark. For some roles, all facts shift demand in the same direction, placing x∗ outside the range of initial estimates; for others, averaging those estimates already gives a close answer. Each fact contains a distinctive numerical marker, allowing its disclosure to be tracked in public messages. Agents report staffing estimates in workers per shift, providing three-dimensional positions whose trajectories can be followed throughout the exchange. Agents are never told x∗.

Fact reconciliation. The second task uses the same staffing setting but requires agents to interpret relationships between facts. Six agents hold facts that include two descriptions of the same contract in different units, and an added contract together with its cancellation. Computing the full-information benchmark requires counting the duplicate contract once and applying the cancellation while retaining the contributions of other facts. 

Outcome measures. Progress is measured along the line from the initial group mean (0) to the full-information answer 

x∗ (1); the centroid summarizes the group’s trajectory. The task specifies the coordinate-wise median as the final decision. We record median reach when this decision is within one worker of x∗ on every coordinate. The joint accuracy-and-agreement criterion requires both median reach and a member-to-member range of at most one worker on every coordinate, using the members present in the final round. This distin-guishes an accurate aggregate decision from accurate decisions accompanied by bounded disagreement. Message conditions. We vary the information shared with peers, separately from the model’s reasoning-effort setting. Peers receive each member’s vector with its publicly stated explanation ( reasoning ), the vector alone ( numbers ), or the vector with a fixed sentence containing no task facts ( filler ). Both tasks compare all three conditions, with reconciliation filler added in the supplementary experiment. Filler tests whether adding fixed text without task facts affects agents’ responses; it is not length-matched to explanations. Members are told each holds a fact and that the decision is a median; they are never asked to agree. Numerical positions can encode private facts through their difference from the shared baseline, so numbers-only retains inferable information. 

Observation settings. The full record provides all reported positions and actual arrivals. The monitor uses sent mes-sages, delivery acknowledgments, and membership records. The transcript-only setting assumes every message arrived and carries forward silent members’ last positions. These settings distinguish information availability and membership assumptions. Results use the full record; comparative observer-error evaluation remains future work. Our implementation evaluates agents communicating over a Docker network. Each agent runs in a separate container and accesses its model through an API. Agents exchange messages using the standard A2A protocol via HTTP, with task data carried in standard data parts and request signatures provided by a declared extension. A coordinator schedules rounds, while logs retain each agent’s inputs and outputs, delivery acknowledgments, and membership changes for evaluation. 

Models and repeats. The design comprises two model families and three tiers: GPT-5.6 Luna, Terra, and Sol, and Claude Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus 5.5. Each task uses six models, three message conditions, and eight fixed instances with five repeats, giving 720 runs per task. Reconciliation adds 160 Sol/Opus explanation/numbers runs and 240 six-model filler runs to the original 320. Exchanges last ten rounds. Low reasoning effort is requested throughout; provider routes, resolved models, runtime overrides, and retries remain in the audit records. Membership experiments vary arrivals, departures, and restarts, including whether changes are announced or silent. The matrix contains six models, nine conditions, and eight instances, giving 432 runs with one repeat per cell. Exit conditions distinguish the departing member’s explanations from numbers-only messages. Within-cell repeat variability is not estimated. Together, the original 1,472 and supplementary 400 runs yield 1,872 valid results after bounded retries; invalid attempts are excluded and separately retained. V. R ESULTS

Convergence process. Replaying all 1,440 task runs yields 14,400 round records and 64,800 member transitions. Under explanations, pooling joint successes rise from 88/240 (36.7%) at round 2 to 220/240 (91.7%) at round 10; reconciliation rises from 75/240 (31.3%) to 92/240 (38.3%). Of 193 reconciliation explanation runs ever accurate, 37 (19.2%) later lose accuracy at least once, including recoveries. Pooling explanations yield 7,522/8,640 (87.1%) unchanged transitions. Among the 1,118 moves, 576 (51.5%) approach only the text-derived target, 43 (3.8%) only a peer target, 475 (42.5%) both, and 24 (2.1%) neither. Final outcomes thus hide intermediate reversals and ambiguous movements. Groups can agree near the full-information answer even after a member leaves. With a departing member’s explana-tions visible, joint successes were 41/48 for silent departures and 32/48 for matched announced departures. This descriptive, single-repeat comparison pairs the same model and instance. VI. D ISCUSSION 

Membership determines whose positions enter the assess-ment; communication history records information shared over time. A departing member’s statements remain in that history, while a new member’s access depends on delivery. Tracking both relates changes in agreement to changes in available information. Our tasks use numerical positions and computable full-information references. Open-ended decisions require a vali-dated mapping from statements to positions. Without reference answers, quality and informational support need task-specific criteria; agreement can still be assessed within the chosen representation. 

Limitations. Quantity markers do not establish under-standing or endorsement; process labels lack independent semantic validation. Our evaluation assumes trustworthy logs and membership records. Forged receipts, compromised keys, and fabricated membership records are outside the validation scope; the experiments do not establish resistance to these attacks. AI U SE DISCLOSURE
