Abstract Purpose: Radiation treatment planning requires balancing competing objectives to satisfy clinical criteria while accommodating physicians’ preferences. This study aims to develop an evidence-driven, multi-agent compound artificial intelligence (AI) system that integrates deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based automated treatment planning with large language model (LLM)-based conversational plan refinement for proton pencil beam scanning (PBS) therapy. 

Methods and Materials: In the first stage, a policy network trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) generates multiple treatment plans without human interven-tion, from which one is selected as the target plan and the others serve as references for subsequent refinements. For challenging cases requiring further adjustments according to physicians’ instructions, an LLM-based multi-agent system enables plan refinement through natural language interactions. The system comprises a planning agent that recommends evidence-supported adjustments, a chatbot providing a unified conversational interface, and an evaluation agent that retrieves clinical metrics and criteria and identifies constraint viola-tions. To enhance domain-specific reasoning and decision-making, the planning agent, based on Qwen3-14B, is designed to justify its recommendations using evidence from previously generated plans and post-trained on task-specific planning data using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The framework was trained and evaluated using 21 patients with head and neck (H&N) cancers, with five PPO-generated

1. Introduction 

Radiotherapy is a conerstone of cancer treatment worldwide, and the quality of a treat-ment plan can directly influence tumor control, treatment-related toxicity, and ultimately patients’ survival and quality of life. Consequently, treatment planning has received con-siderable attention from the radiation oncology community. The planning process can be formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem, in which human planners iteratively adjust planning objectives and constraints for target volumes and surrounding organs at risk (OARs) to achieve an appropriate balance among competing clinical goals. This task becomes particularly challenging when OARs are in close proximity to, abut, or overlap with the tumor, making trade-offs between target coverage and normal-tissue sparing unavoidable. In clinical practice, treatment plans often undergo multiple rounds of refinement before final approval, extending the planning process over several days. Such delays are undesirable because patient anatomy may change during this interval, potentially reducing the accuracy 2of dose delivery in a treatment modality that relies on precise spatial targeting. In addition, plan quality can vary substantially with factors such as planner experience, available planning time, and the capabilities of commercial treatment planning systems (TPSs). Suboptimal trade-offs among competing objectives may compromise either tumor coverage or normal-tissue sparing. Together, these challenges have created a strong need for efficient and reliable methods that can consistently generate high-quality treatment plans, thereby motivating the rapid development of automated treatment planning. In last decades, automated treatment planning has been rapidly developed from knowledge-based planning (KBP) [1, 2] and multi-criteria optimization (MCO) [3] to virtual plan-ners built with deep reinforcement learning (DRL) [4, 5, 6, 7] and large language models (LLMs) [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. KBP estimates dose-volume histogram (DVH) curve bands from historical data for new patients by mathcing their anatomy to previous cases. MCO gener-ates alternative plans by navigating the trade-off space along the approximate Pareto sur-face. However, the former collapses three-dimensional anatomy into two-dimensional DVH outputs, resulting in inverse optimization converging to inferior local minima and the ne-cessity of mannual plan adjustments. The latter demands considerable manual effort on base plan generation, Pareto surface exploration and final plan selection [12]. Both solutions fail to convert the time-consuming and expertise-demand treatment planning into desirable automatic process. Recently, DRL, a powerful approach to decision-making, has been in-creasingly explored for automated treatment planning. DRL-based methods typically enable policy networks adjustment intelligence through a trial-and-error learning process, using algo-rithms such as Q-learning [4], Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) [5], and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [6, 7], etc. Although challenges remain in generalizing across diverse treatement sites and formulating reward functions that quantitatively capture the multifactorial assessment of plan quality, these methods have demonstrated promising progress and offer a viable approach to automated treatment planning. Recent years have also witnessed a surge in LLM-based applications in healthcare, particularly in high-stakes clinical decision-making tasks such as open-ended medical diagnostics [13, 14, 15] and multidisciplinary treatment planning [16]. Among these applications, radiotherapy treatment planning has emerged as an active area of research. For example, Nusrat et al. [9] proposed the Dose Optimiza-3tion Language Agent (DOLA) for autonomous prostate radiatherapy planning, integrating chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and reinforce-ment learning (RL) with Llama 3.1. Liu et al. [10] developed GPT-RadPlan for volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) planning, leveraging the multimodal model GPT-4V for plan evaluation and objective adjustment through in-context learning. Wei et al. [8] em-ployed prompt engineering to guide LLMs in adjusting optimization parameters for cervical cancer VMAT planning, incorporating feedback from medical physicists to improve plan-ning decisions. More recently, Maniscalco et al. [12] introduced a compound AI framework combining seven specialized LLM agents with a directive-conditioned 3D dose predictor. Through a structured multi-agent pipeline, these agents navigated the multi-objective opti-mization landscape by collaboratively performing clinical knowledge retrieval, DVH analysis, spatial dose assessment, plan compilation, quality assurance and tool execution. Collectively, these studies highlight the growing potential of LLMs to support complex planning decisions and advance automated radiotherapy treatment planning. Although DRL and LLMs have significantly pushed the boundaries of automated treat-ment planning, DRL-based methods lack the flexibility to incorporate human instructions, while LLM-based approaches remain constrained by the limited domain-specific reasoning capabilities of base LLMs. Previous studies have demonstrated that existing LLMs may gen-erate guideline-inconsistent or potentially harmful clinical recommendations [17, 18, 19]. Al-though their outputs can provide complementary insights and support personalized decision-making, their treatment recommendations in precision oncology have not yet matched the quality and credibility of those made by human experts. Our experiments also indicate that, when given high-level instructions such as “improve [OAR] sparing without compro-mising [TARGET VOLUME] coverage”, or “reduce hot spots within [TARGET VOLUME] while maintaining its V100% above 92%”, base LLMs often recommend overly aggressive or ineffective adjustments, failing to appropriately balance improvements in the intended clin-ical objectives against potential deterioration in competing objectives. Moreover, medical LLM agents remain limited in their ability to dynamically adapt to changing patient con-ditions, further undermining their reliability in generating patient-specific treatment plans. Another limitation is that existing LLM-based agentic planning frameworks primarily focus 4on automated initial plan generation, overlooking the time-consuming iterative paln refine-ment guided by physician-planner communication, as illustrated in Figure 1. This stage is particularly challenging when competing clinical objectives cannot be simultaneously sat-isfied, and physicians prioritize different objectives when making trade-offs. Furthermore, existing LLM-based automated plannig methods have primarily focused on photon-based radiotherapym such as VMAT and IMRT, while proton therapy planning remains relatively underexplored. In this work, we propose a multi-agent compound AI system for proton pencil beam scanning (PBS) radiotherapy planning. Specifically, a DRL-based policy network is first employed to automatically generate multiple treatment plans without human intervention. Subsequently, a conversational planning platform comprising three agents is developed to enable interactive plan refinement through natural language instructions. In particular, the planning agent is post-trained using SFT and GRPO to recommend patient-specific ad-justments supported by evidence derived from previously generated plans, enhancing the reliability and interpretability of its recommendations. Compared with the base LLM, the post-trained model demonstrates improved instruction-following, information retrieval and clinical reasoning capabilities, enabeling more effective plan adjustments aligned with physi-cians’ preferences while maintaining reasonable trade-offs among competing clinical objec-tives. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first compound AI framework specifically designed for proton PBS treatment planning that integrates automated initial plan genera-tion with interactive, physician-guided plan refinement. 52. Methods and Materials 

2.1. Patient Cohorts 

This study was conducted under approval from the institutional review board of Univer-sity of California San Diego. We retrospectively selected 21 bilateral or ipsilateral H&N cases treated at the California Protons Cancer Therapy Center between 2017 and 2024. Across these cases, treatment courses consisted of 5–60 fractions delivered twice daily, with no more than five beams per plan. Each case contained one to four prescription levels spanning 4.0– 74.2 Gy. Rather than redesigning the clinical setup, we retained the original prescriptions, beam angles, target definitions, and field-specific targets from the delivered plans. More-over, following Wang et al. [7], proton spots were arranged on a hexagonal grid and dose was calculated using the Monte Carlo simulation. To ensure machine deliverability, the beam energy constraint of 70-240MeV and the minimal monitor units (MUs) of 3-300MUs per spot are enforced in inverse optimization. 

2.2. Automatic Treatment Planning Framework for Proton PBS 

We perform treatment planning with the system depicted in Figure 2, where an initial planning module built with an adjustment policy network is employed to automatically produce plans for uploaded patient cases without human intervention. The policy network is trained with the PPO framework proposed in Wang’s previous work [7]. Moreover, the advanced learn-to-optimize (L2O) inverse optimization engine designed in work [7] is used as the backend of the planning system given its promissing effectiveness and efficiency. For easy cases, the initial planning module is likely to produce clinically accepted plans, while for challenging cases that physicians may give further refine instructions to, an LLM-based multi-agent planning module is designed to adjust a selected plan by reasoning over prior plans generated in the initial planning stage. 2.3. LLM-based Multi-agent Planning System 

Physicians have different treatment preferences as they prioritize different objectives, thereby a conversational interface that enables human-in-the-loop planning is crucial. Recent developments in LLM-based agents provide a promising paradigm for facilitating nature language interaction between physicians and automated planning systems. Here, we propose an LLM-based multi-agent planning system as shown in Figure 2. Specifically, the system consists of three collaborative agents: a chatbot agent, an evaluation agent and a planning agent. The first two agents are prompted GPT models, while the planning agent is a post-trained Qwen3-14B model. Functionally, the chatbot agent serves as the only user interface, responsible for answering general questions on radiation oncology and routing corresponding queries to other agents, e.g., routing high-level plan refine instructions and specific plan adjusting commands to the planner agent, and those relevant to clinical metrics, clinical criteria and constraint violations to the evaluation agent. The evaluation agent is designed to look up clinical requirements, evaluate plan qualities and check constraint violations by retrieving information from the memory module or calling pre-defined APIs. It also compares plans before and after adjustment and provides feedback to the planner agent. The planning 7agent is post-trained to adjust plans according to user’s queries. For specific adjusting commands like “reset objective parameters to [SPECIFIC VALUES]”, it invokes APIs directly and then confirms with the user. After being confirmed, the inverse optimization agent will be engaged to generate new plans. For high-level instructions like “improve sparing of [OAR NAME]”, the planning agent recommends adjustments and a sequence of API calls that implement proposed adjustments by following a predefined planning workflow. It reasons over the objective-metric mapping of prior plans and justifies its recommendations with reliable evidences. The system is empowered with a memory module and a set of API tools. The mem-ory module stores patient information, clinical criteria and plan’s objective-metric mapping information for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), while the API tools implement operations including DVH metric calculating, objective modifying, plan optimizing, etc for the evaluation and planning agents. As mentioned above, pre-trained LLMs may produce semantically meaningful adjustment recommendations, but the resulting plans may not be clinically acceptable because the simulated dose distributions frequently show unexpected degradations on competing objectives. Therefore, in this work, Qwen3-14B is post-trained on our planning-specific dataset to align its behavior with clinical plan-adjustment practices, thereby reducing the number of adjustment iterations and, consequently, LLM inference costs and overall planning time. Moreover, widely used LLM evaluation metrics are not directly applicable to our task because recommended adjustments that appear semantically correct may still be either too aggressive or too conservative to effectively produce a clinically acceptable plan. Instead, the performance of a planning agent should be evaluated based on the final simulation results of the adjusted plan, taking into account both the improvement of the targeted objectives specified in the user’s instruction and the degradation of competing objectives that may not be explicitly mentioned. In addition, a single user instruction may contain conflicting goals that cannot be simultaneously satisfied. Therefore, the reasonableness of the trade-offs among targeted objectives should also be considered in the evaluation. 82.4. Post-training of the Planning Agent 

When user proposes specific adjusting commands like “reset the limit value of the [OB-JECTIVE] for [OAR NAME] to [VALUE]”, the planning agent is allowed to invoke API calls directly without reasoning. However, when high-level instructions are given, the planning agent is prompted to follow a planning pipeline as below to recommend justified adjustments: 1. Retrieve the objectives and corresponding clinical metrics from memory for all target volumes in the current plan; 2. Retrieve the objectives and corresponding clinical metrics from memory for all targeted OARs and critical OARs whose clinical metrics fail to meet or are close to failing the clinical requirements; 3. Compare the clinical metrics of the current plan and with those of prior plans, and select a prior plan that better reflects the user’s intended trade-off while causing less degradation in competing objectives as the reference plan; 4. Retrive the objectives and corresponding clinical metrics from memory for all target volumes, all targeted OARs and critical OARs in the selected reference plan; 5. Recommend adjustments by comparing the objectives and clinical metrics of the cur-rent plan and the reference plan to satisfy user’s intent, and suggest API calls that implement above adjustments; 6. Output above API calls as the final response and ask the user to confirm proposed API calls. The first several steps are placed within a reasoning block enclosed by the “<think>...</think>” tag, and the last output step ensures that any modification to the current plan is confirmed by the user before execution. We collect training data using manually created queries and ground-truth responses, and post-train the Qwen3-14B thinking model using SFT and GRPO. SFT teaches the model what a good response looks like, while GRPO further optimizes which response is better in a group. Given GPU memory constraints, the widely used parameter-efficient fine-tuning 9method Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is employed for both training stages. Moreover, the LLM-as-a-judge strategy is used to estimate rewards for the generated responses in GRPO. 

2.4.1. Data Collection 

For each of the 21 cases, we generate five plans using the PPO-trained policy network from the initial planning stage. One plan is designated as the current plan, while the remaining plans are used as prior plans for reference. To improve the robustness of the LLM-based plannig agent by increasing the diversity of the training data, we select up to three different plans as the current plan for each case, and for each selected plan, we manually create one user query as plan-refinement instruction, resulting in a total of 50 training samples (42 for training and 8 for evaluation). For each sample, the ground-truth adjustment is first generated using a GPT model, which is prompted to recommend plan adjustments following the pipeline described above. For a small-scale, domain-specific training dataset, even a few low-quality samples can sub-stantially affect model performance during post-training. Therefore, we carefully verify the correctness of the retrieved information and manually revise the generated response for each sample. In particular, we select appropriate reference plans and refine the recommended adjustments to ensure that they are consistent with the clinical planning practices of human planners. 

2.4.2. Supervised Fine-tuning 

The supervised fine-tunning (SFT) stage aims to improve the model’s instruction-following and format-following capabilities, enabling the planning agent to reason over prior plans ac-cording to the pipeline described above and recommend appropriate adjustments that refine the current plan toward user’s intent. The think block incorporates retrieved objective set-tings and corresponding clinical metrics of the current and the reference plans, providing explicit evidence to support the recommended adjustments. By imitating well-structured expert planning behavior during SFT, the planning agent learns to retrieve more accurate and complete information, produce more consistent outputs and reduce invalid API calls, establish a strong initialization for subsequent reinforcement learning. The base LLM is fine-tuned for 10 epochs with a learning rate of 5×10 −5 and batch size of 10 4 using the VeRL framework [20]. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [21], a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that learns compact low-rank updates to selected weight matrices while keeping the original model parameters frozen, is employed to enable efficient task-specific adaptation while largely preserving the general knowledge encoded in the pre-trained model. Specifically, given a pre-trained weight matrix W0 ∈ Rd×k, LoRA introduces a trainable low-rank decomposition:

2.4.3. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) 

LLMs acquire substantial knowledge of oncology and treatment planning from large-scale pre-training corpora, and their strong reasoning capabilities enable them to recommend ad-justments in generally appropriate directions. However, treatment planning is inherently case-sensitive. For example, both increasing the priority of an OAR objective and decreas-ing its dose limit value may improve OAR sparing, while different parameter values can lead to substantially different trade-offs with other competing objectives for different cases. Therefore, a key capbility of a planning agent is to determine which adjustment is better for the current case. From our experiments, despite access to prior plans as references, base LLMs may still recommend overly aggressive or ineffective adjustments. To further improve the agent’s ability to distinguish among candidate adjustments and optimize case-specific planning decisions, we continue post-training the planning agent using GRPO [22], a 11 reinforcement learning algorithm that optimizes the LLM policy without requiring a separate value model. We train the supervised fine-tuned Qwen3-14B model using GRPO for 3 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5 and a batch size of 2. The reward ri for each generated response yi is assigned by an LLM-as-a-Judge built on GPT-5.6, which evaluates the consistency between the generated response and the corresponding ground-truth response by estimating the clinical effects of their proposed API calls rather than relying on superficial similarity in wording. Specifically, the judge is instructed to evaluate consistency across four aspects: the adjusted structures, retrieved prescription doses, adjustment directions, and 12 adjustment magnitudes, and to assign each generated response a score between 0 and 1. By leveraging a strong language model as a judge for this difficult-to-verify planning task, complex outputs can be efficiently evaluated at scale while maintaining strong alignment with human judgments. 

2.5. API Tools and Memory 

The tool-use capabilities of LLMs have substantially extended their problem-solving ca-pacity beyond text generation. In this work, we define a set of APIs that enable the evaluation agent to assess clinical plan metrics and the planning agent to modify planning objectives. 

For the evaluation agent: 

• api_evaluator_compute_DMax/DMin/DMean(structureName): computing the maximum dose, minimal dose, and average dose of a specified structure; • api_evaluator_compute_Dx/Dcc(structureName, value): computing the Dx and Dcc metrics of a specified structure; • api_evaluator_compute_Vx(structureName, value): computing the Vx metrics for a speci-fied structure. 

For the planning agent: 

• api_planner_modify_objectives(structureName, metric, limitValue, weight): modifying the constraint or priority of an existing objective for a specified planning structure; • api_planner_inner_optimization(): running inverse optimization to calculate spot MU values and generate a new dose distribution; • api_planner_modify_Rx(targetVolumeName, newRx): modifying the prescription dose level of a specified target volume; • api_planner_dose_constraint(structureName, lower_dose, upper_dose, metric, weight, lim-itValue): cropping a subregion receiving dose in between [lower_dose, upper_dose] from an existing structure and constraint its dose by setting an objective on that subregion. This is used to detect the hot or cold areas from the target volumes or OARs; 

13 • api_planner_volume_constraint(structureName, volume, volume_percentage, metric, weight, limitValue): finding the most irradiated subvolume of a structure and constraint its dose by setting an objective on that subvolume. 

To enable the proposed multi-agent planning system to generate patient-specific re-sponses, a customized JSON-formatted memory is constructed to store treatment-related information, including beam configurations, patient information, clinical criteria, planning structures, prescriptions, and fractionation schemes. In particular, the memory stores objective-metric mappings from prior plans generated during the initial planning stage, which are cru-cial for the planning agent to infer appropriate adjustments and provide supporting evidence for its recommendations. 

3. Experiments 

We qualitatively and quantitatively compare the planning performance of the base Qwen3-14B model and our post-trained Qwen3-14B model. First, we examine the models’ natural-language outputs to assess their instruction-following, information retrieval, and reasoning capabilities. We then introduce a quantitative evaluation method to assess how well the plans adjusted by the base and post-trained Qwen3-14B models satisfy physicians’ requirements, taking into account both improvements in the targeted clinical metrics and degradation in other competing objectives. As our primary goal is to evaluate the performance gains achieved through the proposed post-training strategy, both models are provided with the same prompts and memory to ensure a fair comparison. Finally, we present a conversa-tional treatment planning system implemented using the proposed multi-agent framework to demonstrate how LLMs could reshape treatment planning workflows in the future. 

3.1. Comparison of LLM Outputs 

The LLM is instructed to recommend plan adjustments by following the predefined plan-ning procedure. The intermediate steps within the think block provide supporting evidence for the recommendation, while the final API calls must be presented to the user for confirma-tion before execution. Retrieving metrics and objectives from the current and reference plans 14 primarily tests the model’s information retrieval capability, whereas adjustment recommen-dation rely more heavily on its reasoning ability. To illustrate how these capabilities change after post-training, we present a representative example with a challenging user query. As shown in Figure 3, although the instruction explicitly prescribes the reasoning proce-dure and requires API calls to be enclosed in brackets and separated by semicolons, the base Qwen3-14B model does not consistently follow the format, and instead tends to produce responses in a format resembling its pre-training behavior. It also fails to request user con-firmation after proposing API calls and frequently omits key information during retrieval, particularly objective settings for planning structures. Information retrieval is especially challenging because each prior plan contains numerous clinical metrics and planning objec-tives, resulting in a large number of values stored in memory. The base model therefore often retrieves incorrect objective or metric values, which may further lead to hallucinated adjustment rationales. Nevertheless, the base model demonstrates basic treatment planning knowledge. It recognizes that OAR sparing can generally be improved by increasing objec-tive weights and hot spots can be surpassed by adding an additional DMax objective to the hottest subregion. However, its recommendations are often generic rather than adapted to the settings of the current and the reference plans. In Figure 3, the base model increases the objective weights of all OARs to 2.0 without considering the objective settings of prior plans. The base model can also violate explicit planning constraints. For example, the DMin objective of .CTVhd is limited to 67.32 Gy, and therefore the DMax objective for any of its subregions should exceed 67.32 Gy. This rule is explicitly stated in the instruction. However, the base model introduces a DMax objective with a limit of 66 Gy for a subregion of .CTVhd receiving doses above 66 Gy, thereby creating conflicting objectives. In contrast, the post-trained Qwen3-14B model follows the instructions substantially bet-ter. The think block and API calls are formatted as required, and the model explicitly asks the user to confirm the proposed API calls. It also correctly and completely retrieves the relevant objective and metric values from both the current and reference plans. In particular, the quality of the recommended adjustments improves substantially after post-training. 3.2. Comparison of Recommended Adjustments 

When acting as a planning agent, an LLM should be evaluated based on how effectively its recommendations improve plan quality toward the user’s intent, rather than on the semantic similarity between its natural language outputs and a ground truth response. Therefore, we apply the adjustments recommended by the LLM to the current plan, followed by inverse optimization and Monte Carlo dose calculation to generate the adjusted dose distribution. The LLM can then be evaluated using a quantitative framework that jointly considers: 1) how effectively the resulting plan improves the targeted objectives toward the physician’s intent, and 2) the extent to which these adjustments degrade other conflicting planning objectives. The comparison results of plans adjusted by the base and the post-trained Qwen3-14B are presented in Figure 4. To reflect the diversity of plans to be adjusted by the proposed planning agent, we selected multiple plans with different trade-offs for challenging patients 17 with bilateral cancer in the test set, such as Patient B25 and Patient B26. 3.3. Conversational Planning Using Proposed Multi-agent framework 

LLMs are transforming workflows across various domains, as exemplified by the rapid rise of vibe coding in software development and AI-assisted diagnosis in healthcare. These advances have the potential to improve efficiency, reduce time and labor costs, and alleviate workforce shortages. In this work, we demonstrate how LLMs faciliate treatment planning by developing a novel, user-friendly conversational planning system built upon the proposed multi-agent framework, as illustrated in Figure 5. For relatively simple cases, the initial DRL-based automatic planning module can gener-ate clinically acceptable plans with a high success rate without human intervention. Users only need to compare the generated plans and select the one that offers the best trade-offs among competing clinical objectives. For more challenging cases, users can provide addi-tional instructions to further refine the plans. Users, including physicians and dosimetrists, can instruct the planning agent to modify planning objective parameters through specific adjustment commands or request plan modifications using high-level instructions. Users 24 can also inquire about clinical criteria, treatment plan metrics, clinical constraint violations, and general oncology-related topics through natural language interactions. Overall, by mim-icking the workflow of an experienced treatment planner in both the initial and subsequent planning stages, and enabling users to efficiently refine treatment plans with the assistance of a post-trained planing agent, the proposed system has the potential to substantially reduce the workload of treatment planners and the overall planning time.

25 4. Discussion 

Rapid advances in LLMs have the potential not only to transform treatment planning workflows but also, more importantly, to improve the quality of generated treatment plans by supporting better planning decisions. To fully realize this potential, developing domain-specific LLMs tailored to treatment planning is crucial. In this work, we post-trained a planning agent for a multi-agent treatment planning framework and demonstrated the effec-tiveness of the proposed plannig pipeline and post-training stategy in enhancing the LLM’s ability to recommend patient-specific plan adjustments. By enabling users to guide plan refinement through high-level instructions, the proposed planning agent addresses a key lim-itation of existing automatic treatment planning systems: their limited ability to interact with physicians and refine treatment plans according to clinical preferences in a manner similar to human planners. Moreover, the planning agent can justify its recommendations using evidence derived from the planning objectives and clinical metrics of previously gen-erated plans, improving the interpretability and reliability of the proposed adjustments. By integrating the planning agent with a DRL-based automatic planning module capable of generating high-quality plans without human intervention, the proposed multi-agent frame-work supports both initial automatic planning and subsequent interactive plan refinement, addressing two essential stages of the clinical treatment plannig workflow. This pilot study represents an initial effort to bridge automatic plan generation and instruction-guided plan refinement. While the proposed framework explores the key compo-nents of such an integrated planning system, further development is needed to enhance its capabilities, improve user experience, and facilitate its translation into clinical practice. First, prompt-guided planning structure creation is essential for improving system flexibil-ity. In this work, the APIs api_planner_dose_constraint() and api_planner_volume_constraint() enable the planning agent to extract subregions from existing planning structures based on specified dose ranges or highly irradiated volumes. However, users may also need to create auxiliary planning structures before plan optimization through natural language instruc-tions, such as “Subtract the overlapping region between the brainstem and CTV from the brainstem”, or “Create a planning structure encompassing the portion of the brainstem clos-est to the tumor to facilitate localized dose sparing”. Wang et al [6, 7] generated auxiliary 26 planning structures using empirical rules, but more flexible, prompt-guided approaches could provide greater convenience. One possible solution is to develop a set of APIs supporting geometric and Boolean operations on anatomical structures, allowing the planning agent to sequentially execute these operations to generate the desired structures. Alternatively, the framework could be integrated with an advanced prompt-guided medical image segmentation model to enable more flexible structure generation. Second, automatic beam angle selection, an important component of treatment planning, has not been addressed in this work. Fer-rante et al [11] demonstrated the potential of pretrained vision-language models (VLMs), such as GPT-4V, for determining gantry angles in photon radiotherapy. These findings sug-gest that VLM-based approaches may also be promising for beam angle selection in proton PBS therapy. Incorporating automatic beam angle selection into the proposed framework could further improve planning automation and reduce the need for manual intervention. Third, robust optimization, a critical component of clinically acceptable proton treatment planning, was not incorporated into the current framework. One major challenge is the com-putational cost associated with repeated Monte Carlo dose calculations. GPU-accelerated Monte Carlo dose calculation offers a promising approach to improving computational ef-ficiency. Therefore, integrating GPU-accelerated Monte Carlo dose calculation and robust optimization into the proposed framework will be an important direction for future research. Finally, expanding the training dataset and incorporating explicit supervision of intermedi-ate decision-making steps, particularly reference plan selection, could further enhance the planning agent’s ability to generate accurate and consistent recommendations. 

5. Conclusion 

In this work, we developed a compound AI system integrating DRL-based automated treatment planning with multi-agent conversational plan refinement for proton PBS treat-ment planning. To enhance the decision-making capabilities of the core planning agent, we proposed a structured planning pipeline and an SFT-GRPO post-training strategy to en-able evidence-driven, patient-specific plan adjustments. Experimental results demonstrated that post-training substantially improved the agent’s instruction-following, information re-trieval, and clinical reasoning capabilities, enabling more effective adjustments aligned with 27 user intentions while maintaining reasonable trade-offs among competing clinical objectives. This study represents an initial effort to integrate automated initial planning with physician-guided interactive plan refinement for proton PBS treatment planning. Beyond enabling a more flexible and user-friendly planning workflow, our findings highlight the importance of domain-specific post-training in improving the decision-making capabilities of LLM-based planning agents. The proposed framework provides a promising foundation for developing more intelligent, interactive, and clinically adaptable agentic treatment planning systems.
