# From Content Sharing to Opinion Shaping: A Theoretical Framework for Cascade-Driven Opinion Dynamics 

Elisabetta Biondi, Chiara Boldrini, Andrea Passarella, Marco Conti 

Abstract —Online social networks (OSNs) tightly couple in-formation diffusion and opinion formation, yet most existing models treat these processes separately. Building on the Fried-kin–Johnsen on Cascades (FJC) model, which integrates opinion updating within stochastic information cascades, we advance its analytical characterization and systematic validation. We derive an exact conditional layer-wise recursion that reveals the statistical coupling between diffusion realizations and opinion evolution, showing that a closed-form recursion in unconditional expectations is not available. To overcome this limitation, we in-troduce a mean-field decoupling approximation (MFA) that yields a tractable linear recursion for the expected opinion trajectory. We also develop scalable tree-based reductions that drastically reduce computational complexity while preserving structural diffusion properties, and validate the analytical recursion and its approximations through extensive simulations. Using Stochastic Block Model networks with controlled initial opinion config-urations, we show that, unlike the classical Friedkin–Johnsen model, which typically leads to global homogenization, the FJC dynamics generate richer regimes of bounded assimilation and sustained polarization. Overall, this work bridges diffusion and influence modeling, providing a theoretical foundation for understanding how cascade-driven interactions reshape collective opinion landscapes in structured social networks. 

Index Terms —information cascades, opinion dynamics, polar-ization, Friedkin-Johnsen model, viral content, homophily, SBM 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# ONLINE social networks (OSNs) have profoundly re-shaped how individuals access information, commu-nicate, and form opinions. Platforms such as X (formerly Twitter), Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok have turned com-munication into a large-scale, real-time, and highly interac-tive process, transcending geographical and social boundaries. Nowadays, OSNs are used to share and express opinions, related to news [1], [2], review products or services [3], and even health advice [4]. For this reason, political actors, marketers, and even news organizations now complement traditional channels with posts, reshares, and user-generated content to enhance persuasion, visibility, or engagement [5], [6]. Empirical evidence shows that these processes are tightly intertwined: in a 24-hour snapshot of Twitter activity, more than half of all posts were retweets, while only one-fifth were original messages [7]. Consequently, information dissemina-tion and opinion formation are no longer separable processes: they co-occur and mutually reinforce each other within the same digital infrastructure.

Traditionally, research on opinion dynamics and information diffusion has largely developed along independent trajectories, where opinion dynamics models describe how individuals revise their beliefs under social influence, and diffusion models study how information, innovations, or behaviors propagate across networks. On the one hand, among classical opinion dynamic frameworks, the Friedkin-Johnsen (FJ) model [8] remains a cornerstone. Belonging to the family of averaging models [9], [10], it formalizes opinion evolution as an iterative weighted averaging process in which each agent updates its belief as a convex combination of its initial predisposition and the opinions of its neighbors. Due to its mathematical tractabil-ity and its empirical validation in small- to medium-scale social settings, the FJ model has gained considerable popu-larity, and has been used to explain collective wisdom [11], polarization [12], filter bubbles [13], and even negotiation processes [14]. On the other hand, the dissemination of content is often modeled as an information cascade [15], where each reshare depends on prior exposures and perceived relevance. These cascades act as vehicles through which both information and opinions travel, dynamically shaping collective beliefs. Recently, there have been efforts to blur the lines of this disciplinary separation, where information cascades are also cascades of opinions, chains through which attitudes are amplified, reshaped, or contested as they spread [16], [17]. The Friedkin–Johnsen on Cascades (FJC) model [18] is an analytical and computational framework that explicitly links information diffusion and opinion dynamics in online networks, extending the classical FJ formulation by embedding opinion updates within the temporal and structural process of an information cascade. As content spreads, each reshare transmits not only the post but also the sharer’s current opinion, allowing beliefs to evolve through exposure. Thus, diffusion becomes a mechanism of influence: opinion change propagates along the same paths as information. In [18], we analyzed the FJC model through simulations, showing that cascade-mediated exposure can drastically alter classical FJ dynamics by amplifying the influence of central nodes and reshaping polarization patterns observed in the classical FJ. However, a rigorous analytical characterization of cascade-driven opinion trajectories has remained open. In this paper, we provide such a characterization. First, we derive an exact conditional layer-wise recursion that explicitly reveals the statistical coupling between diffusion realizations and opinion evolution. This result shows that, in general, a closed recursion involving only unconditional expectations arXiv:2610.19867v1 [cs.SI] 14 Oct 2026 2

cannot be obtained. Second, to achieve analytical tractability, we introduce a mean-field decoupling approximation (MFA) that neglects the diffusion–opinion correlation at the expecta-tion level. This approximation yields a closed linear recursion for the expected opinion trajectory, enabling scalable com-putation and theoretical analysis. Importantly, we empirically validate the accuracy of this analytical recursion through extensive simulations of the full stochastic process. We further develop scalable tree-based reductions that drastically lower computational complexity while preserving the structural prop-erties of diffusion, and we validate their performance against both the analytical model and simulations. Finally, we extend the analysis of FJC and study its behavior in networks composed of communities, where initial opinion configurations may or may not align with the community structure. In other words, the network topology itself is not assumed to be homophilic; instead, homophily emerges (or does not) depending on how initial opinions are assigned. To explore these scenarios, we conduct experiments on synthetic social structures generated through Stochastic Block Models (SBMs) with two communities and controlled initial opinion configurations, conducting a series of what-if analyses to probe the conditions under which cascades lead to homog-enization, bounded assimilation, or radical divergence. Our findings show that the averaging property of the FJ model, which by design brings opinions closer together, is dramat-ically altered in the presence of cascades. Firstly, cascades preserve larger intergroup opinion distances ( polarization ). Secondly, even in scenarios where the classical FJ model leads to global homogenization , the FJC model exhibits bounded assimilation , whereby groups of initially aligned opinions maintain their original structure. These results suggest that the FJC model captures more realistic online behavior, in which initially aligned opinion groups tend to preserve their structure. Overall, the proposed framework bridges a long-standing divide between diffusion and influence modeling, offering a theoretical foundation for understanding how cascade-driven interactions reshape collective opinion landscapes in networks with structured communities, independent of whether those communities initially exhibit opinion homophily. II. R ELATED WORK 

Research on FJ-based opinion dynamics has evolved along two main directions. The first focuses on optimization and intervention strategies [19]–[23], where the goal is to steer col-lective opinions through minimal or targeted influence actions. The second extends the classical model to incorporate richer social mechanisms, such as antagonism [24], [25], temporal interactions [26], and algorithmic filtering [27]. Several studies explicitly incorporate homophily , the ten-dency of similar individuals to connect [28]. In [29], the social network is time-varying and homophily-dependent, al-lowing users to rewire their connections to increase opinion agreement. In contrast, we focus on static networks, assuming that social ties reflect relatively stable affective or identity-based relationships. Dandekar et al . [30] incorporate biased assimilation, whereby agents are more strongly influenced by opinions aligned with their own. In [27], Chitra et al .introduce an algorithmic mediator that modifies network edges to reduce exposure to opposing views, thus modeling the emergence of filter bubbles. Both approaches examine ho-mophily within SBM structures, as we do; however, their polarization measures capture only local disagreement [30] or global divergence [27]. Our approach extends beyond these metrics by analyzing polarization in both opinion space and network topology, revealing how cascades can sustain, amplify, or mitigate polarization. This enables us to link micro-level diffusion to macro-level structural polarization, providing insights into how opinion diversity and network segregation jointly shape collective outcomes in online social systems. In parallel, research on information diffusion , notably the Independent Cascade (IC) model [31]–[33], has explored the spread of content and behaviors. While the diffusion literature primarily focuses on the reach and speed of propagation, less attention has been paid to how these diffusion processes co-evolve with opinion formation. Few works couple diffusion with opinion formation [16], [17]. Both couple diffusion and opinion dynamics only loosely: in the former, content affects opinions once through initial bias shifts, while in the latter, opinion adoption depends solely on similarity to the source, modeling virality rather than belief revision. Our prior study [18] introduced the FJC model, integrating the FJ influence mechanism into the temporal structure of diffusion cascades, linking opinion revision directly to exposure events. In the present study, we build on that framework analytically, deriving theoretical results and approximate solutions to esti-mate steady-state opinions under cascade-driven exposure. III. The results showed that, despite these simplifications, the model achieves high predictive accuracy in capturing observed opin-ion dynamics. IV. A NALYTICAL DERIVATION OF OPINION TRAJECTORIES 

The FJC framework described in Section III-D establishes the operational rules by which opinion updates are embedded within stochastic information cascades. We now advance the framework from an algorithmic description to a rigorous analytical formulation. In this section, we develop a theoretical formulation of the FJC dynamics. We begin by formalizing the temporal indexing and layer-based progression of the cascade (Section IV-A), which establishes the domain for the matrix representation of the opinion updates (Section IV-B). We then quantify the probabilistic structure of cascade layers (Section IV-C). These components are then combined to estab-lish an exact conditional recursion that captures the coupling between diffusion and opinion dynamics, and to introduce mean-field and structural approximations that provide tractable predictions for large-scale networks (Sections IV-D and IV-E). 

A. Sequential Opinion Updates 

Opinion updates in the FJC model (Section III-C) occur sequentially, following the order induced by the cascade realization. For each post pn, nodes update their opinions layer by layer along the cascade tree, starting from the root and proceeding outward. Rather than tracking each individual update time, we exploit this ordering to define aggregated layer-wise states. Specifically, we denote by y(n) 

> l

the opinion vector after all nodes in layer l of the n-th cascade have updated, with y(n)0 representing the opinion vector at the beginning of the cascade. This construction induces a well-defined sequential update process, in which each node update depends on the current system state resulting from all prior updates. As a result, the FJC dynamics can be equivalently represented as a sequence of linear state transitions, which we formalize in the following section.

The theoretical framework developed in this work provides an accurate characterization of diffusion and opinion propa-gation in complex networks. Its precision, however, comes at a non-negligible computational cost, as it requires evaluating all possible diffusion paths, each corresponding to a potential realization of the stochastic process. This computation is performed once for fixed parameters r, Λ, and θ, allowing the resulting matrices to be stored and reused for rapid evaluation of final opinions from initial conditions, making the framework suitable for extensive what-if analyses. To enhance scalability to larger systems, we replace the full cascade structure with a tree-based approximation, illustrated in Figure 3 on the cascade structure of Figure 1.(b). In the original network, a node may be reachable from the cascade root through multiple parents and multiple diffusion paths, which induces the combinatorial enumeration of predecessor sets. The approximation retains, for each reachable node, only the predecessor lying on the most probable path from the root. As a result, each node has at most one parent in the approximated cascade structure, so its predecessor set is either empty or a singleton. 213456

B. Opinion dynamics and polarization 

We now examine how cascade-driven exposure reshapes opinion dynamics relative to the classical FJ model. Rather than analyzing each configuration in isolation, we interpret the results through three recurring mechanisms that differentiate FJC from FJ: 

Cascade selectivity: influence propagates only along realized cascade paths, limiting exposure compared to the global averaging of FJ. 

Bridge amplification or suppression: nodes with high be-tweenness centrality disproportionately shape FJC dy-namics, since cascades amplify their exposure patterns. 

Susceptibility–structure interaction: the qualitative effect of cascades depends on whether structurally central nodes are more or less susceptible to influence. These mechanisms arise from a fundamental structural difference between the two models. In the FJ model, influence propagates simultaneously along all social ties, producing symmetric averaging across the network. In contrast, the FJC model transmits influence only along realized cascade paths. As a consequence, interactions between communities occur only when cascades traverse bridge nodes, and such interactions are typically asymmetric, originating from one community and reaching only a subset of the other. Figures 6 and 7 illustrate these mechanisms under low and moderate diffusion ( θ = 0 .1 and θ = 0 .5). Each arrow represents a node’s trajectory from its initial opinion to its final opinion under FJC, while black dots denote the corresponding FJ equilibria. Unless otherwise specified, differences across θ

primarily affect the magnitude of shifts rather than their direction. We now analyze how these mechanisms manifest across the four opinion configurations considered in our experiments. We begin with the PC configuration. When the two com-munities start with opposite extreme opinions, the classical FJ model reduces polarization through symmetric averaging: both groups move toward the center and the distance between communities decreases, although distinct clusters may still persist depending on the susceptibility pattern. In contrast, FJC dynamics preserve separation between communities. Cascade selectivity limits cross-community exposure and concentrates influence on bridge nodes. Peripheral nodes, deeply embedded within their own community, remain close to their initial positions, while bridge nodes shift most. As a result, inter-community separation persists and complete homogenization does not occur. When susceptibility is proportional to central-ity (Panel A), this effect intensifies, as bridge nodes become highly influential and sustain polarization. When susceptibil-ity is inversely proportional to centrality (Panel B), bridges become stubborn, further reducing cross-community mixing. We next consider the AP configuration, where one com-munity is neutral and the other extreme. In this regime the two models behave differently but with smaller overall shifts. Under FJ dynamics, the extreme community is par-tially moderated by the neutral one, producing partial conver-gence. Under FJC, cascade selectivity further weakens cross-community influence, so opinion shifts remain limited and the two communities largely preserve their initial separation. This configuration therefore illustrates how cascade-mediated interactions dampen cross-group influence when the initial opinion distance is moderate. In the NB configuration, bridge nodes are initially neutral and connect two extreme communities. Under FJ dynamics, this neutral group acts as a mediator, pulling both communities 11 

toward moderation and producing three internally coherent clusters with reduced polarization. Under FJC, this mediat-ing role weakens. Because exposure is restricted to realized cascades, neutral bridges do not transmit influence uniformly across the two groups. Peripheral nodes tend to shift more than bridges, and the final separation between communities remains wider than under FJ. When bridge nodes are stubborn (Panel B), partial blending occurs; when they are highly susceptible (Panel A), polarization is maintained. Finally, we analyze the PB configuration, where bridge nodes hold extreme opinions and the remaining nodes are neutral. The outcome depends strongly on the susceptibility of the bridge nodes. Under the classical FJ dynamics, highly susceptible bridges (Panel A) have their extreme prejudices mitigated through averaging, leading to a weakly segregated or mixed state. If bridges are stubborn (Panel B), their extreme opinions persist and communities remain separated, while uniform susceptibility (Panel C) produces an intermediate regime with multiple clusters but reduced distances. Under FJC, cascade selectivity again modifies the outcome: influence propagates only along realized cascade paths, so bridges do not necessarily mediate interactions symmetrically across commu-nities. For moderate diffusion intensity ( θ = 0 .5), cascades reaching peripheral nodes may induce partial blending and a more continuous opinion spectrum; when cascade paths are sparser or bridges are effectively stubborn, separation is preserved. Across all configurations, the key distinction is that FJ en-forces global averaging across all edges, whereas FJC restricts influence to cascade-realized exposure paths. This restriction can preserve polarization when cross-community cascades are rare or bridges are stubborn, or produce asymmetric blend-ing when cascades traverse highly susceptible bridges. Thus, cascade-mediated exposure does not simply accelerate opinion change: it qualitatively reshapes which interactions occur and which structural nodes dominate influence. VI. C ONCLUSIONS 

This paper provided an analytical characterization of the Friedkin–Johnsen on Cascades (FJC) model, explicitly linking stochastic information diffusion with opinion dynamics. We showed that cascade-driven exposure induces a statistical coupling between diffusion realizations and opinion updates, which prevents an exact closed recursion in unconditional expectations. To obtain a tractable characterization, we intro-duced a mean-field decoupling approximation (MFA) leading to a linear recursion for the expected opinion trajectory. Simulations validated that this approximation captures the main statistical effects of the diffusion-opinion interaction. We also developed scalable tree-based reductions that sub-stantially reduce computational complexity while preserving key structural properties of cascade-driven influence. Finally, by comparing FJC with the classical FJ model on SBM networks with two communities and controlled initial opin-ion configurations, we highlighted a fundamental difference between global averaging and cascade-mediated exposure. Whereas FJ typically promotes homogenization, FJC filters influence through realized cascade paths, enabling regimes of bounded assimilation and persistent polarization depending on community structure and susceptibility patterns. Overall, the proposed framework provides a foundation for studying how cascade-driven interactions reshape collective opinion landscapes in community-structured networks. REFERENCES [1] E.
