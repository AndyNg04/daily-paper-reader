ABSTRACT 

Multi-agent inverse reinforcement learning offers a powerful framework not only for imitation learning but also for understanding the neural computations support-ing social interactions and decision-making. Inferring rewards from interacting agents is challenging because observed choices reflect both personal preferences and expectations about others. Existing methods often require additional envi-ronment interaction, repeated policy optimization as an inner loop, or restrictive assumptions about environment dynamics and action spaces, limiting their applica-bility to fixed behavioral datasets. We introduce Multi-Agent Unilateral Inverse Temporal Difference Learning (MA-UITD), an offline method for inferring explicit agent-specific rewards from joint demonstrations in continuous-state environments with either discrete or bounded continuous actions. Its core idea is to represent the long-term consequences of each agent’s own actions through belief-conditioned successor features that average over other agents’ behavior. Shared reward features and agent-specific preference vectors connect these unilateral values to the target re-ward function, while inverse temporal-difference consistency and noise-contrastive behavioral learning enable inference without known dynamics or an inner policy-optimization loop. Across simulated cooperative, competitive, and general-sum tasks, MA-UITD outperforms baselines in discrete-action settings and infers be-haviorally compatible reward profiles in continuous-action settings. When applied to experimentally collected social-foraging trajectories, MA-UITD inferred dis-tinct spatial and interaction-dependent reward structures for leaders and followers, providing a quantitative characterization of latent behavioral objectives that may be relevant for understanding the neural computations underlying social behavior. We make our code available at https://github.com/ZaynZh/MA-UITD 

1 INTRODUCTION 

A central goal of behavioral science and neuroscience is to understand the objectives that organize individual behavior. Although accurate action prediction is an important benchmark, an explicit reward function provides a more interpretable computational account by specifying which outcomes an animal values, how it trades off competing outcomes, and how these valuations change with experience. Subjective-value signals have been identified in orbitofrontal cortex and across a broader valuation network involving ventromedial prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum (Padoa-Schioppa & Assad, 2006; Bartra et al., 2013), whereas phasic activity of midbrain dopamine neurons has 1arXiv:2610.29237v1 [cs.LG] 21 Oct 2026 been linked to quantitative reward-prediction errors and corresponding distribution (Schultz et al., 1997; Bayer & Glimcher, 2005; Dabney et al., 2020). Consequently, inferred rewards—and the values and prediction errors they imply—can serve as trial-by-trial computational variables for model-based analyses of neural recordings, enabling tests of latent-computation hypotheses beyond action prediction alone (O’Doherty et al., 2007). Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) provides a principled framework for recovering such latent objectives by inferring reward functions under which observed behavior is likely. In single-agent settings, IRL has been used to recover both stable and dynamically evolving goals from behavior (Ziebart et al., 2008; Ashwood et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025; Ke et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Model-derived reward and value representations have also begun to provide computational vari-ables for characterizing neural activity and examining how valuation-related computations may be altered in neuropsychiatric disorders (Bromberg-Martin et al., 2024; Moneta et al., 2024). However, conventional IRL was developed primarily for single-agent settings and treated each individual as an isolated decision-maker, making it difficult to distinguish intrinsic preferences from socially contingent choices that depend on other individuals. Inferring these objectives becomes substantially more challenging when individuals interact. In social settings, an animal’s choice depends on both its own preferences and its expectations about others. Approaching a shared resource, for example, may reflect one’s own resource preference and anticipated interaction with other agents such as cooperation and competition. Similar observed actions can consequently arise from different incentives. Computational models have framed such social inference as IRL in a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), where a focal agent acts according to beliefs about an incompletely observed environment (Jara-Ettinger, 2019; Baker et al., 2017; Velez-Ginorio et al., 2017). Although this formulation captures uncertainty and false beliefs, other individuals are typically incorporated into the focal agent’s environment rather than represented as autonomous decision-makers with their own actions, policies, and rewards. By contrast, a multi-agent MDP, or Markov game, explicitly represents multiple decision-makers whose joint actions determine state transitions and whose policies are mutually interdependent. Multi-agent inverse reinforcement learning (MAIRL) builds on this formulation to infer agent-specific rewards while accounting for how each agent anticipates and responds to the behavior of others (Yu et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2021; Neumeyer et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2026). MAIRL thus offers a systematic framework for separating what each individual values from what it expects others to do, providing interpretable computational variables for studying cooperation, competition, and general-sum interactions. Applying MAIRL to animal behavior imposes several practical constraints: experiments typically yield a fixed dataset of joint trajectories without reward labels, known transition dynamics, or opportunities to collect additional behavior under a learned policy, and the recorded states and actions are often continuous. Existing methods address parts of this setting: Multi-Agent Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning(MA-AIRL) combines reward estimation with adversarial policy optimization, requiring repeated policy updates and environment interaction during training (Yu et al., 2019). Multi-Agent Marginal Q-Learning(MAMQL) avoids adversarial training by learning through opponent-marginalized values, using discrete actions and additional policy rollouts in its reported training procedure (Haynam et al., 2025). These constraints motivate a fully offline approach that infers explicit agent-specific rewards directly from fixed joint demonstrations, without known dynamics or an inner policy-optimization loop, while supporting continuous behavior .We introduce Multi-Agent Unilateral Inverse Temporal Difference Learning (MA-UITD), an offline method for inferring explicit agent-specific rewards from joint demonstrations. We focus on arrival-state rewards that describe the value individuals assign to attained environmental and social states. The inferred rewards provide interpretable explanations of behavior while supporting expert-level behavioral imitation. Building on successor features and inverse temporal-difference learning (Filos et al., 2021), our main contributions are: • We introduce belief-conditioned successor features (BCSF), which marginalize over other agents’ anticipated behavior to represent unilateral long-term values and recover interpretable agent-specific rewards. 2• We develop an offline learning procedure that combines inverse temporal-difference con-sistency with noise-contrastive behavioral learning, requires no known dynamics or policy-optimization loop, and supports continuous states with either discrete or continuous actions .• Across cooperative, competitive, and general-sum simulations, MA-UITD outperforms discrete-action baselines and captures task-relevant reward structure in continuous-action settings. • Applied to animal social-interaction data, MA-UITD reveals distinct spatial and interaction-dependent incentives underlying leader–follower dynamics. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Multi-agent inverse reward learning. In multi-agent settings, MA-AIRL (Yu et al., 2019) couples agent-specific reward estimation to an adversarial discriminator and policy optimization. Such adversarial approaches involve an inner policy loop and outer loop, which could be computationally expensive and unstable. MAMQL (Haynam et al., 2025) instead learns opponent-marginalized reward through a non-adversarial inverse soft-Q objective; however, it assumes a discrete action set and obtains explicit rewards through a separate reconstruction loss, with its reported training procedure drawing additional policy rollouts. Other work models strategic beliefs through latent quantal level-k

reasoning (Tian et al., 2021), but requires recursive value iteration and identifies continuous state and action spaces as a limitation. GS-CIOC (Neumeyer et al., 2021) supports non-adversarial reward inference with continuous states and actions, yet presumes known dynamics and relies on local linear-quadratic approximations around demonstrations under a specified equilibrium model. 

Successor-feature-based inverse reward learning. A complementary approach connects explicit rewards to long-term values through a shared feature representation. Filos et al. (2021) introduce inverse temporal-difference (ITD) learning, which jointly estimates shared cumulants, demonstrator-specific successor features, and preference vectors from fixed demonstrations without reward labels. By combining behavioral likelihood fitting with successor-feature Bellman consistency, ITD yields explicit rewards from the learned cumulants and preferences. However, its formal setup considers different reward tasks within a common controlled Markov process with discrete actions, without an explicit Markov-game formulation of strategic interactions. 

Offline reward inference for animal behavior. These considerations are particularly important for studies of animal behavior, where joint trajectories can be recorded experimentally but constructing a faithful interactive simulator is often difficult because the underlying behavioral dynamics are only partially known (Fujii et al., 2025). In such settings, methods that require repeated environment interaction, additional policy rollouts, or an accurate model of the dynamics may therefore be impractical. Offline reward-inference methods can recover explicit rewards from fixed demonstrations without known dynamics or additional environment interaction, but are formulated for single-agent settings with discrete actions (Chan & Schaar, 2021). This motivates methods that can infer explicit, agent-specific rewards directly from fixed datasets of continuous joint trajectories in multi-agent systems. Building on these, our method learns belief-conditioned successor features directly from a fixed dataset of joint transitions and uses noise-contrastive estimation to avoid the intractable continuous-action normalizer. It thereby recovers an explicit agent-specific reward profile in continuous state and action spaces, without adversarial optimization, additional environment interaction, or a forward policy-optimization loop; moreover, equilibrium and finite-depth reasoning enter as restrictions on a common predictive belief rather than as hard-coded solution concepts.

Arrival-state rewards. Motivated by behavioral and neuroscience settings in which the inferred reward is interpreted as the value assigned to attained outcomes or configurations, we focus on agent-specific arrival-state rewards. That is, the reward received by agent i following a transition 

(st, at, s t+1 ) is assumed to take the form 

Ri(st, at, s t+1 ) = ri(st+1 ), ri : S → R.

Because s is the joint environmental state, ri(s) may still encode both individual preferences and interactions among agents. Thus, this assumption restricts where reward is assigned—to the resulting state rather than directly to the executed action—without requiring agents to share the same reward function. When deterministic choices produce distinguishable outcome states, action-contingent rewards can be represented equivalently as arrival-state rewards. This includes common cognitive tasks such as multi-armed bandits and the prisoner’s dilemma when the outcome state records the selected action or joint action. 

Reward compatibility rather than reward identification. Inverse reinforcement learning is generally non-identifiable, and this ambiguity is further compounded in multi-agent settings. In particular, rewards in general-sum Markov games are not uniquely identifiable from demonstrations without additional assumptions (Freihaut & Ramponi, 2025). We therefore seek a representative agent-specific reward profile bR = (ˆ r1, . . . , ˆrn) within the arrival-state reward class, rather than claim recovery of the unique ground truth. We assess compatibility operationally: under agent i’s predictive belief μi, −i, the reward-induced belief-conditioned score should assign higher relative likelihood to demonstrated actions than to counterfactual alternatives. Equilibrium and finite-depth reasoning enter only through the choice of this belief, rather than as imposed solution concepts. 

4 MULTI -A GENT UNILATERAL INVERSE TEMPORAL DIFFERENCE LEARNING 

(MA-UITD) 

We introduce MA-UITD, an offline method for inferring agent-specific rewards from fixed joint demonstrations. It involves three key components: a shared reward feature map Φ, an agent-specific preference vector wi, and agent-specific belief-conditioned successor features (BCSFs). BCSFs represent the long-term consequences of an agent’s own action after averaging over its prediction of the other agents’ behavior. This separates strategic assumptions, encoded by the predictive belief, from reward preferences, encoded by wi.Learning combines two complementary objectives. A contrastive behavioral loss makes the inferred values assign greater relative likelihood to demonstrated actions than to sampled alternatives, while a unilateral inverse temporal-difference loss enforces Bellman consistency between the BCSFs and the learned reward features. Both objectives are estimated from the fixed dataset, without a transition model, additional environment interaction, or an inner policy-optimization loop. 5 RESULTS 

We evaluate MA-UITD in three simulated multi-agent environments spanning cooperative, compet-itive, and general-sum interactions. Our experiments examine 1) whether MA-UITD reproduces expert-level behavior and 2) recovers interpretable rewards compatible with the demonstrations. We first compare MA-UITD with baselines in discrete-action settings and then evaluate it in continuous-action settings. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Environments. We evaluate three games: (1) cooperative Simple Spread from MPE2 (Lowe et al., 2017), where two agents cooperate to cover two landmarks; (2) competitive Simple Push , where 6a good agent attempts to reach a target landmark while an adversary tries to prevent it from doing so through physical interaction; and (3) general-sum Gems , adapted from Haynam et al. (2025). In 

Gems , Red and Blue maximize individual returns by collecting their respective private gems for 1

reward or jointly collecting a purple gem for 6, after which the episode terminates. Agents choose among nine directional actions in the discrete setting or bounded two-dimensional displacements in the continuous setting, with an 80 -step horizon. 

Baselines. We evaluate MA-UITD against two baselines. The first is MA-AIRL (Yu et al., 2019), an adversarial learning-based multi-agent IRL method. Then, we benchmark against MAMQL (Haynam et al., 2025), an IQ-Learn-style MAIRL algorithm that similarly marginalizes the joint-action value function over the opponents’ continuation policies but is designed only for discrete action spaces. 5.2 POLICY AND REWARD RECOVERY

8Application to real animal trajectories without trajectory binning. We next evaluated MA-UITD using joint trajectories from pairs of mice performing a social-foraging task (Cheng et al., 2025). In this task, the two animals had to cooperate by navigating to the same reward zone to obtain reward (Fig. 3a). Over training, pairs developed stable asymmetric behavioral roles, with one animal acting as a leader and the other as a follower, potentially reflecting distinct internal reward functions shaped by uncertainty under decentralized, simultaneous movement. Because MA-UITD models a separate reward function for each agent, it can directly represent this form of behavioral asymmetry. Without prior knowledge, however, an animal’s internal reward could depend on arbitrary task variables, leaving inverse reward inference severely underconstrained. We therefore restricted the reward representation to the two animals’ positions in the arena and their inter-animal distance, defining reward functions on separate self-position, other-position, and distance components. Figure 3b,c shows distinct learned reward structures for a representative leader–follower pair. The leader’s learned reward depended primarily on its own spatial location, whereas the follower’s position-dependent contributions were weaker relative to its proximity-dependent component. This pattern was consistent across mouse pairs: follower rewards increased strongly at short inter-animal distances, while leader rewards showed substantially weaker modulation by distance (Fig. 3d,e). These observations support leader–follower dynamics in which the leader navigates according to spatial goals and the follower is motivated to remain close to the leader. 6 DISCUSSION 

In summary, we developed MA-UITD, an offline multi-agent inverse reinforcement learning method that uses belief-conditioned successor features to recover explicit agent-specific rewards from fixed demonstrations in continuous-state environments with discrete or bounded continuous actions. The current method conditions reward inference on predictive beliefs estimated from observed behavior; 9an important direction for future work is to infer each agent’s internal beliefs jointly with its reward function and to extend the framework to interactions involving larger numbers of agents. One limitation is that demonstrations provide little evidence about rewards outside the support of the observed states. Reward estimates in these regions therefore depend largely on regularization and the inductive biases of the chosen function approximator, and should be interpreted cautiously until supported by additional data. B IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

For Simple Spread , the reward-feature network takes the scalar agent–landmark and inter-agent distances as input, whereas the BCSF networks take the agent and landmark positions, agent velocities, and each agent’s own action. For Simple Push , the reward-feature network takes the agent and landmark positions as input, while the BCSF networks additionally receive the agent velocities 12 and each agent’s own action. For Gems , a shared torso network, MLP ([32 , 32]) , encodes the agent positions. The reward-feature head operates on this shared state representation, while each BCSF head additionally receives the corresponding agent’s own action. To ensure a fair comparison, all algorithms were provided with the same task-specific state and action information.
