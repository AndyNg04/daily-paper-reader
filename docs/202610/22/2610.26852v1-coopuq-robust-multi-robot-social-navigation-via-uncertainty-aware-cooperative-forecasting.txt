Abstract: Reinforcement learning enables mobile robots to make fast and scal-
able navigation decisions in constrained human-shared environments. However,
directly extending single-robot approaches to multi-robot settings often fails to
ensure safety and efficiency, especially under occlusions and out-of-distribution
(OOD) scenarios, where individual robots may overlook potential risks or make
inaccurate predictions. Leveraging cooperation effectively for both prediction and
navigation remains a major challenge. We propose CoopUQ, a multi-robot social
navigation framework that bridges uncertainty-aware cooperative occupancy fore-
casting and robust decision making. CoopUQ predicts future occupancy grid maps
through cross-robot spatiotemporal feature fusion and further leverages forecasts
and their uncertainty to guide policy via constrained multi-agent reinforcement
learning, where the cost penalizes navigation through regions predicted to be oc-
cupied or highly uncertain. This design enables the policy to adapt its risk sensitiv-
ity under distribution shifts. Experimental results across multiple Unity3D-based
simulation environments with both in-distribution and OOD settings, including
diverse layouts, pedestrian densities, and rushing behavior, demonstrate improved
prediction performance and safety over representative baselines. Real-robot ex-
periments further demonstrate its robustness across various scenes. Our code and
videos are available at https://coopuq.github.io/.
Keywords: Social Navigation, Multi-Robot Systems, Occupancy Forecasting

Introduction
Safely navigating among humans is fundamental to deploying mobile robots in real-world shared
spaces such as hospitals, offices, and warehouses [1, 2, 3, 4]. Multiple robots may operate simul-
taneously in these constrained spaces while pedestrians move unpredictably through narrow corri-
dors, blind corners, and cluttered layouts. Navigation decisions are shaped by both static geometry
and dynamic human motion. Reinforcement learning (RL) and multi-agent reinforcement learn-
ing (MARL) support reward-driven policy learning and preference-based refinement [5, 6], provide
fast decision-making, and have shown promising performance in multi-robot socially-aware navi-
gation [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Previous studies have shown that prediction-aware policies
can help robots shape their decisions on future human motion [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. While trajectory
prediction is widely used to represent pedestrian futures, it often focuses on dynamic agents alone
and does not model the surrounding static geometry that is crucial in constrained spaces. Occupancy
grid maps (OGMs) [21, 22] provide a more unified representation, encoding static obstacles, free

However, forecasting future occupancy remains challenging in environments with evolving pedes-
trian intentions, diverse motion patterns, denser crowds, and unseen occlusion-heavy regions.
Learning-based policies often degrade under such out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, since dis-
tribution shifts can introduce prediction errors and severely mislead planning. This challenge is pro-
nounced when multiple robots operate in constrained environments shared with pedestrians, where
each individual robot may overlook risks caused by occlusions or inaccurate predictions. Therefore,
effective cooperation among robots is essential to ensure safety, efficiency, and human comfort.
Moreover, to ensure robust and adaptive navigation, robots need not only to predict future states
but also to estimate the uncertainty of predictions. Such uncertainty indicates when a prediction is
reliable and when the policy should adapt its behavior. For instance, as shown in Figure 1, a robot
may get stuck in a confined area due to static obstacles or fail to detect pedestrians hidden behind
a wall when turning a corner. In contrast, cooperative multi-robot systems can coordinate planning
and exchange information to improve task execution and situational awareness [23, 24, 25]. By shar-
ing spatiotemporal features when a nearby robot detects a pedestrian in an occluded region, robots
can make more informed and proactive decisions, improving safety, efficiency, and human comfort
under challenging distribution shifts.
To this end, we propose CoopUQ, a multi-robot social navigation framework. CoopUQ forecasts fu-
ture OGMs and estimates uncertainty maps by modeling the stochasticity of future occupancy over
cross-robot spatiotemporal features, improving prediction quality over raw-LiDAR or prediction-
level fusion. Beyond using uncertainty as an extra observation, we employ constrained multi-agent
reinforcement learning (CMARL) [26] to explicitly guide risk-aware control, allowing robots to
avoid ambiguous regions under distribution shifts while maintaining efficiency in well-observed ar-
eas. Our main contributions are three-fold. First, CoopUQ leverages uncertainty as a bridge between
stochastic cooperative forecasting and robust decision making. Second, we design a deviation-
guided cross-robot fusion module that adaptively aggregates complementary features in regions af-
fected by occlusions or cross-robot disagreement. Third, we introduce a risk-aware safety cost in
CMARL to explicitly guide the navigation policy. Extensive Unity3D simulations and real-robot ex-
periments demonstrate safer and more robust navigation across in-distribution and OOD scenarios.

Related Work
Social Robot Navigation. Mobile robots that navigate in spaces shared with humans should adhere
to social norms [27, 4, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Social navigation is often modeled as a Partially Observable
Markov Decision Process, where agents act based on incomplete local observations [27]. A key
challenge is modeling partial and uncertain observations in real-world environments [32]. Many
prior works focus on predicting trajectories of dynamic obstacles, modeling pedestrians as sim-
ple position-radius tuples. These rule-based or learning-based methods perform well in simulation
[16, 17, 10, 7, 8, 9, 33], but often assume full visibility in open spaces and overlook static obstacles,
limiting their applicability in occluded or cluttered environments. In contrast, we adopt OGM fore-
casting with uncertainties as a holistic spatiotemporal representation and aggregate complementary
observations through multi-robot cooperation, enabling safer decisions in constrained environments.
Occupancy Grid Map (OGM) Forecasting. Predicting the future environment remains challeng-
ing due to its inherent stochasticity and uncertainty. OGM is an effective spatial representation
that unifies both static and dynamic obstacles. Since OGMs can be treated as images, multi-step
forecasting is often formulated as a video prediction problem [34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. Recent stud-
ies use OGMs to model dynamic human crowds [39, 40]. However, individual OGM forecasting
remains challenging in constrained environments, where occlusions can lead to incomplete or less
accurate predictions. In contrast, cooperative forecasting allows multiple robots to fuse spatiotem-
poral features and produce uncertainty-aware predictions, constructing more reliable guidance for
downstream navigation.
Cooperative Perception and Prediction. Cooperative perception and prediction are crucial for
addressing partial observability and perceptual limitations, particularly in environments with oc-
clusions [41]. By sharing sensor data, intermediate features, or predictions, multiple agents can
construct a more comprehensive scene understanding, enhancing detection and motion prediction
in occlusion scenarios [42, 43, 44, 45]. In this work, we bridge cooperative occupancy forecasting
with uncertainty-aware planning for multi-robot social navigation, allowing robots to leverage pre-
dictions and their corresponding uncertainties to make more robust decisions under occlusion and
distribution shifts.

3.2
Method Overview
Figure 2 provides an overview of the proposed framework. We first encode each robot’s LiDAR
sequence into spatiotemporal features and employ cross-robot fusion to generate multi-step OGM
forecasts and uncertainty estimates, providing a holistic and risk-aware view of the future scene. We
then augment the policy observation with the predicted OGMs and uncertainty maps, and guide safe
and efficient multi-robot social navigation via constrained MARL.

bution rather than predicting a deterministic map. Given the fused feature Fi

occupancy induced by dynamic pedestrians and ambiguous motion. To preserve sharp static geom-

the current OGM directly to the decoding stage. We further sample a separate latent variable for
each future step and decode all h future OGMs non-autoregressively, avoiding error accumulation
and cost from recursive prediction. To estimate prediction uncertainty, we draw M samples from
the model to obtain M candidate future OGM sequences. Their pixel-wise average forms the pre-

normalized Shannon entropy over the sampled OGMs. Larger uncertainty values often occur near
dynamic pedestrians, occluded regions, or ambiguous future motion.
Early and Late Fusion. We also consider two alternative fusion strategies for comparison. In the
early fusion setting, robots share their LiDAR scans with neighboring robots within the communica-
tion range. For each ego robot, the scans received from neighboring robots are transformed into the
ego frame using relative poses. The ego robot then removes self-hits, converts the aggregated scans
into local OGMs, and performs future occupancy forecasting based on the fused sensor input. This
strategy preserves low-level geometric information but is more sensitive to communication delay or
localization noise. In the late fusion setting, each robot first predicts future OGMs independently
using its own observations. The predicted OGMs are then shared with neighboring robots, trans-
formed into the ego frame, and aggregated at the prediction level. This strategy may lose useful
intermediate features and can dilute safety-critical predictions.

4
Experiments
4.1
Simulation Environment and Dataset
We build a Unity3D-based multi-robot social navigation simulator with static obstacles, dynamic
pedestrians, and multiple robots. Pedestrians are simulated using Unity NavMesh with randomized
goals and speeds. For training, three robots navigate in three layouts with 5–10 pedestrians; for
in-distribution testing, we use the same layouts with 7–10 pedestrians. OOD evaluation includes
unseen layouts, denser crowds with 12–15 pedestrians, and faster pedestrians with speeds up to
2 m/s. We report results with three robots in Table 2 and evaluate scalability to five and ten robots
in App. A.2. For OGM forecasting, we collect LiDAR and pose data at 5 Hz from the simulator and
generate 64 × 64 OGMs with 0.1 m resolution. The history window is set to τ = 5. The dataset
contains 361,152 training and 30,498 testing samples from multi-robot, multi-pedestrian scenarios.
4.2
Evaluation Metrics and Baselines
Cooperative Forecasting. We evaluate future OGM forecasting over the next 5 steps using metrics
from the video forecasting literature [47]. WMSE measures weighted pixel-wise prediction errors,
SSIM reflects the structural consistency of the predicted scene geometry, and IoU measures the
overlap between predicted and ground-truth occupied regions. We compare SOGMP++ [40], where
each robot forecasts OGMs without cooperation, against three cooperation strategies: early fusion,
late fusion, and our spatiotemporal (middle) fusion. We further compare performance with clean,
200 ms delay, and random localization (Loc.) noise during communication. Details in App. B.2.
Social Navigation. We evaluate social navigation using metrics, including success rate (SR), colli-
sion rate (CR), timeout rate (TR), average navigation time (NT), and path length (PL). We report av-
erage acceleration (AA), intrusion time ratio (ITR), and social distance during intrusions (SD) [16].
AA measures trajectory smoothness, while ITR and SD evaluate social awareness around pedestri-
ans. We compare against NavMesh, ORCA [48] for reciprocal collision avoidance, SAMARL [10]
for learned multi-robot coordination, SCOPE [22] for non-cooperative prediction-aware navigation,
and HEIGHT [49] for heterogeneous interaction modeling. We augment SAMARL with locally
perceived static obstacles and extend HEIGHT to include neighboring robots without peer shar-
ing. For ablation, we compare Prediction, which uses predicted OGMs without uncertainty, and
CMARL, which uses predicted OGMs and uncertainty maps with MAPPO-Lagrangian constraints,
under different cooperation modes.

Cooperative Forecasting.
Table 1 reports the mean and standard deviation over three test
seeds. Under clean inputs, SOGMP++ performs worst across horizons, showing the limitation
of individual-only forecasting under partial observability. All fusion strategies improve prediction
quality by incorporating complementary views, with early fusion outperforming late fusion by rea-
soning over a more complete context before prediction. CoopUQ (middle fusion) achieves the best
across all metrics by preserving richer spatiotemporal context. At the 5-step horizon, it improves
SSIM from 0.71 to 0.86 and IoU from 0.57 to 0.72 over SOGMP++, yielding relative gains of 21.1%
and 26.3%. Under a 200 ms delay at the same 5-step horizon, it shows mild degradation, with a 2.3%
SSIM drop and unchanged IoU, compared with larger drops for early and late fusion. Under local-
ization noise, it retains the highest SSIM, with a 7.0% drop versus 15.0% and 6.6% for early and
late fusion, as the learnable spatial transformation mitigates small misalignments. Overall, CoopUQ
improves forecasting performance and remains robust.
In-distribution Navigation. Table 2 summarizes in-distribution navigation performance with three
robots. NavMesh avoids static obstacles with privileged map access, but suffers the lowest SR and
high human/robot collision rates in dense dynamic crowds. SCOPE [22] improves over NavMesh
but is limited by single-robot prediction. ORCA suffers from frequent human and obstacle colli-
sions, while SAMARL and HEIGHT improve SR but remain limited by local observations. ORCA
attains the shortest paths, whereas CoopUQ achieves the highest SR, lowest human CR and ITR,
shortest NT, and largest SD. This contrast highlights the importance of considering navigation effi-
ciency together with success and safety. Cooperative forecasting consistently improves navigation
performance. Middle fusion achieves the strongest prediction-only result, reaching 0.84 SR and
reducing human collision rate to 0.05, indicating that spatiotemporal fusion better preserves cross-
robot context. Adding uncertainty-aware CMARL further improves safety. Without cooperation, SR
increases from 0.71 to 0.75, and the human collision rate drops from 0.13 to 0.10. Lower ITR and
higher SD further suggest that uncertainty maps help the policy avoid ambiguous high-risk regions
while maintaining efficient motion. Overall, CoopUQ improves SR from 0.75 to 0.90 and reduces
the human collision rate from 0.10 to 0.03 compared with non-cooperative CMARL. These results
show that uncertainty-aware cooperative forecasting and risk-aware control jointly improve safety,
efficiency, and social compliance in constrained dynamic environments.

Qualitative Results
Cooperative Forecasting.
Figure 4 (a) and (b) compare the predicted OGMs of SCOPE and
CoopUQ in a scenario where three robots navigate through human crowds and diverse obstacle ge-
ometries. As the red ego robot approaches a wall corner with limited visibility, a pedestrian emerges
from behind the corner and crosses its path. SCOPE reacts only after the pedestrian becomes fully
visible, leading to a collision. In contrast, CoopUQ leverages shared spatiotemporal features from

namic occupancy, enabling the ego robot to detect the risk earlier and proactively avoid the collision.
Social Navigation.
Figure 4(c) and (d) compare SCOPE and CoopUQ in a constrained scene
with dense, randomly moving pedestrians. SCOPE reacts late to pedestrians and obstacles, brakes
abruptly, and can become trapped due to limited field of view and inaccurate obstacle estimation. In
contrast, CoopUQ leverages shared spatiotemporal features to better forecast obstacle geometry and
pedestrian motion, enabling earlier deceleration and proactive direction changes. Its uncertainty map
highlights ambiguous regions around pedestrians, allowing the constrained policy to avoid high-risk
areas before collisions become imminent. Overall, uncertainty-aware cooperative forecasting and
constrained control lead to safer, more efficient, and socially compliant navigation.

4.5
Real Robot Experiments
We deploy our method on two ROSMASTER X3 robots. Each uses an Ultra-Wideband (UWB)
for localization, with measurements fused via a Kalman filter. Robots connect to a laptop with an
NVIDIA RTX 3070 GPU for inference. The policy is trained in Unity simulation with holonomic
dynamics and deployed to the robot without fine-tuning. We test in four everyday environments
with one to three humans walking around. CoopUQ achieves an average SR of 0.94 across 48 trials.
SCOPE obtains an average SR of 0.58, as it reacts only after pedestrians become fully visible in
occluded scenarios, leading to collisions. Detailed quantitative results are provided in App. A.1.
5
Limitations and Future Work
This work focuses on homogeneous 2D LiDAR-equipped robots and short-horizon occupancy fore-
casting. This may limit CoopUQ under heterogeneous sensing or scenarios requiring longer-term
human intent reasoning. Future work will explore heterogeneous robot teams with richer sensor
modalities. The uncertainty map also does not explicitly capture semantic information. Incorpo-
rating semantic occupancy from LiDAR is a promising direction. Finally, while we evaluate OOD
performance under unseen layouts, denser crowds, and faster pedestrian motion, real-world distri-
bution shifts are broader. Future work will test more diverse environments and pedestrian behaviors.
6
Conclusion
We introduce CoopUQ, a multi-robot social navigation framework that bridges uncertainty-aware
cooperative forecasting with robust decision making. CoopUQ uses cross-robot spatiotemporal fu-
sion to produce accurate occupancy forecasts and incorporates prediction uncertainty into a con-
strained MARL policy. This enables robots to avoid ambiguous high-risk regions while maintain-
ing efficiency in well-observed areas. Extensive Unity3D simulations and real-world experiments
demonstrate improved safety, efficiency, and robustness across in-distribution and OOD scenarios.

As shown in Figure 5 and Figure 6, we further enhance the real-world experiments to four dis-
tinct everyday environments with pedestrian density variations. The corridor environment has more
limited free space, and the lobby environment has obstacles with more diverse shapes. For each en-
vironment, experiments are conducted with one, two, and three pedestrians walking in the scene, and
each configuration is repeated four times, resulting in a total of 48 trials. During each experiment,
each of the two ROSMASTER X3 robots navigates toward a predetermined target. In most cases,
each robot’s goal is set to the initial position of the other robot. Pedestrians were not instructed to
avoid the robots. Detailed real-world quantitative results are summarized in Table 3. Across all runs,
CoopUQ achieves an average success rate of 0.94, exhibiting smooth trajectories and early detouring
behaviors under occlusion. The success rate with one, two, and three pedestrians is 1.0, 0.94, and
0.88, respectively. SCOPE obtains an average success rate of 0.58, as it reacts only after pedestrians
become fully visible in occluded scenarios, often leading to collisions. We observe that collisions
mainly occur in narrow corridor scenarios with higher pedestrian density, where the available free
space is highly constrained. When pedestrians surround the robot and block the feasible space, the
robot may collide with nearby walls. Timeout cases are primarily attributed to conservative behav-
iors, in which the robot slows down or goes back and forth to maintain safety when the surroundings
have cluttered obstacles (e.g., tables, sofas, and chairs) and pedestrians are continuously walking
near the robot. These observations suggest several directions for future work. First, incorporating
a stop-and-wait strategy could help reduce collisions in highly constrained environments. Second,
adopting risk modulation under prolonged stalling, or integrating longer-horizon pedestrian intent
prediction, may further improve navigation efficiency under denser spaces.

C
Implementation Details
For cooperative navigation, we train a recurrent MAPPO policy with a Lagrangian cost constraint
using three robots per simulation environment. The actor and critics use a three-layer MLP with
64 hidden units per layer, followed by a one-layer GRU. All networks are optimized by Adam with
a learning rate of 5 × 10−4, linearly decayed to zero. We use discount factor γ = 0.99 and GAE
parameter λ = 0.95. Each PPO update uses 5 epochs, 2 mini-batches, and a recurrent data chunk
length of 20. The PPO clipping coefficient is 0.2 and the entropy coefficient is 0.01. The value loss
coefficient is 0.5, the Huber loss threshold is 10, and the maximum gradient norm is 0.5. We train
with 8 parallel rollout threads for 106 steps.
