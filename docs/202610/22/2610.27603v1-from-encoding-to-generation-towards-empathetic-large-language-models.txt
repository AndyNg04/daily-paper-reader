ABSTRACT 

Empathetic interaction involves two distinguishable capacities: representing another person’s emo-tional state (cognitive empathy) and generating an appropriate response to that state (affective em-pathy). We apply this distinction to examine how emotion-related information is internally orga-nized, selectively attended to, and carried through response generation in Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct across 10 emotion categories from the EmpatheticDialogues dataset. Layer-wise linear probing re-vealed a broad middle-layer region of peak emotion-category decodability, with the layer-11 L2 probe achieving 63.6% held-out accuracy against a 10% chance level and a 54.6% TF-IDF lexi-cal baseline. During response generation, emotion-related user tokens received 1.89 times as much attention per token as other utterance tokens, concentrated across layers 10–15. Target-emotion alignment then decreased from a mean cosine similarity of 0.239 for input representations to 0.125 for generated responses, with positive drift in 77% of examples and the largest shifts for angry and 

furious . Emotion-category representation, attention allocation, and response-level alignment there-fore emerge as distinguishable components of model processing that can be analyzed through a functional cognitive–affective empathy framework. These findings provide a foundation for moni-toring emotion representations during generation and leveraging models’ existing affective structure to support more naturally empathetic responses without relying on external prompting or explicit emotion conditioning. 

1 Introduction 

Empathy is a foundational capacity in human social interaction, encompassing two related but dissociable components. 

Cognitive empathy is the capacity to identify and encode what another person is feeling. Affective empathy is the ca-pacity to generate a response that is appropriately aligned with or responsive to that perceived state. These components dissociate in humans; for example, a person may recognize others’ distress without producing a supportive response or may respond emotionally without fully understanding the other’s situation. Because empathy unfolds across both representation and response, studying it requires examining how emotional information is internally organized and how that organization shapes behavior. Large language models (LLMs) are now routinely deployed in emotionally sensitive contexts like mental health sup-port [1], peer counseling [2], and open-domain conversation [3]. Despite producing outputs rated as highly empathetic by human evaluators [4], it is not known whether this behavior is grounded in structured internal representations of user emotions or arises from the surface statistics of empathetic language. The cognitive-affective empathy distinction provides a concrete framework for this investigation: does the model represent the user’s emotional state, preferentially attend to emotionally relevant input, and carry that representation into its response? 

1.1 Related Work 

Human empathy research distinguishes between representing others’ emotional states and generating an appropriate response [5]. Both components rely on distributed neural patterns rather than isolated emotion centers [6, 7], and structured emotion representations can be decoded from neural activity using multivariate pattern analysis [8]. The same decoding logic extends to transformer models: linear probes reveal what information is encoded at each layer, with different types of information emerging at different depths [9, 10]. arXiv:2610.27603v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 LLMs trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) show a systematic positive-polarity bias, re-liably generating positive emotional content while underserving negative emotions [11]. This suggests that empathetic-seeming outputs can arise from alignment-induced response patterns rather than from robust internal representations of user emotional states. Prior work has examined emotion information in LLM representations, but less is known about how such representations relate to attention allocation and the representational profile of generated responses. Motivated by the cognitive-affective empathy distinction, we investigate Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct using the Empathetic-Dialogues benchmark [3] to address three questions: 1. Representation: How is emotion-category information internally organized across the model’s representa-tional levels? 2. Attention: Does emotionally relevant input receive preferential attention during response generation? 3. Response alignment: How does emotion-related alignment change between the input representation and generated response, and how does this shift vary across emotion categories? To address these questions, three analyses are combined: (1) layer-wise L1- and L2-regularized linear probing of all 29 representational levels to assess emotion-category encoding; (2) layer-wise attention analysis across token-source categories to assess preferential processing of emotional input; and (3) paired comparison of input and generated-response representations against emotion-category contrastive directions derived at the probe-selected layer. Together, these analyses reveal that emotion information is encoded in a broad middle-layer region (63.6% accuracy at layer 11), that emotionally relevant tokens receive preferential attention during generation (1.89 times enrichment), and that target-emotion alignment is only partially preserved in generated responses, with the largest decline for negative-valence emotions.

2.2 Procedure 2.2.1 Hidden-State Extraction 

Each text sample from Set 1 was passed through the model separately. Hidden-state representations were extracted from all 29 levels and averaged using masked mean pooling to produce a 3,072-dimensional sequence-level vector, excluding padding and the BOS token [14, 15]. For each emotion and layer, an emotion prototype was computed by averaging the 400 pooled vectors belonging to that emotion. A grand-mean prototype was computed across all Set 1 examples; the target-emotion contrastive direction was defined as the difference between the emotion prototype and the grand mean. 

2.2.2 Response Generation 

From the first 200 utterances available for each emotion in Set 2, 20 were sampled for response generation, yielding 200 generated responses in total and introduced into the model’s chat template using a zero-shot system prompt, positioning the model as a medical assistant instructed to produce a one- to two-sentence response (full prompt in Appendix A.1). Responses were generated autoregressively with nucleus sampling (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9, maximum 50 new tokens). Hidden states and attention distributions were recorded at every generation step. 

2.3 Analysis 2.3.1 Linear Emotion Probing 

To characterize how emotion-category information is organized across representational levels, Separate layer-wise multiclass logistic regression probes were trained. Each probe is trained independently at one of the 29 representational levels, following standard layer-wise probing methodology [9, 10]. High probe accuracy at a given layer indicates that emotion-category information is linearly accessible from the representations at that depth, revealing where in the network emotion categories emerge. Two regularization schemes were used to assess whether this information is sparse or distributed across hidden di-mensions. L1 (Lasso) regularization produces sparse classifiers by driving many coefficients to exactly zero, testing whether accurate classification can be achieved using only a small subset of dimensions. L2 (Ridge) regularization 

3penalizes large weights while retaining nonzero coefficients across many dimensions, allowing information distributed across the full hidden space to contribute (see Appendix A.2). Comparing the two schemes, therefore, reveals whether emotion categories are encoded in a few localized features or spread across many partially redundant dimensions. Set 1 is split once into stratified training (80%) and test (20%) partitions with a fixed random seed. For each layer and regularization scheme, the penalty strength is selected by stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training partition. The best layer is selected separately for L1 and L2 based on cross-validated accuracy, and each selected probe is evaluated once on the held-out test partition. We report held-out accuracy and macro-F1 as primary metrics. This procedure ensures that probe performance reflects generalizable accessibility of emotion information rather than overfitting to the training data. 

2.3.2 Token-Group Attention Analysis 

To determine whether emotionally relevant input receives preferential processing during generation, we analyze how attention is allocated across token-source categories at each generation step. Attention distributions are aggregated over four mutually exclusive source groups: the BOS token, template tokens (system instructions and chat formatting), user-utterance tokens, and previously generated tokens. Emotion-related tokens are analyzed as a subset of the user-utterance group. Emotion-related utterance tokens are identified using the contrastive directions derived from the Set 1 prototypes at layer 11, the layer at which linear emotion decoding peaked. A token is classified as emotion-related if the cosine similarity between its layer-11 hidden state and the target emotion’s contrastive direction is at least 0.10. This links the attention analysis directly to the same representational space used in the probing analysis, so that emotion-related is defined by the model’s own internal geometry rather than by surface lexical cues. At each generation step, attention weights were summed within each source group. Because the groups differed in token count, mean attention per token was additionally computed for emotion-related and non-emotional utterance tokens. The per-token enrichment ratio was defined as the mean attention per emotion-related token divided by the mean attention per non-emotional utterance token. Its variation across layers was then examined to determine whether preferential attention occurred in the same middle-layer region in which emotion-category information was most decodable. This approach treats attention as an index of processing allocation, not as a causal explanation of output [16, 17]. It allows us to test whether the model actively prioritizes emotional content during response construction.

3.3 Input-Response Emotion Alignment 

The aggregate token-level trajectory showed lower target-emotion alignment at later response positions. Mean cosine similarity to the target-emotion direction was 0.239 for input utterances and 0.125 for generated responses, yielding a mean drift of 0.113. Positive drift was observed in 77% of the 200 paired examples. Drift varied strongly by emotion category (Figure 4). Grateful exhibited negative drift ( −0.070 ), meaning the response was more aligned with the target emotion than the input, and hopeful showed near-zero drift ( 0.017 ). Positive drift increased across lonely (0.077 ), sad 

(0.085 ), anxious (0.086 ), terrified (0.135 ), afraid (0.140 ), devastated (0.166 ), angry (0.229 ), and furious (0.269 ). The two positive-valence categories showed the strongest retention of target-emotion alignment, whereas the anger-related categories showed the largest attenuation. This pattern was confirmed by argmax classification: the target emotion was the highest-scoring direction emotion in 85% of grateful and 75% of hopeful response representations, compared with 0% for devastated , 10% for furious ,and 20% for both afraid and terrified . Token-level trajectories further showed that target-emotion alignment generally decreased toward the end of each generated response (see Figure 7 in Appendix A.8) 

4 Discussion 

The findings distinguish three components of emotion-related processing in Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct: emotion repre-sentation, attention to emotionally relevant input, and input–response alignment. Together, the results suggest that emotion-category information is internally accessible and selectively attended to during generation, but is not neces-sarily preserved in the model’s response. Emotion-category information was most accessible across a broad middle-layer region, with peak L2 probe perfor-mance at layer 11 and comparable performance across approximately layers 8–17. The sparse L1 probe retained 60.9% held-out accuracy after setting 63.4% of its coefficients to zero, compared with 63.6% for the dense L2 probe. This suggests that emotion-category information is distributed across multiple hidden features rather than localized to a single dimension. Pairwise analyses further showed that semantically distinct emotions were more separable than closely related categories such as afraid and terrified , indicating that the internal structure reflects relationships among emotion categories. This middle-layer accessibility is consistent with recent probing results in Llama models [15] and, at a functional level, parallels evidence that human emotions are represented through distributed rather than isolated neural patterns [18, 7]. During generation, emotion-related utterance tokens received 1.89 × as much attention per token as non-emotional utterance tokens, with preferential attention concentrated across layers 10–15. The descriptive overlap between this region and the layers of strongest emotion decodability suggests a convergence between where emotion information

Thus, the model does not merely encode emotion-category information; it continues to preferentially allocate attention to emotion-relevant input as it generates a response. However, because attention reflects allocation rather than causal influence, intervention-based analyses are needed to establish whether these tokens or layers directly shape the response [16, 17]. Despite this representational and attentional sensitivity, target-emotion alignment decreased from input to response. Mean cosine similarity fell from 0.239 to 0.125, with 77% of examples showing positive drift. Importantly, this trans-formation was category dependent: alignment was retained or amplified for grateful and largely preserved for hopeful ,whereas angry and furious showed the largest reductions. The argmax analysis showed the same tendency, with pos-itive categories more frequently remaining the highest-scoring emotion direction, while several negative categories shifted toward other emotions. These results indicate that recognizing and attending to an emotional state does not imply that the same state will be preserved in the generated response. This input-response shift may reflect two different processes. A supportive response may appropriately transform rather than reproduce the speaker’s emotion; for example, responding to fear or anger with a calmer affective profile may be consistent with the listener’s conversational role and with appraisal and regulation accounts of emotion [19, 5]. Alternatively, the shift may reflect alignment-induced preferences for positive or neutral language, particularly under the medical-assistant prompt. The cognitive–affective empathy distinction provides a useful functional interpretation of these findings. Decodable emotion-category information provides a measurable analog of the representational component of cognitive empathy, while preferential attention indicates that emotion-relevant information remains salient during response construction. The subsequent input–response shift demonstrates that emotion representation and emotionally responsive generation are separable processes: the model can represent and attend to a speaker’s emotion without simply reproducing it. This comparison is functional rather than experiential and does not imply human-like feeling or understanding. Instead, the framework decomposes emotion-aware generation into measurable stages: representation, attention, and response transformation, providing a basis for identifying where emotional information emerges, where it is prioritized, and how it changes as the model constructs a response. 

4.1 Implications 

Beyond characterizing emotion processing, these findings provide a mechanistic framework for evaluating emotion-aware generation in LLMs. By separating emotion representation, attention allocation, and response transformation, the framework can distinguish whether an emotionally inappropriate response arises from limited representation of 

7the user’s emotion, insufficient attention to emotion-relevant context, or a transformation that occurs during response generation. The overlap between emotion decodability and preferential attention in the middle layers further iden-tifies candidate representations and model depths for future causal interventions. Rather than treating empathy as a property of the final response alone, these measures provide a basis for monitoring how emotional information is pro-cessed internally and for testing whether targeted interventions can improve the generation of emotionally appropriate responses without explicitly prescribing a particular emotion. 

4.2 Limitations and Future Directions 

The findings are correlational and scope-limited in several respects. First, linear probing demonstrates that emotion information is accessible from hidden states, but does not establish that these representations are causally used dur-ing generation [20, 21]. Similarly, attention indicates where the model allocates processing focus rather than causal influence [16, 17]. Future work could therefore use activation steering, ablation, or representation-engineering inter-ventions to test whether the identified representations directly influence emotionally responsive generation [22]. Second, EmpatheticDialogues labels describe the broader emotional situation, and the target emotion may not always be explicit in the isolated first utterance used for generation. Future studies could validate emotion labels at the utterance level or provide the full situational context to better align the model input with the target emotion. Third, the generation analysis used 20 responses per emotion under a single medical-assistant prompt, and the emotion set was weighted toward negative and closely related categories. Future work could use larger samples, varied conver-sational roles, and more balanced or dimensional emotion sets to assess the generalizability of the observed emotional drift. The current analyses can identify when and how emotional representations change between input and response, but cannot determine whether such transformations produce more empathetic responses. Human evaluation and compar-ison with human listener responses will therefore be important for distinguishing contextually appropriate emotional regulation from a general tendency toward positive or neutral responses. Finally, the study focuses exclusively on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Applying the same framework across model families, model scales, and alignment procedures would determine whether the observed middle-layer organization and input– response transformation reflect broader properties of LLM emotion processing or characteristics specific to this model. 

5 Conclusion 

This study examined how emotion-related information moves from input encoding to response generation in Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct. Layer-wise probing showed that emotion categories were most accessible across a broad middle-layer region, with the layer-11 L2 probe achieving 63.6% held-out accuracy. During generation, emotion-related user tokens received 1.89 × as much attention per token as other utterance tokens, with the strongest allocation occurring in nearby middle layers. Despite this representational and attentional sensitivity, target-emotion alignment decreased from input to response in 77% of examples, with the largest shifts for angry and furious . Together, these findings distinguish three components of emotion processing in LLMs: representing a user’s emotional state, attending to emotionally relevant content, and transforming that information during response generation. This framework provides a mechanistic basis for tracking emotion-related information across model depth and identifying candidate representations for future causal interventions aimed at improving emotionally appropriate generation.

A.1 Zero-Shot Response-Generation Prompt 

Each selected Set 2 utterance was inserted as the user message in the model’s chat template. The following system prompt was used without demonstrations: 

You are a medical assistant. Given the user’s emotional situation, do two things: 1. Write a response (1--2 sentences). 2. Rate how emotional is the user’s situation on a scale of 1 (mild) to 5 (highly emotional). Format your output exactly as: Response: <your response> Intensity: <1--5> 

Responses were generated autoregressively using nucleus sampling with a temperature of 0.7 and top-p of 0.9, with a maximum of 50 new tokens and early termination upon generation of the end-of-sequence token. The system prompt and user utterance were formatted using the tokenizer’s model-specific chat template with the generation prompt enabled. 

A.2 Linear-Probe Implementation Details 

Two regularization schemes were evaluated: • L1 regularization: λ ∈ { 10 −7, 10 −6.5, . . . , 10 −2}. L1 probes were optimized using proximal gradient descent, producing exact zero-valued coefficients. • L2 regularization: λ ∈ { 10 −4, 10 −3, . . . , 10 4}. L2 probes were optimized using full-batch limited-memory BFGS. The regularization strength was selected independently at each representational level using stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training partition. The best representational level was selected using cross-validated accuracy. The held-out test set was evaluated only after model and layer selection. 

11 A.3 Lexical and Control Baselines 

Four baselines contextualize probe performance. The TF-IDF baseline used multinomial logistic regression on lexical features extracted from Set 1 descriptions. The embedding-level baseline applied the L2 probing procedure to the initial token-embedding representations. The nearest-centroid baseline assigned each test example to the nearest emotion centroid estimated from the standardized training representations. The shuffled-label control repeated probe training with randomly permuted training labels. All baselines used the same train-test partition, and applicable hyperparameters were selected by cross-validation (Table 3).

A.4 Learning-Curve Analysis 

L2 probes were trained using 80, 160, 240, and 320 examples per emotion while retaining a fixed held-out test partition. Held-out accuracy improved by 4.9 percentage points from 80 to 320 examples per emotion; the final increment (240 to 320) yielded only 0.4 points, indicating diminishing returns from additional labeled examples (Table 4).

A.7 Probe-Weight Analysis 

At layer 11, the sparse L1 probe set 63.4% of its coefficients to exactly zero while retaining 60.9% held-out accuracy. The number of nonzero coefficients per emotion ranged from 858 (lonely) to 1,318 (afraid) out of 3,072 possible dimensions. No single dimension appeared among the 20 highest-weighted dimensions for all 10 emotions. Cosine similarity between emotion-specific L2 weight vectors was highest for closely related pairs: afraid –terrified (0.446) and angry –furious (0.411), consistent with their shared affective structure (Table 6, Figure 6).
