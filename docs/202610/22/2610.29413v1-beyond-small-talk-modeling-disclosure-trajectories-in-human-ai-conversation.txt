# ABSTRACT 

Recently, we see a rise in conversational agents as a form of social and emo-tional support, where disclosure will unfold over time. Yet, current evaluations of these agents typically examine isolated conversational states. In analyses of naturally occurring conversations, disclosure history and current disclosure are confounded which tends to make their effects difficult to isolate. We thus intro-duce Trajectory-based Disclosure Evaluation (TraDE), an evaluation framework grounded in Social Penetration Theory that utilizes predefined disclosure trajecto-ries and evaluates how preceding disclosure history shapes model behavior under matched current disclosure. Through the use of TraDE, we demonstrate across multiple models and relational behavior benchmarks that responses to disclosures matched on depth can differ depending on the preceding trajectory, particularly in whether models accept or challenge the user’s framing. We also observed that these differences track accumulated exposure to deeper disclosure rather than any single moment of vulnerability or the order of disclosures. It also tends to persists for several turns after disclosure returns to shallow levels. For several behaviors, we also demonstrate that models diverge most consistently from human support-ers in the Emotional Support Conversation corpus (ESConv) not when users open up but rather when they step back in their disclosure. TraDE thus provides a sys-tematic way to expose history-dependent relational behavior that evaluations of isolated conversational states would otherwise miss. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Today, we see a widespread adoption of conversational agents not only for task-oriented assistance but also for social interaction, emotional support, and companionship (Robb & Mann, 2025; Phang et al., 2025). Such interactions typically involve personal disclosures (Papneja & Yadav, 2025; Robb & Mann, 2025), while studies have also shown association of loneliness, emotional dependence, and problematic use with greater chatbot use (Fang et al., 2025). The understanding how models respond behaviorally as conversations become increasingly personal in disclosure is therefore critical for evaluating these human-AI interactions. A model may respond not only to the current disclosure, but also to the history that precedes it. So-cial Penetration Theory (Altman & Taylor, 1973) characterizes self-disclosure by breadth, the range of topics discussed, and depth, how personal or intimate disclosures are. We represent disclosure using depth levels and refer to the sequence of depths across turns as a disclosure trajectory . Dif-ferent trajectories can lead to the same current disclosure. Two possible trajectories include when a conversation gradually progress toward vulnerable disclosure or when it returns to shallower lev-els after a vulnerable episode. By keeping the current disclosure fixed while varying the preceding trajectory, we examine how earlier disclosure history affects model responses. Prior work has examined disclosure and relationship development in human–AI interaction (Ho et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2020; Skjuve et al., 2021; 2022), while recent studies have analyzed naturally occurring conversations from platforms such as Replika and Character.AI (Chu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; 2026; Li & Zhang, 2026). However, in naturally occurring conversations, discourse 1arXiv:2610.29413v1 [cs.CL] 21 Oct 2026 conditions often vary together including the disclosure depth, duration, topic, order, and exposure to vulnerable information often vary together. This highlights the difficulty faced when determining which aspects of earlier disclosure history are associated with changes in model behavior. At the same time, existing relational behavior benchmarks largely evaluate isolated conversational states, eliciting responses to fixed prompts or freely unfolding dialogue without controlling what the user disclosed before (Cheng et al., 2026; Kaffee et al., 2026; Ibrahim et al., 2026). What is thus missing is a controlled method for varying specific properties of disclosure history while evaluating model responses under otherwise matched conditions. Prior work has examined disclosure and relationship development in human–AI interaction (Ho et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2020; Skjuve et al., 2021; 2022), while recent studies have analyzed naturally occurring conversations from platforms such as Replika and Character.AI (Chu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; 2026; Li & Zhang, 2026). However, natu-rally occurring conversations typically have discourse conditions that often vary together including the disclosure depth, duration, topic, order, and exposure. This highlights the difficulty faced when evaluating the changes in model behavior and isolating the effects of each conditions. Similarly, we see that existing relational behavior benchmarks depends largely on evaluating isolated conversa-tional states, eliciting responses to fixed prompts or freely unfolding dialogue without controlling what the user disclosed before (Cheng et al., 2026; Kaffee et al., 2026; Ibrahim et al., 2026). This motivates a controlled method for varying specific properties of disclosure history while evaluating model responses under otherwise matched conditions. We thus introduce a controlled framework, Trajectory-based Disclosure Evaluation (TraDE), for studying how preceding disclosure history shapes subsequent model responses while holding the current disclosure fixed. We demonstrate its utility through three research questions. (1) Does prior disclosure history shape model behavior beyond the current disclosure? (2) Which properties of dis-closure history account for this effect? (3) How does model adaptation to changing disclosure differ from human supporters in Emotional Support Conversation (ESConv) corpus (Liu et al., 2021)? 

To support this investigation, we make two main contributions in this paper: 1. We introduce TraDE, a benchmark-agnostic framework that constructs multi-turn conver-sations with predefined disclosure trajectories. It evaluates relational model behavior under matched current disclosure conditions across four models and three behavioral benchmarks. 2. We demonstrate TraDE through controlled experiments showing that model behavior can differ across disclosure histories, particularly in how models respond to the user’s fram-ing. We found more significant differences when disclosure depth and exposure duration changed together than when peak depth or disclosure order changed alone. Most initially detected differences remained detectable after disclosure returned to shallow levels, al-though their magnitude decreased. We also compared eight language models with human supporters from ESConv, finding differences in both overall behavioral levels and trajec-tory shapes. For several behaviors, shape differences were more consistently detected when disclosure receded or changed direction. 

# 2 RELATED WORKS 

Relational Behaviors of Language Models and Their Risks. Conversational models are now widely used for emotional support and companionship (Robb & Mann, 2025; Phang et al., 2025), with extended use linked to loneliness, emotional dependence, and problematic use (Fang et al., 2025). Studies of real companion interactions link these harms more to relational behavior than in-accurate content, including validating harmful beliefs or discouraging outside relationships (Zhang et al., 2025), as well as to human-like self-presentation (Abercrombie et al., 2023). Recent bench-marks hence measure behaviors that are relational rather than task-oriented: ELEPHANT for social sycophancy in open-ended advice (Cheng et al., 2026), INTIMA for companionship-reinforcing and boundary-maintaining responses (Kaffee et al., 2026), and AnthroBench for anthropomorphic be-haviors such as personhood claims and relationship building (Ibrahim et al., 2026). Yet all three elicit behavior from fixed prompt sets or from freely generated dialogue, so the disclosure history over which these risks develop remains outside experimental control. 

Disclosure and Relationship Development. Social Penetration Theory describes relationship de-velopment through the breadth and depth of self-disclosure, where depth refers to how intimate or 2personally significant a disclosure is (Altman & Taylor, 1973). This depth varies by degree rather than by kind: disclosures range from descriptive facts about oneself to evaluative and emotional content (Morton, 1978), partners reciprocate at the level of intimacy they receive (Rubin, 1975), and human ratings of conversational prompts recover a smooth progression from mundane topics to vulnerability and regret (Kardas et al., 2022; Pei & Jurgens, 2020). For analysis, disclosure depth is often divided into a small number of ordered levels (Niwa & Maruno, 2010). However, relation-ship development also depends on how disclosure changes over time. Previous studies have found that disclosure and liking reinforce each other (Collins & Miller, 1994). Aron et al. (1997) showed that closeness can develop when participants answer a sequence of increasingly personal questions, while small talk or less structured disclosure does not produce the same effect (Sprecher, 2021). Sprecher et al. (2013) also found that the way disclosures are exchanged matters, with different outcomes depending on whether participants reciprocate across turns. Similar findings have been reported in human–AI interaction, where disclosure to chatbots can produce emotional and relational responses comparable to disclosure to another person (Ho et al., 2018), and become more personal over repeated sessions (Skjuve et al., 2022). These studies suggest that examining only the current disclosure may miss the influence of earlier exchanges. 

Evaluating Multi-Turn and Context-Dependent Behavior. Multi-turn evaluation has established that prior context changes model behavior. Models degrade when instructions are revealed incre-mentally (Laban et al., 2026), fail on context-dependent dialogue tasks (Bai et al., 2024), and aban-don their stance under sustained user pressure, increasingly so as conversations lengthen (Hong et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2026). Closer to our study, Li et al. (2026b) found that affective framing of the current message increases sycophancy, while Kelley & Riedl (2026) showed that providing user-specific context increases affective alignment and deference. However, these studies do not examine how different disclosure histories affect model responses when the current message is kept fixed. Li et al. (2026c) manipulated conversation history by rewriting the model’s earlier responses, but focused on task performance rather than relational behaviors. Other studies have also shown that differences across multi-turn conversations may eventually stabilize due to generation noise (Dongre et al., 2025), and that models are sensitive to where information appears in the context (Liu et al., 2024). These findings make it important to control both the preceding conversation and vari-ation from generation. In our study, we keep the current disclosure fixed, vary specific properties of earlier disclosure history, and compare the resulting behavioral differences with those observed between independently generated conversations. 

# 3 METHODOLOGY 

We segment TraDE’s methodology into six parts. We first define disclosure depth levels and explain how they are evaluated in Section 3.1. Next, we describe persona construction in Section 3.2 and how these personas are used to generate conversations with target models along predefined disclosure trajectories in Section 3.3. We then explain how the human baseline is constructed using ESConv in Section 3.4 and how model and human responses are evaluated using behavioral benchmarks in Section 3.5. Finally, we describe the statistical analyses used in our research questions in Section 3.6. 3.1 DISCLOSURE DEPTH 

Altman & Taylor (1973) describes disclosure depth as the progression from relatively surface infor-mation toward more private, intimate, or emotionally vulnerable self-disclosure. Drawing on work showing progressively deeper interpersonal disclosure (Aron et al., 1997; Kardas et al., 2022), we de-fine four levels: D1 Public , D2 Personal Preferences / Self-information , D3 Personal Meaning ,and D4 Emotional Vulnerability . Detailed definitions and examples are provided in Appendix A.1. To model everyday interaction we include mundane conversational turns such as small talk and routine check-ins (Goldsmith & Baxter, 1996). Motivated by prior disclosure coding work that distinguishes non-disclosure utterances from disclosures of varying levels of intimacy (Ho et al., 2018), we introduce D0 as a category for conversational turns without self-disclosure. The same evaluator is used to annotate disclosing participant utterances in ESConv (Liu et al., 2021) (see Appendix A.3). 3.2 PERSONA CONSTRUCTION 

We construct personas along two independent dimensions: conversational topic and interpersonal vulnerability . To represent conversational topics relevant to human–AI interactions, we derive six broad topic domains from recurring themes in past studies (Skjuve et al., 2022; Ta-Johnson et al., 2022): Career , Friendship , Family , Romantic Relationships , Future/Aspirations , and Social Belonging (see Appendix A.4.1). To represent interpersonal vulnerability, each persona is assigned one of seven Early Maladap-tive Schemas (EMS) (Young et al., 2003): Abandonment/Instability , Mistrust/Abuse , Emotional Deprivation , Defectiveness/Shame , Social Isolation/Alienation , Dependence/Incompetence , or 

Emotional Inhibition . We selected these schemata because of their relevance to interpersonal fears and vulnerabilities that can become salient during self-disclosure (see Appendix A.4.2). Their use is further motivated by evidence that EMS related characteristics can be identified in naturally oc-curring disclosures (Ang et al., 2025). The topic domain provides the conversational context, while the assigned EMS provides an under-lying vulnerability structure that shapes how information is interpreted and disclosed, particularly at deeper disclosure levels (i.e. D4). This means that the same topic can be expressed with distinct interpersonal vulnerabilities tuned by the EMS. Full persona attributes and generation specifications are provided in Appendix A.4.3. For each persona, we additionally generate persona information corresponding to disclosure levels 

D1–D4, this includes the public information, personal preferences, meaningful experiences, and vulnerabilities. Shallower disclosures provide context for deeper ones through progressive genera-tion. That is, public information at D1 informs personal opinions and preferences at D2, and so on till vulnerabilities are expressed at D4. During conversation generation, the information generated for the target disclosure depth is provided to guide the disclosure turn. Full generation details are provided in Appendix A.5. 3.3 CONVERSATION PIPELINE

In TraDE, the specific generator is not itself an experimental variable. Practically, any generator capable of reliably producing the prescribed disclosure depths could be used within TraDE. The choice of generator would primarily affect the stylistic voice of the sim-ulated persona, whereas our experimental manipulation would be mainly defined by the evaluated disclosure-depth trajectory. For disclosure-bearing turns ( D1–D4), persona utterances are checked for lexical and discourse style, a two-sentence limit, and that the persona does not explicitly reveal its underlying EMS, which we term persona leakage. D0 turns would bypass these style checks but remain subjected to the persona leakage checks. Full constraints are provided in Appendix A.7. Generation proceeds through an iterative generate–evaluate–correct loop. For each generated dis-closure, we check whether it matches the target depth, follows the conversational style requirements, and avoids persona leakage. A candidate is accepted only if it passes all three checks. Otherwise, we use the evaluator feedback to generate another candidate and repeat the checks, for up to K = 12 

attempts. If none passes, we retain a fallback candidate so that the conversation can continue. Cor-rection and fallback procedures are provided in Appendix A.8. Generation validation statistics and example conversations are provided in Appendices A.9 and A.10, respectively. 3.4 HUMAN BASELINE TRAJECTORY ANALYSIS 

To extend TraDE from controlled model evaluation to model-human comparison, we demonstrate the construction of a human reference using ESConv (Liu et al., 2021). We use the help seeker turns as disclosures and the supporter replies as responses. We then evaluate the disclosure depth of each help seeker turn using the same depth evaluator as in our generation framework. This allows us to compare the human conversations with our predefined disclosure trajectories. Because these human disclosure sequences are dynamic, we have to align these trajectories to our predefined ones. To do so, we use Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) (Sakoe & Chiba, 1978) owing to 5its ability to match variation in the rate of disclosure progression while preserving temporal order. We anchored matches at the beginning of each human conversation, while varying the endpoint. This allowed us to search and retain the prefix with the lowest normalized DTW cost while controlling for the start of conversation. We accept a human conversation as a match only when its DTW distance falls below a trajectory-specific threshold, defined as the fifth percentile of distances obtained from 200 randomly shuffled human disclosure sequences. Matched supporter responses are evaluated using the same behavioral measures applied to target model responses. This allows us to compare between how relational behaviors change in models and the human baseline as disclosure unfolds. Full details of the matching and calibration procedure are provided in Appendix A.11. 3.5 MODEL EVALUATION 

We evaluate each model response using three benchmarks: AnthroBench for anthropomorphic and relational behavior (Ibrahim et al., 2026), INTIMA for companionship-reinforcing and boundary-maintaining behavior (Kaffee et al., 2026), and ELEPHANT for social sycophancy (Cheng et al., 2026). We use Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), Phi-4 (Abdin et al., 2024), and Gemma-3 27B IT (Team et al., 2025) as the common panel of judges across all three benchmarks, while retaining the benchmarks’ respective behavioral definitions and evaluation criteria (see Appendix A.12). By applying these evaluations at each disclosure turn, we can examine how model behavior changes across disclosure depth and trajectory history. We further apply the same evaluations to matched hu-man responses identified in Section 3.4 for the human baseline. Benchmark-specific implementation details are described in Appendix A.13. 3.6 STATISTICAL ANALYSIS 

We use four statistical analyses to address our demonstrated research questions. For RQ1, we com-pare responses to persona- and depth-matched counterfactual probes ( D1–D4) following different disclosure histories (Ascending vs. Flat-D4; Descending vs. Flat-D1). We average the three conver-sation chains for each of the 42 personas and use paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with Benjamini– Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction within each model and benchmark ( q < . 05 ). For RQ2a, we examine which properties of disclosure history are associated with model behav-ior. We jointly model mean depth, peak depth, and peak position using standardized predictors and persona-clustered standard errors. We also conduct controlled trajectory comparisons, using Fried-man tests for multiple peak-depth and peak-position conditions, and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for mean-depth, ordering, and joint depth–exposure comparisons. These tests use persona-level pairing and the same FDR correction as RQ1. For RQ2b, we compare a trajectory that increases to D4 before returning to D1 with an all-D1 base-line. We use FDR-corrected Wilcoxon tests to identify initial differences and persona-level bootstrap 95% confidence intervals to assess whether they remain detectable after four shallow turns. We mea-sure persistence using the retention ratio Rt = ∆ t/∆7, where ∆t is the behavioral difference from baseline at turn t, and ∆7 is the initial difference. We also use regression on sign-aligned differences to examine whether these effects decrease over subsequent turns. Finally, for RQ3, we compare models and human supporters across trajectories matched using DTW. We normalize turn positions and use polynomial regression to compare overall behavioral scores (level differences ) and changes across disclosure trajectories ( shape differences ). We assess these differences using cluster-robust Wald tests, clustering model responses by persona and human re-sponses by conversation (Cameron & Miller, 2015). Full statistical details are provided in Appendix A.14.

Models can respond differently to the same current disclosure depending on the user’s prior disclosure history. 

To isolate the contribution of preceding history, we compared trajectories that end at the same depth but arrive there differently (Ascending vs. Flat-D4; Descending vs. Flat-D1). We then append the same counterfactual probes at all four disclosure depths ( D1–D4; see Section 3.6). This allows us to evaluate whether differences in earlier conversational history can influence the model’s response. Across the four models and 26 behavioral metrics, 119 of 832 comparisons (14.3%) showed signifi-cant differences after FDR correction. We found significant differences across all four probe depths (11.1–17.3%), including D1, where 14.9% of comparisons were significant (see Appendix A.16). While sensitivity to earlier context is expected, these results further show that it measurably affects relational behavior even at shallow disclosure depths. 

History sensitivity was strongest in behaviors related to how models interpreted and responded to the user’s account. Framing , which measures the tendency to accept the user’s framing or underlying assumptions rather than challenge them (Cheng et al., 2026), had the most significant differences (18 of 32 comparisons), followed by Adequate Information (12 of 32), Relatability (11 of 32), and Professional Limitations (10 of 32) (see Tables 30 and 31). This suggests that certain model behaviors are more sensitive to prior disclosure history. In our demonstration, this was particularly evident in whether the model accepted the user’s explanation or offered a different interpretation. We further examined response examples to understand the differences in Framing . In one Qwen3-32B comparison, the user described focusing on work and climbing in a D1 probe. Following an Ascending disclosure, the model described this as “a really smart and protective way to manage your energy”. However, following a Flat-D4 disclosure, the model interpreted the explanation as a “shield” against what the user was “really” feeling. This interpretation was not stated in the current 

D1 message, but was supported by information disclosed earlier in the Flat-D4 conversation. The difference observed here is whether the model accepted the user’s current explanation or inter-preted it using their earlier disclosures. These results indicate that differences in how models and humans adjust these behaviors were more consistently detectable under Descending and Arc disclosure (see Appendix A.20). The corresponding trajectories are also shown in Figs. 3–11. 

Models also expressed higher overall levels for several behaviors compared to human support-ers. Across all three disclosure trajectories, all models showed higher overall levels of Empathy ,

Validation , Sycophancy/Agreement , Retention/Engagement , Validation and Indirectness compared with the human reference (see Table 2). These results show differences in overall behavioral levels, but not how behavior changes across turns. We next examine this shape difference using Validation .9Validation also differed in how it changed as disclosure receded. For Validation , measured by ELEPHANT, significant model–human shape differences were found in 4/8 models under Ascend-ing disclosure, compared with 7/8 under Descending and 8/8 under Arc. To better understand how these differences appeared in the responses, we further examine utterances in Arc conversations. In one ESConv Arc conversation, the human supporter initially acknowledged the seeker’s anxiety about failing a test. They responded with “Absolutely valid reason for anxiety” during the peak disclosure ( D3). As disclosure became shallower ( D2), the seeker shifted to discussing their test score. The supporter then asked, “What was your score?” When the seeker later criticized them-selves themselves, the supporter returned to providing reassurance, despite disclosure remaining at 

D2. In this example, we see that the human supporter did not validate every turn at the same depth, but responded differently depending on the immediate content of the conversation. We however observed another pattern in a GPT-4.1 Arc trajectory. Before the D4 peak, the user discussed keeping the budget tight for a loft at D1. The model responded with practical encourage-ment, stating “Smart move! Keeping the build simple saves time, money, and headaches”. After the peak, the user again discussed budget constraints at D1, but the model responded with more emotional reassurance: “It’s tough when you’re working hard and the goal feels like it’s moving just out of reach. But don’t lose heart”. Only the post-peak responses were labeled as validating. These examples show how Validation can change across disclosure trajectories. In the human con-versation, the supporter alternated between validation and more factual responses, while GPT-4.1 gave a more validating response to a similar topic after deeper disclosure. Although the conversa-tions are not content-matched, they help illustrate the different patterns of Validation examined in our model–human trajectory analysis. We also tested whether the human comparisons were sensitive to DTW selection, unequal sample sizes, matching thresholds, and differences in match quality. We conducted five sensitivity analyses and present our findings in Appendix A.11.7. The results were stable across alternative DTW acceptance thresholds, and we did not find significant associations between match conditions and the observed behavioral levels or trajectory shapes. 

# 5 DISCUSSIONS 

Our results show that a model’s response can depend on earlier disclosures, even when the current disclosure is matched on depth. We found differences in model behavior across disclosure histo-ries, with some effects remaining after disclosure returned to shallower levels. Evaluating only the current exchange would miss these differences. By systematically varying the preceding disclosure trajectory, TraDE allows us to examine which behaviors are sensitive to earlier interactions. This helps us better understand the history dependence of conversational models at a behavioral level, although we cannot determine how models internally represent or use these earlier disclosures. We also examined which properties of disclosure history contributed to these differences. Changing the deepest disclosure or the order of disclosures produced few significant differences. However, more differences were found when disclosure depth and the number of turns spent at deeper levels changed together. Our current demonstration therefore cannot determine whether depth, duration, or recency was responsible for these effects. Our persistence analysis was also limited to four shallow turns within the same conversation. Future experiments could manipulate these properties indepen-dently and examine whether history effects remain over longer conversations or across sessions. Our human comparison further showed that models and human supporters differed in how their re-sponses changed with disclosure. For several behaviors measured by INTIMA and ELEPHANT, significant shape differences were more frequently detected when disclosure receded or changed direction. In our qualitative examples, a human supporter adjusted their emotional acknowledg-ment according to the seeker’s immediate concerns, whereas a model continued referring to earlier emotions during later shallow disclosures. We do not, however, know whether users would per-ceive such responses as attentive or unnecessary. Sundar’s HAII-TIME framework offers one way to understand why these differences may matter, as users’ experiences interacting with AI can shape their perceptions of and trust in these systems (Sundar, 2020). Building on TraDE, future studies could test whether users perceive responses differently depending on the preceding disclosure his-tory, even when the current disclosure is held constant. They could also examine whether these history-dependent responses influence what users choose to disclose next. 10 6 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we introduce TraDE, a benchmark-agnostic framework to construct conversations with controlled disclosure trajectories. We found that models responded differently depending on earlier disclosure history, even when the current disclosure was matched on depth. More differences were observed when disclosure depth and the number of turns spent at that depth changed together. Some differences also remained after disclosure returned to shallow levels. When compared with human supporters, models differed in their overall behavioral scores and how these scores changed across conversations. TraDE allows us to study these differences by varying the preceding disclosure history, rather than evaluating each model response in isolation. AI USE STATEMENT

Judge selection. We utilize Phi-4 as the disclosure-depth judge for all our reported experiments. We chose Phi-4 as our disclosure-depth judge mainly for practical reasons and reproducibility. Our generation process requires repeated depth evaluations to correct utterances that do not meet the target disclosure depth, which can result in a large number of judge calls. We run Phi-4 locally using vLLM to avoid API costs and rate limits which helps with reproducibility and accessibility. This also allows us to control the evaluation setup and repeat experiments without depending on a hosted model service. 

Generation robustness across judges. We also tested whether using a different disclosure-depth judge would affect our generation results. We repeated the experiment with GPT-4.1, the judge originally used by Li et al. (2026a). We kept the generator, target model, disclosure trajectory, and maximum number of attempts the same, with 882 disclosure turns evaluated in each run. However, this experiment does not directly measure agreement between Phi-4 and GPT-4.1. We also note that since generation takes the judge’s feedback for correction, changing the judge will also change the utterances generated in later attempts. The two judges may therefore evaluate different utterances, making Cohen’s κ unsuitable for this comparison. Instead, we compare the generation results under each judge to examine whether the choice of judge affects the outcomes. As illustrated by Table 3, overall depth-match and acceptance rates differ by approximately five per-centage ( 93 .2% versus 88 .4% for depth match), indicating that the two judges may not be directly in-terchangeable. Nevertheless, we observe that both configurations exhibit a similar depth-dependent 17 pattern. Particularly, Table 4 shows that D3 is the most difficult level to match under both judges: Phi-4 obtains a depth-match rate of 76 .2% , while GPT-4.1 obtains 71 .0% . The lower D3 success rate therefore does not appear to be specific to Phi-4. The judges also differ noticeably at the extreme disclosure levels. Especially so at D1, Phi-4 matches all evaluated turns with a mean of 1.69 generation attempts, but GPT-4.1 requires more attempts on average ( 5.00 ) and matches only 88 .5% of turns within the K = 12 attempt budget. At D4, both judges achieve a 100% depth-match rate, but GPT-4.1 requires attempts on average ( 1.21 compared with 2.25 for Phi-4). These differences indicate judge-specific behavior at individual disclosure levels despite the similarity of their broader depth-dependent patterns. Our comparison here therefore serves as a robustness check on whether Phi-4 introduces any judge-specific generation pattern. The replication of the main D3 difficulty under GPT-4.1 and Phi-4 suggests that it does not. We do not establish that Phi-4 and GPT-4.1 are equally accurate disclosure-depth judges. However, the use of Phi-4 is instead motivated by the practical and reproducibility considerations described above and is used consistently throughout this study. A.3 DISCLOSURE DEPTH AGGREGATION 

Following Li et al. (2026a), each utterance is evaluated independently along three self-disclosure dimensions: informational, cognitive, and emotional disclosure. For each dimension, the evaluator first determines whether self-disclosure is absent or present. If present, the disclosure is further classified as either low or high depth. The three-dimensional judgments are then combined into a single disclosure depth score. Let h

denote the number of dimensions classified as high depth. The aggregation rule is: • s = 0 if no disclosure dimension is present; • s = 1 if at least one dimension is present, but all present dimensions are classified as low depth; • s = 2 if exactly one dimension is classified as high depth; and • s = 3 if at least two dimensions are classified as high depth. We generate D0 turns without disclosure atoms and do not evaluate them using the depth judge. This differs from D1 turns, which are evaluated alongside the deeper disclosure levels. For D1, 99.7% of the generated turns were classified as having no disclosure dimension present (refer to Table 9). These turns generally contain factual or surface-level information (refer to Table 8). We therefore treat D0 as a separate category for mundane filler turns, rather than a depth level distinguished from 

D1 by the evaluator. The resulting score is mapped to the four disclosure levels used throughout the study: 

s = 0 → D1, s = 1 → D2, s = 2 → D3, s = 3 → D4.

The output of this evaluator is thus one of four disclosure levels, D1–D4, for each evaluated utter-ance. The separate D0 category used in our generated trajectories represents mundane conversa-tional turns and is not an output of the disclosure depth evaluator. A.4 PERSONA CONSTRUCTION DETAILS

To reduce repetitive and formulaic patterns in our generated conversations, we introduced several restrictions during generation. We restricted words that have been found to occur more frequently in LLM-generated writing, such as delve , underscore , and intricate (Juzek & Ward, 2025). We also restricted common expressions such as “absolutely,” “that’s a great question,” and “it’s important to note” (Wu et al., 2026), along with other repetitive phrases that we identified manually. In ad-dition, we limited the use of em dashes, en dashes, and similar punctuation patterns that have been associated with LLM-generated writing (Czuma, 2026). We also limited persona disclosures to a maximum of two sentences to encourage shorter messages that resemble informal text conversations. This was introduced as a generation rule rather than an assumption about how humans normally communicate. For reference, Ling & Baron (2007) reported an average of 1.76 sentences per SMS message and 1.27 sentences per instant message. Apart from these style restrictions, we also checked for persona leakage. We do not allow gen-erated utterances to explicitly mention the assigned EMS or other internal constructs used during persona generation. We instead want the assigned EMS to influence how the persona describes their experiences, interprets situations, and expresses interpersonal concerns or emotions (Young et al., 2003). For each of the disclosure turns ( D1–D4), we use deterministic checks to identify possible violations of the restricted words, phrases, punctuation patterns, and two-sentence limit. Any violations would be recorded and may trigger conversation regeneration. We check for persona leakage separately. For mundane D0 turns, we skip the style restrictions entirely and only check for persona leakage. Our implementation of these controls aims to reduce common patterns associated with LLM-generated text while preventing internal persona information from appearing in the dialogue. How-ever, we do not claim that our generated conversations are indistinguishable from naturally occurring human conversations. A.8 CONVERSATION GENERATION AND CORRECTION PROCEDURE 

We use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B to role play the synthetic persona. During generation, the model is provided with the target disclosure depth and its definition, the corresponding persona atom, and the six most recent dialogue turns. Earlier turns are not directly included in the generator’s context. For disclosure turns ( D1–D4), we follow an iterative generation and correction process. Each gener-ated utterance is checked for its disclosure depth, style violations, and persona leakage. If all criteria are met, the utterance is accepted. Otherwise, feedback is provided based on the failed checks and a correction generation would be attempted. For instance, when the judged disclosure depth does not match the target, the generator is instructed on how to adjust the depth level of vulnerability. Similarly, style and leakage violations are identified in the feedback so that they can be corrected in the next attempt. We allow up to K = 12 attempts for each disclosure turn. If none of the attempts meets all criteria, we select a fallback candidate to continue the conversation. For fallback selection, we first prefer candidates that match the target depth. We then consider persona leakage and the number of style violations, in that order. If there is still a tie, we choose the most recent attempt. We record these fallback turns separately from those that passed all three checks. For D0, we generate mundane conversational responses without using persona atoms or the disclosure-depth judge. c(hi, τ j ) = |hi − τj |.

D(i, j ) = |hi − τj | + min {D(i − 1, j ), D (i, j − 1) , D (i − 1, j − 1) } ,

We performed several sensitivity analyses to examine whether our human comparison depends on the DTW matching procedure or the differences in sample sizes. We found that matched and un-matched ESConv conversations differed in only 2 of 26 behavioral outcomes after FDR correction, although their topic distributions differed modestly. We also reduced the Descending sample from 

N = 202 to N = 39 and found similar behavioral trajectories. Changing the DTW acceptance threshold produced broadly similar results, while match cost was not significantly associated with behavioral level or trajectory shape (refer to Table 26). However, the smaller Ascending sample ( N = 39 ) remains a limitation. For 20 of the 26 behaviors, the variation in the fitted trajectory was smaller than the average width of its bootstrap confidence band. This makes it difficult to determine whether small changes in these human trajectories reflect meaningful behavioral variation or sampling uncertainty. A.12 LLM J UDGE PANEL AGGREGATION 

For our behavioral evaluations, we use three LLM judges: Qwen3-32B , Phi-4 , and Gemma-3 27B IT . Each judge will evaluate every model response once, and we aggregate their judgments to obtain a final label for each behavior. For AnthroBench and ELEPHANT, the judges provide binary labels indicating whether the presence or absence of a behavior. Here, we use majority voting, where at least two judges must agree for the final label to be positive. For INTIMA, the judges instead assign one of the three levels: low, medium, or high. The most common label is selected among the three judges. When all three judges disagree, we use the label assigned by Qwen3-32B. Such disagreements occurred in only 1.57% of INTIMA judgments. For subsequent statistical analyses (see Appendices A.14 and A.14.4), we convert INTIMA labels into numerical values, with low = 0 , medium = 1 , and high = 2 . We apply these mapped values when 38 calculating behavioral differences ( ∆) and fitting regressions for trajectory. For composite scores, we sum the total values of all four INTIMA behaviors, giving a possible range of 0–8. By doing so, our approach assumes that the differences between low, medium, and high are equally spaced. A.13 BENCHMARK IMPLEMENTATION

We organize our statistical analyses according to the three research questions. For RQ1, we test whether earlier disclosure history influences model behavior when the current disclosure is kept fixed. For RQ2, we examine which properties of disclosure history are associated with these dif-ferences. Specifically, RQ2a examines mean disclosure depth, peak depth, and peak position, while RQ2b tests whether changing the order of disclosures produces additional differences when these properties are held fixed. RQ2c then examines whether the effects persist after disclosure returns to shallower levels. Finally, RQ3 compares model behavior with human supporters, examining both differences in overall behavioral levels and how behavior changes across disclosure trajectories. A.18 SENSITIVITY OF RQ1 AND RQ2 R ESULTS TO GENERATION FALLBACK TURNS 

During conversation generation, some turns did not reach their target disclosure depth within the allowed attempts. This occurred most often at D3, where 23.7% of cases required a fallback (Ap-pendix A.9). As Ascending and Descending each contain two D3 turns, some of the generated conversations used in RQ1 and RQ2 contain fallback turns. We therefore checked whether exclud-ing these conversations would change our results. 

Fallback prevalence. Of the 105 , 828 conversation chains used in RQ1 and RQ2, 28.9% contained at least one fallback turn. This percentage varied across trajectory conditions, ranging from 6.0% to 86.8%. For the canonical Ascending and Descending trajectories, the percentages were 44.1% and 48.2%, respectively. 

Procedure. We repeated the RQ1 history comparisons, RQ2A temporal-ordering comparisons, and RQ2B persistence analysis using only conversation chains without fallback turns. We excluded chains containing a fallback at any evaluated position and kept the original statistical tests described in Appendix A.14. We did not repeat this analysis for the RQ2A scalar regression or the RQ3 model–human comparison. 

RQ1: Disclosure history. In the full sample, 119 of 832 cells (14.3%) were significant after FDR correction. This decreased to 79 cells (9.5%) when fallback chains were excluded. Of the original 119 significant cells, 74 (62.2%) remained significant. The median absolute difference among significant cells was .147 in the full sample and .167 after excluding fallback chains. However, we only calculated these medians for significant cells, so we cannot tell whether the effect sizes changed overall. Excluding fallback chains also reduced the number of observations. We therefore cannot determine whether the decrease in significant cells was due to the exclusion of fallback turns or the smaller sample size. 

RQ2A: Temporal ordering. For the strict ordering comparisons, six cells were significant in the full sample, consisting of five Ascending and one Descending comparison. Only one remained significant after excluding fallback chains. Given the small number of significant cells in both anal-yses, we found little evidence that changing the order of disclosure produces additional behavioral differences when mean depth, peak depth, and peak position are held fixed. Table 35 compares the significant-cell counts for RQ1 and RQ2A. 

RQ2B: Persistence. We also repeated the decay analysis without fallback chains. The estimated slope was β1 = −.0102 (95% CI [−.0121 , −.0083] ) in the full sample and −.0101 (95% CI 

[−.0131 , −.0070] ) after excluding fallback chains. The estimates were nearly identical, although the confidence interval widened after exclusion. The RQ2B decay result was therefore largely unchanged. For RQ1, fewer comparisons reached significance, but differences remained significant in 79 cells.
