Abstract 

Psychotherapy fidelity assessment measures how faithfully a therapist adheres to a treat-ment protocol, a critical determinant of pos-itive patient outcomes. However, fidelity as-sessment is bottlenecked by manual expert re-view, rendering the process labor-intensive and unscalable. To scale therapy supervision and support clinician self-learning, we present an integrated application of large language mod-els (LLMs) that automates psychotherapy fi-delity workflows and generates actionable su-pervisory feedback from authentic, long-form clinical transcripts. We evaluate three open-weight models across multiple task decompo-sition configurations and reasoning strategies, demonstrating that evidence-first reasoning im-proves fidelity estimation. Furthermore, we establish the framework’s external construct va-lidity on an independent randomized controlled trial (RCT), where it reliably distinguishes Cog-nitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) from non-CBT sessions across core therapeutic skills. Through a user study that evaluates the gen-erated feedback, mental health experts indicate that the pipeline synthesizes actionable clini-cal feedback. Ultimately, our work offers a practical, scalable solution toward bridging the community mental health supervision gap. 

1 Introduction 

Serious mental illness (SMI) affects approximately 14.6 million people in the United States, with preva-lence rising sharply among young adults, particu-larly for depression and psychosis (American Psy-chological Association, 2019). At the same time, mental health systems are under increasing strain: over half of clinical practices report no availability for new patients (American Psychological Associa-tion, 2023). This capacity gap is compounded by the high labor cost of delivering and monitoring evidence-based therapy, since even gold-standard treatments depend on consistent, high-fidelity de-livery that is currently difficult to sustain at scale. Evidence-based psychotherapies for SMI, such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), rely on structured protocols, and adherence is one of the strongest predictors of positive patient out-comes (Roberts and Yeager, 2004; Spring, 2007; Cook et al., 2017). Fidelity is how faithfully and competently a therapist delivers the treatment man-ual. For psychosis, it is measured using the Cogni-tive Therapy Scale for Psychosis (CTS-Psy) (Had-dock et al., 2001). However, fidelity assessment is a labor-intensive process requiring trained experts: reviewing a single session often takes longer than the session itself (Waltman et al., 2017) (Figure 1). As a result, systematic fidelity assessment is rarely performed at scale in community settings, depriving clinicians of the timely, performance-based feedback needed for training and reducing the burden on expert supervisors. LLMs offer a path to closing this supervision gap. Prior automated fidelity work is dominated by transformer classifiers and feature-engineered mod-els (Creed et al., 2022; Stanco, 2021; Flemotomos et al., 2022; Zainal et al., 2025). In contrast, LLMs 1arXiv:2610.28772v1 [cs.CL] 20 Oct 2026 can reason over an entire de-identified transcript in natural language, apply rubric criteria item-by-item, and return both a rating and supporting evi-dence, augmenting a human supervisor’s judgment at a fraction of the time and cost. This has moti-vated a growing line of work that operationalizes clinical rubrics as LLM evaluation pipelines, typi-cally adapting the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) to score CBT adherence and benchmark model outputs against human clinicians (Huang et al., 2026; Chiu et al., 2024; Bedi et al., 2026; Te-ferra et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2024; Rollwage et al., 2026), with recent results suggesting LLMs can approach or exceed clinician-level competence at scale (Rollwage et al., 2026). Together, this evi-dence suggests that rubric-level clinical reasoning is within reach for current models. However, this body of work overwhelmingly evaluates LLMs in the role of the therapist rather than as an assessment tool for real clinical ses-sions. It remains unclear whether the same models can serve as a practical, therapist-facing tool that assesses authentic, expert-rated human sessions, where transcripts are noisy and run far longer than a typical chat. In this work, we take an application-first stance: rather than proposing a new architec-ture, we ask whether existing open-weight LLMs can support real fidelity-assessment workflows. Toward this goal, our contributions are as fol-lows. (1) Automated fidelity assessment: We sys-tematically evaluate three open-weight LLMs on automated fidelity assessment across 420 authentic, long-form CBT transcripts. Grounded in 1,800 fine-grained expert ratings across the 60-item CTS-Psy instrument, this supervisory dataset is comparable in scale to prior benchmarks while reflecting full-length, real clinical encounters. Using prediction powered inference, we quantifying uncertainty on 288 unlabeled sessions by correcting for systematic biases. We demonstrate that fine-grained category decomposition paired with evidence-first reasoning achieves the highest accuracy and tightest human alignment, with a 9B model (Qwen3.5-9B) mini-mizing systematic evaluation bias. (2) External validation: We establish external construct valid-ity on an independent RCT dataset of 102 tran-scripts, demonstrating that our approach reliably distinguishes CBT from non-CBT arms across core therapeutic skill dimensions. (3) Feedback gen-eration and evaluation: Finally, we develop an automated pipeline that translates underlying fi-delity assessments into structured, clinician-facing supervisor feedback. Through a structured user study with mental health experts, we demonstrate that the pipeline successfully synthesizes structured and actionable clinical suggestions. 

2 Related Work 

Early work on automated psychotherapy fi-delity assessment predicts CBT fidelity codes with transformer-based or neural models (Creed et al., 2022; Stanco, 2021), scores motivational-interviewing fidelity with LSTMs and support vec-tor regression (Flemotomos et al., 2022), or com-bines classical NLP features with machine learn-ing to evaluate coach fidelity in guided digital CBT (Zainal et al., 2025). A more recent line of work uses LLMs to operationalize clinical rubrics directly, typically adapting the CTRS to score CBT adherence over multi-turn sessions. Here, some use these scores as reward signals for training ther-apy models (Huang et al., 2026), others benchmark LLM-generated therapy against expert-labeled ses-sions (Chiu et al., 2024) or compare model out-puts against human clinicians in CBT (Bedi et al., 2026) and motivational interviewing (Teferra et al., 2026). Specifically, Rollwage et al. (2026) show enhanced LLMs can approach or exceed clinician-level CTRS competence at deployment scale. Our work differs from this LLM-based line of work in three ways. First, these studies evaluate LLMs in the role of the therapist, scoring synthetic, role-played, or model-generated dialogue; we in-stead use LLMs as a therapist-facing assessment tool applied to authentic sessions delivered by hu-man therapists to real patients, providing scalable fidelity feedback rather than benchmarking gener-ated therapy. Second, we assess fidelity using the CTS-Psy rather than the more general CTRS, cap-turing psychosis-specific competencies that CTRS does not target. Third, prior work typically treats fi-delity scoring as a single end-to-end judgment; we instead treat it as a task whose accuracy depends on inference structure, decomposing the rubric and comparing reasoning strategies, and we evaluate generalization beyond a single dataset. 

3 Data 

3.1 Study: Open Trial 

Our open trial examined the effectiveness of in-person therapy combined with mobile sensing for improving social functioning in individuals with SMI. Here we describe only the aspects relevant to 2therapy fidelity estimation. The intervention con-sisted of eight weekly, 60-minute individual ther-apy sessions delivered in person to 46 participants aged 18 to 65 over a 24-week period. Eligibil-ity required a clinical diagnosis of schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, bipolar I disorder with psychotic features, or depression with psychosis (National Institute of Mental Health, 2025), along with clinically significant social withdrawal and sta-ble medication status; demographics, full inclusion, and exclusion criteria are listed in Appendix §D. 

Therapy. Each participant received eight weekly, 60-minute in-person CBT-based therapy sessions delivered by Master’s-level therapists, who guided patients in developing skills to support their social goals through cognitive restructuring, a structured method for identifying and challeng-ing dysfunctional thoughts (Burns and Beck, 1978; Clark and Beck, 2011; Clark, 2013), and the 3Cs framework to reduce defeatist beliefs and social avoidance. A total of 331 therapy sessions were obtained, of which 13 were excluded due to in-complete or corrupted recordings, yielding a final sample of 318 sessions (45 participants). 

CTS-Psy Scale (Haddock et al., 2001). The CTS-Psy assesses fidelity across two 5-category sections (60 items total per session; Appendix §B): general skills (Table 2; agenda, feedback, under-standing, interpersonal effectiveness, collaboration) and specific skills (Table 3; guided discovery, key cognitions, choice of interventions, homework, so-cial skills training). Items are rated on a non-sequential scale: 0 (inappropriately omitted), 1 (appropriately included), or 9 (appropriately omit-ted). We map both 1 and 9 directly to a binary compliance score of 1, as both represent "clinical non-errors" where the therapist acted appropriately. As the original instrument collapses 9 to 1 post-hoc, we sought to target this final compliance threshold directly to streamline inference. Note that, if a ther-apist establishes an agenda, item 2 of that category receives a 1, otherwise 0. Two expert supervisors rated 30 unique session transcripts sampled from the 318 recordings, with an overlapping 10-session subset evaluated by both to assess reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0 .71 , substantial agreement). Across these 30 full-length sessions evaluated on the 60-item instrument, our protocol yields 1,800 fine-grained expert judgments. This volume is comparable to prior work while extend-ing evaluation to long-form, real clinical dialogues (Table 4; Appendix §D.2). Notably, reliability is computed over 600 ratings (10 sessions), exceeding the sample size needed to detect κ ≥ 0.70 (Sim and Wright, 2005). For the final 30-session evalua-tion dataset, we randomly selected one supervisor’s ratings for the 10 dual-rated sessions. 

3.2 Study: Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) 

To validate our fidelity estimator, we use therapy sessions (N=102) from a live, independent random-ized controlled trial (RCT). This trial applies the same inclusion criteria as our open trial but random-izes participants into a supportive contact condition (control; n=9) or a CBT condition (intervention; n=13). Per protocol, each participant is offered up to 8 sessions. A single therapist, distinct from those in the open trial, delivered both arms; condition dif-ferences therefore cannot be attributed to therapist identity. Because the trial is ongoing, participants have completed differing numbers of sessions, so the analyzed sample comprises of 102 sessions (57 CBT, 45 non-CBT). By design, control-arm ses-sions are expected to score lower than CBT-arm sessions on the CTS-Psy specific section. We there-fore hypothesize that our fidelity estimator will mirror this pattern, yielding lower scores for the control arm and higher scores for the intervention arm. This constitutes a known-groups validity test, assessing whether the LLM can reliably distinguish CBT (N=57) from non-CBT (N=45) sessions using total fidelity scores alone. 

3.3 Datasets 

We utilize three datasets derived from the two stud-ies for our analysis: (D1) Open Gold: 30 expert-rated sessions providing gold-standard ground truth. This dataset is used to compute performance met-rics and calibrate the LLM pipeline. (D2) Open Unlabelled: 288 unrated sessions, used to estimate the statistical uncertainty of the Open Gold find-ings. (D3) RCT: 102 transcripts used for external validation to determine if our approach extends to an independent study population. Audio recording transcription, de-identification, and pre-processing details are provided in Appendix §D. 

4 Methods 

4.1 Fidelity Estimation 

Given a deidentified therapy transcript T , a set of m CTS-Psy items Q = {q1, q 2, . . . , q m}, and a prompt template function P, we frame fidelity 3estimation as a multi-label binary classification problem. The prompt function P maps the un-structured transcript and evaluation criteria into a prompt I = P(T, Q ). We formalize the LLM as a parameterized mapping Fθ : I → { 0, 1}m, where 

I denotes the prompt space. The model processes the prompt I to produce a binary decision vector: 

Y = [ y1, y 2, . . . , y m]T ∈ { 0, 1}m. Here, yi = 1 

if the therapist adhered to criterion qi (either by successfully performing the clinical action or ap-propriately omitting it), and yi = 0 otherwise. To evaluate how task complexity and cognitive load affect estimation accuracy across the 60-item scale, we analyze the level of decomposition and reason-ing required for accurate fidelity assessment. 

4.1.1 Decomposition 

This refers to the cardinality and thematic compo-sition of the item subset Q presented to the LLM during a single inference pass. We evaluate five distinct task decomposition configurations from a spectrum of low to high decomposition: (1) Full 

(|Q| = 60 ): The baseline configuration where all CTS-Psy items are evaluated simultaneously by a single LLM call without any task partitioning. 

(2) Observability : Items are partitioned based on their clinical observability into an Explicit group (|Q| = 24 ), which consists of highly manifest be-haviors (agenda, feedback, homework, and social), and an Implicit group ( |Q| = 36 ), which requires deeper latent inference (understanding, interper-sonal effectiveness, collaboration, discovery, cog-nition, and intervention). (3) Section : Items are naturally divided by scale sections into the General clinical skills section ( |Q| = 30 ) and the specific skills section ( |Q| = 30 ), with each evaluated inde-pendently. (4) Clinical : Items are decomposed into three core clinical functional domains: Relation-ship ( |Q| = 18 ; capturing understanding, interper-sonal effectiveness, and collaboration), Structure (|Q| = 18 ; capturing agenda, feedback, and home-work), and Delivery ( |Q| = 24 ; capturing discov-ery, cognition, intervention, and social items). (5) Category (|Q| = 6 ): The finest level of granularity we evaluate, where each of the 10 distinct CTS-Psy categories are evaluated across 10 independent inference passes. 

4.1.2 Reasoning 

We investigate three distinct inference procedures that vary in their structural design and computa-tional cost: (1) Rationalization (Predict-then-Explain): To evaluate the model’s immediate scor-ing capabilities under a direct mapping strategy, we adopt a "predict-then-explain" paradigm (Rajani et al., 2019). The LLM first generates the binary score yi for an item i and subsequently rational-izes its choices by producing supporting textual evidence. (2) Evidential Estimation (Extract-Assess-Predict): This approach implements a pro-gressive, bottom-up chain of reasoning: the model first isolates relevant clinical text spans from the transcript T , assesses these extracted spans against the operational definitions of the target CTS-Psy items Q, and finally produces the binary fidelity score based on that synthesis. This is function-ally an explain-then-predict strategy, the order-ing standard CoT prompting typically uses. How-ever, we constrain the "explain" step to evidence extraction rather than free-form reasoning, since we lack ground-truth clinician reasoning chains. This constraint also mirrors how human raters ap-ply the CTS-Psy in practice: identifying evidence before judging it against the criterion. (3) Self-Reflection (Critic-in-the-Loop): This approach extends evidential estimation by introducing an it-erative critique-and-revision loop (Madaan et al., 2023). Upon generation of the initial evidential response, a secondary LLM instantiated to act as an expert clinical supervisor evaluates each rating across three strict dimensions: rubric adherence, evidence coverage, and rater confidence calibra-tion. This critical evaluation is fed back to the primary generator as supervisor feedback, which then executes a final revision of the binary deci-sions and supporting evidence. Prompt templates are presented in Appendix §H. 

4.2 Feedback Generation 

After fidelity estimation, we investigate whether the best-performing LLM can translate these rat-ings into feedback that is both actionable and therapist-facing. For each session, we use the fidelity decision vector Y to identify the three specific competency categories (from guided dis-covery, key cognitions, choices of intervention, homework, and social skills training) with the lowest total score, and construct an evidence set 

E from the 18 items spanning these categories, where each item’s evidence tuple (yi, s i, a i) con-sists of its binary score, relevant_spans , and 

criterion_assessment from evidential fidelity estimation. A generation LLM is then prompted with E to produce feedback consisting of one sen-4tence of positive feedback grounded in the session, followed by one concrete, behaviorally specific sug-gestion per weak competency area. We compare two feedback conditions using the same LLM back-bone: (1) Basic: direct feedback generated from 

E alone, and (2) RAG: This pipeline retrieves pas-sages relevant to E from a manual vector store of the CTS-Psy therapy manual and prepends them to the prompt. 

5 Experiments 

5.1 Models 

We evaluate our framework across three models spanning different architectures and capacities: Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026), Gemma4-E4B-it, and Gemma4-26B-A4B-it (Google DeepMind, 2026). By testing models ranging from compact, edge-deployable sizes (4–9B) to larger configura-tions (26B), we can determine whether advanced decomposition strategies require emergent capabil-ities native to large parameter frontiers or if they remain effective at smaller scales. Implementations details are provided in Appendix §C. 

5.2 Tasks & Metrics (T1) Fidelity Performance. The Open Gold dataset contains ground-truth labels, allowing us to evaluate classification quality directly. We select balanced accuracy (BA) to ensure equal weighting across heavily and lightly represented classes, and Matthew’s correlation coefficient (MCC) to pro-vide a rigorous, balanced assessment of all four confusion matrix quadrants. Further, we include 95% confidence intervals (CI) computed using the bootstrap method (N=1000). 

(T2) Fidelity Uncertainty. To address the ab-sence of ground-truth labels in the Open Unlabelled dataset, we utilize the Prediction-Powered Infer-ence (PPI) framework (Angelopoulos et al., 2023). As unverified LLM outputs introduce systematic bi-ases, we leverage PPI to rectify the raw model base-line ( μunlabeled ) using the expert-annotated Open Gold dataset. The framework adjusts the raw baseline to output an unbiased population preva-lence rate ( ˆμPPI ) by computing a statistical recti-fier, ∆ = ˆ μPPI − μunlabeled , derived from the vali-dation subset. Furthermore, PPI constructs valid, distribution-free CIs. Methodologically, a smaller absolute delta ( |∆| → 0) and a narrower CI in-dicate a superior, more consistent model whose clinical reasoning tightly aligns with human ex-perts, informing our statistical uncertainty while maximizing downstream inferential power. 

(T3) Known-Groups Validity Test. We test whether our framework can distinguish the control (Non-CBT) and intervention (CBT) arms in the RCT dataset. For each of five CBT skills (guided discovery, key cognitions, choices of intervention, homework, and social skills training), we fit a lin-ear mixed-effects model with arm as the fixed ef-fect and participant as a random intercept, and cor-rect for multiple comparisons across skills using a permutation-based maximum-statistic (max-T) procedure (Westfall and Young, 1993) with 1,000 participant-level permutations, preserving each par-ticipant’s repeated observations under the null. The corrected p-value for each skill is the proportion of permuted max-T statistics at least as extreme as the observed |z|, with continuity correction. 

(T4) Social Skills Administration Validity Test. Because the therapy manual explicitly pre-scribes social skills training during weeks 7–8, these are expected to be higher in this period than in weeks 1–6. We test this with a linear mixed-effects model, with total social fidelity score as the outcome, time block (weeks 1–6 as reference) as a fixed effect, and a random intercept for participant to account for within-subject correlation; the time-block coefficient tests whether weeks 7–8 fidelity differs significantly from weeks 1–6. 

(T5) Feedback Generation: Paired Evalua-tion. We compare RAG and Basic feedback in a paired setting, using the model with the best fidelity performance to keep the LLM backbone consis-tent across fidelity assessment and feedback gen-eration. Generated feedback is evaluated on two dimensions, Actionability and Clarity (exact defi-nitions in Appendix §G). (T5.1) LLM-as-a-judge :two LLMs from distinct families (Nemotron-3-30B, gpt-oss-20B) each rate a randomized pair of feed-backs (RAG vs. Basic) on both dimensions using a 3-point scale (-1: A preferred, 0: neutral, +1: B preferred). (T5.2) Expert user study : three ex-perts rate a stratified sample of 30 RAG/Basic feed-back pairs (15 low-, 10 medium-, 5 high-similarity; Appendix §F). This three-member panel balances recruitment feasibility (Ayers et al., 2023) with optimal reliability design, where adding raters be-yond three yields limited precision gains (Shoukri et al., 2004). 7 Conclusion 

Our work presents an LLM application that system-atically evaluates whether open-weight LLMs can automate psychotherapy fidelity assessment and generate high-quality supervisory feedback from real clinical transcripts. Clinical reasoning perfor-mance depends less on parameter scale than on the interplay between architecture and task decompo-sition. Under category-level decomposition and evidence-first reasoning, locally deployable mod-els minimize systematic bias and support construct validity, while running locally to protect patient privacy. Our expert user study shows the pipeline produces behaviorally specific, actionable feedback closely matching expert judgment. In summary, our work offers a scalable application for bridging the mental health supervision gap and enhancing clinician training. 8Limitations 

Several limitations are worth noting. First, our anal-ysis relies on de-identified text transcripts rather than audio, since recordings were not retained; this omits paralinguistic cues (tone, pacing, affect) rel-evant to a few CTS-Psy items (e.g., empathetic tone under Understanding), which may partly ex-plain the remaining LLM–human gap. Second, de-identification occasionally removes contextual details (e.g., names, places) relevant to fidelity judgments, an acceptable tradeoff given the pop-ulation’s sensitivity but one that may modestly affect transcript completeness. Third, our gold-standard set comprises 30 expert-rated sessions (10 doubly rated). While adequate for clinical evalua-tion studies of this scope (Appendix §D.2), larger samples would improve estimate precision. Given that optimal configurations are selected using this cohort, conclusions should be interpreted holisti-cally alongside confidence intervals, bias assess-ments, and external validity. Fourth, our study pop-ulation is drawn from two related trials targeting social functioning in SMI using a specific CBT-based protocol. Although we examined widely used therapeutic techniques such as agenda setting and homework review, future work should evaluate whether these findings generalize to other therapeu-tic modalities (e.g., motivational interviewing) and broader patient populations. Relatedly, while our external validation demonstrates construct validity, the unannotated RCT cohort precludes evaluating ground-truth accuracy. Future work should there-fore benchmark classification performance on fully labeled external cohorts. Finally, we evaluate three open-weight models, chosen for their suitability to sensitive clinical data; results may differ for propri-etary frontier models, and our findings suggest the benefits of decomposition and reasoning depend heavily on the underlying model.

14 C Implementation Details 

All open-weight models were served locally us-ing vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) and queried via LangChain 1 on 4 × NVIDIA RTX A4500 GPUs. 

Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) is a hybrid rea-soning dense model. We use the following parame-ters in our setup: temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, presence penalty 1.5 to encourage extended de-liberation before producing structured outputs. In reflection runs, a separate instance with thinking disabled served as the critique model. 

Gemma4-E4B-it (Google DeepMind, 2026) is a 4B parameter Mixture-of-Experts instruction-tuned model. We use the following parameters in our setup: temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 64, pres-ence penalty 1.5. In reflection runs, the critique model’s output was capped at 512 tokens. 

Gemma4-26B-A4B-it (Google DeepMind, 2026) is a 26-billion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts instruction-tuned model sharing the same inference parameters as Gemma 4B. Due to its size, the model was served across 4 GPUs with tensor parallelism, bfloat16 precision, and a 131k-token context window. 

gpt-oss-20b (Agarwal et al., 2025) is a 20-billion-parameter open-weight reasoning model, used exclusively as an LLM-as-a-judge. We serve it locally with deterministic decoding (temperature 0.0, top-p 1.0) to minimize scoring variance across paired feedback comparisons. 

Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B (Blakeman et al., 2025) is a 30-billion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts reasoning model, used only as an LLM-as-a-judge and served with deterministic decoding settings (temperature 0.0, top-p 1.0).

D.2 Evaluation Comparison to Prior Work 

As shown in Table 4, our evaluation expands both the scale and annotation granularity relative to ex-isting benchmarks. While prior datasets rely on brief exchanges such as Cactus (16.6 turns) and TherapyGym (10 turns), our protocol evaluates full-length, authentic clinical interactions averag-ing approximately 180 turns per dialogue, repre-senting an increase in dialogue length of more than 10 ×. In total, our evaluation encompasses 420 com-plete sessions, spanning 30 gold-standard sessions, 288 unlabelled sessions for uncertainty quantifi-cation, and 102 sessions for external randomized controlled trial (RCT) validation. Across roughly 5,400 annotated authentic turns, our methodology provides 1,800 fine-grained expert judgments. This corresponds to an evaluation density of 60.0 rating items per dialogue, offering over 5 × the granu-lar supervision of TherapyGym (11.0 items) and substantially exceeding corpora like Cactus, which contain no itemized per-dialogue ratings. Ground-ing our evaluation in authentic clinical transcripts preserves critical ecological validity.

E.1 Fidelity Performance (T1)

E.2 Repeated-Run Stability 

LLM parameters are chosen based on recommen-dations. To evaluate the stability of stochastic decoding, we run the Qwen3.5-9B EV reasoning with category decomposition three times. Across categories, performance varied in both BA and MCC. F Feedback Generation Evaluation 

Rationale for the stratified 30-pair sample. Eval-uating every feedback pair with expert clinicians is infeasible, so the expert study operates on a 30-pair subset. Rather than sampling uniformly at random, we stratified by the similarity between the RAG and Basic feedback for each session, com-puted as the cosine distance between bge-small-en-v1.5 embeddings (Xiao et al., 2024) of the two outputs. We binned pairs into three equal-frequency strata – high, medium, and low similar-ity—and drew 15 low-similarity (most divergent), 10 medium-similarity, and 5 high-similarity (most similar) pairs. The motivation is that a uniform random sam-ple would be dominated by pairs in which the two systems produce near-identical feedback. On such pairs a preference judgment carries little informa-tion: there is no substantive difference for a rater to detect, and forcing a choice would manufacture preferences from noise, inflating apparent disagree-ment and biasing the comparison. Because the goal of the paired study is to determine whether the dif-ference between RAG and Basic feedback, when it exists , favors either system, oversampling divergent pairs concentrates expert effort on the cases that are actually diagnostic. H Prompt Templates 

Fidelity Estimation Prompts. We evaluate three reasoning strategies over the same transcript, decomposition-specific task instruction, and CTS-Psy item set Q (Table 8). Under Rationalization (Figure 8), the LLM assigns each item’s binary score yi directly, then produces a brief rationale as post-hoc supporting evidence. Under Evidential Estimation (Extract-Assess-Predict, Figure 9), the model instead reasons bottom-up: it first extracts 1–2 direct relevant_spans per item (an empty list if none exist, with no fabrication permitted), assesses whether that evidence meets the criterion (criterion_assessment ), and only then assigns the binary score , enforcing an evidence-first or-der that prevents rationalizing a score chosen in advance. Self-Reflection (Critic-in-the-Loop, Fig-ure 10) extends Evidential Estimation with an it-erative critique-and-revision loop (Madaan et al., 2023): after the primary generator’s initial ev-idential response (Step 1), a separate reflection LLM instantiated as an expert clinical supervisor critiques each item’s rating in exactly two sen-tences covering rubric adherence, evidence cover-age, and confidence calibration (Step 2), and the pri-mary generator then revises its relevant_spans ,

criterion_assessment , and score for every item accordingly (Step 3), a loop that may be ap-plied for k ≥ 1 iterations. 

Feedback Generation. Once fidelity estimation completes for a session, we select the three lowest-scoring specific competency categories. These are drawn from five categories: guided discovery, key cognitions, choice of intervention, homework, and social skills training. Together, these three cate-gories span 18 items. For each item, we compile the underlying evidence: the fidelity statement, the score, relevant transcript spans, and the criterion as-sessment. The generation LLM is then prompted to synthesize this evidence into supervisory feedback. The feedback includes one sentence of genuine positive feedback grounded in the session. It also includes one concrete, actionable suggestion for each weak competency area. Figure 11 shows the corresponding system and user prompts. In the RAG condition, we also prepend passages from the therapy manual to the user turn before generation. These passages are retrieved based on relevance to the flagged evidence. 

LLM-as-a-Judge. To compare RAG-grounded and non-RAG feedback for the same session, a judge LLM is shown the extracted fidelity evidence for the targeted categories alongside both feedback variants. The two variants are presented in ran-dom order as Feedback A and Feedback B. Judges are instructed to rate only against the provided ev-idence. They do not reward unsupported claims or length. For each dimension, the judge returns a single comparative score in {-1,0,+1}. We evalu-ate feedback quality along two dimensions, each scored by a separate model call using the single-dimension template in Figure 12. This ensures no judge call sees more than one dimension at once. Actionability asks whether the therapist could do something concretely different next session; spe-cific named behaviors or techniques score higher than vague encouragement.
