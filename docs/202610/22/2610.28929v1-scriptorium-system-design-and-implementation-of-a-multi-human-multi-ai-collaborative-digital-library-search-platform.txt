This technical report documents the design and implementation of Scriptorium, a digital library search interface designed to support 

multi-human, multi-AI collaboration : full cross-user transparency of search activity combined with a Manager–Worker architecture of seven role-specialized generative AI agents. A companion short demonstration paper describes Scriptorium’s interaction design [27]; because a demonstration format is constrained to a few pages, it necessarily omits most implementation detail. This report documents Scriptorium’s complete system architecture, data model, collaborative-interaction design rationale, and AI orchestration mechanism, including verbatim agent prompts, the deterministic guardrails that constrain them, the language model and retrieval configuration, and the steps needed to run the system, so that the design can be inspected, critiqued, reproduced, and reused by other researchers. We also discuss the system’s current limitations and the user study we are planning to evaluate its central hypothesis: that combining collaborative transparency with scoped multi-agent AI assistance increases serendipitous cross-pollination between collaborators’ research threads. 1 Introduction 

Interactive information retrieval research has, over the last two decades, separately established that (1) complex academic search tasks benefit from collaborative support, awareness of collaborators’ activity, mechanisms for dividing labour, and persistence of shared state [22]; and, more recently, that (2) such tasks benefit from generative-AI-augmented workspaces that scaffold an individual searcher’s metacognitive planning, monitoring, and evaluation [6, 7, 25]. No existing system we are aware of combines both: a workspace in which several people can see and build on one another’s activity, and in which several , differently scoped AI agents, rather than one general-purpose assistant, can be invoked to reason over that shared activity. We built Scriptorium to explore this combination directly. A companion short demonstration paper describes Scriptorium’s interaction design and its planned interactive demonstration [27]; that paper necessarily omits most implementation detail in order to fit a demonstration track’s page limit. This report is the companion technical document referenced there. It is intended for readers who want to understand, reproduce, critique, or extend Scriptorium’s architecture, rather than to argue for the research contribution itself, which is the demonstration paper’s purpose.

2 Related Work 2.1 Collaborative Search 

SearchTogether [22] is the foundational system in this space, establishing that a collaborative Web search interface should provide awareness (per-user query histories, page-visitation metadata, ratings and comments visible to teammates), 

division of labour (integrated messaging, a recommendation mechanism, and automatic query-splitting across a group), and persistence (an automatically generated shared summary of a session). Its evaluation is particularly instructive for Scriptorium’s design: awareness features were rated the most valuable part of the system, while the automatic division-of-labour features (split search, multi-engine search) saw little use, in part because they implicitly required one group member to act as a leader pushing work onto others, a role that did not suit the peer collaborators in that study. Scriptorium’s transparency-by-default workspace model can be read as pushing SearchTogether’s awareness principle to its logical extreme: rather than requiring an explicit “recommend” or “split search” action to share information, all 

activity within a workspace is visible to all members continuously, without requiring anyone to take on a coordinating role. 

2.2 AI-Augmented Exploratory and Workspace-Based Search 

A separate line of work has embedded structured workspaces and, more recently, generative AI into exploratory search interfaces to support metacognition, the planning, monitoring, and evaluation of one’s own search progress [6, 7]. Cross-session search, resuming a complex task after a gap of hours, days, or weeks, has been studied both as a behavioural phenomenon [19] and as a design target, with visualized search-history timelines shown to support task reacquaintance [10, 12]. WESearch [25] extends this line with generative-AI-produced summaries at both a nested-workspace and whole-workspace level, explicitly framing this as workspace-augmented generation (WAG) rather than retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), since the summarized content is exactly what a searcher has deliberately chosen to save, not what a retrieval ranker judged relevant to a query. We adopt this WAG framing in Scriptorium’s own design (Section 6) and extend it to a setting where the workspace being summarized, critiqued, or bridged is the product of 

multiple searchers’ saved and discarded resources, not one person’s. Accessibility-focused work such as SearchPath [29] demonstrates a related but distinct use of a persistent timeline, as a working-memory aid for students with dyslexia, reinforcing a broader point in this literature that exploratory search interfaces should not assume uniform reading, memory, or attention capacity among their users [20]. Hoeber’s design principles for exploratory search interfaces [11] synthesize much of this body of work and directly informed several of Scriptorium’s interaction choices, in particular the preference for lightweight, persistent, and interactive additions to a familiar search-engine-results-page paradigm over novel, complex visualizations. Prior work from our own group on dynamic tagging, highlighting, and filtering of Scriptorium: System Design and Implementation of a Multi-Human, Multi-AI Collaborative Digital Library Search Platform 3saved search results [1] and on keyword-linked exploratory browsing [13] similarly motivated specific Scriptorium mechanisms (tag-based saving, filterable saved-result views), though, again, all of this prior work is single-user. The rise of AI agents as active participants in the search process, rather than as a single conversational answer engine, is a recent framing [39] that Scriptorium operationalizes concretely through its seven-agent Manager–Worker architecture (Section 6), and which relates to, but is architecturally distinct from, retrieval-augmented generation approaches [9] more broadly. Self-regulated learning during search, an individual’s process of goal-setting, monitoring, and adapting strategy [34], becomes, in a multi-user workspace, a process that a group must additionally negotiate and reconcile, a phenomenon Scriptorium’s transparency mechanisms are designed to surface rather than hide. Finally, motivating the underlying complexity Scriptorium is designed for, graduate student searchers report specific, persistent difficulty tracking what has already been searched and by whom, even when working entirely alone [14], and idea-generation tools such as SearchIdea [3] demonstrate that AI-generated prompts, when properly scoped, can support creativity rather than merely convenience during academic search, a design lesson reflected in several of Scriptorium’s individual agents. 

2.3 Precedents for the Seven Agents 

Each of Scriptorium’s seven agents operationalizes a form of search support with an established research record. We summarize that record agent by agent, and then state what Scriptorium adds to it. 

HERMES (Navigator). Query suggestion is among the longest-studied aids in interactive search. Kelly et al. [16] compared query and term suggestion features and found that searchers preferred suggested queries to suggested terms. In exploratory literature search, Medlar et al. [21] showed that alternative queries can also act as keyword summaries of the results page: with them, participants opened about half as many documents per query yet were exposed to over 40% more results. Large language models can now write such queries; Wang et al. [37] found that ChatGPT generates high-precision Boolean queries for systematic review search, at a cost in recall. Closest to HERMES, Khakshoor and Hoeber [17] use generative AI to suggest broader and narrower queries grounded in a searcher’s recent exploratory search activity. HERMES shares this grounding in search history but differs in whose history it reads: its suggestions are conditioned on the whole group’s queries and on the titles of documents the group has saved and discarded , so that a proposed query avoids ground any collaborator has already covered or rejected. 

JANUS (Bridge). Two precedents frame JANUS. Literature-based discovery began with Swanson’s observation that two literatures that had never cited one another, one linking dietary fish oil to reduced blood viscosity and one linking Raynaud’s syndrome to high viscosity, jointly implied a hypothesis that neither contained [32]. In collaborative search, Pickens et al. [26] introduced algorithmic mediation, in which a system combines the activity of two or more people searching together, simultaneously, so that one can survey for promising documents while the other judges them in depth. We return to how JANUS differs from both below. 

ARIA (Synthesizer). Tombros and Sanderson [33] showed that summaries tailored to the query let searchers judge relevance more accurately and more quickly than static summaries built from a document’s leading sentences. Orin and Hoeber [25] carried summarization into the workspace, generating summaries of what a searcher has saved rather than of what a ranker retrieved. ARIA narrows this further, summarizing only the documents a collaborator attaches to the current message. 

MINERVA (Methods). Structured extraction from research abstracts has a long history in evidence-based medicine, where Nye et al. [23] annotated 5,000 abstracts of randomized controlled trials with their populations, interventions, and outcomes to support automated extraction. With large language models, Khraisha et al. [18] found that GPT-4 4 Pirmoradi et al. could rival humans on some systematic-review tasks under specific conditions, but that its data extraction was only moderate once chance agreement was accounted for. MINERVA is accordingly bounded to what an abstract states, and reports a missing element as not stated rather than inferring it. 

CLIO (Citations). Walters and Wilder [36] found that 55% of the citations GPT-3.5 produced in short literature reviews, and 18% of those produced by GPT-4, were fabricated, and that many citations to real works contained errors. CLIO therefore formats references from catalogue metadata rather than generating them, and marks a missing field instead of supplying one. 

SOCRATES (Critic). Crescenzi et al. [7] designed a workspace tool to support the metacognitive planning and monitoring of exploratory search, and Danry et al. [8] showed that AI explanations framed as questions improved people’s logical discernment compared with explanations that simply asserted a conclusion. SOCRATES combines the two: it critiques the group’s evidence base for gaps, and ends with a question rather than a verdict. 

MOMUS (Counterpoint). Devil’s advocacy is an established structure for group decisions: Schweiger et al. [28] found that groups using devil’s advocacy or dialectical inquiry produced higher-quality recommendations than groups seeking consensus. Chiang et al. [4] gave the role to a large language model and found that an LLM devil’s advocate arguing against an AI recommendation can promote a group’s appropriate reliance on AI without substantially increasing workload. The need is acute in search. White [38] showed that searchers favour positive information over negative, that search engines strongly favour one, usually positive, perspective irrespective of the truth, and that around half of the answers searchers settled on were incorrect; Sharma et al. [30] found that LLM-powered conversational search led participants to more biased querying than conventional search, especially when the model reinforced their views. MOMUS applies devil’s advocacy to the corpus a group has assembled rather than to a single decision. 

What is new. None of these capabilities is new on its own, and we do not claim otherwise. What is new is the setting in which all seven operate: one multi-author evidence base that includes what the group discarded, which HERMES, SOCRATES, and MOMUS read as negative relevance judgements. JANUS goes further: to our knowledge, it is a new kind of search support, and it differs from algorithmic mediation [26] on four counts. (1) Synchronous versus asynchronous. Algorithmic mediation supports people searching at the same time. JANUS works over persistent activity accumulated across sessions, and is useful precisely when collaborators are not searching together. (2) Retrieval versus interpretation. Algorithmic mediation acts on retrieval, deciding which documents each partner is shown. JANUS acts on interpretation: it produces a natural-language account of what each collaborator’s thread is about and where the threads connect, together with one bridging query to pursue that connection. (3) Assigned roles versus peers. Algorithmic mediation divides the work between complementary roles. JANUS assumes peers without roles, consistent with SearchTogether’s finding that division-of-labour features which presume a leader went largely unused [22]. (4) Inside the retrieval loop versus in the open. Algorithmic mediation operates within the system’s retrieval of results. JANUS is invoked on demand and posts its account into the shared chat, where any collaborator can read and contest it. Its input, the difference between collaborators’ activity, has no single-user counterpart, which is why its preflight guard (Listing 2) refuses a request until at least two people have activity. Swanson’s literature-based discovery [32] is the closer analogy in spirit, joining bodies of work that do not reference each other; JANUS applies that idea to the live, partial threads of a research group rather than to published literatures. 3 Research Framing and Design Goals 

Scriptorium is a research prototype, not a production system. It deliberately makes search activity transparent across all users rather than siloed per account: any logged-in user can see and resume any workspace, view the full, colour-coded activity timeline of every collaborator, and read the shared chat thread, including the AI’s responses within it. This is a design choice made in direct service of a research question, whether full transparency increases serendipitous cross-pollination between collaborators’ independent research threads, rather than a default that a production multi-tenant SaaS product would responsibly make. Four concrete design goals follow from this framing: (1) Ambient awareness over explicit sharing. Following the SearchTogether finding discussed in Section 2, information about a collaborator’s activity should be visible by default, not only when someone explicitly recommends or pushes it. (2) Auditable, not monolithic, AI assistance. A group working together should be able to trust not only that an AI agent produced an answer, but see what evidence, specifically , it was allowed to see when producing it. (3) Genuine, not synthesized, activity records. Every event surfaced in the collaborative timeline corresponds to a real, tracked system event (a query issued, a document saved, a document’s removal flag flipped, a document opened); no event type is fabricated purely to make the timeline appear more complete, since the timeline itself may become a data source for studying the group’s actual behaviour. (4) Lightweight extension of a familiar paradigm. Consistent with Hoeber’s design principles for exploratory search interfaces [11], collaborative and AI features are layered onto a conventional search-engine-results-page interaction model rather than replacing it with an unfamiliar paradigm. 

4 System Architecture 4.1 Technology Stack 

Scriptorium is built on the MERN stack (MongoDB, Express, React, Node.js) [31]. The frontend is a React single-page application [15] using Redux Toolkit for state management (one slice per data domain) and a combination of Tailwind CSS and hand-written CSS modules for an archival visual design system. The backend is an Express REST API running on Node.js [2], with Mongoose object-document mapping onto a MongoDB database [5] (Atlas-hosted or local, depending on deployment). Figure 1 summarizes the high-level architecture. The agent orches-trator ( backend/ai/AIOrchestrator.js ) runs on the server: the chat interface sends each agent request to POST /api/ai/chat , and the backend assembles the request’s context from the database, applies the orchestrator’s scoping and guards, and calls the OpenAI API (Section 7). The model API key is therefore held only by the server and never reaches the browser. Document-search results shown on the search engine results page (SERP) are retrieved live from Ex Libris Primo [35], an institutional library discovery and metadata API; Scriptorium is a collaborative layer over an existing discovery system, not a replacement for one, which is what makes its mechanisms portable across any institution using a compatible discovery service.

4.3 Live-Update Mechanism 

The collaborative timeline and chat refresh via a three-second polling interval rather than WebSockets or server-sent events: the chat panel refetches the workspace’s messages, and the results page refetches the workspace detail (every query with its saved and removed documents) and the view log, each every three seconds. A separate, slower poll (sixty seconds) refreshes the current topic’s cross-user document list, which supplements the workspace detail when agent context is built (Section 7). This was a deliberate simplicity choice: at the scale of a research prototype’s target cohort (at most approximately nine concurrent testers in our planned study), a three-second poll is imperceptibly different from a push-based update, and avoids the operational complexity of running and maintaining a WebSocket server for a system that will not be deployed at production scale.

5.1 Transparent Workspaces 

Any logged-in user sees every workspace on the dashboard, a flat list sorted most-recent-first, not only workspaces they created or were invited to. Each card shows the creator’s username, search and document counts, and two actions: 

View (open the workspace’s activity overview) and Resume task (jump directly into the SERP for that workspace’s most recent search). Edit, archive, and rename actions remain restricted to the workspace’s creator, enforced in both the UI and, for the create/update path, server-side, so that “everyone can view or resume” is deliberately decoupled from “everyone can modify.” This is the system’s central methodological choice: full cross-user visibility is what makes serendipitous discovery, a collaborator stumbling onto a teammate’s search thread without being explicitly invited into it, observable and possible at all. 5.2 The Search Timeline 

A single chronological stream merges the four genuinely tracked event types described in Section 4 (search, save, delete, view), filterable by type, grouped by day, and live-updating via the polling mechanism above. Two independent colour channels are used, deliberately kept visually disjoint so that a reader’s eye never confuses what happened with 

who did it. The first channel encodes event type: the left border, icon, and type label of each timeline entry share one colour per event type (blue for search, emerald for save, red for delete, violet for view). These specific hues were not chosen arbitrarily: green and red for save and delete mirror the near-universal version-control diff convention (additions in green, removals in red), and violet for “viewed” mirrors the browser :visited -link convention dating to the earliest CSS specifications. The second channel encodes the acting user: a small coloured avatar-initial chip next to “by {username}” draws from a separate 15-colour palette (ColorBrewer “Set3”), assigned first-come-first-served per session via a session-scoped username-to-colour map. This first-come-first-served assignment was chosen deliberately over a hash-based scheme, which was tried first and rejected, because hash collisions could silently assign two different users the same colour; the first-come-first-served map guarantees no two users share a colour for cohorts of up to fifteen concurrent participants. 

5.3 Persistent Chat 

A shared chat thread is scoped to a workspace by default, persisting across however many distinct searches happen inside that workspace over its lifetime. An earlier version of the system scoped chat per search-topic instead; this caused chat history to visibly disappear the moment a new search was issued within the same workspace, a usability problem that was identified during internal testing and corrected by re-scoping chat to the workspace level. On the workspace-details page specifically (a page for reviewing past search topics one at a time via a sidebar), chat can optionally be re-scoped to whichever topic is currently selected in that sidebar, since that page’s entire interaction model, unlike the active-search SERP, is built around browsing discrete past topics rather than a single continuous session. 

5.4 Adapting Interface Complexity to Task Context 

The same Timeline-and-Chat dock component renders two different ways depending on context. On the active-search SERP, it is collapsible to an icon strip, offers a combined mode with a resizable drag-divider between Timeline and Chat, and persists its configuration across sessions, on the premise that a researcher actively working a search benefits from maximum layout flexibility. On the workspace-details page, the same underlying component is instead always expanded and fills the space beside the topic sidebar. When that space is at least 690 pixels wide, Timeline and Chat sit side by side, keeping activity and conversation in view together as the SERP’s combined mode does; narrower than that, the panel falls back to a two-way tab switch that opens on Timeline, and the hidden chat keeps polling so that its tab can show an unread count. The SERP’s collapse and resize controls are omitted there, since that page already provides its own primary navigation (a sidebar of past topics) and the SERP’s extra chrome would add complexity without benefit to a “review what happened” task rather than an “actively searching” one. Search-result pagination follows the same task-sensitive philosophy in a smaller way: rather than rendering every page number for result sets that can exceed 200 pages, or a sliding window that recenters on every click, results pages are shown in fixed blocks of up to ten page numbers, jumping to the next block of ten once a user pages past the current window, matching how large result sets are conventionally paginated. 10 Pirmoradi et al.

6 The Multi-Agent AI Orchestrator 6.1 Design Rationale 

Rather than a single general-purpose assistant, Scriptorium implements seven named, specialized agents behind a Manager–Worker routing layer. The underlying design hypothesis is that giving each capability a distinct persona, a strictly scoped input, and a hard output-length limit produces responses that are more predictable, more citable, and more trustworthy to a group of collaborators than a single do-everything chatbot would, and additionally lets the interaction log itself become research data: which agent a group invoked, and at what point in their research process, is a meaningful behavioural signal that a single undifferentiated assistant would not expose. The orchestrator runs on the server behind POST /api/ai/chat and reaches the model through a pluggable provider interface ( BaseLLMProvider , a single call(systemPrompt, userPrompt) method), implemented for the OpenAI API. Earlier in development a second, hand-maintained copy ran in the browser against a free model proxy, and the two copies drifted: the browser copy at one point lacked two of the seven agents and passed every agent the same broad context rather than its scoped slice. Moving orchestration entirely to the server removed that copy, so that every listing in this section and in Appendix A describes the only code that runs. Each request is first built into six context slices (Section 7), from which the scoping function below selects.

6.4 Routing 

Routing is hybrid. An explicit @agentname mention in a chat message bypasses the manager entirely and delegates directly. Otherwise, a manager persona classifies the free-text request, calling an LLM with a short, rule-based routing prompt (Listing 3) whose user content is the message alone ( {"userMessage": ...} ), so that no workspace content is exposed at the routing step; the call returns structured JSON indicating either a delegation target and a confidence score, or a request to clarify. Below a confidence threshold (0.7 in the current implementation), the manager asks a clarifying question listing all active agents rather than guessing, so that a low-confidence routing decision never masquerades as a confident one. If the LLM routing call itself fails, the system falls back to a keyword-based heuristic classifier rather than crashing or silently guessing.

6.5 Prompt Design and Hard Output Limits 

Every agent’s instruction string states its exact expected input object (e.g., input.attachedDocs , input.queryHistory )and a hard output-length limit, either a word count or a per-item line limit, stated directly in the prompt. Six of the seven agents rely on the language model to respect this stated limit; HERMES is the exception; because its structured JSON output feeds a UI control (individually clickable query-suggestion chips) that would break on malformed input, its output is additionally clamped in code (a clampQueryWords function enforcing a maximum of seven words per query and five queries total) rather than trusted entirely to the model. This asymmetry, prompt-only limits for free-text agents, code-enforced limits for the one agent with a machine-consumed output, reflects a general principle in Scriptorium’s AI integration: constraints that affect only a human reader’s experience are prompted; constraints that would break a downstream UI component if violated are additionally enforced in code.

This section collects the configuration a reader needs to run Scriptorium and to interpret its agents’ behaviour. 

7.1 Language Model 

Every model call, routing and worker alike, is made by the backend through the OpenAI Chat Completions API, using the official openai Node.js library, in the provider backend/ai/OpenAILLMProvider.js . The model is gpt-5 [24], con-figurable through the OPENAI_MODEL environment variable, and the provider sets no sampling or reasoning parameters, so the API’s defaults apply. Each call sends two messages: 

> system: <agent instruction, Appendix A> user: <JSON: {"userRequest": ..., "input": <scoped slice>}>

For routing calls the user message is {"userMessage": ...} instead (Section 6). 8 Known Limitations 

We state these plainly, consistent with the goal of this report as a companion technical document rather than a promotional one. 

No production-grade authentication. There is no signed session token (no JWT); the client stores a plain {_id, username} object in localStorage , and every backend endpoint trusts whatever identifier the client sends. This is acceptable for a controlled study among cooperative, consenting participants, to whom the property is disclosed, but would require real authentication before any adversarial or production deployment. 

Dependence on a hosted model. The agents depend on a commercial model served by OpenAI: the snapshot behind the gpt-5 alias, its defaults, its pricing, and its availability are outside our control and may change over time. A reader reproducing agent behaviour at a later date should pin a dated model snapshot through OPENAI_MODEL , and should expect outputs to differ for this reason as well as the next. Workspace content sent to agents, including titles, abstracts, queries, and recent chat, is transmitted to OpenAI, which participants in a study must be told. 

Polling, not push. Chat and the activity timeline refresh via a three-second setInterval poll rather than Web-Sockets or server-sent events, simple and reliable at the intended small scale, but not the mechanism a production-scale version of this system would use. 

Model non-determinism. Because agent responses are generated by a language model, two invocations of the same agent on the same workspace state may produce different, though similarly scoped, output. Hard output-length limits and precise, narrowly scoped prompts (Section 6) reduce, but do not eliminate, the practical impact of this non-determinism. 

9 Discussion and Planned Evaluation 

Scriptorium’s AI orchestrator is, in the terms proposed by Orin and Hoeber [25], a workspace-augmented generation (WAG) system rather than a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) one: every agent’s output is grounded only in resources that one or more human collaborators have explicitly saved (or, for SOCRATES and MOMUS, explicitly discarded), not in documents a retrieval ranker judged relevant to a query. We extend their single-user framing of WAG to a multi-user one: the workspace an agent reasons over in Scriptorium is the joint product of a group’s saved and discarded resources, and at least one agent, JANUS, is defined specifically in terms of the difference between two or more collaborators’ individual activity within that shared workspace, a construct that has no single-user analogue. We are in the process of designing a controlled user study, structured similarly in spirit to the SearchTogether evaluation [22], in which pairs or small groups of participants complete a realistic, multi-session academic search task using Scriptorium. Beyond standard search-interface metrics, we intend to specifically examine (1) transactive memory, whether and how quickly a collaborator becomes aware of a teammate’s relevant prior activity without being explicitly told about it, comparing workspaces where the collaborative timeline is available against a condition where it is not; (2) which of the seven AI agents groups invoke, and at what point in the exploratory-to-focused search transition [40] they invoke it; and (3) whether the auditable, per-agent input scoping described in Section 6 measurably increases 16 Pirmoradi et al. participants’ stated trust in AI-generated content relative to a single undifferentiated assistant baseline, an axis of comparison the companion demonstration’s interactive session is also designed to probe informally with conference attendees. 

10 Conclusion 

We have documented the complete system architecture, data model, collaborative-interaction design rationale, and multi-agent AI orchestration mechanism behind Scriptorium, a digital library search interface built to support multi-human, multi-AI collaboration. This report is intended to make the system’s design inspectable and reusable independent of the page constraints of its companion demonstration paper [27], and we welcome scrutiny of, and extensions to, the mechanisms described here, particularly the deterministic preflight guards and per-agent input scoping approach, which we believe generalize beyond the specific context of digital library search to other collaborative sensemaking tools that incorporate generative AI.

Should the manager’s routing call fail, a keyword classifier substitutes for it, tested in a fixed order so that the more specific collaborative intents are matched before the general ones. Terms including contradict , refute , devil , and 

bias route to MOMUS; bridge , overlap , teammate , collaborator , and who is looking route to JANUS; gap and missing 

to SOCRATES; citation and method terms to CLIO and MINERVA respectively; summar , overview , and compare to ARIA; and query-reformulation terms such as keyword , boolean , broaden , and narrow to HERMES. Every branch is additionally conditioned on the agent being enabled in the workspace’s agent roster, and an unmatched request returns the clarification response listing the active agents rather than a guess. The heuristic is a degraded mode, not a design goal; we report it because a reader reproducing the system will otherwise observe routing behaviour that the manager prompt alone does not explain.
