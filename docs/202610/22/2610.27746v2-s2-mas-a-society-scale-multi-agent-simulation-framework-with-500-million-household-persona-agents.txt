Abstract 

Simulating economic responses at society scale requires agents whose decisions reflect household circumstances and the effects of earlier actions. We introduce S 2-MAS, a society-scale multi-agent simulation framework built on 500 million synthetic household personas grounded in real-survey data. Its Genese engine separates decisions from state updates, while reusable financial scenarios and application ledgers track consumption, cash, debt and payments. sharp-421m-mlx, a compact decision model trained with SFT + RLCD on 2.93 million examples, achieves 74.55% accuracy and 38.37% question-macro F1 in consumer financial-choice scenarios, outperforming Jev 1.13 and GLM-5.3-Flash on both measures. We evaluate eight financial task families and simulate 12 million distinct household agents in paired purchase-funding conditions. Jointly raising purchase goals and approved credit limits increases consumption, with responses differing across age and income groups. Education, employment and household-size differences persist after age–income standardization and mainly reflect transitions from deferral to purchase. Asset and debt groups differ in cash drawdown, repayment and closing debt. A 24-month panel varies purchase goals under a common credit product, with repayment and arrears updating subsequent offers. S 2-MAS links heterogeneous household decisions to evolving consumption, cash and repayment paths, supporting economic experiments across household groups, population scales and time horizons. 

# 1. Introduction 

Language-model-based multi-agent simulation offers a way to study how heterogeneous populations respond to changing en-vironments and interventions. At society scale, three challenges arise together: decisions must respond to individual cir-cumstances, actions must produce consistent changes in resources and obligations, and inference and execution must remain efficient enough to repeat experiments across conditions. We introduce S 2-MAS, a society-scale multi-agent simulation frame-work integrating household populations, trainable decision policies and explicit state transitions (Figure 1). Survey-conditioned agents allow predictions to be evaluated against recorded human responses [26], [27]. MatrAIx con-structs heterogeneous persona populations for simulated-user evaluation [1], while OneSim and OASIS support large-scale agent execution in social environments [17], [18]. Economic experiments require these capabilities together: differences among households shape their decisions, and the resulting changes in resources and obligations shape what they can do next. Household finance provides observed responses for decision learning and budget rules for calculating the consequences of selected plans. After an income loss, a household may draw on reserves to sustain consumption, reduce spending to preserve cash, or leave payments unmet. Buffer-stock saving, consumption commitments and heterogeneous-household models explain why resources and obligations matter for these adjustments [23], [24], [32]. Consumption, cash reserves and payment histories therefore reveal how the same financial condition affects households with different starting positions. S2-MAS combines 500 million synthetic household personas grounded in real-survey data, domain-adapted decision models, reusable financial tasks and the Genese execution engine. sharp-421m and sharp-4b implement encoder and decoder decision policies, respectively. sharp-421m uses 2,925,263 examples for SFT + RLCD; sharp-421m-mlx is its runtime used in the com-pleted financial applications. Genese separates decision proposals from state updates, while independent application ledgers calculate consumption, cash, debt payments and shortfalls. Paired experiments retain the same households and accounting rules across conditions. Monthly experiments carry realized resources, obligations and payment histories into subsequent decisions. Component evaluation measures the contribution of household information, policy substitution and execution efficiency. In consumer financial-choice scenarios, sharp-421m-mlx achieves 74.55% accuracy and 38.37% question-macro F1. Its accuracy exceeds Jev 1.13 by 9.30 percentage points and GLM-5.3-Flash by 5.68 points. In a controlled 1,000-agent benchmark with the same model allocation, Genese completes accepted decisions and state writeback faster than OneSim and OASIS (Section 5.5). sharp-421m-mlx runs eight financial task families. A paired one-shot purchase-funding experiment with 12 million distinct synthetic households shows that jointly raising purchase goals and approved limits increases consumption. Responses differ by age and income; education, employment and household-size differences persist after standardization to a common age– income distribution. Much of this variation reflects transitions from deferral to purchase. Asset and debt groups differ in cash 1arXiv:2610.27746v2 [cs.MA] 21 Oct 2026 drawdown, repayment and closing debt. A 24-month extension follows the same 10,000-household panel across five shock paths and an unshocked reference, comparing higher and lower purchase goals under the same credit product and offer rules. Repayment and arrears update subsequent credit offers. Persistent income loss erodes cash buffers and narrows the consumption gap between the goals, while sustained income gains widen it. 

# 2. Related work 

2.1. Survey-grounded populations and user simulation Survey-conditioned agents connect language modeling to empirical response distributions. Argyle et al. condition GPT-3 on sociodemographic backstories and compare generated subpopulation responses with survey samples [26]. Park et al. construct interview- and survey-grounded agents for 1,052 Americans and evaluate held-out responses against the represented individ-uals’ test–retest consistency [27]. sharp-421m learns direct decisions from observed consumer survey responses. Question-majority and matched-profile controls measure the contribution of respondent information to held-out answer prediction. The application ledger calculates the financial consequences of selected plans. MatrAIx combines dependency-aware population construction with biographies, reviews, surveys and self-reports [1]. S 2-MAS uses a 36-field household representation constructed through processing and statistical calibration of real-survey data. PCA characterizes the economic structure of the resulting population. Published MatrAIx AI–human within-one agreement is 93.8% for Claude and 79.2% for GPT on its own 100-persona cohort [1]. Its protocol provides methodological background for comparing a judge with a human reference; the present study evaluates its judges on the processed-field cohort described in Supplementary Methods M7. 2.2. Direct decision models and financial supervision Direct decision models score named alternatives or return structured probabilities without generating a prose answer. Jev’s official introduction names RLCD as its training approach and its API defines typed outputs [36], [12]. Kev-4B publishes option-classification CE for its LoRA/pointer adapter; Laya’s open trainer combines noisy-logit proper-reward policy gradients with soft CE [38], [37]. Encoder interfaces include Laya and Von; decoder decision readouts include Kev, Clef and Nimble [3]–[5], [9], [10]. S 2-MAS adapts Laya’s existing option-scoring interface to recorded household answers and coded states. Supplementary Methods M5 compares available interfaces on the same held-out consumer questions. Table S7 records the decision interfaces, backbones and readouts; Table S8 distinguishes financial adaptation from general decision training, instruction tuning and financial-language pretraining. Table S9 reports the populations and execution units of selected simulation studies. Financial language adaptation spans large-corpus pretraining, instruction tuning and expert numerical QA. BloombergGPT uses 363B financial tokens plus 345B general tokens for pretraining [13]. PIXIU’s NeurIPS 2023 FIT release contains 128,640 instruction samples for financial NLP and prediction [14]. FinGPT distributes task-specific instruction sets, including 76.8K sentiment, 82.2K headline and 17.1K financial-QA training rows [15]. FinQA targets expert-written numerical reasoning over financial reports [16]. These resources measure document interpretation, linguistic tasks and market prediction. Financial Laya checkpoints adapt the same direct-decision interface to narrower tasks. The public Laya-Finance card lists financial sentiment, topics, central-bank text, headlines and terminology [28]. A Banking77 adaptation trains typed decision heads on 7,994 customer-service messages while keeping its encoder frozen [29]. sharp-421m updates its encoder and decision head with observed household and respondent survey targets. Supplementary Methods M5 evaluates the external Laya-Finance checkpoint on the same held-out consumer questions. Laya-Fintiment uses 61,017 gradient-training examples for three-class financial sentiment [33]. The Ariadne-Laya finance-topic model trains a 1,049,600-parameter interface over a frozen 421M base for twenty news topics [34]. Household survey supervision adds income, spending, wealth, debt, product participation and attitudes to the decision target, connecting the model’s inputs to a consumer’s economic circumstances. The training corpus combines recorded financial answers with coded household-state, education and occupation targets. Its 2,925,263 training examples correspond to approximately 48 times Laya-Fintiment’s 61,017 examples and 122 times Chinese-Jev Finance’s 24,000 decisions [33], [35]. 2.3. Simulation execution and household financial analysis OneSim, OASIS, AgentSociety and Generative Agents study multi-agent activity at different population sizes and interaction depths [17]–[20]. Their published workloads use environment populations, events, calls or trials (Table S9). S 2-MAS connects a completed 12M-agent purchase-funding application with a 24-month extension of the purchase task across five shock paths and an unshocked reference. Household-finance research studies consumption responses through the distribution of resources and obligations. Kaplan, Moll and Violante’s HANK framework links uninsured income risk and assets with different liquidity to heterogeneous con-sumption responses [32]. S 2-MAS represents income, spending, assets and debt jointly and evaluates how those states change the executable response to a shared financial condition. Population counts in Table S10 distinguish the S 2-MAS resources and executed cohorts beside aggregate and banking-study sample sizes. A real-survey aggregate reference reports 494,157,423 family households [2]. Wheat and Eckerd analyze account 2inflows covering 10 million U.S. households [30]. The JPMorgan Chase Institute healthcare study uses a two-million-family framing and 2.3 million core customers aged 18–64 over 2013–2016 [31]. 

# 3. From population resources to executable experiments 

An experiment in S 2-MAS specifies four inputs: a population, a policy adapter, a task and condition specification, and state-transition rules. The population supplies whole-agent attributes. The task converts current state and available information into a question with explicit alternatives. The adapter returns an option and its probabilities; the execution layer applies the registered consequence and records the resulting state. Ordinary agent, condition and task IDs connect these stages across partitions and retries. A static episode evaluates a selected plan through its horizon. A stateful episode builds the next observation from the previous transition, retaining dated obligations, realized payments and unmet quantities. sharp-4b and sharp-421m provide two trainable policy routes within the same contract. Changing a policy or designated condition preserves the other inputs and the same-household pairing. Policy proposals and their feasible execution remain separately observable. The financial task library instantiates this contract across eight families (Table 1). A medical-bill task changes the outflow to be funded; a loan-term task changes payment dates; an asset-sale task changes when proceeds enter cash. Each task defines its alternatives and action-to-ledger mapping while retaining the population and choice interfaces. Outputs link selected actions to monthly consumption, cash, debt and shortfalls. Consider a synthetic household assigned CNY 6,000 monthly income, CNY 3,000 opening cash, CNY 3,500 essential and CNY 1,800 usual adjustable spending, and no opening debt. A CNY 20,000 purchase goal offers deferral, half financing or full financing at 5% nominal annual interest over 24 installments. Selecting full financing buys CNY 20,000 immediately and records the dated purchase contract. Month-0 cash is 3,000 + 6,000 + 20,000 - 20,000 - 3,500 - 1,800 = CNY 3,700, with repayment beginning in month 1. A later observation contains this closing cash, the remaining purchase need and the next dated obligation. The supplied CPU saved-action example executes these transitions without model calls. 

# 4. Data, computable personas and simulation components 

4.1. Survey data and computable household personas Real-survey data support the household attributes, recorded responses and coded financial states used here. Cleaning, trans-lation and statistical harmonization produce a common 36-field representation of demographics, household structure, income, expenditure, assets, debt and attitudes. Conditional construction preserves the dependence of financial attributes on household characteristics, and statistical calibration aligns selected population margins. Numeric codes support population operations, while decoded fields form model-visible personas. PCA summarizes joint economic structure after construction (Supplemen-tary Methods M1–M3). The reusable 10M consumer bank supports descriptive analysis of the constructed representation. The 12M execution cohort selects whole records without replacement from two 10M-row shards of the 500M resource, with the cohort fixed before inference. The monthly shock extension uses the same 10,000-household panel in every route and purchase-goal condition (Supplementary Methods M11). The same preparation produces supervision for policy learning. The 4,172,421 underlying QA observations have aligned bilingual views, retaining wording, alternatives and monetary units. Target-related fields are omitted from the input, and all questions and language copies for a respondent group share a split. The grouped training partition contains 2,925,263 examples per language. sharp-421m uses the full training partition; the matched downstream comparisons use nested 50k/200k training sets, including the selected 200k sharp-4b route (Appendix S10). 4.2. Decision execution and financial accounting Genese separates decision proposals from authoritative state updates (Figure 2). World stores numeric state in entity– component–system arrays indexed by entity ID. Brain receives bounded decision partitions, reuses model connections and returns policy intents. World validates these intents, resolves registered effects and applies updates. Production PostgreSQL commits world state, the event outbox and command result together; a failed pre-commit transition restores the tick snapshot. An outbox relay publishes committed events asynchronously. The household application evaluates financial consequences in a separate monthly ledger. Input preparation, decision in-ference and ledger calculation overlap across partitions, while household and condition IDs retain the pairing. Integer-cent accounting applies income, selected financing, consumption priorities and scheduled debt payments in a fixed order. The resulting paths record funded amounts and shortfalls. The controlled engine benchmarks measure accepted decisions and writeback. The 12M application measures the asyn-chronous policy-to-ledger runner, and the monthly application adds evolving household states and a synchronization point for bank feedback. Bank offers combine individual affordability with a portfolio multiplier (Figure 13d). The offer contrast starts at zero; financing the larger goal then uses more principal headroom and adds installments, reducing subsequent individual capacity. The rule is g = min (1 , max (0 .25 , 1 − 0.5(1 − c) − 2d)) , where c is repayment coverage and d the thirty-day-arrears share over the preceding three completed months. The assigned 0.25 floor caps the portfolio-wide reduction at 75% of individually affordable principal. For example, CNY 20,000 of individual capacity becomes a CNY 5,000 offer at the floor; a household 20 frozen by recent arrears still receives zero. Existing contracts retain their dated terms. A one-percentage-point fall in coverage reduces the multiplier by 0.005, while a one-point rise in the arrears share reduces it by 0.02, until the floor binds. Persistent income loss is the only route in which the portfolio factor reaches 0.25. Income remains at 72.9% of its original level from month 9, while contractual payments continue and unpaid amounts enter later payable obligations. Repayment cov-erage falls from 49.97%/52.27% in the month-12 offer window to 16.92%/18.10% in month 24. Coverage is higher in the 30k world within each window, while deteriorating over time in both worlds. Between months 12 and 15, the offer gap narrows from CNY −193 .12 to −36 .13 , while offers fall from 6,081.26/5,888.14 to 3,475.76/3,439.63. The negative individual-capacity contribution shrinks from −515 .37 to −273 .29 , outweighing a fall in the positive factor contribution from 322.24 to 237.28 (Table S50). # 7. Discussion and future work 

In S 2-MAS, personas provide decision information and household states, tasks specify alternatives and consequences, and policy adapters let different models use the same accounting rules. The applications cover task families, a population-scale paired comparison and repeated monthly choices. Purchase choices and financial channels The household results distinguish the decision to purchase from the resources used to carry that purchase through time. Age and income organize different purchase responses. Education, employment and household size retain distinct responses under a common age–income mixture, largely through transitions from deferral to purchase. Assets and existing debt then distinguish cash drawdown, actual payments and the obligation remaining at the horizon. Standardizing the profile mixture reverses the ordering of the illustrated asset groups’ consumption increments while preserving differences in cash use and payments. The high-debt group carries much of its added consumption as closing debt. Risk preference and housing tenure add less variation in this application. The age profile also identifies a boundary of the current sharp-421m-mlx policy and purchase menu. All 2,364,307 house-holds with decision makers aged 65 or above selected deferral in both conditions, comprising 19.70% of the panel and con-tributing zero to its consumption increment. Income standardization retains this behavior. Its correspondence to observed lifecycle demand remains untested. Resource paths and monthly financing conditions The monthly extension separates resource adjustment from sensitivity to the purchase goal. Persistent income loss removes more cash than a temporary loss plus one expense because the loss continues longer. Sustained gains support purchases and larger reserve accumulation, while insufficient buffers leave some households with exhausted liquidity. These resource paths narrow or widen the consumption difference between purchase goals under common financing rules. Under the assigned accounting order, purchases and routine spending use resources before dated debt is serviced. Realized payments, arrears and contracts enter the next household observation, and the common bank rule converts recent repayment histories into subsequent offers. The simulated paths reflect both sharp-421m-mlx choices and these financial rules. Finan-cial diaries, contract schedules and bank-offer histories would provide targets for estimating the behavioral and institutional responses represented by those rules. Trainable policies and framework extension sharp-4b and sharp-421m demonstrate two trainable policy routes within the shared interface. sharp-4b uses LoRA and a decision head on Qwen3.5-4B-Base; sharp-421m uses full-data encoder adaptation and is deployed through sharp-421m-mlx in the completed financial applications. The matched controls show the value of LoRA over head-only adaptation and of 21 supervised adaptation for native Laya; the evaluated 4B and 9B routes reach similar accuracy. Full-data sharp-421m-mlx exceeds GLM-5.3-Flash in accuracy and question-macro F1 on the common financial questions. Task-level financial responses also vary by model and input language (Appendix S11). Controlled Genese benchmarks time decision execution and writeback; the large applications measure policy-to-ledger workloads. The monthly application adds state histories and a synchronization point for aggregate bank feedback. Further applications could add asset repricing through changes in holding values and liquidation proceeds, and represent employment transitions and transfers as additional resource channels. 

# 8. Conclusion 

S2-MAS combines household personas, trainable decision policies and financial state updates. sharp-4b and sharp-421m provide two training routes; completed sharp-421m-mlx applications cover eight task families, a twelve-million-household paired one-shot experiment and a 24-month panel under five shock paths and an unshocked reference. Purchase responses differ across household groups, largely through transitions from deferral to purchase. Cash drawdown, repayments and closing debt show how households fund those purchases. In the monthly panel, income losses narrow the consumption gap between purchase goals and sustained gains widen it, while repayment histories update subsequent offers.

A new monthly payment budget is the positive part of half the income remaining after base necessary needs, less existing scheduled installments. The offer is the smaller of the principal supported by that budget and the remaining product ceiling. Material thirty-day arrears in the previous three months freeze the new offer. Each world’s portfolio factor uses its last three completed months: repayment coverage c is actual bank payments divided by payable obligations including arrears, capped at one; d is the material thirty-day-arrears share of active borrower-months. The factor is min (1 , max (0 .25 , 1 − 0.5(1 − c) − 2d)) , rounded to basis points. It multiplies individual offer capacity. Initial coverage and factor equal one. These assigned simulation rules link liquidity and repayment stress to subsequent credit capacity. The 0.25 floor retains a quarter of individual capacity after the portfolio adjustment, with recent-arrears freezes applied before scaling and the CNY 100 minimum draw applied afterward. # Supplementary analysis: training trajectory and prediction controls 

Training procedure and input lengths 

SFT minimizes cross-entropy against the observed answer or coded target. Native Laya RLCD adds a policy-gradient term over four zero-mean Gaussian logit perturbations to this cross-entropy [37]. Proper scoring rules motivate its probability-based rewards [40]. Choice examples receive a clipped log-score and spherical-score reward, with spherical weight 0.75. Each example’s sample rewards are centered, their advantages are standardized, and those advantages weight the perturbed logits’ Gaussian log-density. Noise decreases linearly from 0.4 to 0.1. The ranked-probability reward is used for Score-typed targets; the survey examples use Choice targets. Training uses AdamW, weight decay 0.01, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, 5% linear warm-up and cosine decay to 5% of the initial learning rate. Separate encoder and head rates, batch sizes and stage seeds appear in Table S19 and Table S21. Option order is shuffled during training. Checkpoint temperatures are fitted on the independent calibration split. Development question-macro negative log-likelihood records checkpoint selection before test evaluation; the released 6-round model is final rlcd-2. The QA training encoding uses 512 total tokens, a 256-token question/options allocation and up to 48 tokens per option. One-shot financial deployment uses a 2,048-token complete input, a 1,024-token question/options allocation and up to 192 tokens per option so that monetary paths and repayment terms remain in the model input. The monthly shock extension uses the 8,192-token service budget specified in M11. The original option markers, weights, probability calibration and decoder are retained. The 12M purchase-funding application processes a mean 529.98 actual nonpadding candidate input tokens per choice. Two independent full continuation replicas repeated the 6-round recipe from the same completed 3-round initialization. Each processed 2,925,263 training QA in one SFT pass and one RLCD pass, using six cards, effective batch 768 and 3,809 optimizer updates per stage. Stage-specific temperatures were fitted on the fixed calibration split. All three runs evaluated the same 119-version, 240,458-QA financial-question test with RLCD fixed as the final stage.

# Appendix S9. Auxiliary financial workflow evaluation 

A separate controlled behavioral study contains 1,120 decisions with DeepSeek Flash as actor. Twenty personas drawn from a 1,000-persona parent cohort complete four financial tasks under two conditions. Single-pass choice (SP), candidate generation with self-check (SC), and multi-agent planning, constraint checking and execution use one, two and three model calls per decision. All 480 workflow decisions return legal options (Table S23). Strict adherence requires all hard ledger constraints and the assigned persona goal. Across 160 decisions per workflow, adherence is 67.5% for SP, 81.9% for SC and 86.9% for multi-agent workflow. Hard-constraint violations are 23.1%, 14.4% and 6.9%. Multi-agent workflow improves adherence over SP by 19.4 percentage points, with a persona-clustered exploratory 95% bootstrap interval of 13.1–25.0; the multi-agent workflow–SC difference is 5.0 points, with an interval of -1.9 to 11.9 (Supplementary Figure S7a). Borrowing-amount adherence rises from 22.5% with SP to 77.5% with SC and 90.0% with multi-agent workflow. Consumption adjustment rises from 57.5% to 65.0% and 80.0%. Risk allocation stays near 95%; debt-payment allocation is 95.0%, 87.5% and 82.5%. Additional decision steps are most useful for borrowing and consumption in this task set (Supplementary Figure S7c). A second study fixes ledger and options while changing one goal at a time in 20 constructed personas. Four axes, two polarities, two workflows and two repeats produce 640 decisions and 160 pairs per workflow. Multi-agent workflow changes choices in 96.2% of pairs, compared with 77.5% for SC. Both ends satisfy their goals in 66.2% and 58.1% of pairs. Persona-clustered exploratory intervals are 15.0– 22.5 points for the 18.8-point response difference and 1.2–15.0 for the 8.1-point attainment difference (Supplementary Figure S7b). On the borrowing-versus-purchase axis, multi-agent workflow changes choices in 87.5% of pairs and attains both targets in 60.0%; SC reaches 10.0% for both metrics. On the loss-cap axis, both workflows change every choice, while two-end attainment is 10.0% for multi-agent workflow and 22.5% for SC. Goal sensitivity and successful goal attainment capture different aspects of persona-conditioned financial behavior.

# Appendix S13. Supporting two-million-household fixed-plan application 

This application samples two million constructed households uniformly without replacement from the 10M experimental bank. Each house-hold selects one plan under unavailable and available credit, producing four million one-shot choices and 64 million monthly ledger records over months 0–15. Both conditions share the same initial household state and an income reduction of 20% in months 0–2, with full recovery from month 3. The available product has a CNY 20,000 limit, 7.68% annual rate, twelve installments due in months 1–12 and zero fees (Table S37). A selected plan governs the full ledger path. The supporting application uses the delivered 36-field consumer-bank representation and the assigned old-debt contracts in Table S37. The 12M main application draws from the separately calibrated 500M resource and uses the opening-debt assignment specified in M10. C0 adjusts spending to preserve cash; C1 uses reserves to sustain consumption. Credit options combine partial financing with half-normal adjustable spending or seek to sustain normal spending while preserving reserves. The ledger applies necessary spending, dated debt payments and adjustable spending with integer-cent cash constraints. Unfunded needs and unpaid obligations remain explicit. The selected adjustable-spending target defines the corresponding execution shortfall. Across the full panel, available minus unavailable credit yields CNY 293.76 less cumulative consumption, CNY 311.50 more closing cash and CNY 17.74 more closing old debt (Table S38).
