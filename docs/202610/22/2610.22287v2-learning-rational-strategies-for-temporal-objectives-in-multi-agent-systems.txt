## Abstract 

We study rational synthesis for stochastic multi-agent systems with unknown dynamics, where each agent is equipped with its own probabilistic temporal objective specified using Probabilistic Linear Temporal Logic on Finite Traces (pltl 𝑓 ). We impose solution con-cepts (e.g., Nash equilibrium, subgame-perfect equilibrium) as con-straints on the joint strategy profile, using payoffs derived from each agent’s probability of satisfying its temporal objective. In particular, we formulate per-agent rational synthesis as finding a strategy pro-file – if one exists – such that (i) the designated agent’s probabilistic temporal constraint is satisfied, and (ii) the profile represents a sta-ble solution under a chosen solution concept. We give conditional convergence guarantees for tabular Nash-Q learning, certify sat-isfaction with simultaneous finite-sample confidence bounds, and assess strategic stability with approximate best-response checking. Our approach operates in a stochastic Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) environment, where model-free learning algo-rithms attempt to attain extremal satisfaction probabilities for tem-poral objectives automatically compiled into reward automata. Our approach is empirically validated using 8 general-sum concurrent stochastic games. 

## Keywords 

Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning, General-Sum Games, Rational Learning, Probabilistic Temporal Objectives 

## 1 Introduction 

Strategic reasoning in multi-agent systems (MAS) hinges on de-termining what outcomes groups of agents (termed coalitions) can bring about through their interactions. In many multi-agent settings, especially safety-critical systems, properties of interest go beyond plain reward signals and include temporally extended goals such as reachability/liveness (e.g., eventually reaching a tar-get region or completing a mission), or safety invariants (e.g., al-ways avoid unsafe regions, or never violate constraints) [4, 23]. Similar objectives are naturally captured by temporal logics [38] which provide precise languages and semantics to specify and ver-ify such properties. Languages relevant to specification of strate-gic properties include Alternating-Time Temporal Logic (atl [3]), Dynamic Coalition Logic [22], Probabilistic Alternating-𝜇 Calcu-lus [47], Game Description Logic (gdl [16]), and See-To-It-That (stit [7]). However, such logics typically resolve non-determinism by restricting to coalitional modalities which aim to universally and unilaterally (inevitably) force a property to be true. On the other hand, rational synthesis [20, 52] aims to find strate-gies that are self-enforcing through stable equilibria, instead of quantifying against all possible behaviour (including irrational be-haviour) by other agents who have their own objectives. Therefore, rational synthesis is typically less conservative than adversarial synthesis. Settings we consider can be represented formally as (general-sum) stochastic games , which model multiple decision-making agents that interact over time in a dynamic environment with probabilistic behaviours. Each agent repeatedly selects an action, and the combination of agents’ non-deterministic choices and the environment’s probabilistic transitions determines the evolution of the game. Stochastic games have been applied in domains as diverse as cybersecurity [60], epidemics [29], auctions [39], and routing in stochastic traffic networks [59]. We use an expressive specification style in which each agent has its own probabilistic temporal objective as a logical formula inter-preted on finite traces. Finite traces have a natural correspondence with reinforcement learning (RL) as tasks are often trained and executed episodically. Rationality is captured by imposing that the learned joint strategy profile satisfies a specific solution concept (e.g., ( 𝜀 -)Nash equilibrium, subgame-perfect equilibrium), where each agent’s payoff reflects the preferred direction of its satisfaction probability. Our approach emphasises learning a single committed strategy profile, allowing tractability by not requiring an explicit transition model of the underlying stochastic game which gener-ally grows combinatorially with respect to the number of agents involved. 

Contributions. Our contributions are as follows: (i) We formulate a learning-based rational-synthesis objective for finite-horizon sto-chastic games in which each agent has a probabilistic ltl 𝑓 objective and utilities encode the preferred direction of satisfaction probabil-ities; (ii) We compile per-agent ltl 𝑓 objectives into deterministic reward automata and show that terminal automaton rewards have expectation equal to satisfaction probability; (iii) We provide theo-retical analysis for the reward-automaton encoding, discounting effects, finite-sample satisfaction certification, and finite-horizon equilibrium existence and convergence; (iv) We evaluate satisfac-tion and strategic stability on 8 benchmark environment instances. For reasons of space, proofs are deferred to Appendix D. A high-level overview of our approach is presented in Appendix B.

## 3 Encoding Quantitative Objectives 

We outline how to translate pltl 𝑓 objectives into Markovian re-ward signals for applying standard (model-free) multi-agent RL algorithms, while preserving the single strategy profile witness re-quirement of strategy synthesis. We compile each ltl 𝑓 path objective into a deterministic reward automaton, then take the synchronous product of these automata with the stochastic game. Terminal acceptance rewards recover satisfaction probabilities (Theorem 1), and the product makes the objectives Markovian while preserving payoffs and equilibria (The-orem 2). Discounting results quantify how terminal rewards re-late to satisfaction probabilities under fixed and early-terminating horizons. Sampling results provide finite-sample certificates for probabilistic thresholds. Algorithm 1 summarises the procedure.

Reward Automata. For each agent, a deterministic finite automa-ton (DFA) tracks whether the labelled trace of an episode satisfies the ltl 𝑓 path formula inside its probabilistic constraint. Its state summarises the history relevant to that formula and is updated as each new state label is read. For example, the automaton in Figure 1 remembers whether a breach has occurred and whether a collision has made the objective impossible. At episode end, its accepting states identify successful traces, and averaging these binary out-comes estimates satisfaction probability. Constructing this automa-ton takes 2EXPTIME in the worst case [17]. In practice, temporal objectives are often simple and avoid deep temporal nesting, allow-ing existing tools to construct their automata in milliseconds. Thus, the worst-case complexity need not make compilation a practical bottleneck. Figure 1 illustrates a simple example.

## 3.3 Existence of Equilibria 

For infinite-horizon concurrent stochastic games with qualitative objectives, equilibrium existence is only known for certain low levels of the Borel hierarchy (e.g., safety objectives). For example, existence of 𝜀 -NE for general 𝑛 -player concurrent reachability games remains an open question [10]. In the finite-horizon case, however, existence is classical: every finite extensive-form game with perfect recall has a subgame-perfect equilibrium [45], and a Markov one is obtained by backward induction, solving at each state and remaining time the one-shot stage game whose payoffs are the continuation values, which has a mixed equilibrium by Nash’s theorem [40] (see e.g., [21]). We restate this standard result for our setting in Proposition 2: Proposition 2 (Existence of SPNE in Finite Horizon [45]) . Ev-ery fully observed concurrent stochastic game with finite state and action spaces, Markovian stage and terminal rewards, and horizon bounded by 𝐻 admits a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE )in randomised strategies that is Markov , i.e. each agent’s strategy depends only on the current state and the remaining time 𝐻 − 𝑡 . In particular, this holds for the fully observed product game ̂︁ ℳ𝐻 with utilities from Definition 2. 

Proof in Appendix D.13. Proposition 2 ensures an equilibrium in the unrestricted class of randomised policies over the fully observed product state. However, existence does not guarantee feasibility for agent 𝑖 in Definition 6: ℰ𝜁 may be nonempty while ℱ𝑖 𝜁 is empty.

## 5 Related Work 

We provide an extended review of related work in Appendix F, but summarise the most relevant works here. Hahn et al. consider verify-ing minimax objectives using the logic patl, although they consider a model checking problem rather than a synthesis problem as they allow relabelling, and only allow an objective considered between a single coalition and its complement. A closely related model-based tool is PRISM -Games [33], which supports verification and strat-egy synthesis for rpatl-style probabilistic and reward objectives in stochastic games, including multi-objective and equilibrium-based extensions [31, 32, 34]. These methods assume explicit transition models. Varricchione et al. construct a reward machine from an atl specification, but their approach assumes a fully cooperative grand coalition, which in turn allows delegating to reward ma-chines generated for individual agents. Hu et al. consider reward machines for stochastic games, but are limited to exact Nash equi-libria, non-quantitative specifications, and two-player general-sum games. Jothimurugan et al. provide a bespoke enumerate-and-verify procedure for a non-probabilistic task specification language that is designed exclusively for equilibria with high social welfare. Ham-mond et al. studied temporal-logic reward optimisation of a sum of probabilities, but restricted their analysis to cooperative MARL. Our use of equilibrium constraints is inspired by classical rational synthesis [14, 20, 52]. Subramanian et al. study reward-machine based learning in general-sum stochastic games, proposing CROM and QROM, which combine counterfactual experience updates over reward-machine states with opponent modelling and provide tab-ular convergence and finite-time guarantees for Nash Q-values under additional assumptions. However, our paper deals with prob-abilistic thresholds rather than discounted rewards. We provide sampling-based certification guarantees and bounds on discounting effects, as well as equilibrium stability and convergence analysis. 

## 6 Conclusions and Future Work 

Our approach targets multiple strategic agents, each equipped with its own probabilistic temporal-logic objective in pltl 𝑓 over finite traces. Each agent seeks a strategy satisfying its own probabilistic threshold within a profile that is stable under a chosen equilibrium notion (solution concept), interpreted over the utilities induced by the probabilistic requirements. Compared to existing approaches, our work focuses on model-free strategy synthesis in a learning setting, without requiring known transition dynamics. This allows considering objectives beyond those that a strategic coalition can unilaterally enforce, as required by logics such as atl and patl. We analyse how dis-counting affects the learning of probabilistic objectives, establish conditional convergence guarantees for tabular Nash-Q learning, and provide algorithm-agnostic finite-sample certificates of objec-tive satisfaction. Future work could target nested probabilistic properties and the undiscounted infinite-horizon case for 𝜔 -regular properties, which would require learning maximal end components [11, 24], although this is non-trivial as end components for multi-agent sys-tems depend on the dynamics of other agents. Another extension of interest could be evaluating different solution concepts, such as a jointly satisficing objective [43]. It may also be worth investigating confidence-aware equilibrium selection with Nash-Q ; however this introduces statistical noise into the selector and non-local depen-dence across states.

Probabilistic Strategic Logics and Synthesis Tools. Hahn et al. [25] translate reachability-normal patl objectives into rewards for Min-imax Q-Learning. Their model-checking procedure recursively re-labels states for nested modalities, rather than finding one strategy witnessing all subformulae. It considers a single coalition in turn-based games. Bozkurt et al. [8] similarly study model-free learning for ltl objectives in two-player turn-based zero-sum games, using deterministic Rabin automata. The zero-sum fragments of PRISM -Games [31] synthesise strategies for probabilistic and reward-based rpatl objectives from known transition models. Single- and multi-objective properties concern one coalition against its complement; where optimal strategies need not exist [12], the tool computes 𝜀 -approximations of Pareto-optimal strategies. Equilibrium-based extensions synthesise subgame-perfect, social-welfare-optimal Nash equilibria in concurrent stochastic games [32–34], also assuming known models. Varricchione et al. [53] construct reward machines from atl specifications for a fully cooperative grand coalition. 

Rational Synthesis. Rational synthesis [20, 52] considers agents pursuing their own temporal goals. Fisman et al. [20] reduce ltl rational synthesis to satisfiability in Extended Strategy Logic, ob-taining 2EXPTIME -completeness for dominant-strategy, Nash and subgame-perfect solution concepts. Ummels [52] studies pure strategies in infinite multiplayer turn-based games with 𝜔 -regular objectives, establishing exis-tence, payoff-constrained decision procedures and finite-state implementations for subgame-perfect Nash equilibria. Condurache et al. [14] study complexity refinements and cooperative and non-cooperative variants. Our setting combines per-agent probabilistic ltl 𝑓 requirements with learning from unknown dynamics under a single committed profile. Policy Space Response Oracles [35] instead train approxi-mate best responses against a meta-strategy over a policy popula-tion. 

Rational Verification. Rational verification [58] asks whether a temporal property holds on some or all equilibrium behaviours. Equilibrium Logic expresses such properties through modalities 

[NE ] and ⟨NE ⟩ over Nash-equilibrium computations. Model check-ing is 2EXPTIME -hard in general, with matching upper bounds for restricted fragments. 

Commitment-aware Logics. Strategy Logic (sl) explicitly quanti-fies and binds strategies, subsumes atl ⋆, and can express equilib-rium through stability against deviations. Model checking for full sl is decidable but non-elementary. Extensions of atl evaluate nested formulas under previously selected strategies, including strategy contexts (atl sc ) [15], explicit strategies (atl es ) [55], and irrevocable semantics [2].
