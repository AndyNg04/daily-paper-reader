Abstract 

Conversational AI systems built on Large Lan-guage Models (LLMs) require routing mech-anisms that balance answer quality and re-sponse latency at scale. However, existing LLM routing benchmarks assume full observ-ability of all candidate model outcomes, under-emphasize latency as a first-class metric, and fragment evaluation across heterogeneous pro-tocols misaligned with real-world settings. We introduce ROUTER WORLD , a comprehensive benchmark for LLM routing in conversational GenAI. R OUTER WORLD unifies 371k single-turn responses and 76k multi-turn agentic tra-jectories, adopts natural user interactions with a standardized LLM-as-a-judge protocol, and decouples efficiency from transient pricing via hardware-grounded latency annotations. Cru-cially, R OUTER WORLD formalizes routing un-der partial observability through two setups: 

Cold-Start , which restricts learning to sparse, static logs, and Voyager , which models con-tinuous data streams where routing decisions shape future training distributions. Evaluat-ing 13 routing baselines, we find that most routers over-emphasize accuracy while ignor-ing latency under partial observability. Code and benchmark data are publicly available. 

1 Introduction 

Conversational AI systems powered by large lan-guage models (LLMs) increasingly operate at mas-sive scale, serving millions of user queries per minute across chat assistants (Team et al., 2023; Llama, 2023; Chiang et al., 2024; Amazon, 2025), search interfaces (Chen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023), and interactive agents (Yao et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2024). In these settings, every query is user-facing and latency-sensitive. Rather than relying on a single monolithic model,

modern conversational systems deploy heteroge-neous pools of LLMs that vary widely in capability, specialization, and inference latency. Intelligent routing, which dynamically selects the most appro-priate model for each query, has thus emerged as a central mechanism for scaling Generative AI while balancing response quality and cost. Recent work has explored various routing strate-gies, including supervised classifiers trained on of-fline logs (Ong et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025b), bandit-based methods that optimize routing policies via online interaction (Panda et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025), and cascade architectures that progressively escalate queries to stronger mod-els (Chen et al., 2024). To facilitate comparison across methods, benchmarks have been proposed (Hu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025). Despite this progress, existing evaluation methodologies for LLM routing remain misaligned with real-world deployment constraints. A cen-tral limitation is the widespread assumption of full observability: as illustrated in Figure 1 (Left), stan-dard protocols assume the router has access to cor-rectness and efficiency outcomes of all candidate models per query. This diverges sharply from de-ployed systems operating at million-scale through-put, where running all candidates per request is pro-hibitively expensive and incompatible with rapid iteration cycles (Chapelle and Li, 2011; Joachims et al., 2017). In practice, outcomes of unchosen models remain counterfactual and unavailable for learning. Existing benchmarks also frequently use monetary costs as efficiency proxies (Dean and Bar-roso, 2013), neglecting latency. Because natural dialogue relies on ∼200ms inter-turn gaps (Stivers et al., 2009), multi-second delays break conversa-tional fluidity, so evaluation metrics must ground efficiency in stable, hardware-level characteristics. jointly measure routing quality and efficiency, we introduce L-DCG , a latency-aware variant of Dis-counted Cumulative Gain that weighs each cor-rect selection by how close its latency is to the fastest correct model (formal definition in Sec-tion 4.4). Even under idealized full observability, the strongest baseline reaches only 39.6% latency-penalized accuracy (Acc@1-L) compared to the 68.0% empirical upper bound, capturing just 62% of the optimal efficiency ceiling as measured by L-DCG, indicating substantial headroom in latency-aware routing. This gap widens substantially un-der more realistic partial observability: deprived of counterfactual feedback, routers usually over-emphasize task accuracy (Acc@1) at the expense of latency, inflating accuracy by an average of 24.7% while overall efficiency deteriorates sharply. In the most extreme case, A VENGER (Zhang et al., 2025b), a cluster-voting router, collapses into a degenerate Route-to-Largest policy (Lai and Ye, 2026), achieving 89.1% Acc@1 but suffering a 79% reduction in optimal efficiency. These feedback-induced failures persist in the Voyager setting, where standard routers show little improve-ment across streaming interaction, while bandit-based methods with explicit exploration, such as BARP (Wei et al., 2025), continue to improve, high-lighting observation diversity. These findings moti-vate the next generation of router design. Further, we provide analyses to further clarify routing behavior. We examine long-horizon agen-2tic tasks, where policy collapse emerges through cumulative latency effects rather than single-step decisions (Section 5.3), and study warm-up ini-tialization, finding that most routers function as policy distillers rather than independent optimizers (Section 5.4). By open-sourcing R OUTER WORLD -Core and R OUTER WORLD -Agentic 2, we provide a scalable, deployment-aligned environment for diag-nosing these failures and advancing robust, latency-aware routing for conversational AI. 

2 Related Work 

Non-Predictive and Sequential Routing. Early LLM routing relied on non-predictive orchestra-tion, where decisions are resolved through iterative execution rather than a single selection step. Fru-galGPT (Chen et al., 2024) formalizes this via the LLM cascade, escalating queries to progressively stronger models when a confidence threshold is not met. More recent work generalizes this into ensemble-like frameworks: AutoMix (Aggarwal et al., 2024) formulates routing as a POMDP with self-verification signals, and Router-R1 (Zhang et al., 2025a) instantiates the router as a reasoning LLM that plans multi-step invocations. While flex-ible, these approaches invoke multiple models per query, leading to compounded inference latency. 

Predictive Routing and Online Model Selection. 

Predictive approaches formulate model selection as a single decision made prior to generation. Hy-bridLLM (Ding et al., 2024) trains a BERT-based classifier to route between a small and a large model, while RouteLLM (Ong et al., 2025) and Carrot (Tsiourvas et al., 2025) learn from prefer-ence or reward signals. GraphRouter (Feng et al., 2025b) models query–model interactions via graph-based message passing, BaRP (Wei et al., 2025) formulates routing as learning from bandit feed-back using REINFORCE-style policy gradients, and Avenger (Zhang et al., 2025b) employs train-free cluster-based voting over embedding neigh-borhoods. IPR (Feng et al., 2025a) targets pro-duction settings with user-controlled quality–cost trade-offs, and PILOT (Panda et al., 2025) ex-plores online contextual bandits with exploration– exploitation schedules tailored to routing. 

Fully-Observable Routing. Despite this variety of methods, almost all predictive routers are de-signed and evaluated under full observability : at

training time, the learner has access to the out-come of every model on every query, reducing routing to supervised learning over complete la-bels. This assumption underlies RouteLLM (Ong et al., 2025), HybridLLM (Ding et al., 2024), GraphRouter (Feng et al., 2025b), Carrot (Tsiour-vas et al., 2025), Avenger (Zhang et al., 2025b), and the Best-of-N style oracles commonly reported as upper bounds (Hu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025). While convenient, full observability requires run-ning every model per query, an operational pattern that does not scale to production-scale conversa-tional deployments which preclude exhaustive par-allel inference (Chapelle and Li, 2011; Joachims et al., 2017). It also obscures feedback-loop dy-namics (confirmation bias, policy collapse, self-reinforcement) that only emerge when the router’s choices shape the data it subsequently learns from. A few methods acknowledge partial feedback, no-tably BaRP (Wei et al., 2025), but its evaluation still assumes rich offline supervision. Our benchmark makes partial observability a first-class evaluation regime and exposes how existing routers behave when counterfactual outcomes are not available. 

Router Benchmarks. Routing methods are typi-cally compared using benchmarks such as Router-Bench (Hu et al., 2024), RouterArena (Lu et al., 2026), RouterEval (Huang et al., 2025), and LLM-RouterBench (Li et al., 2026), which release pre-computed outcomes for many query–model pairs. These resources assume full observability and ei-ther omit latency or treat it as a coarse boundary constraint rather than a primary optimization ob-jective. They also rely on raw benchmark prompts and monetary pricing as efficiency proxies, both of which introduce artifacts and drift that are ill-suited to conversational deployment. ROUTER -WORLD instead (i) formalizes partial observability via the Cold-Start and Voyager setups, (ii) grounds efficiency in hardware-measured latency that is stable across pricing changes, (iii) adopts natural conversational-form queries, and (iv) spans both single-turn and long-horizon agentic tasks, offering a more deployment-aligned evaluation. 

3 Problem Definition 

3.1 LLM Routing 

Let M = {m1, . . . , m K } denote a fixed set of candidate LLMs. A routing policy 

π : S → M 

3maps an observed state s ∈ S to a selected model 

m ∈ M . The state s represents the full or par-tial input available to the router. After selecting a model, the router observes a signal reflecting task performance and computational efficiency. In this work, we focus on latency-derived efficiency (Sec-tion 4.3) rather than monetary cost. The routing objective is to learn a policy π that maximizes the 

expected utility of the system under these perfor-mance and latency constraints. 

3.2 Fully-Observable LLM Routing 

In the fully-observable setting, the router has ac-cess to outcomes of all candidate models for each input during training, reducing routing to super-vised learning over complete labels. This has been the dominant assumption in the LLM routing lit-erature to date, underlying most predictive routers and benchmarks surveyed in Section 2 (e.g., Ding et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025b; Tsiourvas et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Hu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025). It is convenient for of-fline analysis but relies on counterfactual outcomes that are typically unavailable in practice without costly offline replay across all candidate models. 

3.3 Partially-Observable LLM Routing 

We propose a more realistic scenario of partial observability, where the router observes feedback 

only for the selected model. Formally, after choos-ing m = π(s), the router observes r(s, m ), but the counterfactuals {r(s, m ′)}m′̸ =m remain missing. The learner must contend with incomplete informa-tion. We study two modes of partial observability: 

Cold-Start Routing. The Cold-Start setting rep-resents the initialization phase of a routing system. The router must learn from a static historical log generated by a uniform random policy, where each task yields a single model outcome (response, la-tency, and correctness). This evaluates the router’s ability to extract a viable policy from high-entropy, incomplete data in a single offline training round. We explore other warm-up policies in Section 5.4. 

Voyager Routing. In real-world deployments, routing decisions are made over streaming inputs, and the router’s own policy determines which model outcomes are observed. Voyager mode cap-tures this: training proceeds in iterative stages over temporally ordered data chunks {C1, . . . , C T }.The process is initialized with C1 (ground-truth feedback under an initial random policy); at stage 

t, the policy is trained on {C1, . . . , C t} and used to route Ct+1 , with checkpoint selection on a held-out development set. To keep evaluation repro-ducible without live model inference, we simulate deployment via a hidden fully-observable engine 

derived from R OUTER WORLD : it stores outcomes for all candidates but reveals only the feedback corresponding to the router’s selected action. 

4 Benchmark Construction 

4.1 Dataset Selection Principles 

We construct R OUTER WORLD according to four guiding principles: (1) Task Diversity and Bal-ance , spanning six major categories (e.g., math, code, reasoning) to prevent domain skew; (2) 

Discriminative Power , selecting examples where model performance varies to necessitate intelligent routing; (3) Latency Awareness , integrating real-world timing metrics (Yu et al., 2022; Kwon et al., 2023) to evaluate trade-offs; and (4) Reproducibil-ity , enforcing strict train-test separation and auto-matic metrics. 4.2 RouterWorld Benchmark Construction 

ROUTER WORLD comprises two complementary subsets. ROUTER WORLD -Core contains single-turn, end-to-end tasks assessing a router’s ability to select appropriate models for standalone queries of varying difficulty. It integrates 12 established benchmarks covering logical and commonsense reasoning (Zellers et al., 2019; Clark et al., 2018; Thorne et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018), math (Cobbe et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2023; Hendrycks et al., 2021b) and tool use (Patil et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025), code generation (Hendrycks et al., 2021a; Li et al., 2024), data analysis (Li et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2023), and open-ended chit-chat (Yamashita et al., 2023). ROUTER WORLD -Agentic focuses on long-horizon, multi-turn agentic tasks that require envi-ronment interaction, covering computer use, house-hold assistance, and e-commerce workflows. All benchmarks are standardized into a unified rout-ing evaluation framework; full preprocessing and standardization procedures are in Appendix C.

4.3 Annotation Model Selection & Latency. We select 7 popular open-weight models (2B–235B parameters), cre-ating a heterogeneous landscape of capability and inference cost, where latencies range from ∼200ms to over 7s. We model E2E latency for query-model pairs (q, m ) using empirically measured Time-To-First-Token (TTFT m) and Inter-Token La-tency (ITL m): latency (q, m ) = TTFT m + ( Nq,m − 1) × ITL m,

(1) where Nq,m is the number of tokens generated. For multi-step agentic trajectories, latency is accumu-lated across all generation steps. Since raw re-sponse and trajectory tokens are released for each task, the framework can be easily adapted to mone-tary cost. Full profiling workflow and model speci-fications are in Appendices D.1 and D. 

Dataset Evaluation. Source datasets employ heterogeneous evaluation metrics, and individual models often require distinct prompting and post-processing pipelines. To streamline the setup and comparison while mitigating biases introduced by dataset-specific metrics and prompt engineering, we adopt an LLM-as-a-Judge framework that pro-duces a standardized binary judgment ( correct or 

incorrect ) for each model output via a strong LLM, i.e. Claude-4.5-Sonnet . This binary signal is intended to approximate coarse-grained user feed-back in interactive systems, where users typically either adopt a satisfactory response or reject it to ask a follow-up query, rather than providing dense or calibrated rewards (Amershi et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2022). We validate this protocol through a blind expert annotation study on 150 (query, model, response) triples: six PhD-level an-notators achieve high inter-annotator agreement (Fleiss’ κ=0 .84 , Krippendorff’s α=0 .87 ), and the LLM judge matches the 6-expert majority-vote con-sensus at κ=0 .84 (F1 =0 .93 , 92% agreement), ap-proaching the human ceiling and consistent with prior LLM-judge evaluations (Zheng et al., 2023a). Full validation details are provided in Appendix H. 

Conversational-Form Query Generation. Real user inputs to voice assistants are typically brief and goal-oriented rather than carefully structured prompts. Further, since benchmarks have been publicly accessible for years, downstream LLMs are susceptible to data contamination and may have memorized surface-level queries and prompt formats during pre-training (Tseng et al., 2025; Golchin and Surdeanu, 2024; Sainz et al., 2023), artificially inflating performance and introducing spurious signals that mislead routers. We there-fore apply a multi-stage transformation pipeline: original prompts establish task specifications; a strong external LLM ( DeepSeek-V3.1 , excluded from the routing pool) rewrites each as a natural user query; rewritten queries are validated against the original specs, with semantically drifted ones filtered out (Li et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2023b; Wang et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023).

5.2 Main Results ROUTER WORLD is Challenging. ROUTER -WORLD poses a substantial challenge for exist-ing routers (Table 3). Under full observabil-ity, the empirical upper bound Oracle (Best-of-8) achieves 67.97% Acc@1-L / 0.7140 L-DCG, whereas the strongest non-oracle baseline Gen-SFT (Qwen-2.5-1.5B-ins) reaches only 39.61% Acc@1-L / 0.4439 L-DCG, leaving a large gap in latency-aware routing. Effective routing re-quires context-aware decision-making rather than coarse model attributes, as Route-To-Largest at-tains 89.11% Acc@1 but just 9.10% Acc@1-L Transitioning to partial observability drives a drastic shift: on average, Acc@1 inflates by 24.7 pp while L-DCG drops by over 0.16. Without cross-model comparisons, routers cannot deter-mine whether a faster model would have also been correct, and become risk-averse (favoring accuracy at the cost of latency). In the extreme, Avenger collapses into Route-To-Largest (89.11% Acc@1, 0.1494 L-DCG), losing any ability to navigate ef-ficiency trade-offs, a fundamental challenge that ROUTER WORLD explicitly captures. 

Training Corpus is Important for Routers. To illustrate the necessity of domain-specific train-ing data, we probe Generative AI Routers. Zero-shot prompting with explicit latency profiles severely underperforms (Qwen-2.5-0.5B-Instruct: 7.38% Acc@1-L, 0.1211 L-DCG), while SFT on the R OUTER WORLD corpus drives Acc@1-L to 37.55% and L-DCG to 0.4282. This shows that routing cannot be reduced to a linear relationship between model size and query difficulty: in ∼27% of cases, lightweight models like Gemma-2-2b ex-hibit verbose generation patterns that yield higher latency than larger models. LLM routing is a com-plex, non-linear decision-making process that re-quires internalizing empirical interaction logs. 

5.3 Routing in Agentic Tasks 

We further evaluate routing baselines on R OUTER -WORLD -Agentic, where models interact with en-vironments over multiple steps across three bench-marks with new 3-model and 6-model candidate pools (14B–235B). Figure 3 (see Appendix B) shows a striking phenomenon: routing baselines exhibit severe performance compression. In both pools, non-collapsed methods cluster with low vari-ance ( σ2 = 0 .81 for Acc@1-L in the 3-model fully-observable setting; σ2 = 4 .35 in the 6-model set-ting; vs. σ2 = 29 .5 in R OUTER WORLD -Core), and compression intensifies under partial observabil-ity, where multiple routers collapse into degenerate near-single-model policies. We attribute this to a factor overlooked by current routing paradigms: the impact of planning steps. High-capability mod-els often resolve complex tasks in far fewer inter-action steps than weaker but faster models, neutral-izing or even inverting the conventional accuracy– latency trade-off and leaving no meaningful Pareto frontier to exploit. Per-benchmark breakdowns and planning–latency analysis are in Appendix I.

Cold-Start v.s. Voyager The Voyager setting in-crementally accumulates streaming data over five rounds. Surprisingly, this yields only marginal im-provements for most methods (Figure 5, see Ap-pendix B): KNN and MLP L-DCG remains effec-tively flat, while only BaRP shows a noticeable gain (0.294 → 0.320 ), consistent with its REINFORCE-based formulation. Supervised methods suffer from confirmation bias under partial observability, rein-forcing their initial blind spots by training on their own past decisions; policy-gradient methods in-stead use observed feedback as an unbiased reward estimator and naturally explore alternatives. Col-lecting more data under the same biased policy is thus insufficient to mitigate distribution shift: ob-servation diversity matters more than volume. 

Warm-Up Initialization. To study how initial policy quality influences downstream learning, we simulate three warm-up oracles of increasing so-phistication: Qwen-1.5B-Instruct, Claude, and Claude augmented with RAG retrieval from the dev set. Each rolls out selections to generate warm-up logs, on which we then train routers under cold-start, partially-observable conditions. As Figure 4 (see Appendix B) shows, downstream routers cor-relate near-perfectly with the metric distribution of each warm-up policy: no method surpasses the oracle’s own L-DCG, and the ordering of Acc@1, Acc@1-L, DCG, and L-DCG is preserved. Current predictive routing thus largely distills the logging oracle’s biases rather than discovering an indepen-dent policy, motivating exploration–exploitation strategies that actively seek diverse observations. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced R OUTER WORLD , a benchmark that unifies 371k single-turn responses and 76k multi-turn agentic trajectories to evaluate LLM routing under realistic deployment constraints. Router-World addresses key gaps in existing benchmarks by formalizing partial observability through Cold-Start and Voyager settings, grounding efficiency in hardware-measured latency rather than volatile pricing, and adopting natural conversational-form queries. Evaluating 13 routing baselines reveals large headroom for latency-aware routing even un-der idealized conditions. Our analysis highlights the critical importance of partial observability as a design constraint. By open-sourcing our bench-mark and evaluation infrastructure, RouterWorld serves as a foundation for advancing efficiency-aware LLM routing in scalable conversational AI. 8Limitations 

Multi-modal Tasks. ROUTER WORLD currently evaluates routing only among text-only LLMs. As foundation models increasingly accept vision and audio inputs, practical routing systems may need to decide both which model and which modality to invoke. Extending R OUTER WORLD to multimodal candidate pools requires new datasets and evalua-tion protocols that capture heterogeneous latency and cost profiles across modalities, which we leave for future work. 

Closed-source Models. ROUTER WORLD fo-cuses on open-weight LLMs to enable reproducible, hardware-grounded latency measurement, which is central to our latency-aware routing framing. As a result, widely used closed-source models (e.g., Claude, GPT-4o) are not included in the candidate pool. Because we release raw responses, trajec-tory tokens, and generation metadata for every task–model pair, the benchmark can be extended to closed-source APIs by substituting our hardware-grounded latencies with API-measured latencies or per-token pricing. 

Ethical Considerations 

ROUTER WORLD is built by aggregating 12 pub-licly released research benchmarks; we redistribute only data permitted under each source’s license and preserve original attribution. By design, the bench-mark encourages directing simple queries to small, efficient models rather than defaulting to expensive frontier systems. Widespread adoption of latency-aware routing could meaningfully reduce the en-ergy and compute footprint of large-scale conver-sational AI, benefiting both service providers and the environment. At the same time, our findings show that routers trained under partial observability can collapse into degenerate policies that consistently favor a single model, potentially degrading response quality for specific query types or user populations without op-erators noticing. The Voyager setting we propose is designed to surface exactly these failure modes so that future routing algorithms can be stress-tested before deployment.

A Dataset Selection Principles 

The construction of R OUTER WORLD is guided by the following design principles to ensure a compre-hensive and fair assessment of routing capabilities: • Capability Diversity: We include bench-marks spanning six major task categories. Mathematical reasoning, code generation, structured query generation, commonsense and multi-hop reasoning, factual verification and tool use, and open-ended dialogue. This diversity ensures that no single model dom-inates across all tasks, making routing deci-sions non-trivial. • Discriminative Power: Each benchmark is selected to induce heterogeneous performance across the model pool. In particular, smaller or specialized models (e.g., deepseek-math 

on GSM8K or codegemma on APPS) may outperform larger general-purpose models on domain-specific tasks. Such performance di-vergence is essential for revealing the benefits of intelligent routing over static model selec-tion. • Latency Awareness: Beyond answer correct-ness, we incorporate real-world serving la-tency measurements, including time-to-first-token and end-to-end response time. This en-ables evaluation of quality–latency trade-offs that are critical in practical deployment set-tings. • Reproducibility: We prioritize benchmarks with well-established evaluation protocols and automatic metrics, avoiding subjective hu-man judgments to ensure consistent and repro-ducible comparisons across routing methods. • Balanced Task Distribution: Unlike prior benchmarks that are often skewed toward spe-cific capabilities, RouterWorld enforces a bal-anced distribution across task categories. This design mitigates evaluation bias introduced by over-represented task types and enables fair assessment of routing performance across diverse capabilities. • Training–Testing Separation: Our study evaluates both router models and routing algo-rithms, so we prioritize benchmarks that pro-vide train and test splits. C Benchmark Dataset Details 

The R OUTER WORLD benchmark comprises the following datasets, selected to represent a diverse range of linguistic and symbolic reasoning tasks: 

HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019). A sentence completion benchmark that challenges models to select the most plausible continuation of a given context. It employs adversarial filtering to con-struct distractors that are deceptively plausible to language models while remaining clearly incorrect to humans, effectively testing commonsense rea-soning. 

GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021). A dataset of 8.5K linguistically diverse grade-school math word prob-lems. Each problem typically requires 2–8 steps of elementary arithmetic, testing a model’s ability to perform reliable sequential calculations. 

ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018). The par-tition of the AI2 Reasoning Challenge contain-ing grade-school science questions that cannot be solved via simple retrieval or pattern matching. These items require deeper reasoning and scien-tific understanding. 

APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021a). A code gener-ation benchmark comprising 10,000 programming problems collected from open-access platforms like Codeforces. The dataset spans three difficulty lev-els (introductory, interview, and competition) and requires generating executable Python programs from natural language specifications. 

BIRD-SQL (Li et al., 2024). A large-scale, cross-domain text-to-SQL benchmark featuring real-world databases with noisy and incomplete values. It emphasizes schema understanding and database content reasoning over complex relational structures. 

FEVER (Thorne et al., 2018). A fact verifica-tion dataset containing 185K claims derived from Wikipedia. Models must classify claims as Sup-ported , Refuted , or NotEnoughInfo based on re-trieved evidence, testing evidence-grounded factual reasoning. 

Gorilla APIBench (Patil et al., 2024). An eval-uation of LLM-based API call generation across major hubs (TorchHub, TensorHub, HuggingFace). It tests the ability to produce accurate, executable API invocations from natural language instructions. 

HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018). A multi-hop question answering benchmark requiring reasoning over multiple supporting documents. Questions ne-cessitate combining information from two or more Wikipedia passages, testing compositional reason-ing and information synthesis. 

PersonaChat (Yamashita et al., 2023). A chit-chat dialogue dataset where each speaker is as-signed a specific persona defined by profile sen-13 tences. Models must generate coherent, engaging responses consistent with the assigned persona. 

TabMWP (Lu et al., 2023). A collection of 38K tabular math word problems combining natural lan-guage questions with structured tables. Solutions require extracting relevant numerical information and performing multi-step quantitative reasoning over the tabular data. 

Hendrycks-MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021b). 

A dataset of 12,500 competition-level mathematics problems across seven domains, including algebra, geometry, and number theory. These problems de-mand formal, multi-step mathematical reasoning beyond elementary arithmetic. 

MCPToolBench++ (Fan et al., 2025). Evaluates tool-use capabilities under the Model Context Pro-tocol (MCP). It tests the model’s ability to correctly invoke tools via structured function calls and ac-curately extract parameters from natural language instructions.

D.1 Latency Profiling Protocol 

To obtain per-model latency estimates for the rout-ing objective, we profile the inference latency of all candidate LLMs using NVIDIA GenAI-Perf, a standardized benchmarking tool built on top of Triton Perf Analyzer. 

Setup. Each model is served independently via vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) inside a Docker con-tainer with NVIDIA GPU support, using ten-sor parallelism across 4 GPUs. The benchmark-ing client runs inside a separate NVIDIA Triton SDK container ( tritonserver:24.06-py3-sdk )on the same host, communicating with the vLLM server over the OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint. All models are evaluated under identical conditions: 1,000 synthetic prompts with a fixed input length of 2,000 tokens and output length of 1,000 tokens, at a concurrency level of 10, with streaming enabled.

I.2 Performance Compression and Policy Collapse 

Under the 3-model pool, fully-observable methods cluster tightly with near-zero Acc@1-L variance (σ2=0 .81 ), compared to σ2=29 .5 in RW-C ORE .Expanding to 6 models does not alleviate this— among non-collapsed methods, variance remains low ( σ2=4 .35 for Acc@1-L in fully-observable), and the gap to the Oracle upper bound (70.65% Acc@1-L) remains substantial. Under partial ob-servability, the compression worsens further, with multiple routers collapsing into degenerate single-model policies (Table 10). The root cause lies in the interaction between model planning capability and episode length. Models with stronger planning capabilities resolve tasks in fewer steps (Figure 15): Qwen3-235B re-quires a median of 18 steps on ALFWorld, while GPT-OSS-20B requires 50. Despite Qwen3-235B having the highest per-step inference latency (15.7s median episode latency), its superior planning yields lower total episode cost than weaker models that compensate with many cheap but inefficient steps (Figure 16). This inverts the conventional latency–accuracy trade-off: the largest model is simultaneously the most accurate and the most cost-efficient, leaving routers with no meaningful trade-off to exploit. Without counterfactual feedback, partially-observable routers greedily exploit this dynamic, collapsing into static “Route-To-Best” policies. In the 3-model setting, collapsed routers default to Qwen3-235B; in the 6-model pool, the pattern per-sists with routers favoring the dominant model. This collapse is rational given the reward structure— the strongest planner minimizes both error and ac-cumulated latency—but it renders routing trivial. Breaking this plateau requires routing algorithms that reason about multi-step execution dynamics rather than treating agentic tasks as single-turn queries. Capturing non-linear planning–latency interactions demands large-scale rollout environ-ments for exploration-guided training. By provid-ing over 76,000 standardized agentic interactions across 6 models, RW-A GENTIC establishes the largest-scale environment to date for studying these routing bottlenecks in realistic agent deployments. 

I.3 Planning Capability and Step Efficiency 

Figure 15 shows the median number of interac-tion steps per model across all three benchmarks. The relationship between model capability and step efficiency is benchmark-dependent: on ALF-World, GPT-OSS-20B requires a median of 50 steps while most other models cluster around 15– 18; on WebShop, Qwen3-235B and Qwen3-Coder-30B are the most step-efficient (median 5 steps); on WorkArena, all models converge to 5–10 steps. These patterns confirm that planning capability— not raw inference speed—is the primary driver of episode cost in agentic settings. Figure 16 shows the resulting median episode latency. Despite having the highest per-token cost (18.8 ms ITL), Qwen3-235B achieves competitive total episode latency because its superior planning reduces the number of required steps. Conversely, GPT-OSS-20B has low per-token cost (4.1 ms ITL) but incurs the second-highest episode latency due to verbose, multi-step execution.
