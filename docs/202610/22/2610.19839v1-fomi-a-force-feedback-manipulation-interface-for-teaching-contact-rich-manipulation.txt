Robot-free collection with handheld interfaces enables scalable acquisition of human demonstra-
tions for policy learning. However, existing handheld interfaces often provide limited intuitive
force feedback, making contact-rich demonstration collection challenging for human operators.
To address this, we present FOMI, a force-feedback manipulation interface with a scissor-shaped
mechanism, which transmits object interaction forces directly to the operator’s hands through
actuation links without force sensors. This intuitive force feedback supports real-time action
adjustment during robot-free collection of contact-rich demonstrations. Around FOMI, we
build a demonstration collection system that integrates sensor calibration, data acquisition, and
synchronization for robot-free collection. By further mapping pose changes and gripper opening
of FOMI to robot end-effector poses and gripper commands, the system also supports teleop-
eration. Across four contact-rich tasks, FOMI improves collection efficiency by approximately
29% and 18% over the best baselines in robot-free collection and teleoperation, respectively.
Demonstrations collected with FOMI in both modes enable policies to perform challenging
contact-rich manipulation tasks with high success rates.

1. Introduction
Human demonstrations provide behavioral supervision for manipulation policy learning. Teleopera-
tion (Wu et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2023) collects demonstrations directly on the target robot but requires
robot hardware, limiting data scale and diversity. Robot-free collection with handheld interfaces such as
the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) (Chi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024) has therefore been widely
explored for scalable demonstration acquisition in diverse real-world environments. However, collecting
contact-rich manipulation demonstrations with these handheld interfaces remains challenging because a
lack of intuitive force feedback can limit human operators’ ability to perceive object interaction forces
and adjust actions in real time (Liu et al., 2025c), resulting in more failed attempts and lower collection
efficiency (Wu et al., 2025).
A key factor is the interface’s mechanical design, which determines how directly interaction forces are
transmitted to the human operator. Common handheld interfaces in robot-free demonstration collection
employ trigger-driven rack-and-pinion mechanisms (Chi et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2026). These mechanisms
transmit object interaction forces through multiple intermediate components, where mechanical backlash
weakens force feedback (Wu et al., 2025). Alternatively, interfaces with active force feedback (Zhang
et al., 2025; Citron et al., 2026) combine force sensing with motorized actuation to convey interaction
forces to operators, requiring additional sensing hardware, actuators, and feedback control. These

2. Related Work
2.1. Demonstration Collection Interfaces
Human demonstrations for policy learning are commonly collected through robot-free collection or
teleoperation. In robot-free collection, UMI (Chi et al., 2024) and its variants (Choi et al., 2026; Liu
et al., 2024) enable demonstration collection across diverse real-world environments. In teleoperation,
leader-follower interfaces such as GELLO (Wu et al., 2024), ALOHA (Zhao et al., 2023), and Mobile
ALOHA (Fu et al., 2024) use joint-space mapping for arm control to collect demonstrations, whereas
VR systems (George et al., 2025) and smartphone systems (Wang et al., 2023; Agarwal et al., 2026)
provide end-effector pose control. Recent interfaces have been developed to support both robot-free
collection and teleoperation (Seo et al., 2025; Surendran et al., 2026).

The interface’s mechanical design can influence force feedback and action adjustment during demon-
stration collection (Georgadarellis et al., 2026). Existing manipulation interfaces provide force feedback
through mechanical transmission or active feedback. UMI (Chi et al., 2024) and UMI-FT (Choi et al.,
2026) use trigger-driven rack-and-pinion mechanisms, while other handheld interfaces use trigger-driven
linkages (Fang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025). These mechanisms transmit interaction
forces to the hand, but multiple transmission stages and backlash may obscure force feedback. In
contrast, finger-driven interfaces (Ohkawa et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2025) transmit interaction forces to
the actuating fingers, but supporting the interface with the actuating fingers may affect force perception
and action adjustment. Alternatively, interfaces with active force feedback provide bilateral gripper
force feedback (Luo et al., 2026) or reflect interaction forces from the follower robot (Liu et al., 2025b;
Zhang et al., 2025; Citron et al., 2026), requiring additional actuators and feedback control. FOMI
integrates each handle with an actuation link to provide direct mechanical force feedback and handle
support during robot-free collection. Table 1 compares the mechanical designs, force feedback, and
support handles of representative interfaces.

3. Methodology
We describe FOMI’s mechanical design, kinematics, and force transmission (Sec. 3.1), present the
collection system for robot-free collection and teleoperation (Sec. 3.2), and outline policy learning and
deployment (Sec. 3.3).
3.1. Mechanical Design
3.1.1. Scissor Mechanism
As shown in Fig. 2(a), FOMI incorporates a scissor-shaped mechanism comprising two mirrored branches
supported by a common base. Each branch includes a handle, a sector gear, and an actuation link
rigidly integrated into a single component. The actuation link and a guide link form a parallelogram
with the gripper carrier and the base. Both links connect to the carrier through bolted revolute joints,
while the parallelogram geometry keeps the gripper contact surfaces parallel to each other. The two
identical sector gears have a module of 1 mm and a pitch radius of 10 mm. Each gear rotates about its
corresponding handle pivot, with the two pivot axes spaced 20 mm apart. The 1:1 transmission ratio
constrains the two branches to rotate through equal angles in opposite directions, producing symmetric
gripper opening and closing.
In robot-free collection, interaction forces at the gripper are transmitted through the actuation links
directly to the handles, providing intuitive force feedback to operators, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The
mechanism also has no return springs, so operators actively drive both opening and closing, and the
forces they perceive arise from object interactions rather than spring preload. The handles also serve as
the grip for holding and supporting the interface during manipulation.

3.2.2. Robot-Free Collection
During robot-free collection, the human operator uses the handheld FOMI interfaces, one in each hand,
to manipulate objects, relying on intuitive force feedback to perceive contact and adjust actions in real
time. For each interface, each demonstration records images from its fisheye camera, the calibrated
end-effector pose W TE,t, and the calibrated gripper command ˆyt.

3.2.4. Data Synchronization
The fisheye camera operates at 100 Hz, the pose tracker at 120 Hz, and the magnetic encoders are
sampled at 200 Hz. The acquisition workstation assigns timestamps to all data streams using a common
clock. Before synchronization, timestamp gaps exceeding 20 ms between consecutive images are flagged
as frame loss. These streams are aligned to an 80 Hz reference timeline by selecting the nearest sample
in time from each stream. We record and visualize timestamp differences between data streams to check
temporal alignment. We exclude episodes with dropped frames flagged by the timestamp criterion or
with tracking failures detected through visual inspection of the synchronized trajectories.

4. Experiments
We first introduce the tasks and robot platforms (Sec. 4.1) and then address two questions: Q1: Does
FOMI improve demonstration collection efficiency and completion rate compared with baseline interfaces
in both collection modes (Sec. 4.2)? Q2: Can demonstrations collected with FOMI train policies that
perform contact-rich manipulation with high success rates (Secs. 4.3 and 4.4)? We additionally evaluate
the gripper-command calibration accuracy in Sec. 4.5.

4.1. Tasks and Robot Platforms
We use four real-world contact-rich tasks to evaluate demonstration collection efficiency, completion
rate, and policy performance:
• Pick Chip (Franka): Pick and place two fragile chips through delicate handling without breaking
them.
• Insert Tube (Franka): Reorient a tube within the gripper, transfer it between the two grippers,
and insert it with precision.
• Wash Dish (Franka): Wash a dish through sustained wiping contact, then place it down.
• Open Pouch (Franka and Flexiv): Open a pouch’s zipper while accommodating the pouch’s
deformability.
These tasks require operators to adjust gripper opening and end-effector motion in response to object
interaction forces during demonstration collection. Both platforms are dual-arm setups that use identical
Robotiq 2F-85 robot grippers and wrist cameras.

We compare FOMI with UMI (Chi et al., 2024) and Ring, our implementation of a finger-driven interface
similar to FreeTacMan (Wu et al., 2025) (Fig. 4, top). As shown in Table 2, FOMI achieves the highest

support: UMI’s trigger-driven rack-and-pinion mechanism transmits interaction forces through multiple
intermediate stages, whereas Ring requires the actuating fingers to also support the interface.

each smaller dataset randomly sampled from the next larger dataset. As shown in Fig. 7, increasing
the number of demonstrations raises the average success rate from 42.0% (25) to 59.1% (50) and
78.4% (100), and the average progress rate from 50.8% to 67.1% and 85.8%. At the average collection
efficiency, collecting 100 demonstrations requires approximately one hour of active collection. With 100
demonstrations per task, policies achieve high success rates on the evaluated contact-rich tasks, and the
consistent gains suggest further improvement with more robot-free data.

5. Conclusion
We presented FOMI, a scissor-shaped manipulation interface, and a demonstration collection system
built around it. The interface provides intuitive mechanical force feedback during robot-free collection,
while mechanical stops indicate gripper width command limits in teleoperation. FOMI achieves higher
collection efficiency and completion rates than the evaluated baselines under the tested conditions.
Demonstrations collected in both modes support policy learning for the evaluated contact-rich tasks
without tactile or force inputs. However, interaction forces are not recorded for policy learning, and the
limited sample size of the user study limits generalization across operators. Future work will expand the
user study and refine the mechanical design. We will also explore additional sensory inputs for policy
learning from demonstrations collected with intuitive force feedback.

D. Policy Training and Inference Details
D.1. Training Details
We initialize each policy from the π0.5 base checkpoint (Intelligence et al., 2025) and fine-tune it for
10,000 steps with a batch size of 64. We use AdamW with a peak learning rate of 1.5×10−5, a 1,000-step
warmup, and cosine decay. Each 720 × 540 image is center-cropped to 300 × 300 pixels and resized to
224 × 224 pixels for policy input. We use the final checkpoint at step 10,000 for evaluation, with 22
rollouts per task under each experimental condition.
D.2. Real-Time Chunking
At deployment, we use real-time chunking (RTC) (Black et al., 2026) and generate each action chunk
using 10 flow-matching integration steps. We use an exponential weighting schedule for prefix guidance.
Actions are executed at 20 Hz, and a new inference call is triggered after every 20 executed actions.
During inference, a committed prefix of 3 actions remains fixed and continues to execute, accommodating
an inference latency of approximately 120 ms.
The client stores each predicted trajectory as absolute TCP poses for both arms in the left robot base
frame BL, together with a copy expressed in each arm’s own base frame for execution. After s = 20
actions have been executed, the remaining H −s = 30 actions form the overlap for the next inference
call. Retaining absolute poses preserves the spatial targets when the reference frame changes.

pose. The server then uses the first left-arm target in the overlap as the model reference. Let T BL
