Recent advances in multimodal models have enabled GPT-6 Astra to perform robotic manipulation
through in-context learning.
Yet contact-rich control and tactile perception are often regarded as
strengths of specialist policies, leaving open whether Astra can directly understand and use tactile
observations. We provide, to our knowledge, the ﬁrst demonstration that Astra directly interprets vision-
based tactile images and uses their contact cues for manipulation. Tactile RGB images enter through
its existing visual interface, enabling few-shot closed-loop control on nineteen simulated UniVTAC and
OpenViTac tasks with 210 sampled scene seeds per setting. With visuo-tactile input, Astra achieves
mean success rates of 93.8% and 81.8%, exceeding the strongest reported specialist baselines in our
comparison by 22.9 and 13.1 percentage points, respectively. Vision-only performance also leads these
baselines, reaching 76.3% and 80.6%. Trajectory analysis reveals active probing, cautious grasping,
contact-guided alignment, and recovery, alongside diﬃculties with tangential loading and corrective
execution. Our ﬁndings challenge the exclusivity of specialist policies in contact-rich manipulation and
show that ﬁne-grained vision can partially compensate for missing touch. They also identify vision-
based tactile sensing as an interface for scaling contact understanding with foundation models and
reveal signs of benchmark saturation. These results motivate joint visual–tactile pretraining, harder
contact-rich evaluations, and physical-robot validation.

1 Introduction
Recent studies show that GPT-6 Astra can translate visual observations and instructions into robot ac-
tions, challenging the assumption that eﬀective manipulation requires a separately trained policy. GPT-as-
Policy (Su et al., 2026), RoboProbe (Zhang et al., 2026), RoboDawn (Guo et al., 2026), and RoboICL (Liu
et al., 2026) explore this capability through diﬀerent control interfaces and forms of contextual feedback.
GPT-Policy and broader embodied evaluations further examine how demonstration following and task de-
cisions translate into physical execution (Cheng et al., 2026; Galbot Team et al., 2026). These advances
shift the question from whether general models can control robots to which capabilities still distinguish
specialist policies.
Contact-rich manipulation is a prominent candidate. Precise insertion, stable grasping, and material
discrimination depend on local geometry, slip, and physical properties that scene images may not reveal.
Motions that work in free space can fail after contact, while a stronger grasp can damage a fragile object.
These demands motivate tactile pretraining and learned visual–tactile integration, including FTP-1 (Yuan
et al., 2026), OpenVTLA (Wu et al., 2026), and SimpleTouch (Yang et al., 2026a). Can Astra directly use
contact evidence for these decisions, or do such interactions require specialist training?
Vision-based tactile sensing makes this question especially relevant. Sensors such as GelSight encode con-
tact geometry and deformation as images (Yuan et al., 2017), exposing touch through the same interface
that supports general visual understanding. Few-shot demonstrations relate local deformation to preced-
ing actions, grasp conditions, and resulting object motion. We explore whether Astra can interpret these
relationships and use tactile evidence to guide a new interaction, extending visual competence to contact
understanding.
We investigate Astra’s direct tactile-image understanding on UniVTAC (Chen et al., 2026a) and OpenVi-
Tac (Wu et al., 2026). Tactile RGB images enter through the model’s existing visual interface, allowing us
to compare vision-only and visuo-tactile control on the same sampled scenes. We connect task outcomes
to recorded contact inference, corrective actions, and recovery. Our contributions are threefold:
1. Direct tactile-image understanding and control. To our knowledge, we provide the ﬁrst demon-
stration of Astra directly interpreting vision-based tactile images and using their contact cues for ma-
nipulation. We investigate this capability on nineteen tasks and 210 sampled scene seeds per input
setting.
2. Leading contact-rich manipulation results. Astra reaches 93.8% and 81.8% mean success, exceed-
ing the strongest reported specialist means in our comparison by 22.9 and 13.1 percentage points
(Figure 1). It outperforms FTP-1 and SimpleTouch on UniVTAC and OpenVTLA on OpenViTac; vision-
only performance also leads these baselines at 76.3% and 80.6%.
3. An analysis of contact understanding and visual compensation. We connect task-level outcomes to
contact-guided probing, ﬁne adjustment, and recovery, and identify subtle visual cues that compensate
for missing touch. This analysis reveals both Astra’s tactile competence and its remaining perceptual
and control limitations.
Together, these results challenge the presumed moat of specialist contact-rich policies and connect tactile
sensing to the development of more capable general models.
2 Related Work
Multimodal Models as Robot Policies. Code as Policies and VoxPoser use general models to generate
robot programs and spatial objectives (Liang et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023). Recent Astra-based work
brings the model closer to direct control: GPT-as-Policy compares direct and hybrid policies (Su et al.,
2026); RoboProbe and systematic evaluations examine manipulation and execution limits (Zhang et al.,
2026; Galbot Team et al., 2026); and RoboDawn and GPT-Policy ground control in demonstrations (Guo
et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026). RoboICL uniﬁes demonstrations and online experience through an
observation–action–receipt–observation grammar, preserving distributed interactions alongside recent
feedback (Liu et al., 2026). Tactile feedback has also been studied in Astra-based peg insertion (Tanaka,
2


2026). This line complements learned action-chunking, diﬀusion, and pretrained VLA policies (Zhao
et al., 2023; Chi et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025). Our study centers on
Astra’s direct interpretation of tactile RGB images and their role in contact-rich control.
End-to-End Policies with Tactile Feedback. VITaL, 3D-ViTac, and ViTacFormer learn visual–tactile rep-
resentations for manipulation (George et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024; Heng et al., 2025). Tactile-VLA,
VLA-Touch, and AT-VLA incorporate tactile feedback into pretrained VLAs (Huang et al., 2025; Bi et al.,
2025; Li et al., 2026). FTP-1 pretrains tactile policies across sensors (Yuan et al., 2026); OpenVTLA
studies tactile representations and integration within OpenViTac (Wu et al., 2026); and SimpleTouch
adds a tactile expert with predictive contact features, without additional tactile policy pretraining (Yang
et al., 2026a). TacDyn-WAM models implicit tactile dynamics by predicting future representations within
a heterogeneous visuo-tactile world action model (Wang et al., 2026). Sparsh, AnyTouch, and T3 provide
reusable sensory representations across sensing conﬁgurations and tasks (Higuera et al., 2024; Feng et al.,
2025; Zhao et al., 2025). We examine how much tactile competence a general model can access directly
through its visual interface, using demonstrations that relate contact images to actions and outcomes.
Contact-Rich Manipulation Benchmarks. LIBERO, ManiSkill3, and RoboDojo evaluate manipulation
learning, transfer, and generalist control (Liu et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b); Bench2Dex
extends evaluation to visuo-tactile bimanual manipulation across dexterous hands (Yang et al., 2026b).
UniVTAC uniﬁes tactile data generation, learning, and benchmarking (Chen et al., 2026a), supporting
established comparisons across grasping, classiﬁcation, placement, and insertion (Yuan et al., 2026; Yang
et al., 2026a). The more recent OpenViTac provides a complementary sim-and-real framework for prop-
erty discrimination and precise contact manipulation (Wu et al., 2026). Our evaluation spans both suites
and examines whether texture, contour, and visible slip can support nominally tactile judgments, help-
ing distinguish ﬁne-grained visual competence from understanding of genuinely unobservable physical
properties.
3 Method: Direct Tactile-Image Control
We present tactile images to Astra as direct visual evidence for contact-rich manipulation. Together with
scene images and robot state, they expose local deformation and contact geometry through the model’s
existing visual interface. Demonstrations connect these observations to actions and outcomes, and online
feedback supports contact-guided corrections. Figure 2 shows this interface with an actual Insert Hole
observation. Demonstration-conditioned execution and interaction memory follow RoboICL (Liu et al.,
2026).
3.1 Scene and Contact Observations
At decision round 𝑡, the model receives task instruction 𝑔, robot state 𝑥𝑡, scene images 𝐼𝑡, and, in the
visuo-tactile setting, the two ﬁnger-contact images 𝑇𝑡. Scene images describe global object pose and tar-
get geometry; tactile images describe local deformation and contact. The input preserves the benchmark’s
conﬁgured cameras, including a wrist view where available. Robot state provides the current end-eﬀector
pose and proprioceptive information needed to relate an observed contact to a proposed motion. Demon-
stration and online observations use the same image interface, allowing Astra to compare contacts in
relation to their associated actions and outcomes. Vision-only control omits tactile images from both
sources.
3.2 Demonstrations and Interaction Memory
The demonstration context D consists of selected blocks from successful task trajectories. A block con-
tains its initial observation and robot state, the recorded action sequence, an execution receipt, and the
resulting observation and state. Blocks are selected before evaluation and remain available throughout
an episode. They expose local state transitions across the trajectory; tasks with distinct classes or shapes
include demonstrations that cover those branches. Our preparation uses temporal targets constrained by
action validity and non-overlap, as detailed in Appendix A.

4 Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and Sampling. We evaluate eight UniVTAC tasks and eleven OpenViTac tasks (Chen et al.,
2026a; Wu et al., 2026). UniVTAC supplies an established tactile-policy comparison across grasping,
placement, classiﬁcation, and precise insertion. The more recent OpenViTac adds property discrimina-
tion and contact-sensitive assembly within a paired sim-and-real framework. Across seven policies on
eight shared tasks, its authors report strong agreement between simulated and real-world performance
(Pearson 𝑟= 0.967), supporting its use for comparative evaluation (Wu et al., 2026). We use a 10% strat-
iﬁed sample, with sampling seed 42, preserving class and layout proportions. Each input setting contains
80 UniVTAC and 130 OpenViTac scene seeds: ten per task, except Insert Block v2, which contains ten per
shape across three shapes.

Model and Demonstrations. The model is GPT-6 Astra with xhigh reasoning eﬀort. We use UniVTAC’s
released training recordings (Chen et al., 2026a) and collect successful OpenViTac demonstrations with
its oﬃcial scripted experts in training mode (Wu et al., 2026). Demonstrations provide context, while
separate evaluation episodes determine success rates. Twelve tasks use one demonstration with twelve
selected segments. Six classiﬁcation or selection tasks use two demonstrations with ﬁve segments each;
Insert Block v2 uses three demonstrations, one per shape, also with ﬁve segments each. Segment length 𝐻
is chosen with reference to trajectory length and ﬁxed per task. Each segment pairs its 𝐻actions with the
initial and resulting observations. Appendix A gives every task’s conﬁguration and the selection procedure.
All selected demonstration blocks remain available at every decision throughout the episode.
Input Conditions and Camera Access. Vision-only (V) and visuo-tactile (V+T) settings use the same
demonstration trajectories, segment starts, actions, and sampled evaluation scenes. V excludes tactile
images from both demonstrations and online observations; V+T additionally includes the left and right
tactile RGB images. Camera selection strictly follows each benchmark’s oﬃcial task conﬁguration. Uni-
VTAC uses only the ﬁxed camera in six tasks and also provides the wrist camera for Lift Can and Insert
Tube. OpenViTac provides both ﬁxed and wrist cameras for every task. All settings also receive task text
and robot proprioception, and generate the seven-dimensional commands in Equation 1.
Success Criteria. All tasks retain their oﬃcial success criteria except Grasp Chip. Its original conditions
impose unnecessarily strict execution details beyond intact object transfer. We simplify these conditions:
an intact chip must be grasped, transferred, and placed with stable physical support in the target re-
gion. The adjusted criterion accepts supported placement without requiring a particular release-and-
withdrawal sequence. The task still requires maintaining the chip’s integrity during manipulation.
Baselines and Metrics. The tables compare Astra with published benchmark results, including action-
chunking, VLA, world-action, and tactile-policy baselines (Zhao et al., 2023; George et al., 2025; Physical
Intelligence et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026). Astra is evaluated on the
sampled scenes; baseline values are the corresponding reported benchmark results. We report per-task
success percentages and equal-weight means across the eight or eleven tasks. The thirty Insert Block
v2 scenes are aggregated into a single task score with the same weight as every other task. The task
breakdown separately examines property judgments, constrained insertion, stable grasping, and transfer.
5 Results: Five Findings
5.1 Leading Performance on Contact-Rich Tasks
With visual and tactile inputs, Astra reaches 93.8% mean success on UniVTAC and 81.8% on OpenViTac
(Tables 1 and 2). The strongest reported baseline means in these tables are 70.9% for SimpleTouch (Yang
et al., 2026a) and 68.7% for OpenVTLA (Wu et al., 2026), yielding gaps of 22.9 and 13.1 percentage
points. Vision-only Astra also exceeds these means, reaching 76.3% and 80.6%, respectively. These
results establish general multimodal models as strong contact-rich manipulation policies, surpassing the
reported specialist baselines in both benchmark comparisons.
Astra also exceeds FTP-1 (Yuan et al., 2026) by 30.9 percentage points on UniVTAC and 20.6 points
on OpenViTac. The improvements extend to precise insertion and fragile transfer, complementing the
semantic and open-ended strengths identiﬁed by earlier Astra evaluations (Zhang et al., 2026; Liu et al.,
2026). Weight Classify remains diﬃcult, while several classiﬁcation, transfer, and insertion tasks reach
100% in the sampled episodes.
The task breakdown reveals that the gains span more than one operation family. On UniVTAC, the largest
changes occur in constrained extraction and hole insertion, while the already strong Lift Bottle and Grasp
Classify results remain unchanged. On OpenViTac, improvements in Hardness Classify and Insert Block
v1 coexist with high vision-only scores on texture discrimination and several transfer tasks. This pat-
tern motivates examining what evidence drives a particular decision, rather than interpreting touch as a
uniform addition to every task.

5.2 Direct Tactile Understanding and Control
Adding tactile RGB input improves four UniVTAC tasks: Pull-out Key increases from 30% to 90%, Lift Can
from 80% to 100%, Insert Hole from 60% to 100%, and Insert HDMI from 50% to 80%. On OpenViTac,
Hardness Classify improves from 70% to 90%, and Insert Block v1 from 40% to 70%. The corresponding
successful trajectories illustrate resistance detection, grasp stabilization, contact-guided alignment, and
material-property judgment.
Vision-based tactile sensing encodes local contact in images (Yuan et al., 2017) that Astra can process
through its existing visual interface. In successful traces, changes in contact shape, symmetry, shear, and
stability are associated with targeted adjustments: unloading a constrained object, changing the approach
direction, testing retention, or limiting closure after contact. These behaviors demonstrate useful tactile
interpretation through the same image interface used for ordinary scene perception. The successful in-
terventions depend on the contact regime. Constrained insertion calls for realignment before renewed
advancement; fragile grasping calls for stopping closure once support is established; material classiﬁcation
uses controlled changes in contact to obtain a more informative comparison with demonstrations.

presents recorded observations and concise action summaries. Step labels are zero-based control-action
indices, not model-request counts; selected intervals are nonuniform. Camera crops improve legibility,
while tactile images retain their original content and aspect ratio. The analysis combines recorded action

5.3 Visual Compensation for Missing Touch
The gain associated with tactile input diﬀers substantially between benchmarks: 17.5 percentage points
on UniVTAC and 1.2 points on OpenViTac. Their camera conﬁgurations provide one possible explanation.
UniVTAC restricts some visual cues in its tactile evaluation setting: six of its eight tasks use only the ﬁxed
camera, while Lift Can and Insert Tube also use a wrist camera. OpenViTac provides both ﬁxed and wrist
cameras across its tasks, yielding richer visual observations. These diﬀerences may reduce the marginal
value of touch for a model that can identify subtle visual structure.
In all three tasks in Figure 12, Astra identiﬁes visible texture or ridges in its action notes and uses these
cues to complete the required classiﬁcation or selection without tactile images. This level of pure-vision
discrimination goes beyond what the reported specialist results demonstrate. Its sensitivity exposes subtle
details that human observers can easily overlook, showing why visually inconspicuous cues still matter
when designing tasks intended to require touch.

usefulness depends on the correspondence between visible appearance and the task categories, as well
as the model’s ability to notice it.
The visual evidence is not limited to static texture. In three inspected Weight Classify pairs where V
succeeds and V+T fails, the vision-only policy relaxes the grasp and uses visible slip to reach the correct
category. The tactile runs instead associate weak shear or a contact pattern resembling the light-object
demonstration with low weight and misclassify the heavy object. Empty-Can Selection shows a related
ambiguity: subtle contact changes can be misread, while repeated comparisons leave insuﬃcient actions
for transfer. These observations connect the visual-compensation result to concrete failures in evidence
interpretation and probe selection, examined in Section 6.
5.4 Scaling Potential of Vision-Based Touch
The preceding results identify an important property of vision-based tactile sensing: it expresses contact
through an interface that a general multimodal model already processes. GelSight-style sensors convert
local deformation into images (Yuan et al., 2017); the model can relate these images to object appearance,
robot state, preceding motion, and demonstrated outcomes. In our experiments, that relationship sup-
ports decisions about constrained motion, retention, and alignment without training a new tactile policy
for each task.
12


This observation complements work on reusable tactile representations. VITaL, Sparsh, AnyTouch, and
T3 learn representations that transfer across tactile settings (George et al., 2025; Higuera et al., 2024;
Feng et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025); FTP-1 and SimpleTouch connect contact-sensitive representations to
action generation (Yuan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a). Astra exposes a further route: a single general
visual interface can interpret both scene images and contact images through demonstration-conditioned
inference. The successful behaviors in Section 5.2 illustrate how temporal context connects contact ob-
servations to corrective actions. A contact image becomes actionable when the model connects it to what
the ﬁngers did and what happened to the object.
Joint visual–tactile pretraining is a natural next step. Aligned scene images, tactile images, actions, and
outcomes could make this correspondence part of general multimodal learning rather than a relationship
supplied only at deployment. The resulting research question concerns how contact understanding im-
proves with data diversity, model capacity, and interaction experience. Our results motivate this direction
through the capability already accessible from tactile RGB inputs. For sensor development, they favor rep-
resentations that preserve interpretable contact structure and support comparison across sensors, grasps,
and motion conditions.
5.5 Emerging Benchmark Saturation
With tactile input, Astra reaches at least 90% success on thirteen of nineteen tasks, including perfect
sampled success on nine. UniVTAC’s mean is 93.8%, while several OpenViTac classiﬁcation and transfer
tasks also reach 100%. These ceilings limit how well the corresponding tasks distinguish stronger policies.
They also shift attention from average completion toward the quality of contact decisions, recovery, and
generalization.
Visual compensation adds a separate benchmark-design requirement. A tactile task should isolate infor-
mation that the chosen visual interface does not already reveal. In the current suites, texture, contour,
or motion can correlate with the required physical judgment. Astra’s results demonstrate that such cues
become useful to a suﬃciently capable visual model, even when they are not salient to a human observer.
UniVTAC’s restricted camera access and OpenViTac’s richer sim-and-real observation setting produce com-
plementary tests of this issue (Chen et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026). Both contact ﬁdelity and control over
alternative visual evidence matter when interpreting a tactile-policy result.
More discriminative evaluations should include visually indistinguishable objects with diﬀerent mass, fric-
tion, stiﬀness, or internal constraints; tighter insertion tolerances; longer contact sequences; and recovery
after disturbed grasps or failed engagement. Varying appearance independently of physical properties
would test whether a policy infers contact rather than memorizes a visual correlate. Evaluating the same
judgments across sensors and real robots would further separate robust tactile understanding from a par-
ticular rendering or contact conﬁguration. The broader benchmark goal is reliable physical inference and
correction, not simply a larger set of scenes with the same available cues.
6 Remaining Limitations of Astra
Tactile input does not consistently improve performance: Weight Classify, Empty-Can Selection, Insert
Tube, and Insert Block v2 decrease in the sampled results. Three recurring limitations emerge from the
trajectories: limited sensitivity to tangential motion and force, diﬃculty with ﬁne adjustments, and active
probing that fails to converge. Weak tactile cues can be misread, a plausible correction can fail to change
the object conﬁguration as intended, and repeated observations can leave too little time to ﬁnish the
manipulation.

6.3 Active Probing May Fail to Converge
Active probing must reduce uncertainty while preserving enough actions to complete the task. Repeated
observations are useful only when they lead to a timely decision about how to proceed.
The Weight Classify comparison in Section 5.3 illustrates how an added modality changes the evidence
prioritized by the policy. Comparing contacts under consistent probing conditions is essential for distin-
guishing a material or load diﬀerence from a change in support or grasp geometry.
Empty-Can Selection reveals a related eﬃciency problem. Repeated measurements can yield a plausible
eventual judgment while leaving insuﬃcient opportunity to ﬁnish the task. The issue is not only percep-
tual accuracy, but also whether a probe resolves uncertainty at a reasonable cost in remaining actions.
In the Empty-Can Selection failure (Figure 16), the eventual category judgment and the completed ma-
nipulation are separate outcomes. A probe is useful only if the information it produces can still guide
the remaining grasp and transfer. Repeatedly changing the contact conditions also makes successive ob-
servations harder to compare directly. The correct selection is recorded only at action 370, leaving 30 of
400 actions for the remaining grasp, transport, and placement. This trajectory exposes a stage-transition
problem: gathering more evidence must be balanced against the manipulation still required after the clas-
siﬁcation decision. Consistent probing conditions and a stopping rule should preserve enough opportunity
to ﬁnish those later stages.
These failures expose a gap between locally plausible actions and reliable task completion. Astra must
relate contact cues to the underlying physical constraint, turn that diagnosis into eﬀective corrective
motion, and stop probing in time to complete the remaining manipulation.

8 Limitations of This Study
All experiments are conducted in simulation; robustness on physical robots remains to be validated under
sensor noise and drift, calibration error, actual contact dynamics, and execution delays. Cost constraints
limit most tasks to ten episodes per input setting instead of the full 100-episode evaluation, so a single
outcome changes the task success rate by ten percentage points. Larger samples, multiple sampling seeds,
and repeated executions within the same scenes are needed to quantify variation from scene selection,
model decisions, and execution. The experiments also cover only two benchmarks and use task-speciﬁc
successful demonstrations, with corresponding categories or shapes represented where applicable. Gen-
eralization to unfamiliar objects, new tasks, diﬀerent tactile sensors and robot conﬁgurations, and longer
multistage manipulation requires systematic evaluation.
9 Conclusion
GPT-6 Astra directly interprets vision-based tactile images and uses their contact cues for manipulation,
achieving 93.8% on UniVTAC and 81.8% on OpenViTac and exceeding the reported specialist baselines in
our comparison. Tactile images support contact-state inference, cautious grasp formation, alignment, and
recovery; ﬁne-grained vision also reveals cues that partially compensate for missing touch. These ﬁndings
challenge the view that tactile manipulation is intrinsically reserved for specialist policies. The next step is
to develop the capability and its evaluation together: joint visual–tactile pretraining can connect contact
understanding to foundation-model scaling, while harder tasks with visually hidden physical properties,
tighter tolerances, and repeated evaluation on real robots can establish reliable physical competence.
The remaining errors in tangential-load interpretation, contact diagnosis, probe selection, and corrective
control provide concrete targets for improving policy reliability and the execution harness.

Temporal Selection of Demonstration Blocks. For a source trajectory with 𝑁observations, each selected
start 𝑠deﬁnes 𝐻consecutive actions and the two observations at 𝑠and 𝑠+ 𝐻. Our preparation calls the
released RoboICL deterministic selector (Liu et al., 2026): temporal targets distribute non-overlapping
blocks across the trajectory, including its initial and terminal windows, and invalid action windows are
excluded. The same selector is used with twelve blocks for one-shot context and ﬁve blocks per trajectory
for multi-shot context. It selects blocks oﬄine; online actions do not change their starts. For example, the
two Weight Classify demonstrations use starts {0, 33, 66, 99, 132} and {0, 35, 69, 104, 139} with 𝐻= 5.
The corresponding images are the block endpoints, rather than every intermediate action frame.
Execution and Observation Timing. Demonstrations and generated commands use a 60 Hz control ca-
dence, with two 120 Hz simulator substeps per command. Demonstration motion increments and gripper
targets are reconstructed from recorded states under the benchmark’s position-control interface. Both
input settings use this execution protocol.

Insert HDMI. The vision-only run ends during another attempt to regrasp the displaced connector. With
touch, shifting contact without plug motion is interpreted as rim contact, prompting unloading and fore-
aft corrections. Advancement resumes once the plug descends with stable tactile contact. This sequence
connects contact interpretation to correction of the object-to-socket alignment.
Hardness Classify. Vision-only control labels the hard block as compliant, places it on the blue plate,
and continues observing the stable placement. The visuo-tactile run probes the grasp, associates a sharply
bounded indentation with the hard category, and moves to the yellow plate. The pair distinguishes secure
object transport from the material judgment that determines the required destination.
Insert Block v1. Both runs reach the square opening. In the vision-only trace, the cube remains on the
rim after release, leading to repeated regrasping and centering attempts. The visuo-tactile run maintains
retention and checks alignment before a shallow descent and gradual release complete insertion. The
outcome depends on maintaining alignment from transport through release.
