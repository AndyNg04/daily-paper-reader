Abstract 

Omni-modal assistants must remember not only what users say, but also what they show and convey. Existing long-term memory bench-marks are mostly text-and-image or report a single end-to-end score, so they cannot locate whether evidence is lost when it is represented ,

retrieved , delivered , or read . We introduce 

MOSAIC , a benchmark of 923 questions over persona-grounded histories of 11–24 sessions spanning text, image, video, and audio. MO-SAIC constructs targeted spatial, temporal, and prosodic signals rather than decorative media— with the strongest causal validation for spa-tial information and selector-level validation for annotated prosody—and pairs them with a matched diagnostic protocol that freezes the question, reader, prompt, and budget while in-tervening on representation, retrieval, and deliv-ery. Matched decomposition shows that repre-sentation loss is modality-dependent, residual reading dominates even under gold evidence, and abstention remains weak on these controls: strong answerable scores coexist with failures to refuse. MOSAIC thus turns omni-modal memory evaluation from a leaderboard into a diagnosis of WRITE , RETRIEVE , DELIVER , and  

> READ

failures. 

1 Introduction 

Omni-modal assistants such as GPT-4o, Gemini, Qwen-Omni, and MiMo (OpenAI et al., 2024; Team et al., 2025; Qwen Team, 2026; Xiaomi LLM-Core Team, 2026) are moving from single-turn de-mos to persistent, multi-session deployments. A user may share a product photo in January, leave a dietary voice note in March, and later expect both to inform a recommendation. Answering requires not only retrieving temporally scattered evidence, but chaining it across modalities (Fig-ure 1). Prior conversational memory benchmarks omit video/audio, treat images as optional, or stop at text–image co-reference; none combine four-

3 Preliminaries 

Every MOSAIC item is a persona-grounded, multi-modal query embedded in a chronological haystack of user–assistant sessions. Three orthogonal axes define it, each worked through end-to-end in Ap-pendix A: the question type fixes the reasoning skeleton (§3.1), the modality gap defines what each non-text modality must contribute (§3.2), and the reasoning chain composes N ARROWER , S E-

> LECTOR

, and A NSWER facts into a deterministic retrieval path (§3.3). 

3.1 Question Types 

MOSAIC covers six types: single-session informa-tion extraction; cross-session selection and bridg-3ing; knowledge updates; temporal-plan disruptions; and refusal after evidence removal. Questions re-quire one to three facts; Appendix A defines each type, and Appendix D reports per-type counts. 

3.2 Modality Gaps 

A modality gap is information that survives only be-cause its native modality preserves it, while a text-only or caption-based agent would lose it. Required non-text facts are assigned a gap strategy (Ap-pendix A): image (background detail, fine-grained attribute, spatial configuration), video (action or-dering, state transition, repetition count), or audio 

(happy/unhappy prosody with neutral wording over 

≥ 2 candidates); Appendix D breaks down tag coverage. Audio prosody is the most frequent an-notated strategy (450 facts / 399 of 784 answerable items), so Caption-Text / ASR write paths lose the tone discriminator by design—a pipeline exposure, not a gold-delivery perception cost (§6.2). 

Challenge by construction. A media attachment is insufficient to establish multimodal reasoning, since a caption or transcript may preserve the en-tire answer. MOSAIC therefore assigns a challenge 

before dialogue and media realization, specifying the native relation that carries a fact together with constraints that keep that relation out of the sur-rounding text; every answerable item contains at least one required challenge-bearing fact (Table 2). Action Repetition Count is representative: a fixed-camera clip repeats one action with no cuts and no on-screen counter, and the dialogue says only “a few times,” so a generic caption preserves the event 

but loses the selector needed to continue the chain (per-challenge specifications: Appendix A). 

3.3 Reasoning Chains and Fact Roles 

Every answerable item has a reasoning chain of re-quired facts with roles N ARROWER (NAR; reduces candidates), S ELECTOR (SEL; identifies an entity), and A NSWER (ANS; carries the final value), in the pattern NAR ∗ SEL ∗ ANS . Valid chains are bridge-entity-linked and deletion-tested (§4): removing any required fact makes the question unanswer-able, and audio/video discriminators choose among 

≥ 2 same-type entities. A chain is cross-modal 

when required facts span at least two modalities; gap-bearing detail usually sits in a N ARROWER or SELECTOR , and the A NSWER ’s final value is al-ways a short text-expressible string (enabling judge scoring), though the fact carrying it may itself be multimodal (full constraints and templates: Ap-pendix A). 

4 The MOSAIC Generation Pipeline 

MOSAIC is produced by a fully automated, config-driven four-stage pipeline (Figure 7; details in Ap-pendix B). Each item is grounded in a Nemotron-Personas persona (Meyer and Corneil, 2025). An LLM designs a caption-distinct primary entity, then builds the chain question-first under the bridge-entity and entity-pair constraints (§3.3). A validate-and-repair loop combines programmatic checks with a per-fact deletion test against twelve fail-ure modes; unrepaired chains are discarded, and ANSWER REFUSAL items strip required facts af-ter auditing. Surviving facts become chronologi-cal evidence and filler sessions under leak guards. Stage 4 synthesises media (FLUX-2-pro / Kling– Seedance / Qwen3-TTS). Filtering then yields the exact lineage 1,050 → 1,004 → 879 (automated) before a three-annotator audit of 100 items (94.3% well-posed) rescues 44 probe false negatives, giv-ing MOSAIC-Eval : 923 items (784 answerable + 139 refusal). Reweighting that audit with a 100-item probe-YES follow-up estimates ≈97.8% gold-correct and ≈96.6% evidence-sufficient on the 784 answerable releases (Appendix I); the 784/864 (90.7%) figure remains a filtering retention rate ,not a human answerability ceiling (Appendix E). The release spans 70 personas (11–24 sessions, mean 17.5), 10,765 multimodal artefacts (Table 10), and ∼18.8M tokens ( ∼269K/persona). Among an-swerable items, 85.2% need ≥2 facts and 75.0% need ≥2 modalities; required evidence appears in image, audio, video, and text for 99.5%, 54.1%, 23.5%, and 35.3% of items (overlapping), so we report per-challenge strata rather than claiming uni-form modality balance; Appendix D collects the remaining haystack statistics.

5.1 Matched Stage Decomposition 

We run seven matched conditions on the same 784 answerable QIDs (Table 3), changing only repre-sentation, retrieval, or delivery under one reader, prompt, packer, and judge. These are interventions 

rather than an assumed ranking: native evidence may remain perceptually difficult, and retrieved context may include useful evidence outside the minimal annotated chain. The main paired contrasts are symbolic ver-sus native (perception cost), structured versus generic text (avoidable write-time compression), gold native versus retrieved-all-delivered (retrieval loss), and all-delivered versus production-capped requests (delivery loss). Delivery is additionally traced as a budget curve over tighter packer al-lowances (top-10 units; top-5 with one media item per modality). Residual errors under gold symbolic facts bound answer integration and grading error. 

Frozen configuration. Matched conditions use Gemini 3.5 as the fixed omni-modal reader (temper-ature 0; extended thinking disabled), held out from dataset construction: MiMo-V2.5 is the answerabil-ity probe, so using it as the primary reader would couple selection with evaluation. The trade-off is disclosed—gold-native failures mix perception error with bounded item noise (§6.1). Retrieval replays a frozen top-50 ranked manifest; the pro-duction budget delivers the top 20 units with at most three items per modality under a 100 MB media cap. Embedders, captioner/ASR, struc-tured writer (query-blind MiMo-V2.5 schemas), and judge (Qwen3.7-Plus) are listed in Appendix C. MiMo-V2.5 as second reader on the 120-item pi-lot reproduces the gold and budgeted stage ladder (Appendix K.2); uncapped all-delivered remains Gemini-only (MiMo timeouts under large multi-modal payloads). 

5.2 Within-Item Modality Interventions 

Within an item we change one required carrier while holding its question, evidence order, and ev-ery other hop fixed. Image variants are raw media, OCR-only text, object-only text, and a spatial de-scription; video variants are raw video, ordered sparse frames, shuffled frames, and an ordered event/count description; audio variants are raw au-dio, ASR, ASR plus a gold prosody tag from the construction annotation (an upper bound on any prosody tagger), and ASR plus the swapped gold tag ( happy ↔unhappy ). Image and video text vari-ants use the same query- and answer-blind MiMo writer as the structured-write condition. Because every retained item was verified answer-able from native evidence alone, a text-over-raw gap indicates a perception failure rather than a miss-ing signal. Each contrast is restricted to the match-ing challenge stratum, and a per-condition leak au-dit verifies that non-intervened hops are unchanged. 

5.3 Two Architecture Tracks 

We separate two questions that end-to-end leader-boards conflate. In Track A (fixed reader), flat RAG over caption-text, omni-text, and native in-dexes, plus A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025) and Memo-ryOS (Kang et al., 2025), export retrieved context to one reader, prompt, packer, budget, and judge— comparing the useful evidence each backend pro-duces . In Track B (native end-to-end), Naive RAG, repaired ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), A-MEM, and MemoryOS use their intended controllers and read-ers, measuring deployable capability descriptively because multiple components change at once. 5.4 Metrics and Uncertainty 

The primary answer metric is a 0–1 semantic judge score from Qwen3.7-Plus (rubric in Ap-pendix C) (Li et al., 2024), reported separately for the 784 answerable items, with correct-refusal and false-refusal rates paired so that an always-abstain system cannot appear calibrated. Retrieval and delivery use required-fact and complete-chain Recall@ k over both ranked units and units actually serialized into the request. Controlled comparisons are paired by QID with item-bootstrap 95% inter-vals and wins/ties/losses; a persona-clustered boot-strap is a dependence check. Reported intervals are per-contrast and unadjusted across the many paired tests and strata, so exploratory challenge-level nulls should be read cautiously. GLM-5.2 and Grok-4.5 evaluate each native end-to-end run (Appendix J); Appendix C documents manifests, media caps, and scoring; Appendix M reproduces the prompts. 

6 Results and Analysis 

All numbers come from one canonical paired run, and every stage claim uses only paired conditions with identical QIDs, readers, prompts, packers, and judges. A retrieved-but-dropped artefact counts as a delivery failure rather than a reading failure, and a write loss is claimed only where structured text recovers an error made with generic text.

Because refusal controls retain question-form cues (Appendix H), RQ4 diag-noses abstention under this control set rather than form-blind calibration. 87 Conclusion 

MOSAIC separates omni-modal memory failures into WRITE , RETRIEVE , DELIVER , and READ . Un-der the matched protocol and fixed reader, read-ing accounts for the largest residual loss under gold facts; retrieval and delivery constrain native pipelines, textualization text-derived ones. These are protocol associations, not universal bottlenecks. Spatial structure has the clearest causal validation and no adequate text surrogate; annotated prosody tags show selector-level sensitivity, while tempo-ral and raw-audio versus ASR contrasts remain inconclusive. No system both answers and refuses reliably: progress requires preserving modality-specific signal, delivering complete chains, and reading them faithfully. 

8 Limitations 

MOSAIC’s personas, evidence sessions, and me-dia are synthetic; evaluation on organically col-lected multimodal histories remains future work. The primary reader is held out from filtering, and stage contrasts are paired, but stage labels are matched-protocol associations rather than fully iso-lated mechanisms. Video nulls are underpowered (Appendix F). The raw-audio versus ASR gap is in-conclusive for this reader, and textualized audio car-riers often retain paralinguistic lexical cues while other gold hops already expose the answer value— so gold-delivery audio nulls do not establish acous-tic recovery (Appendix G). Swapped gold prosody tags nonetheless show selector-level sensitivity (Appendix K.4). RQ4 uses length-matched refusal controls with residual form cues (Appendix H). Further caveats on modality coverage and backend scope are in Appendix G. 

9 Ethical Considerations 

Personas, evidence sessions, and all media arte-facts in MOSAIC are synthetic; we release no pri-vate user logs collected by the authors. Filler dia-logue text is sampled from LongMemEval’s pub-lic filler-session pool (Appendix B.6): UltraChat under MIT and ShareGPT under Apache 2.0 as stated by LongMemEval; multimodal attachments on those fillers are synthetic injections from our pipeline. Stage-localized evaluation is intended as a constructive tool: deployers can see where evidence was dropped rather than only that a system scored poorly. The human audit used short voluntary aca-demic annotation (Appendix I). Real deployments of omni-modal memory should of course obtain user consent and provide retention controls, con-cerns outside the scope of this benchmark release. A.2 Modality Gap Strategies 

Required non-text facts are assigned a gap strategy (1,555/1,582 carry a named tag in the release; see Table 14). A modality gap is information that sur-vives only because its native modality preserves it—a text-only or caption-based agent would lose it. The gaps are not arbitrary: each probes a known encoder failure mode so that recovering the an-swer requires the native channel that mechanism preserves. 

Encoder motivation (deferred from §3.2). Im-age gaps (spatial) target detail that patch-based vi-sual encoders compress away under token-pooling and resolution limits, and that captioners—which describe the salient foreground—omit. Video gaps (temporal) target what sparse frame-sampling and temporal pooling destroy: a caption of “assembling a shelf” retains neither order nor count. Audio gaps (prosodic) target the lexical–acoustic split that ASR collapses; prosody is the signal most completely erased by textualization. 

Image Gaps (Spatial). 

• Background detail: The answer resides in the periphery or background; the foreground depicts an unrelated object. Example: a photo of a chess set where the answer is the lighthouse visible through the window behind it. 

• Fine-grained attribute: The answer requires reading text (brand names, prices, serial numbers, book titles) or counting exact quantities visible only in the image. Example: the brand name “Lorraine” stamped on the base of a fondue pot, never mentioned in chat text. 

• Spatial configuration: The answer depends on the relative arrangement or position of items. Ex-ample: “the left pole leaning higher than the right” in a photo of hiking poles against a fence. 

Video Gaps (Temporal). 

• Action ordering: Which of two distinct actions came first vs. second. Example: a video of a shelf being assembled, where the model must determine whether the photo was placed before or after the candle was lit. 

• State transition: How a change unfolded— gradual, sudden, uneven, patchy. Example: a barn renovation video showing transformation stages across multiple weeks. 

• Action repetition count: How many times an action occurred, banded as 1–2, 4–5, or 8+ to ab-sorb video-generation variance. Example: count-ing how many times a molinillo is spun in a hot chocolate preparation video. 

Audio Gap (Prosodic). 

• Audio prosody (happy/unhappy): Emotional tone conveys preference, while the transcript uses deliberately neutral words naming ≥ 2 candi-dates. Example: a voice message saying “I tried the brass-and-glass telegraph” in a happy tone, while another message mentions a different tele-graph neutrally. 

A.3 Fact Roles and Chain Structure 

Every answerable MOSAIC item is built around a reasoning chain—an ordered sequence of required facts . Each fact has a role: • NARROWER (NAR): Reduces the candidate set (e.g., “I prefer vintage items”). • SELECTOR (SEL): Identifies a unique entity (e.g., “the brass-and-glass telegraph I sounded happy about”). • ANSWER (ANS): Carries the final value (e.g., “8:15 AM”). A chain follows the pattern NAR ∗ SEL ∗ ANS . Every valid chain satisfies four constraints defined in §3.3. A chain is cross-modal when its required facts span at least two modalities. B.1 Stages 1–3 in Detail

B.3 Prompt Templates 

The pipeline uses a modular set of LLM prompt templates, each targeting a specific generation sub-task: • Chain design: Given a persona, question type, and assigned modalities, the LLM designs the reasoning chain question-first: write the question, choose the answer, then construct the chain C

and its facts. Type-specific templates enforce the required hop count and session structure. • Fact extraction: After chain design, the LLM extracts canonical fact statements from the reasoning chain, assigning each fact a role (N ARROWER , S ELECTOR , A NSWER ), a modal-ity, and a gap strategy. • Session planning: Facts are distributed across a chronological timeline of evidence and filler ses-sions. Periods and session groups are assigned programmatically; the LLM determines the nat-ural language realisation of each fact within its assigned session. • Validation and repair: A dedicated validation prompt runs a per-fact deletion test and checks for twelve named failure modes (see below). Failures trigger minimal repairs (1–3 targeted changes); items unrepaired after three iterations are discarded. • Session building: An LLM rewrites each canon-ical fact as a natural conversation turn under two guards: a temporal-leak guard restricts facts to those with hop_index ≤ the current turn, and a forbidden-value list prevents N ARROWER and SELECTOR facts from mentioning the A NSWER

C.4 Diagnostic Conditions 

Beyond the two architecture tracks, the matched decomposition (§5.1) runs seven conditions on the same 784 answerable QIDs under one reader, prompt, packer, budget, and judge: • Gold symbolic facts: the annotated required facts as text, bounding residual read and grading error. • Gold native evidence: the same chain delivered as raw media, isolating native perception cost. • Gold generic text: the same chain as VL cap-tions plus Whisper ASR, isolating write-time tex-tualization loss. • Gold structured text: the same chain under the query-blind structured writer, isolating the avoid-able share of that loss. • Retrieved native, all delivered: every retrieved hit is serialized, isolating retrieval loss at fixed delivery. • Retrieved native, production caps: the iden-tical ranked manifest under top-20 units, three media per modality, 100 MB, isolating delivery loss at fixed ranking. • End-to-end native RAG: adds the frozen fixed-rewrite query ranking. Delivery is additionally swept over two tighter budgets (top-10 units with three media; top-5 units with one media item per modality) against a sin-gle frozen top-50 manifest, so that retrieval quality is constant by construction and only the packer’s 17 allowance varies. The within-item modality inter-ventions (§5.2) supply twelve further conditions: four image variants, four video variants, and four audio variants. 

C.5 LLM-as-Judge Protocol 

All system outputs are evaluated by a single LLM judge (Qwen3.7-Plus; full prompt in Appendix M). A three-judge panel (GLM-5.2 and Grok-4.5 as auxiliaries) re-scores every Track B run to ver-ify that conclusions are robust to judge identity (agreement statistics: Appendix J). Only the pri-mary judge’s scores appear in any reported number. The judge receives the question, expected answer, accepted aliases, and the system’s predicted answer, and assigns a score on a 0–1 scale: For A NSWER REFUSAL items, “I don’t know” or similar refusals score 1.0; any other answer scores 0.0. 

C.6 Metrics Definitions 

• Judge score (answerable): Mean judge score over the 784 answerable items. Answer Refusal is excluded and reported separately as abstention. • Abstention (paired): Correct-refusal rate on the 139 A NSWER REFUSAL items together with the false-refusal rate on the 784 answerable items (Table 28, Figure 6); reporting both prevents an always-abstain system from appearing well-calibrated. • Required-fact Recall@ k: Fraction of an item’s gold required facts whose source unit appears in the top-k set, computed over both the ranked units and the units actually serialized into the request. • Complete-chain Recall@ k / chain delivery: 

Fraction of items for which every required fact appears, ranked or delivered respectively. This is the retrieval metric that matters for multi-hop memory, since a partial chain is not usable. • Paired effect: For a controlled contrast, the mean within-item difference over the shared QID set, with an item-bootstrap 95% inter-val (10,000 resamples), wins/ties/losses, and a persona-clustered bootstrap as a dependence sen-sitivity check. 

D Dataset Statistics 

The final MOSAIC-Eval set contains 923 items across 70 personas, averaging 13.2 items per per-sona and 17.5 sessions per item (range 11–24). The total haystack comprises approximately 18.8M to-kens, with a per-persona average of ∼269K tokens. Multimodal artefact counts by evidence tier are in Table 10. MOSAIC’s per-persona haystack ( ∼269K to-kens) is substantially larger than LongMemEval’s (∼158K). The token distribution is deliberately skewed: evidence-bearing sessions contribute a mean of 840 tokens each, while filler sessions av-erage 1,213 tokens (overall session mean ∼1.2K). Required facts are therefore scattered across mul-timodal evidence and multi-topic filler rather than clustered in a handful of salient messages. Long-MemEval spreads its smaller budget across more sessions ( ∼50 at ∼3.2K tokens each) that typically centre on a single text topic, so the retrieval chal-lenge differs in both scale and modality mix. The six question types are not uniformly dis-tributed. Knowledge Update (20.9%) and Tempo-ral Reasoning (20.5%) are the largest categories, reflecting their importance for evaluating evolv-ing state and causal disruption—two capabilities central to long-horizon memory. Information Ex-traction (19.3%), the simplest type, serves as a single-hop baseline. The two multi-hop types, MSR Bridge and MSR Selection, each account for 12.1%, and together with Knowledge Update and Temporal Reasoning, multi-hop reasoning ques-tions constitute 65.6% of the benchmark. Answer Refusal items (15.1%) provide the abstention sub-set, sufficient for measuring refusal behaviour with-out dominating the evaluation. The modality distribution reflects MOSAIC’s de-liberate emphasis on non-textual evidence. Across all facts (required + context + distractor), 80.0% involve a non-text modality, and among required facts specifically, 85.1% ( 1,582 /1,860 ) are non-text. Image evidence dominates, accounting for 39.1% of all facts and 49.0% of required facts, fol-lowed by audio (28.5% all, 25.6% required) and video (12.4% all, 10.5% required). The relatively lower share of video reflects the higher generation cost and the targeted use of video for temporal gaps (action ordering, state transition, repetition count), where static alternatives cannot suffice. Audio’s substantial presence—over a quarter of required facts—is unique among memory benchmarks and directly enables the prosody-based discrimination that Caption-Text architectures consistently fail on (see §6). G Extended Limitations 

This appendix expands the caveats summarized in §8. 

Stage associations versus isolated causality. 

Matched stage drops associate each paired contrast with the intervened stage under a frozen reader, prompt, packer, and judge. Gold versus retrieved contexts still differ in distractors and composition, so stage labels are protocol associations rather than fully isolated causal mechanisms. 

Underpowered video nulls. Video strata are modest ( n=56 –64 unique QIDs). At 80% power the MDEs are 8.9–11 .2 points (Appendix F), so ordered-versus-shuffled and related nulls are incon-clusive , not proof of lossless temporal compression. 

Gold-delivery audio nulls and lexical leakage. 

Raw audio versus ASR ( +0 .8) and ASR plus a gold prosody tag versus ASR ( +1 .5) are near-null under gold delivery for the primary reader. Amodel-independent lexical audit of the textualized audio carriers used as the ASR condition finds fre-quent paralinguistic cues (e.g., oh wow , ugh , filled pauses) in the script text, and other gold hops of-ten already expose the answer value. These nulls therefore do not establish that the reader recovers sentiment from plain transcripts or raw audio. A separate correct-versus-swapped symbolic tag in-tervention does show selector-level sensitivity (Ap-pendix K.4); human-audited lexically clean subsets defined without conditioning on model failure re-main future work. 

Construction and coverage. Automated filters and a human audit support release quality (Ap-pendix I). Modality coverage is uneven by design (we report per-challenge strata), and text-only back-ends do not yet ingest native media indices. Gold construction metadata must not be exposed to sys-tems under test (Appendix H).

I.1 Annotation Protocol 

Three annotators independently reviewed a strati-fied sample of 100 items (70 where the omni probe verdict was NO, 30 where it was YES). Annota-tors were shown the question, expected answer, all required evidence facts (with modality and gap strategy), and the omni probe’s verdict. For each item, they answered: 1. Well-posed? Is the question unambiguous and answerable as stated? (Yes / Partially / No) 2. Gold answer correct? Is the expected answer factually correct given the evidence? (Yes / No) 3. Evidence sufficient? Are all required artefacts legible/audible and do they show the answer? (Yes / No) 4. Failure mode: If insufficient, what is the pri-mary failure? (Evidence item invalid / Model refused incorrectly / Model attended to wrong modality / Model overlooked gap-bearing detail / Judge format mismatch / No need to answer) A follow-up audit then graded 100 additional probe-YES items drawn from the 740 automated answerable releases (seed 20260801; excluding the 30 YES items already in the first sample), using the same Q1–Q3 rubric with two annotators.

The failure mode distribution clarifies why 

the omni probe fails. The dominant category— evidence item invalid (60.0%)—indicates that the primary bottleneck is media synthesis quality: generated images sometimes render text illegibly, video frames occasionally miscount actions, and audio clips may garble prosodic cues. This is a limitation of current generative models rather than a benchmark design flaw, and as synthesis qual-ity improves, these failures will naturally decrease. The second-largest category, model overlooked gap-bearing detail (16.2%), reveals that even a ca-pable omni model sometimes attends to the cor-rect modality but misses the specific detail that the gap strategy targets—for example, reading the fore-ground text in an image while missing the small background label that contains the answer. This is precisely the failure mode that MOSAIC is de-signed to expose in evaluated systems. Model re-fused incorrectly (14.8%) indicates cases where the omni probe said “I don’t know” despite the evidence being present and legible, likely due to ambiguous question phrasing or insufficient rea-soning depth. The remaining categories—wrong modality (7.6%) and format mismatch (1.4%)—are minor, confirming that the probe generally attends to the correct evidence source. 

I.5 Probe Rejections Overturned (Rescued Items) 

The human audit identified 44 items where the omni probe verdict was NO but annotators restored them to the final set. The selection rule is: an item is excluded only if all three annotators mark the evidence item invalid (26 items); otherwise it is 

rescued (44 items). The 44 rescued items are not unanimous first-pass agreement—first-pass Q2 Yes counts were 35/36/46 across the three annotators across the 70 probe-NO items audited—so “qual-ity 1.0” means inclusion under the three-annotator unanimous-exclusion rule, not unanimous first-pass acceptance. Rescued items span all five answerable question types: Information Extraction (5), Knowl-edge Update (11), MSR Bridge (9), MSR Selection (9), and Temporal Reasoning (10). 

I.6 Inter-Rater Agreement 

Pairwise agreement between evaluators measures the reliability of human judgments: Q1 (Well-posed?): mean pairwise 94.0% (E1-E2=95%, E2-E3=91%, E1-E3=96%). Q2 (Gold answer correct?): mean pairwise 92.7% (E1-E2=99%, E2-E3=90%, E1-E3=89%). Q3 (Evidence suf-ficient?): mean pairwise 84.0% (E1-E2=84%, E2-E3=76%, E1-E3=92%). Q3 shows the highest variance (76–92%), re-flecting genuine perceptual ambiguity in judging whether a synthesised image or audio clip “clearly shows” the intended detail—a finding consistent with prior work on multimodal quality assessment. Q1 has the highest agreement (94.0%), confirm-ing that MOSAIC questions are unambiguously well-posed. 

I.7 Probe Rejections Upheld 

The audit confirmed 26 items where the omni probe verdict was NO and evaluators agreed the item was genuinely invalid. These items remain excluded from MOSAIC-Eval.
