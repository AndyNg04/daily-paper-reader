## Abstract 

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) offers a principled way of populating simulated economies with many heterogeneous de-cision makers. Its adoption, pioneered in finance, has nonetheless been slow in macroeconomics because of two main limitations: the lack of a shared frame of reference connecting MARL systems to dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models the pro-fession already trusts, and the very long training times. The first limitation was recently addressed by the Multi-Agent Reinforce-ment Learning Business Cycle (MARL-BC), which places deep RL households in a real business cycle economy and reduces to classic DSGE models in specific limits. Here we address the second limita-tion. We present JaxMARL-BC, a JAX-accelerated simulator that trains MARL-BC economies in seconds (between 75 × and 724 ×

faster than the reference implementation), and scales to 200 ,000 independent learning households. We verify its correctness against the known DSGE limits, characterise its scaling through a simple cost law, and demonstrate its value by a proof-of-concept calibra-tion of an extended model with heterogeneous returns on capital, in a two-hour experiment that would previously have taken over a month. We release JaxMARL-BC as open source.

## 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) [44] and multi-agent RL (MARL) [3] have gradually become the basis of a new modelling paradigm for simulating the behaviour of agents in economics and finance. In RL, agents learn to maximise their rewards through repeated interaction with the economy they inhabit, so that MARL becomes a principled way of populating a simulated economy with many diverse decision makers, each optimising a reasonable objective.

The adoption of this approach was pioneered in finance, where RL and MARL are now commonly used to represent trading, hedg-ing or market-making agents across a range of markets [5, 9, 20, 25, 29, 34, 46], or within multi-agent market simulators supported by a growing ecosystem of open-source platforms [4, 6, 23, 31]. In macroeconomics and structural macro-financial modelling, adop-tion has been comparatively slower. Indeed, in spite of a growing body of specialised research in the field, from stylised economies [12, 14, 50], to proofs of concept in monetary policy [13, 28], and tax design [2, 15, 17, 35], the methodology has not entered the toolkit of policymakers and practitioners. In our view, the reason is twofold. On the one hand, this is due to scepticism and resistance towards alternative modelling method-ologies, from a community that is long accustomed to a specific way of building models based on the dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) approach. This resistance is understandable, as the DSGE approach provides a shared frame of reference that keeps modelling out of the “wilderness of bounded rationality” [22], where infinitely many deviations from optimality become available. On the other hand, this is due to the considerably higher computa-tional cost of training RL or MARL systems, whereas a typical DSGE model can be specified and solved in seconds. A shared framework and fast solvers, in turn, make these models practical to use, and explain the remarkable success of specialised modelling software such as Dynare [1, 45]. The first limitation of MARL approaches has been convincingly addressed in a recently introduced framework, the Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Business Cycle (MARL-BC) [24]. MARL-BC places deep RL households inside a real business cycle (RBC) economy, so that the MARL system reduces to classic DSGE models in specific limits, recovering the representative-agent RBC and the mean-field Krusell–Smith economy. Hence, MARL-BC extends the classic models rather than replacing them, and it does so in a way that can be checked against results the profession already trusts. However, MARL-BC does not address the second limitation we mentioned, since training requires multiple hours per run [24]. In this work, we leverage JAX [11], a library for just-in-time compilation of numerical code to hardware accelerators, to reduce MARL-BC training time from hours to seconds, thereby addressing the second adoption bottleneck described above, and fostering a wider usage of the methodology by economists and practitioners. JAX has often been proposed as a viable way to lower RL training times by allowing environment, policy and optimiser to live inside a single compiled program directly on the accelerator [32, 39, 41]. The financial community has already extensively explored this arXiv:2610.28907v1 [cs.MA] 20 Oct 2026 route, with the JAX-LOB simulator for limit order book RL trad-ing agents [23], and the JaxMARL-HFT simulator extending it to large-scale multi-agent high-frequency trading [36]. EconoJax [38] applies the same strategy to a stylised economic simulation, but no equivalent exists for structural MARL-driven macroeconomic modelling, a gap this paper fills. More specifically, our contributions are as follows: 

• We propose JaxMARL-BC, an end-to-end JAX simulator which trains MARL-BC economies in seconds rather than hours, and scales easily to hundreds of thousands of independent learning agents. 

• We verify the correctness of our simulator against the known DSGE limits of the MARL-BC, reproducing the results at ten times the population size and at a small fraction of the cost. 

• We extensively measure the scalability to large populations of agents and to large numbers of parallel environments, which we characterise via a simple cost law that we verify empirically. 

• We showcase the capability of the simulator by performing a re-alistic calibration exercise of a large model with heterogeneous returns on capital, with 110 training runs sweeping different degrees of ex-ante heterogeneity in a two-hour computation that would have previously taken over a month. We release JaxMARL-BC as open source 1, together with a pro-totype LLM-augmented dashboard that turns natural-language re-quests into simulation configurations, so that non-technical users can experiment without touching the command line. Removing the computational barrier to MARL in macroeconomics can let the methodology spread beyond toy demonstrations and become com-mon practice for structural macro and macro-financial modelling. We structure the rest of this work as follows. In Sec. 2 we review related work. In Sec. 3 we describe the JaxMARL-BC simulator. In Sec. 4 and Sec. 5 we report the experimental setup and the results of our experiments. Finally, in Sec. 6 we conclude. 

## 2 Related work 

Training RL and MARL systems typically requires substantial time and computational resources, which can limit their adoption. The creation of simulation environments in JAX has therefore become an active line of work. The design pattern was popularised for single-agent RL by PureJaxRL [32] and is now packaged in multi-agent libraries such as JaxMARL [41] and Mava [39], which report speedups of one to four orders of magnitude over comparable CPU runs. Among application domains, finance has been the most active adopter of this pattern. JAX-LOB [23] was the first GPU-accelerated limit order book simulator, processing thousands of books in par-allel and delivering at least a 7 × speedup over comparable CPU implementations. JaxMARL-HFT [36] builds on JAX-LOB and ex-tends it to a heterogeneous set of agents with distinct observations, actions and rewards, reporting up to a 240 × reduction in training time. EconoJax [38] ports the AI-Economist environment [50] to JAX and trains populations of 100 agents in fifteen minutes, down from the several days required by the original implementation. JaxMARL-BC follows the same design philosophy, but targets a 

> 1https://github.com/danmonuni/jax-marl-bc.

domain none of these packages cover, namely structural hetero-geneous macroeconomic modelling. Our work is also close to the ABIDES-Economist [18], which populates agent-based economies with RL agents, but which is not implemented in JAX and does not specifically target established macroeconomic benchmarks. A different line of work uses deep learning, without RL, to solve heterogeneous-agent models [8, 27, 33], and certain fast conven-tional methods, such as the sequence-space Jacobian [7], can also be used for the same task. JaxMARL-BC does not aim to compete with these methods on the classes of models they already solve well. Rather, it aims to facilitate the growth and adoption of a new modelling paradigm based on independent learning agents, which these methods do not represent.

Learning. Households are trained by deep RL, and specifically by Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [43]. PPO is a standard algorithm for RL, and also for MARL where typically, as in our case, all agents are treated as independent learners in the same environment [16]. With 𝑛 > 1 learners in one economy, the tran-sitions and rewards any household observes depend on what the others are currently doing, so each faces a non-stationary problem and the single-agent convergence guarantees no longer apply [3]. For symmetric agents, a standard remedy is parameter sharing, in which a single policy network serves the entire population and is evaluated on each agent’s private observations. This stabilises training without forcing identical behaviour, since household char-acteristics enter through the observation [49]. MARL-BC interprets this configuration as a stylised form of “social learning” [37, 47], and we adopt the same approach here. 

Known limits of the model. The MARL-BC paper shows that the model reproduces standard DSGE models in two limit cases. Specifi-cally, with a single agent ( 𝑛 = 1), individual and aggregate quantities coincide and the framework reduces to the textbook representative-agent RBC, while with many ex-ante identical agents ( 𝑛 ≫ 1 and 

𝜅 𝑖 = 𝜆 𝑖 = 1 ∀𝑖 ), the framework reproduces the well-known mean-field Krusell–Smith model. These two limits make the present work easily testable, since the target behaviour is known from a well-established literature, and they also address the first of the adoption obstacles discussed in the introduction, by anchoring the framework to model classes widely recognised by the community. 

## 3.2 The JAX speedup 

We now describe the JAX design that enables the speedup of our simulator. The left panel of Figure 1 shows the three axes upon which a single rollout of the simulator is parallelised and executed. These axes differ in the kind of dependence that holds among their elements, and each kind calls for a different JAX construct. Along the first axis are the 𝑛 households of one economy, which are 

not independent of one another, as they are coupled through the aggregates of Eq. (1) that determine the prices they all face. The agent axis is therefore a reduction axis, and we represent it as a plain array dimension so that 𝑛 = 10 and 𝑛 = 10 ,000 execute the very same program, only on wider arrays. By contrast, the second axis represents 𝐸 copies of the economy, which are fully independent of one another. This is the kind of parallelism jax.vmap is designed for, as it takes a function written for a single instance and lifts it to a batch of independent ones. Here, a single jax.vmap over the environment’s ‘reset’ and ‘step’ functions produces the 𝐸 parallel economies. The policy forward pass flattens these two axes into one batch dimension, so the policy network only ever sees their product, the batch width 𝑊 = 𝐸 ×𝑛 . The third axis, the 𝑇 periods of a rollout, is instead irreducibly sequential and is carried by a lax.scan . All of these computations happen inside one jit boundary, enclosing the optimiser, the environment reset and every update. The computational design just described suggests a simple model of run time, which we will later use to organise and understand our measurements. To write it down, we first define the sequential depth 𝐷 as the number of irreducible stages that must run one after another. In practice, these are the environment periods and the gradient steps, since period 𝑡 + 1 depends on period 𝑡 and gradient step 𝑗 + 1 depends on step 𝑗 . 𝑊 , while the effective maximum throughput 𝑃 is the number of agent-transitions per second the device can sustain once it is fully occupied. Given these definitions, we can model the total training time 𝑇 train as

## 3.3 The LLM-augmented dashboard 

Figure 2 shows a screenshot of the user-friendly dashboard we built for JaxMARL-BC. The dashboard lets users launch a simulation from a natural-language request, so that non-technical users too can easily experiment with the simulator. Under the hood, the natural-language request is sent to an LLM, currently either an OpenAI model via its API or a local Ollama model. The LLM turns the request into the YAML configuration file read by the simulation engine. Importantly, the resulting configuration is previewed in the dashboard, and can be edited before training starts. The dashboard then launches the training run, streams its log to the page, and renders the resulting artefacts. We note that JaxMARL-BC can also be run from the command line with greater parameter flexibility, and we use this approach in our experiments. The dashboard is intended as a convenience tool for exploration, and we do not evaluate it in this work. 

## 4 Experimental setup 

To validate and demonstrate the capabilities of JaxMARL-BC, we perform three main experiments: (i) a correctness check of our simulator against the original MARL-BC results, (ii) an assessment of its scalability, and (iii) a large-scale calibration exercise that showcases its practical utility. The general setup of all experiments is described in Sec. 4.1, while the detailed specifications of the three experiments are given in Sec. 4.2. 

## 4.1 General setup 

Hardware and networks. We perform our training runs on a P100 GPU, and in experiment (ii) we also use an Intel Xeon CPU. In all the experiments, the policy is a shared-parameter network, a 64 × 64 tanh MLP. We use PPO with the Adam optimiser, a clipping parameter of 0 .2, a GAE decay of 0 .95, a discount factor equal to 𝛽 ,and a learning rate of 3 × 10 −4. Each gradient update iterates for 4 epochs over the collected experience, split into 64 minibatches. All computations use single-precision ( float32 ) arithmetic, the JAX default on accelerators. 

Model parameters. JaxMARL-BC can simulate any MARL-BC economy, and we have also checked its correctness against the 

𝑛 = 1 limit of the model, which is a relatively simple limit since a single household coincides with the representative agent. Here, however, we only report the mean-field ( 𝑛 ≫ 1) limit of the model, as this is much harder to replicate and it is also the limit that can most benefit from the large-scale extensions unlocked by fast training. Specifically, we parametrise JaxMARL-BC to reproduce the mean-field Krusell–Smith model as follows. We set 𝛼 = 0.36, 

𝛽 = 0.95 and 𝛿 = 0.025. Aggregate technology follows a two-state Markov chain, 𝐴 𝑡 = 1.02 in good states and 𝐴 𝑡 = 0.98 in bad ones, with each state persisting with probability 7 /8. Individual employment follows a second chain, where an employed household supplies ¯ ℓ = 1.11 units of labour and an unemployed one supplies none, with the unemployment rate at 4% in good states and 10% in bad ones, as in [30]. ## 5.2 Scaling 

Table 1 reports training times of JaxMARL-BC for an increasing number of households against those of comparable training runs in [24]. The PPO column is the most directly comparable, since PPO is what we use in JaxMARL-BC, and its speedup ranges from about 75 × at 𝑛 = 10 to about 724 × at 𝑛 = 500. The SAC column is also relevant since SAC is the preferred algorithm in [24], and against it the speedup goes from 115 × to 301 ×. These speedups combine the effect of the JAX with that of the GPU. The two can be separated using our CPU times, which are between 94 × and 179 × faster, with the GPU contributing a further factor of up to 7 .7× at 𝑛 = 500. Furthermore, in contrast with the reference implementation, our GPU run time grows slowly across the table, from 22 s to 36 s for a fifty-fold larger population. ## 5.3 Calibration of ex-ante heterogeneity 

Having established correctness and speed, we now show how JaxMARL-BC can be used for an advanced calibration exercise. Specifically, here we extend the Krusell–Smith model with hetero-geneity in the ex-ante capital productivities and demonstrate how the extended model can better match empirical quantities. We set up the model by drawing each household’s capital productivity 

𝜅 𝑖 from a log-normal distribution with dispersion parameter 𝜎 . At 

𝜎 = 0 every 𝜅 𝑖 equals one, and the model is equivalent to the ex-ante homogeneous Krusell–Smith economy of Sec. 5.1. Since productivi-ties enter the equation for returns on capital (see Eq. (3)), we can calibrate their dispersion 𝜎 to match empirically plausible values for the Gini index and for the heterogeneous returns on capital. Figure 6 reports the outcome of the training runs with means and standard deviations over seeds. As expected, a higher dispersion 

𝜎 produces systematically more skewed Lorenz curves, and corre-spondingly higher Gini indices. More specifically, the capital Gini rises from 0 .20 ±0.05 at 𝜎 = 0, the ex-ante homogeneous benchmark of Sec. 5.1, to 0 .72 ±0.05 at 𝜎 ≈ 0.4, and it then plateaus up to 𝜎 = 0.5. Since the wealth Gini of most advanced economies is of the order of 0 .6–0 .8 [10, 42], this indicates that the extended Krusell–Smith economy can be brought considerably closer to the data than the original one, and that a dispersion of 𝜎 ≈ 0.4–0 .5 constitutes a good parametrisation of the extended model. Furthermore, Figure 7 shows that the same values of 𝜎 also imply plausible cross-sectional distributions of returns on capital, whose heterogeneity is widely recognised as an important driver of wealth concentration [21, 48]. This calibration exercise consists of 110 runs, ten seeds at each of eleven dispersion values and the whole computation takes about two hours. To give a rough estimate of the time this would have taken before JaxMARL-BC, we can use the times in Table 1 and assume that similar speedups also hold for the 𝐸 = 12 environments needed here. This is a lower-bound estimate since it assumes that the reference implementation scales as efficiently as ours with 𝐸 ,and it still amounts to more than a month of compute, which shows how JaxMARL-BC can unlock a class of calibration experiments that was previously out of reach. 

## 6 Conclusions 

We presented JaxMARL-BC, a JAX-accelerated simulator for MARL-driven macroeconomic modelling. Training a MARL-BC economy takes tens of seconds rather than hours, and populations of 2 × 10 5

independent learning households come within reach of a single run, a regime never touched previously. Speedups of this size change what can be attempted, as large-scale runs and parameter calibra-tion can become an integral part of model development. Several directions follow naturally. Wider model coverage is the most immediate extension. The RBC and Krusell–Smith economies studied here are natural entry points, but the same machinery should accommodate Huggett economies, New Keynesian economies and their heterogeneous-agent (HANK) versions. At the same time, the rational-expectations counterpart of the economies studied here remains within reach of classical solution methods, and it will be important to address economies with richer heterogeneity and with

JaxMARL-BC is released as open source, together with an LLM-augmented dashboard. The framework is anchored to model classes the profession already trusts, and it is fast enough to quickly iterate on model specifications. We expect these two properties to facilitate the transition of MARL from proof-of-concept demonstration to standard practice in macroeconomic modelling.
