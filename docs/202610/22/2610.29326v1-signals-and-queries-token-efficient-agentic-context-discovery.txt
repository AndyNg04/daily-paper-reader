# Signals and Queries: Token-Efficient Agentic Context Discovery 

## Yam Marcovitz 

yam@parlant.io 

Abstract 

User-facing LLM agents must identify which behavioral policies apply at each turn, but evaluating every policy with an LLM is costly. We introduce Signals and Queries (S&Q), a lightweight first-stage relevance filter developed for Parlant 4.0 that removes at least 30% of candidate policies before downstream evaluation while preserving high recall. For each policy, an LLM generates activating examples and hard negatives offline. An independent logistic classifier trained on their embeddings then predicts relevance using embeddings and vector dot products, with no per-turn LLM calls. This shifts expensive relevance evaluation to a smaller candidate set, while allowing each policy’s classifier to be updated independently. Evaluated against 9,144 LLM-judge relevance labels covering 254 τ 2-bench conversation prefixes and 36 airline policies, S&Q achieves higher precision than top-K

and cosine-threshold retrieval at every evaluated recall level, with consistent results across two embedding providers. These findings support inexpensive, per-policy classification as a practical first stage for reducing downstream evaluation workload in customer-facing agents. 

# 1 Introduction and Motivation 

Agentic LLM applications increasingly separate what the agent could know from what the agent should see right now . A customer-facing agent may be governed by dozens of behavioral policies; it may also carry a domain glossary, tool and skill descriptions, state obser-vations, and reference documents. Placing all of it in every prompt is expensive and counterproductive, for three well-documented reasons. Long contexts suffer from the lost-in-the-middle effect: models systematically under-attend to information positioned in the middle of the prompt [1]. They suffer from attention dilution :as the prompt grows, the attention mass allotted to any individual instruction shrinks, so its influence on generation fades [2]. And they suffer from the curse of instructions : the probability of following all instructions decays approximately as the per-instruction success rate raised to the number of instructions, so adherence de-grades steadily as inventories grow [3]. On top of these quality failures, per-turn token cost scales linearly with inventory size rather than with what the turn actually needs. The problem is acute for policies . In behavioral gov-ernance frameworks such as Parlant [4], policies are normative instructions (“only compensate if the cus-tomer holds travel insurance. . . ”) whose omission is a correctness failure, not merely a quality regression: an agent that does not see the cancellation policy while handling a cancellation may take actions it should have refused. Relevance filtering for policies is therefore a 

recall-critical problem: a false negative can produce a policy violation, while a false positive merely wastes a few hundred prompt tokens that modern models tolerate well. This asymmetry drives the design of everything that follows. One accurate solution is to ask an LLM, per turn and per policy, “is this policy relevant now?”; this is the first-pass ranker used in Parlant’s engine. It works, but its cost scales with the inventory: for our 36-policy benchmark it consumes ∼69 ,000 prompt tokens and 36 LLM calls per user turn (§4.4). The alternative, embedding-based retrieval of the policy text against the conversation, is nearly free but suffers from a genre gap: conversations are informal user utterances while policies are formal normative prose, and their embed-dings separate poorly [5]. A third architecture avoids the problem by construction: routed flow-graphs place different instructions at different nodes, so each prompt stays small. But this fragments the agent’s knowledge. Real customer conversations are multi-turn and multi-topic, and drift freely across node boundaries. The node in control at any given moment often lacks policies the situation has come to require, so the agent is least coherent on the messy, multi-topic conversations that matter most. A fourth approach, taken by skill-based agent frameworks [6, 7], keeps only a short description of each skill resident and loads a skill’s full instructions on demand, once the model, reasoning over those descrip-tions, decides it applies. This keeps whole instructions 1arXiv:2610.29326v1 [cs.CL] 21 Oct 2026 out of the prompt, but makes discovery part of the model’s own reasoning loop, slower and costlier than an out-of-loop lookup. We propose Signals and Queries (S&Q): describe each artifact not (only) by its text but by signals (LLM-generated examples of user messages that should acti-vate it) and anti-signals (hard near-misses that should not). A tiny per-artifact classifier trained on these exem-plars then scores live conversation turns ( queries ) in the embedding space. While we develop and evaluate S&Q on agent policies, nothing in the formulation is policy-specific: any context artifact that can be described by exemplar activating queries is a candidate target, in-cluding state observations, glossary entries, skills and tools [8, 9], and code or document sections. 

Contributions. (1) A formulation of agentic context discovery as recall-first, per-artifact binary relevance, decided by classifiers over LLM-generated signals and anti-signals; the design is decoupled : each artifact’s classifier and threshold depend only on its own exem-plars, so adding or editing an artifact retrains noth-ing else. (2) A benchmark against an LLM judge on 

τ 2-bench conversations [10, 11] with 36 policies, com-paring four embedding-retrieval baselines; S&Q Pareto-dominates all of them and replicates across two embed-ding providers. (3) A cost analysis showing a ∼10 5×

reduction in per-turn cost relative to per-policy LLM ranking. (4) An analysis of failure modes showing that signal and anti-signal quality (coverage, hardness, and domain match) is the dominant factor, motivating ex-emplar generation as a research problem in its own right. This work continues our ongoing pursuit of accuracy in customer-facing agents at Parlant. It complements our earlier work on Attentive Reasoning Queries [12], which optimizes instruction-following once the relevant policies are already in the agent’s context; S&Q addresses the upstream problem of discovering which policies belong there in the first place. 

# 2 The S&Q Method 

## 2.1 Definitions 

Let A = {a1, . . . , a N } be the artifact inventory (policies). For each artifact aj an LLM generates, offline: 

• Signals Sj = {sj1, . . . , s jm }: short, natural user messages that should activate aj (“I need to cancel my flight to Chicago next week”). 

• Anti-signals ¯Sj = {¯sj1, . . . , ¯sjm }: hard near-misses , i.e., messages in the same topical neigh-borhood that should not activate aj because a neighboring artifact governs them (for a cancel-lation policy: “I need to change my flight from Tuesday to Thursday”). We use m = 10 of each. This is the document-side analogue of query expansion: signals play the role of pre-dicted queries as in Doc2Query [13], and are generated without any conversation data, as in synthetic-query domain adaptation [14]; anti-signals additionally supply 

targeted hard negatives , which cosine similarity alone cannot exploit. A query is the live conversation at its current state. At turn t we form two kinds of view, both centered on the artifact-inventory centroid c (centering mitigates the anisotropy of embedding spaces [5, 15]): the cumulative 

view q(t)cum , the embedding of the whole conversation through turn t, and a user-turn view q(u)user , the embed-ding of the single user message sent at each turn u ≤ t.All embeddings are ℓ2-normalized.

## 2.3 Design goals and properties 

Recall-first. As argued above, the loss function of policy discovery is asymmetric. S&Q therefore targets 

≥90% recall per artifact and optimizes precision only subject to that constraint. The retrieved set is a pre-filter : the agent’s own LLM reads the surviving artifacts and can ignore inapplicable ones cheaply, whereas it can never recover an artifact that was filtered out. 

Decoupling. Because artifact aj ’s classifier uses only 

Sj and ¯Sj , inventory edits are local: adding, removing, or rewriting one artifact requires generating its exem-plars and fitting one classifier ( ∼2 ms), leaving all others untouched. Because all three avoid per-turn language-model calls, the decisive question is not cost but capability, namely which properties a method needs in order to serve as a reliable, recall-first policy pre-filter. We identify six. 

• Inference-free per turn. Relevance is decided with-out any language-model call on the live turn, at the cost of a single embedding lookup. All three methods share this property; it is the common premise that makes the remaining, capability-level distinctions the deciding ones. 

• Adaptive selection size. The count of genuinely applicable policies varies per turn, so a method should return however many clear the relevance bar rather than a fixed number. A ranked cutoff cannot: any constant K is at once too large for simple turns and too small for multi-intent ones, discarding the artifact ranked K+1 however relevant it is. 

• Uncapped recall. Because the number of relevant policies varies, a fixed cutoff also bounds achievable recall from above (top-K saturates near 66% on our data, §4). A policy pre-filter, whose omissions are correctness failures rather than mere inefficiencies, must be able to approach full recall. 

• Near-synonym discrimination. Policies often share a topical neighborhood (cancellation, modification, rebooking), and a method must separate an artifact from its closest neighbors, where surface similar-ity peaks. Ranking by cosine similarity, whether against policy text or signals, cannot do this; con-trasting signals against hard-negative anti-signals places the boundary there. 

• Per-artifact decision boundary. top-K and cosine thresholding apply one global knob ( K or τ ) to every artifact, implicitly assuming a shared score scale. S&Q instead fits an independent boundary per artifact, letting each intercept absorb that ar-tifact’s own score offset, which lets a single global margin govern a heterogeneous inventory. 

• Portable operating point. An operating point should transfer across embedding providers and query types without re-tuning. Absolute cosine values re-flect each model’s geometry, so a threshold must be recalibrated whenever either changes; S&Q’s mar-gin, measured from each learned boundary, needs only mild per-provider adjustment. top-K’s K

transfers as an integer but denotes no calibrated relevance level, hence its partial mark. 

# 3 Experimental Setup 

Domain and conversations. We use the airline 

domain of τ 2-bench [11], the leading customer-service agent benchmark suite, maintained by the research de-partment of Sierra.ai [10]. We take 50 simulated cus-tomer conversations produced by a Parlant agent run against the benchmark’s user simulator. Every conver-sation prefix ending at a user turn is a query state: 254 sub-sequences. 

Artifact inventory. N =36 policies: the 6 sections of the benchmark’s airline policy plus 30 additional realis-tic policies authored for this study (identity verification, baggage claims, unaccompanied minors, fare rules, fraud handling, etc.), including deliberate near-synonym clus-ters to stress discrimination. Signals and anti-signals (10+10 per policy) are generated by gemini-3.5-flash 

from the policy text alone, with no conversation data. 

Ground truth. An LLM judge scores every (sub-sequence, policy) pair for relevance on a 1–5 scale: 9,144 labels from gemini-3.1-flash-lite , using the relevance-ranking prompt of Parlant’s production engine. Following the engine’s own definition, a pair is relevant 

iff score ≥4. LLM judges are imperfect [16]; we discuss the implications in §6. Metrics are macro-averaged over the 31 policies with nonzero base rate. 

Baselines. All methods share the same query views and max-pooling of Eq. 2 and the same centered-cosine geometry; they differ only in scoring: (B1) top-K pol-icy : cos( q, a j ) against the policy text, retrieve the top 

K; (B2) top-K signal : max i cos( q, s ji ), top K; (B3) threshold policy : cos( q, a j ) > τ ; (B4) threshold signal :max i cos( q, s ji ) > τ . Sweeping each method’s knob traces its recall–precision frontier. 

Embedders. The full pipeline is run twice: gemini-embedding-001 and OpenAI 

text-embedding-3-large .

Pooling addresses a regime the benchmark does not contain. ## 4.3 Comparison with SetFit 

S&Q freezes the encoder and fits only a logistic head; a natural question is whether fine-tuning the encoder on the same exemplars would do better. SetFit [17] is the direct test: it contrastively fine-tunes a sentence encoder on a few labeled examples before fitting a logis-tic head. We run it per policy on the same signals and anti-signals, with the identical benchmark and pooling, and, to separate the effect of fine-tuning from the choice of encoder, also run S&Q’s frozen-encoder recipe on the same local base model. Table 5 shows that fine-tuning does not help: SetFit trails the frozen head on the same encoder (90 /31 vs. 91 /32), and both match the far larger frozen API embedders. With only 2 m=20 exemplars per policy, contrastive fine-tuning has little to exploit and no headroom over a linear head on frozen features, consistent with our finding that exemplar cov-erage, not the classifier or the encoder, is the binding constraint ( §6). The comparison also reinforces that S&Q is encoder-agnostic: a small frozen local model suffices, so the method depends on no particular or heavyweight embedder. Fine-tuning would additionally forgo S&Q’s central efficiency, requiring a trained model per artifact in place of a single embedding call.

# 5 Scalability 

Every component we varied other than the exemplars proved inert. The classifier family is immaterial: logistic regression and a linear SVM learn nearly the same direc-tion (mean cos =0 .98) and tie, while a nearest-neighbour rule over the same exemplars is clearly worse ( §4.2). The encoder is immaterial: fine-tuning it on the same exem-plars does not beat a logistic head on frozen features, and a small local model matches the far larger API embedders ( §4.3). Exemplar count saturates: extend-ing each artifact’s anti-signal pool from ten to thirty, with per-item hardness held constant, leaves precision at 32 .3% versus 32 .4%, because 94% of the additional anti-signals fall within 0 .6 cosine of one already present and the pool’s internal diversity does not move. That leaves the exemplars themselves, and specifically their provenance. We therefore replaced generated exemplars with mined ones. We split the scenarios in half, mined exem-plars from one half, and evaluated on the other. Within the mined half a signal is the transition turn , the user turn at which the judge’s score for an artifact first reaches its maximum and therefore the turn that ac-tually triggered it, and an anti-signal is a user turn the judge scores as clearly irrelevant; we take four anti-signals per signal, selecting those closest to the artifact’s own signals. Everything else, the classifier, the encoder, the pooling, the artifacts, and the held-out items, is unchanged. Table 7 and Fig. 7 give the result: precision at ≥90% recall rises from 33 .9% to 43 .5% on Gemini and from 35 .3% to 41 .6% on OpenAI, with AUPRC rising by 0 .127 and 0 .112. Fine-tuning is closer in kind, since it absorbs exemplar improvements nearly as well, but it is considerably more cumbersome to operate, for three reasons. First, per-turn inference stops being constant in the encoder: because each artifact carries its own fine-tuned model, a live turn must be embed-ded once per artifact , N transformer forward passes in place of one shared embedding and N dot products, which forfeits the very property the method exists for. Second, the improvement loop stops being interactive: refitting one artifact after an operator promotes an ex-emplar takes ∼7 ms for a logistic head against ∼5 s of gradient fine-tuning on a GPU, some three orders of magnitude (Fig. 9), so updates become a batched re-training job rather than an immediate edit. Third, the inventory turns from data into deployed models: N fine-tuned encoders of ∼90 MB each to version, ship, and roll back, against ∼12 KB per weight vector, produced by a stochastic procedure that need not return the same classifier from the same exemplars, where the logistic fit is convex and has a unique solution. In a governance set-ting, where an operator must be able to reproduce and explain why an artifact fired, that last difference is not incidental. Fine-tuning further requires a self-hosted, fine-tunable encoder, foreclosing the hosted embedding APIs that S&Q uses interchangeably. Three limits qualify the result. Mining requires rele-vance labels on real conversations, whether from a judge or an operator, so the loop is cheap but not free. Cover-age binds before quality does: at a half-and-half mining split only 22 of the 29 occurring artifacts had enough mined material to fit on, and the rarest had none, so generated exemplars remain both the bootstrap and the cold-start path. And exemplar genre must be consistent: mixing generated exemplars with mined ones cancels the benefit in both directions, which we read as the dis-criminant learning to separate synthetic text from real conversation instead of separating the artifact from its neighbours. The held-out set here is 131 sub-sequences, so we report a direction and a magnitude that repli-cate across two embedding providers rather than precise point estimates. 

# 6 Discussion 

How exemplars fail. Section 5 establishes that exem-plars are the lever; the failure modes we observed say what makes one set better than another: (i) coverage : 10 anti-signals under-sample the space of irrelevant conver-sation, so unanticipated off-topic turns can score high (the main precision cost vs. many-negative training); (ii) hardness : anti-signals must be near-misses; replac-ing them with random negatives collapses the discrimi-nant’s usefulness on neighbor confusions; (iii) domain match : generated signals are clean single utterances while queries are messy multi-turn conversations, pro-ducing the systematic score shift of Fig. 2 that forces 

θ < 0 and per-provider calibration, and which mining exemplars from real conversations largely removes ( §5). Each of these is a property of the exemplar set, not of the retrieval machinery, which is why we treat signal and anti-signal construction as a first-class problem rather than a preprocessing detail. Beyond the mining loop of §5, natural directions include coverage-driven gener-ation (generate until held-out exemplars are classified correctly) and adversarial anti-signal mining against the current classifier. 

Precision ceilings are real. Some artifacts are intrinsically faint in embedding space: in our inventory, booking-related turns share vocabulary with the entire flight-administration genre, capping achievable preci-sion at high recall regardless of method; a supervised probe trained directly on labeled conversations confirms the ceiling. A deployment should treat such artifacts with an operator-facing safety valve (e.g. pinning must-fire exemplar conversations, then promoting them into the signal set), which we prototype in the underlying framework but do not evaluate here. 

Deploying S&Q over long conversations. Our analysis so far is per turn. A real agentic deployment, though, runs a single conversation across many turns, and the per-turn retrieved sets ( §2) accumulate. Be-cause the max-over-turns score of Eq. 2 is monotone in 

t, an artifact that once crosses θ keeps a score above it, so the retrieved set only grows. This is the same stickiness that aids multi-topic recall ( §5), but its cost is that in a long, topic-shifting conversation the injected set drifts, unchecked, back toward the always-in-context regime this paper opens against. The stickiness is thus a property of our design, not only of the LLM judge. Judge quality and provenance. Our ground truth is the first-pass relevance ranker of Parlant’s engine (gemini-3.1-flash-lite ), the same component the deployed engine acts on, not a human panel. It there-fore carries the usual LLM-judge caveats [16] and, im-portantly, has no measured inter-rater agreement with human evaluators. This is a real limitation: S&Q is trained to imitate this judge at low cost and latency, so its accuracy is bounded by the judge’s, and some of S&Q’s residual false positives are plausibly judge false negatives. We use the ranker because it is the relevance signal the engine already relies on in practice, and we treat a human-annotated subset as the natural next validation step. We also observed characteristic judge artifacts, a sticky tendency to keep a policy “relevant” once its topic appeared and a diffuse “maybe” band, which evaluating at the judge’s own binary threshold (≥4) mitigates but does not remove. 

Limitations. One domain (airline) and one conver-sation source ( τ 2-bench simulations); 5 of 36 policies never occur in the data and are unevaluated; the recall-first operating point deliberately accepts low precision (∼30%), which is appropriate for policy discovery but not for every retrieval use; and the global margin θ,while a single knob, was selected on this benchmark rather than derived from the inventory alone. S&Q also decides each artifact’s relevance independently from the conversation and does not model inter-artifact depen-dencies : a follow-up policy that applies only because another policy fired, and whose own text the current turn does not match, will not be retrieved on that basis. The decoupling that makes the method scale ( §5) is what precludes this, so dependency handling belongs to an orchestration layer that consumes S&Q’s output rather than to S&Q itself. 

## 6.1 Skill filtering 

A recall-first pre-filter is most valuable where a false positive is bounded and a false negative is total. Skill-based agents present that asymmetry in unusually clean form. Under the agent-skills standard now common to major agent frameworks [6, 7], a skill is a directory whose entry point carries YAML frontmatter, a name and a description stating when the skill applies, above a Markdown body holding the instructions themselves. Agents load these in tiers: the frontmatter of every 

installed skill is resident from the start, on the order of tens of tokens each, while a skill’s body, which may run to thousands of tokens, is read only once the agent decides to invoke it. That tiering makes the setting a natural fit. As a library grows to hundreds of skills, the resident front-matter becomes a standing context cost in its own right, and the agent must choose among many superficially similar descriptions, which is precisely the regime in which instruction adherence degrades [1, 3]. S&Q ap-plies to the frontmatter tier without modification: the artifact text is the skill’s name and description, its sig-nals are user messages that should bring the skill into 11 consideration, and its anti-signals are the near-misses that belong to a neighbouring skill. The filter decides which frontmatter entries enter the prompt at all. Recall-first is the right operating point here because S&Q is not deciding to run anything. A false positive costs one frontmatter entry, tens of tokens, which the agent dismisses after reading a sentence; it does not pull in the skill’s body, where the real context cost lives. A false negative is unrecoverable: a skill whose description never reaches the prompt cannot be invoked, however relevant it was. Precision is thereby delegated to the stage that can afford it, the agent’s own reading of the surviving descriptions, while S&Q supplies the one property that stage cannot recover on its own, namely that the right candidate is present at all. Existing practice retrieves skills by similarity against their descriptions: Voyager, for instance, keys a skill library by the embedding of each skill’s description and retrieves by nearest neighbour [18]. That is the positives-only regime of our B2 and B4 baselines, and our results bear on it directly, since similarity against descriptions cannot represent the near-miss that separates a skill from its closest neighbour, and absorbs exemplar im-provements at roughly a third the rate of a discriminant (§5). We have not evaluated S&Q on a skill corpus; we present this as an application whose shape the method fits, not as a measured result. 

# 7 Related Work 

Per-turn context selection. The problem S&Q ad-dresses, deciding which of many candidate instructions or artifacts to place in the prompt at each turn, re-curs across agent settings under different names. Two strategies dominate. Embedding similarity against arti-fact descriptions is cheap and LLM-free, and underlies tool and skill retrieval [8, 9], dynamic tool management across a conversation [19], in-context example selec-tion [20], and long-horizon memory [21]. It is exactly the regime of our B2 and B4 baselines, and our results show where it fails: on the near-misses a similarity score cannot separate from true matches. LLM ranking , judg-ing each candidate per turn, is more accurate but pays the per-turn token and latency cost that motivates this work ( §4.4), and is the ranker we take as ground truth. A complementary line compresses a fixed context rather than selecting its contents, dropping low-information tokens to shorten the prompt [22]. Compression is or-thogonal to S&Q and, for normative policies, risky, since a partially dropped rule can invert the behaviour it was meant to govern, whereas S&Q injects each selected artifact intact. S&Q sits at the cheap, LLM-free end of this space but replaces similarity with a recall-first, per-artifact discriminant, buying the near-miss discrimi-nation that plain retrieval lacks. 

Retrieval machinery. Dense retrieval represents queries and documents in a shared embedding space [23, 24], typically for retrieval-augmented generation [25]. S&Q differs in objective (per-artifact binary decisions 

under a recall constraint, not top-K ranking) and in bridging the query–document genre gap from the document side: signals are LLM-predicted activating queries, the exemplar-level analogue of Doc2Query [13] and of synthetic-query domain adaptation [14], while HyDE [26] attacks the same gap from the query side with hypothetical documents. Unlike these, S&Q addi-tionally generates hard negative exemplars and trains a discriminant per artifact, bringing contrastive super-vision [27] to inference-free per-artifact classification. The leverage of hard negatives is well established on the retrieval side: the passages most likely to distract the model reading them are the topically adjacent ones, and deliberately mining them improves robustness [28], a principle anti-signals carry to artifact selection by plac-ing each classifier’s boundary along its most confusable neighbors. Embedding anisotropy and its correction motivate our centering step [5, 15, 29]. We evaluate on the conversational agent benchmarks τ -bench and 

τ 2-bench [10, 11]. 

# 8 Conclusion 

Signals and Queries reframes agentic context discovery as a set of tiny, decoupled, recall-first classifiers over LLM-generated exemplars: all the LLM work is spent offline where it amortizes, and per-turn discovery be-comes an embedding lookup. On a 36-policy benchmark judged by the production relevance ranker it dominates embedding-retrieval baselines at every recall level, across two embedding providers, at ∼10 5× lower per-turn cost than per-policy LLM judging. The method ships as part of Parlant 4.0 [4]. Its clearest path forward is better ex-emplars: generation strategies that maximize coverage and hardness, and operator feedback loops that promote real missed conversations into the signal set.
