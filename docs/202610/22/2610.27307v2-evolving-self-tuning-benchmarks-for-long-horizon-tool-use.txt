Abstract 

For AI assistants to move beyond simple chat interfaces and reliably assist users with appli-cations like Gmail, Venmo, and Slack, we need to improve their tool-use capabilities. However, existing tool use benchmarks are still limited and difficult to scale because they rely on hu-man supervision or are too domain-specific. To make data generation scalable, we pro-pose, TURTLE (Tool Use Related Tasks with Long-Horizon Evolution), an Unsuper-vised Environment Design (UED) approach to evolve existing tasks using a proposer, a solver, and a feedback generator. The proposer is re-warded for difficult yet solvable tasks based on the solver’s empirical performance, and the feedback generator summarizes the solver’s execution traces for the proposer to exploit. 

TURTLE produces evolving, self-tuning test suites adaptable to different environment re-quirements, from supporting user simulators and dense document retrieval to interacting across multiple apps. Empirical results on Ap-pWorld, τ -Bench, and AutomationBench using Gemma 4 31B-it show that the evolved tasks target the desired difficulty range (e.g., a 165% increase in the percentage of hard tasks on Ap-pWorld), increase task diversity (in terms of reasoning and simultaneous tool interactions), and elicit a broader range of failure modes than the original tasks. 

1 Introduction 

As Large Language Models (LLMs) become more pervasive and integrated into our everyday lives and workflows, evaluating models across a wide range of scenarios is crucial for safe deployment. Espe-cially for nascent applications like tool-use, evalu-ating models across diverse task suites is important for understanding their capabilities and vulnera-bilities. However, existing tool use benchmarks

are still limited and difficult to scale. Many ex-isting benchmarks are domain-specific (Merzouki et al., 2026; Shepard and Salimans, 2026; Yang et al., 2026), or they rely on humans in the loop for task design and refinement (Trivedi et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2026; Barres et al., 2025). Furthermore, many new datasets are built from scratch and show little to no compatibility with the existing harnesses and testing setups of prior benchmarks, creating engineering overhead when incorporating new tests into an existing evaluation pipeline. Therefore, we need a scalable way to generate new tasks that test the robustness of models without adding to the en-gineering burden. Our key insight is that we do not need to reinvent the wheel . Instead, we can reuse components of existing tasks to build new tasks. Existing tasks give us primitives and steps that we can recombine to produce a vast space of possible new tasks. By searching this space to target gaps in the solver’s performance, we can still generate new tasks efficiently while ensuring task robustness. We propose TURTLE (Tool Use Related Tasks with Long-Horizon Evolution), an Unsuper-vised Environment Design (UED) approach (Den-nis et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2026; Rutherford et al., 2024) based on evolutionary search (Bäck and Schwefel, 1993; Mouret and Clune, 2015). 

TURTLE consists of a proposer, a solver, and a feedback generator, each responsible for new task generation, reward evaluation, and task reflection. Similar to UED and compositional environment design (Gur et al., 2022), we use a Proposer agent to automatically generate new tasks that target the solver’s current level. We implement the proposer using an advanced evolutionary search operator, Al-phaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025). This allows our generation pipeline to incorporate highly capable models as the proposer—even closed-source ones— without requiring access to their weights, since evo-lutionary search hanldes gradient-free optimization. Evolutionary approaches have shown success in environment generation (Faldor et al., 2025), per-sona generation (Paglieri et al., 2026; Chopra et al., 2026), strategy discovery (Wang et al., 2026a), and red-teaming (Samvelyan et al., 2024), but they re-main under-explored for tool-use task generation. 2 Related Work 

Tool-use benchmarks for LLMs. Frontier mod-els still struggle on long-context benchmarks requiring multi-step reasoning, such as Facto-ryBench (Merzouki et al., 2026), Automation-Bench (Shepard and Salimans, 2026), Program-Bench (Yang et al., 2026), and τ -bench (bank-ing) (Shi et al., 2026). Despite their usefulness, 2these benchmarks are difficult to scale because they mainly provide static environments and tasks. Therefore, once models learn to solve problems in these datasets, the benchmarks cannot be reused to discover new tasks or model failures. Methods have been developed to generate new tasks. For example, Tool-R0 (Acikgoz et al., 2026) and GenEnv (Guo et al., 2025b) operate only in text space to generate and verify tasks. However, LLM-simulated environments may suffer from hallucina-tions and inconsistencies (Wang et al., 2024). Alter-natively, Lu et al. (2025); Barres et al. (2025); Yao et al. (2024); Shi et al. (2026); Trivedi et al. (2024); Esfandiarpoor et al. (2025) build tool-interactive sandboxes and database states that more closely resemble real-world applications. However, these are challenging to scale, as they require humans to refine tasks or evaluation environments for ro-bustness. Furthermore, they are often tailored to specific domains (e.g., τ -bench for travel and re-tail (Yao et al., 2024), banking (Shi et al., 2026), and telecom (Barres et al., 2025) are all separate benchmarks). Other methods rely more heavily on prompting for environment design and task dis-covery, such as EnvScaler (Song et al., 2026) and Agent World Model (Wang et al., 2026b). Agent-World (Dong et al., 2026) uses prompts to adjust task difficulty with hand-designed task features, such as the number of reasoning steps and the number of unique tools used per task. Validat-ing task feasibility remains challenging in auto-mated task generation. To guarantee solvability, ToolGrad (Zhou et al., 2026) reverses the task-generation process by first constructing the ground-truth tool-call sequence, then writing a matching natural language instruction for the given sequence. 

TURTLE is different from prior approaches in two ways: (1) evolutionary: using gradient-free evolutionary optimization to incorporate advanced models, and (2) self-tuning: minimizing human oversight in task design and adapting to particular solvers. Our work shares the motivation of Cai et al. (2025); Fang et al. (2025), which use existing tool-use datasets to automate task generation. However, they rely on state-dependency graphs and random walks, which can limit the flexibility of task gen-eration, and building graphs become challenging as the number of tools increases and their inter-dependencies grow complex. TURTLE combines the best of both approaches by leveraging exist-ing benchmarks and verified API implementations, while evolving them to adjust their difficulty based on the solver’s performance. 

LLM-powered evolutionary search. Evolution-ary algorithms are gradient-free optimization in-spired by biological evolution (Bäck and Schwe-fel, 1993; Mouret and Clune, 2015). They itera-tively mutate a population of programs and select candidates based on their fitness scores, while al-lowing fitter individuals to reproduce more often than less fit ones. Incorporating large language models into evolutionary search has proven suc-cessful in scientific and mathematical optimiza-tion (Novikov et al., 2025; ao et al., 2026), red-teaming (Yuan et al., 2026), and social applications using conversational assistants, such as generating diverse user personas (Chopra et al., 2026; Paglieri et al., 2026) and strategy discovery (Wang et al., 2026a). AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025) and OpenEvolve (Rudes et al., 2025) are among the popular frameworks for orchestrating evolutionary search using LLMs. 

3 Method 

TURTLE consists of three key LLM agents: (1) a proposer implemented with AlphaEvolve; (2) a 

solver , which attempts the new task yielding fit-ness scores; and (3) a feedback generator , which identifies the main reason for failure based on the solver’s execution traces and the ground-truth solu-tion. We leverage multi-agent dynamics to propose, evaluate, and refine task mutations according to the solver’s problem-solving competence. After the evolutionary search is run for a fixed number of steps, we add programs with the highest completion rate variance (including ties) per parent task from the original dataset to the final dataset 

D TURTLE . We then apply a filtering step with an LLM judge (Gemini 3.6 Flash) to remove tasks with trivial solver failures, such as formatting mis-matches or paraphrases of the same content, which can be attributed to ambiguous or underspecified instructions (see judge prompt in Appendix Fig. 9). 

3.1 Evolutionary Task Proposer Task structure. A task is presented as a Python program, and the proposer is instructed to act as a programmer. In particular, on AppWorld, we pro-vide minimal instructions to explore how AlphaE-volve discovers task features on its own (proposer instruction prompts are included in Appendix A). 3Depending on the features of the initial task, the proposer is given different items specifying the task. For example, in AppWorld, a task is defined by: 

(1) the initial database state , which specifies the user’s account information, friends list, message history, Amazon product catalogs etc.; (2) the orig-inal task instruction in natural language (e.g., “Give me a comma-separated list of the six most-played EDM songs from my Spotify playlist. "); (3) the ground-truth solution , which shows the se-quence of API calls and their arguments; and (4) optionally, evaluation criteria . If a task changes only part of the database state, the evaluation cri-teria are set automatically to the oracle final state obtained by executing the ground-truth solution in a simulated environment, so the proposer does not need to specify them. If enumerating all state changes is too expensive, the proposer addition-ally specifies which parts of the database must be inspected, and the solver is evaluated only on the changes made to those parts. 

Task mutation. During each evolutionary step, the proposer is given a parent task and five additional tasks sampled from the archive, stochastically cho-sen between the best global and local tasks (highest-scoring per metric). Each task is represented by its instruction text, a corresponding score, and a sum-mary by the feedback generator. The proposer uses this information to propose modifications to the parent task that can maximize the fitness function. Unlike prior work that uses RL post-training to up-date the proposer (Acikgoz et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2025b), we frame task generation as a search prob-lem seeded with a parent task and use AlphaEvolve to discover variants of the original task.

Task verification. Before sending a new task to the solver, we execute the proposed ground-truth solu-tion in the simulated environment to verify there are no system errors. This step filters out tasks with inconsistent state dependencies (e.g., asking to send a message to a friend who is not in the contact list). If the proposer specifies evaluation criteria, we verify that the ground-truth solution achieves a perfect score under those criteria. 

3.2 Solver (Task Difficulty Evaluator) 

A new task is evaluated based on the solver’s em-pirical performance. In particular, we reward tasks that are solvable but not consistently solved per-fectly. Each task is attempted N times, and an attempt scores 1 if the solver fully completes the task; otherwise 0 (partial credits are allowed in Au-tomationBench (Shepard and Salimans, 2026) to avoid reward sparsity on harder domains). To avoid penalizing trivial string mismatches, we addition-ally prompt the LLM judge to evaluate semantic state matching using the original task instruction and the observed versus expected final states. 

How to avoid reward hacking. Some works on multi-agent task generation leverage zero-sum ad-versarial dynamics, where the proposer attempts to minimize the solver’s performance, in order to ensure robustness (Liu et al., 2026). However, in the case of tool-use task generation this objective is badly posed. Adversarial dynamic between the proposer and the solver can lead to reward hacking, since the proposer may be incentivized to gener-ating ambiguous or partially solvable instructions. Therefore, to ensure the tasks are feasible, we check that they are solved perfectly in at least one of the solver attempts. 

Fitness function. Our main objective is to generate long-horizon tasks that can reveal different model failures. To this end, we construct a multi-objective reward function based on the solver’s completion rate. We define c as the solver’s average completion rate across N independent attempts, and L as the average solver’s trajectory length. We propose the following six metrics: • Target difficulty reward: exp {− 4 ln(10)( c −

0.5) 2} how close c is to the target value of 0.5; a bell-curve with a maximum of 1 at c = 0 .5

max {1, L } ∗ exp {− 4 ln(10)( c − 0.5) 2}.To encourage more complex tasks, we reward the proposer for generating longer tasks that the solver is still able to complete. To prevent the pro-poser from trivially extending the ground-truth task length with unnecessary tool calls that do nothing (a reward hacking behavior we initially observed), we base these rewards on the length of the solver’s successful trajectory. During the evolutionary search, the proposer stochastically chooses between global optimality (the highest-scoring task on average across six met-rics) and local optimality (the highest-scoring task for one of the six metrics) to balance exploration and exploitation. When suggesting modifications, the proposer reviews the parent task, along with five sampled in-context examples, and their correspond-ing textual and numeric performance summaries, and is instructed to optimize the average fitness score based on the individual score report. 

3.3 Feedback Generator 

One key advantage of LLM-powered evolutionary search is that the task proposer can use natural-language feedback, in addition to fitness scores, to guide its search for better performing mutations. To leverage this strength, we incorporate a feedback generator LLM into the evolutionary search. For tasks with unsuccessful solver attempts, the feed-back generator outputs a short explanation of what went wrong by comparing the solver’s outcome against the ground-truth solution and the provided task instruction. This feedback is stored in the task archive alongside the solver’s execution traces and provided to the proposer during mutation. 

4 Experiment 

TURTLE is applied to four different benchmarks: AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024), τ -telecom (Bar-res et al., 2025), τ -banking (Shi et al., 2026), and AutomationBench (Shepard and Salimans, 2026). More details of each environment in Appendix B. 

AppWorld is built on 4,571 executable APIs across widely used, everyday software applications. Each task is associated with a particular user and their personal data (e.g., login credentials, transaction history, and product search history), and the agent completes tasks as the user’s personal assistant. 

τ -bench (banking & telecom) requires the model to operate as a customer service agent in a dual-control, multi-turn dialogue setting with a (LLM) simulated user. Certain tool calls can be executed only by the user, so the agent must guide the user through the process step by step. τ -banking is de-signed to test the agent’s document retrieval and long-context reasoning (consisting of 698 docu-ments spanning 21 product categories and financial topics). We test the solver with two of the retrieval conditions presented in Shi et al. (2026): BM25 and golden retrieval. 

AutomationBench uses 47 simulated SaaS applica-tions to model realistic workflows involving tools such as Gmail, Google Sheets, Slack, Salesforce, Zendesk, Jira, and HubSpot. The agent needs to navigate through business objectives and guardrails to solve tasks spanning domains such as Finance, HR, Marketing, Operations, Sales, and Customer Support. Evolutionary search is implemented with Al-phaEvolve (Novikov et al., 2025). Tasks are evolved for 1,000 steps for AppWorld and 500 steps for the other datasets, and every new task is attempted 10 times. TURTLE is equally appli-cable to both closed- and open-weight models. Main empirical results are reported using Gemma 4 31B-it (Team et al., 2026) as both the solver and the feedback generator. We also con-duct ablations with two proprietary models, Gemini 3.6 Flash (Doshi and Google Gemini Team, 2026) and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite (Google Gemini Team, 2026).

5.3 TURTLE enhances diversity 

Another desideratum for task evolution is whether the tasks are modified in semantically and func-tionally meaningful ways beyond making natural-language or numeric perturbations to the original task instructions.

Diversity of simultaneous tool calls. We also ob-serve changes in task requirements (i.e., the number and types of software applications used) after ap-plying TURTLE to AutomationBench. Initially, all 16 original tasks require four apps, but after evolution, 52% of the 23 evolved tasks require five apps and 13% require six. The changes are not limited to the number of apps used. Fig. 7 shows the frequency of app co-occurrences in the origi-nal and evolved tasks. The number of unique app pairs required by each task also increases from 15 to 30 (out of 36 possible unordered pairs), and the evolved tasks now require using new apps like Banboohr, Google Drive, and Stripe. TURTLE makes substantive changes to the underlying tool interactions, rather than modifying instructional styles or function parameters in a templated way.

Broader coverage is desirable, since a robust test suite ideally elicits a variety of failure modes across different task features, rather than exploiting a sin-gle model vulnerability or a source of task difficulty. In the original task heatmap, 11 pairs are missing, whereas in the evolved heatmap, 6 pairs are miss-ing. To further increase the diversity of the tasks (and elicited failures), one could turn this taxonomy into a reward bonus or into guidelines for the task Pro-poser, target each failure mode during the evolu-tionary search, and repeat generation for each seed task and desired failure combination. Despite not using this taxonomy a priori, TURTLE already produces tasks that cover diverse failure categories and feature types, compared to the original bench-marks. 

6 Discussion & Conclusion 

As LLM capabilities advance, we need a scalable task generation approach to understand their cur-rent challenges and ways to improve them. We propose TURTLE , a multi-agent evolutionary approach for generating solvable yet difficult tasks. 

TURTLE shows that hard tasks do not always require building new testing environments or APIs 8Figure 8: Task features versus error categories for the original (left) and evolved (right) tasks. Darker cells indicate higher frequency and cells with 0 errors are highlighted in red. Tasks with 100% completion rates are excluded from the numerator since they do not elicit any failure, but they remain in the denominator. 

from scratch. Instead, existing tasks can be evolved and expanded by modifying instructions and re-combining tool call sequences. 

TURTLE has two clear advantages over exist-ing data generation methods. (1) Evolving: Tasks evolve from the parent seed to target the solver’s competence, rather than converging on a one-size-fits-all static evaluation set. It also uses a gradient-free evolutionary search algorithm, which allows task generation to incorporate closed, frontier mod-els for higher-quality generation. (2) Self-tuning: Unlike prior works that rely on humans for task de-sign and refinement, TURTLE is fully automated, with self-verification in a Dockerized environment and tuning based on the solver’s completion rate to ensure task reliability. We apply TURTLE to popular benchmarks, including AppWorld, τ -bench, and Automation-Bench, and evaluate the difficulty of the generated tasks relative to the original datasets. When the original tasks are too easy, TURTLE yields tasks that are more difficult, leading to a broader range of solver failures. While we mainly use TURTLE for evalua-tion, it can also be integrated into model training by scaling up training data generation with mini-mal human supervision. In particular, Rutherford et al. (2024) show that generating tasks with maxi-mum outcome variance is ideal for learning, since they target the solver’s learning frontier. We can first generate a new curriculum with TURTLE and train the solver using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (Lambert et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025a) or apply filtered behavioral cloning to successful trajectories (Hong et al., 2023). This process of generating new tasks and training the solver can be repeated until the solver’s learning plateaus, without collecting new human data or manually adjusting task difficulty. This can help close the model-improvement loop, from synthetic data generation for evaluation to training. 

Limitations 

TURTLE uses gradient-free evolutionary search, so it can be applied to both open-weight and closed-weight models through API calls. However, the inference cost of proprietary models can be a lim-iting factor. We ran some ablations with different solver models (e.g., Gemini 3.6 Flash and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite) on AppWorld using only a small subset of initial tasks. Our main objective was to show that TURTLE applies to different solver types and is not overfitted to the Gemma solver, and to demonstrate different ways the generated tasks may be used. To make the datasets larger and more useful for future use, the initial task sets would need to be seeded with more parent tasks. 9Ethical Consideration. As LLMs become more pervasive and integrated into our everyday lives and workflows, evaluating their failure modes is crucial for safe deployment and user control over AI sys-tems. Our work contributes to safety by proposing new ways of generating task data to assess model capabilities, while minimizing human supervision, reducing the engineering and data-generation bur-den. However, overreliance on AI systems based on their benchmark performance can be harmful, since benchmarks are designed to assess only certain as-pects of a model’s competence and can become saturated as models are continuously optimized against publicly released datasets.

17 B Appendix: Datasets 

AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024) includes a set of 4,571 executable APIs covering everyday mobile applications like Spotify, Amazon, Venmo, and Gmail. Tasks look like: ‘Send a Venmo request to {a friend’s name}" and “Download a song by {artist name} on Spotify and add it to my playlist", and re-quire the LLM agent to take actions on behalf of the assigned user (i.e., human supervisor). The dataset is split into a larger test set and relatively smaller datasets for training and validation, which include the initial database states, task specifications, and ground truth API sequences. 

Our design choice. To verify whether a task is fully solved, we initialize two identical envi-ronments with a given database state, execute the ground-truth API calls and the solver’s actions in parallel across each copy, and compare their final states. For any key-value pairs in the final state dictionary that do not match between the ground truth and the solver, we run an LLM judge as a fallback state-equivalence check to filter out dif-ferences caused by formatting or exact string mis-matches. 

τ -bench is a tool use benchmark for evaluating the model’s capability as a customer-facing service agent across different domains. In particular, we use telecom (Barres et al., 2025) and banking (Shi et al., 2026). In both domains, since the instruction is provided through the interactions with the user simulator, the user ends the conversation when their goal is satisfied. 

τ - telecom (Barres et al., 2025) evaluates a dual-control, multi-turn dialogue setup, where both the user and the agent are capable of making non-overlapping sets of tool calls. The LLM solver is prompted to act as a customer service agent re-solving the simulated user’s problem. Certain tool calls can only be executed by the user, so the agent must guide the user through step-by-step instruc-tions to achieve the desired tool execution. In our setup, we use the same base model for both roles. The user is given an additional prompt specifying their persona and guidelines on how to respond to requests or information-gathering questions made by the agent. The full dataset features 2,285 tasks across three user persona types alongside different tasks. When evaluating solver attempts against the ground-truth tool calls, we need to check for all environment changes, including those invoked by the solver (agent) as well as the user. 

Our design choice. We allow the solver to con-tinue interacting with the user for maximum 25 turns per task (since the longest task requires 12 actions), and three consecutive errors in a conver-sation result in early terminations with a 0 reward. Following Barres et al. (2025), we only allow atomic solver actions per turn. 

τ - Banking (Shi et al., 2026) is specialized for test-ing an agent’s document retrieval and long-context reasoning capabilities. The agent must navigate large, unstructured knowledge bases covering fi-nancial topics, like checking and savings accounts, credit card terms, annual percentage yields, and internal protocols to resolve users’ transaction dis-putes and product inquiries. The internal database consists of 698 documents spanning 21 product cat-egories, and the dataset has 97 complex problems that require information retrieval, multi-step tool execution, and policy constraint resolution, while navigating multi-turn user dialogue. Each task re-quires on average 18.6 documents and 9.5 tool calls, with 33 maximum number of tool calls. Different configurations are available for retrieval: dense re-trieval with text embeddings, sparse retrieval with BM25, terminal use (knowledge base is exported as files and the agent can use shell commands), and golden (ground truth documents are directly supplied in context, no retrieval required). 

Our design choice. Our solvers are evaluated with BM25 and golden retrieval, and allowed up to 50 max turns and 3 consecutive errors per task. Following Shi et al. (2026), we allow solvers to execute simultaneous tool-calls in a single turn. Our evaluation covers both state-mutating and non-mutating tasks. For state-mutating tasks, we initialize the environment and compare the result-ing state changes between the ground truth exe-cution and the solver’s tool calls. We assign a score of 1 for an exact match and 0 otherwise. For non-mutating tasks (e.g., escalating an issue to a human agent), we evaluate action matching based on whether the agent makes the correct tool with the correct parameters. 

AutomationBench (Shepard and Salimans, 2026) is a complex domain built on 47 simulated SaaS applications, such as Gmail, Google Sheets, Slack, Salesforce, Zendesk, Jira, and HubSpot. The dataset consists of 657 tasks spanning Finance, HR, Marketing, Operations, Sales, and Support with a max turn of 50 steps. The agent is tested on automating SaaS workflows while navigating business objectives and guardrails. The agent is 18 also tested on autonomous API discovery, where it needs to figure out which apps are relevant and which REST API endpoints to execute. Unlike the other domains, evaluations with AutomationBench specify guardrails and target changes to the final states after tool-call execution. The solver’s goal is to match all target changes executed by the ground truth model and avoid any changes to the guardrails (violations nullify the successful changes).

When evaluating interaction requirements, consider the difference between weak and strong interactions: - Weak Interaction Requirement: The task could theoretically be solved in one shot if the agent had perfect knowledge of the APIs (e.g., direct filtering and passing IDs).
