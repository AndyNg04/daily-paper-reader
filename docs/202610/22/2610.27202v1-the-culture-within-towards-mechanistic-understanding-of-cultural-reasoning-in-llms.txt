Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly operate across culturally diverse settings, yet existing evaluations largely measure cultural behavior at the output level. We introduce CultureLens, a reference-grounded benchmark and mechanistic study of how LLMs use cul-tural information. CultureLens contains 9,261 scenarios across 75 countries and systemat-ically varies country, value, true-norm, and contradictory-norm evidence. Across Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-3-12B, and Qwen-3.5-27B, stating a contradictory norm lowers aligned an-swers by 32–47 percentage points, even when the model answers correctly from the coun-try alone. Mechanistically, models default to Anglophone norms when no country is given. The failure under a contradictory norm is not one of missing knowledge, as blocking the norm inside the model restores the correct an-swer in most cases. Models accept a stated norm according to how it is presented and not whether it is true, which leaves them most ex-posed to errors in retrieved content. Supervised fine-tuning with contradictory-norm examples raises aligned answers under a contradictory norm to 85–93%, including on unseen coun-tries, at a small cost under a true norm. 

1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed across countries and communities with different social expectations, values, and norms. A response that seems reasonable in one cultural context may be inappropriate or unhelpful in an-other. Recent work shows that LLMs exhibit un-even cultural knowledge and behavior across coun-tries, languages, and value systems (DURMUS et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Myung et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Other work goes beyond factual cultural knowledge to whether models adapt their judgments and responses when culturally relevant context is provided (Shi et al., 2024; Rao et al., 2025). Yet they evaluate only model outputs. Cultural competence involves not only knowl-edge of cultural contexts, but also the ability to interpret and act appropriately within them (Dear-dorff, 2006). For LLMs, this raises the question of whether cultural knowledge that is internally avail-able guides behavior, since a model may encode a norm without relying on it (Elazar et al., 2021). In deployment, models rarely answer from the coun-try alone. Prompts also carry cultural claims from users and retrieved documents (Lewis et al., 2020), which may be outdated, stereotyped, or wrong (Pan et al., 2023), and models often defer to stated context over their own knowledge (Longpre et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2024) and agree with users’ be-liefs (Sharma et al., 2024). A stated norm that contradicts what the model knows about a country thus tests whether its cultural knowledge survives a conflicting claim. A wrong answer may mean that the knowledge was absent, or that it was present and overridden, which behavioral evaluation can-not distinguish. Recent mechanistic work identifies cultural information in activations, neurons, and sparse features (Yu et al., 2026; Yamamoto et al., 2026; Zou et al., 2026), motivating a study of how these relate to culturally situated behavior. We introduce CultureLens, a reference-grounded benchmark and mechanistic study of how LLMs use cultural information. CultureLens contains 9,261 scenarios across 75 countries and varies coun-try, value, true-norm, and contradictory-norm ev-idence, testing what a model knows, whether it uses correct evidence, and whether it can tell cor-rect evidence from incorrect, in both a two-option choice and open-ended generation. Using probes and causal interventions on the stated norm, we then study why models abandon a correct country-based answer under a contradictory norm. 2 Related Work 

Prior work evaluates cultural knowledge, values, and commonsense in LLMs across countries, lan-guages, and populations (DURMUS et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Myung et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024; Chiu et al., 2025). More recent work moves beyond cultural knowledge toward whether models adapt their judgments and responses to culturally relevant context (Shi et al., 2024; Rao et al., 2025; Alhassoun et al., 2025; He et al., 2026). In partic-ular, NormAd varies context from abstract values to explicit norms, while Saudi-Alignment and Cul-Shield highlight gaps between recognizing cultural information and applying it in generated behavior. CultureLens builds on these directions by evalu-ating recognition and enactment on matched sce-narios while independently varying country, value, reference-norm, and contradictory-norm evidence. A growing line of work studies how cultural information is represented within LLMs. Prior studies identify cultural structure across layers, culture-associated neurons, and sparse features (Yu et al., 2026; Namazifard and Galke Poech, 2025; Yamamoto et al., 2026; Zou et al., 2026). These works show that cultural information can be lo-calized, decoded, and in some cases causally ma-nipulated. However, decodability alone does not establish that represented information is used be-haviorally (Elazar et al., 2021). We therefore con-nect probing with causal interventions on judged behavior, distinguishing information that is acces-sible from information that influences decisions. Existing cultural adaptation methods use cultur-ally targeted knowledge, instructions, interactions, or specialized adapters to modify model behavior (Shi et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Pham et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2026). Representation-and mechanism-guided approaches use internal structure to determine how or where adaptation is applied (Wu et al., 2024; Cho et al., 2025; Abouza-hir et al., 2026). We instead apply supervised fine-tuning on reference answers and test whether it improves resistance to contradictory norms while preserving responsiveness to true ones. 

3 Methodology 

3.1 CultureLens: Benchmark Design 

We construct CultureLens from the Cultural At-las profiles for 75 countries, 1 which describe com-mon social norms and behaviors. We treat these descriptions as cultural preferences rather than uni-versal truths, since variation exists within countries and across individuals (Taras et al., 2016). All content is generated with the help of DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026) in four stages. First, the model extracts atomic behavioral norms from each section of a country’s profile. Second, for each norm it writes a scenario that instantiates the behavioral decision without naming the coun-try, together with independently controllable coun-try, true-norm, and contradictory-norm evidence. Third, it writes two response options, one supported by the true norm and one by the contradictory norm. Fourth, it assigns each norm zero to three refined Schwartz values, such as humility , tradition , and 

benevolence–caring (Schwartz, 1992), and writes the corresponding value evidence. Table 1 presents two records. CultureLens contains 9,261 records including 3,495 with value evidence. Appendix A lists the countries, Appendix B the Schwartz values, and Appendix C.1 the system prompts.

3.2 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate Llama-3.1-8B (Meta), Gemma-3-12B (Gemma Team), and Qwen-3.5-27B (Qwen Team) with deterministic decoding in two settings. In the 

constrained setting models choose between two behavioral options, and in the unconstrained set-ting they generate advice freely. Each record is evaluated under eight evidence conditions. No-evidence gives only the situation and question. Six conditions add country, value, or true-norm evi-dence, alone or combined, namely country-only ,

value-only , norm-only , country-value , country-norm , and country-value-norm . Finally, country-contradictory-norm pairs the country with a con-tradictory norm, testing whether models follow the stated claim or what they know about it. In the constrained setting, we report accuracy against the option supported by the true norm. In the unconstrained setting, two LLM judges, Gemma-4-31B (Gemma Team, 2026) and Qwen-3.6-27B (Qwen Team), label each response as 

aligned , partially aligned , or misaligned with the documented norm, and we report the percentage labeled aligned, averaged over both. Each judge sees the country, the documented norm, any values, the situation, the question, and the response, but never the evidence condition or the contradictory norm, so its label cannot depend on what the model was told. Appendix C.2 gives the judge prompt. 

Human Evaluation. We validate the two LLM judges against three human annotators. Each la-beled 35 model responses, 20 of them shared, with the same labels and information given to the judges. Human labels agree with the Gemma judge on 85.7% of the 105 annotations and with the Qwen judge on 86.7%, with Cohen’s κ of 0.72 and 0.73. The judges are the stricter raters, as annotators agree with 96% of the responses a judge labels aligned, so the alignment rates we report are con-servative. The two judges agree with each other on 94.4% of all responses in the behavioral evaluation, with κ = 0 .88 . Appendix D.2 gives further details.

Value evidence tells the model what matters in a situation, but not what to do. In the constrained set-ting models therefore pick the right option, with an accuracy of 81.6%–94.1%, yet in the unconstrained setting they often fail to produce it. On the 3,495 records with value evidence, a value alone gives 64.3%, 60.8%, and 75.1% unconstrained align-ment, no better overall than the 62.9%, 69.4%, and 77.4% from the country alone. The two work best together, as giving both raises alignment by 8.4– 13.0 points over the country alone. The country brings the model to the right culture, and the value tells it which aspect of the situation to address. Norm evidence is more actionable because it states the behavior, yet the country still matters. A true norm alone gives 76.0%, 77.4%, and 82.4% alignment for Llama, Gemma, and Qwen, and adding the country raises this to 92.6%, 93.1%, and 95.5%, with a gain in at least 74 of the 75 coun-tries. Almost all of this gain comes from norms that go against what the model answers with no evi-dence. On non-Anglophone records, models follow such a norm in only 61–67% without the country and 88–92% with it. The country thus legitimizes a norm that departs from the model’s default. All three signals together give the highest alignment, though values add little beyond the norm. This legitimizing effect turns against the model when the stated norm is wrong. A contradictory norm reduces alignment to 17.6%, 16.2%, and 42.9% for Llama, Gemma, and Qwen, lower than with no evidence at all in every model. Constrained accuracy falls to 36.0%, 29.1%, and 44.0%, be-low the 50% expected from guessing, so models actively choose the option the contradictory norm supports. Models hold out somewhat more for countries they know better, as alignment under a contradictory norm rises with country-only align-ment across countries, with ρ = .31 , .49 , and .56 .

5 Mechanistic Analysis 

Appendix F extends each analysis in this section with formal definitions and results at every layer. 

5.1 How the Country is Represented Setup. We ask how a model represents a country before any norm is stated, and whether that repre-sentation explains when naming the country helps. We extract the state at the answer start token under the no-evidence and country-only prompts, at lay-ers 16, 26, and 36 for Llama, Gemma, and Qwen, fixed in an exploratory run on separate records. The shift of a record is the difference between the two states, with size relative to the no-evidence 

state. We average shift size over each of the 75 countries and compute its Spearman correlation with the gain in aligned answers from naming the country. A country’s vector is the mean state of its records, and distances between countries are cosine distances between these vectors. We compare them with World Values Survey (Haerpfer et al., 2022) distances for the 43 countries it covers. 

Results & Discussion. Figure 1 shows that a model given no country already behaves as if it were given the United States. The less naming a country moves the state, the closer that country is to what the model assumes by default. Naming the United States moves the state least in all three mod-els, and the other Anglophone countries also move less than most. This default has a behavioral cost, as the further a country’s state must move when it is named, the more naming it helps, with a corre-lation of .56 for Llama, .49 for Gemma, and .65 for Qwen that remains at .46, .40, and .59 without the Anglophone countries. 5.2 When the Model Decides Setup. We ask whether a model has already de-cided how to respond to a contradictory norm be-fore it begins to answer. We extract the residual-stream state at the answer start token for each record and layer. A response is resisting when the unconstrained answer to the country-contradictory-norm prompt is judged aligned with the docu-mented norm. A ridge probe is trained to pre-dict resistance from the state, using five-fold cross-validation, with regularization strength selected on a development fold and all scores computed on held-out records. We report AUROC, ranking probe scores within each country so that country differences cannot produce the result, with 95% bootstrap intervals. As baselines, we use a bag-of-words probe on the prompt text and the same probe on states from the no-evidence and country-only 

prompts. We also probe the contradictory norm without the country. Finally, we take the direction along which the state under the contradictory norm differs from the state under the true norm for the same country, without the judged answers, and test whether this conflict signal predicts resistance. 

Results & Discussion. Figure 2 shows that resis-tance is predictable before any answer token is pro-duced. At the reported layer, a linear probe on the full prompt reaches an AUROC of .791 for Llama, .841 for Gemma, and .917 for Qwen. Two baselines show that this reflects how the model processed the norm. A bag-of-words probe on the same prompt scores .139, .158, and .234 lower, and the same linear probe on the no-evidence and country-only 

states stays within about .03 of it. Restricting the test to records the model answers correctly from the country alone leaves the AUROC nearly un-changed at .779, .831, and .915, so the probe is not only reading whether the model knows the norm. The contradictory norm without the country already raises the AUROC to .747–.844, and adding the country context adds only .045, .051, and .073, with 95% intervals excluding zero. The response thus depends mostly on the stated norm and little on what the model knows about the country, and the country contributes most in Qwen, the model that resists most. A signal built without behav-ioral labels gives the same picture. The part of the state that separates a contradictory norm from a true one only when the country is named predicts resistance at an AUROC of .575 in Llama, .575 in Gemma, and .731 in Qwen, and across the 75 countries it correlates with the resistance rate at .28, .31, and .77. The models therefore register some conflict between the norm and the country, but only Qwen does so strongly enough to act on it. The country’s contribution emerges at mid-depth. The full-prompt and norm-only curves are indis-tinguishable through the early layers and separate at layers 10–14 of 32 in Llama, 18–22 of 48 in Gemma, and 28–34 of 64 in Qwen.

Results & Discussion. Figure 3 shows that a stated norm reaches the answer through a narrow mid-depth route, and that this route does not distin-guish a true norm from a contradictory one. Block-ing the norm only in layers 12–15 of Llama or 18–23 of Gemma recovers 73% and 86% of the effect of blocking it in every layer, whereas a later band and the two control spans do not raise re-sistance. In Llama the route is narrower still, as eight of the band’s 128 heads recover nearly the whole band’s effect, at 28.5 against 29.0 points. In Gemma eight selected heads yield only 9.2, so the route is more distributed. Blocking the norm in every layer raises resistance from 18.0% to 58.0% in Llama and 14.2% to 66.5% in Gemma, match-ing the 58.8% and 68.0% reached when the norm is never stated, so the intervention removes only the norm. Norm erasure agrees and extends this to Qwen. Erasing the norm before the band raises resistance by 43.0 points in Llama, 54.0 in Gemma, and 29.5 in Qwen, while erasing after it recovers only 8.2, 7.5, and 7.5. Repeating the erasure at each layer shows that a true norm is read at the same layers as a contradictory one in all models. A model thus takes in a stated norm the same way whether or not it is correct, and does little to check it against what it knows about the country. These are also the layers where resistance became pre-dictable, so the response is decided where the norm is read. 

5.4 Why Knowing the Country is Not Enough Setup. We ask whether a model that follows a contradictory norm has lost what it knows about the country, or keeps that knowledge and is overridden. We split the intervention sample by the model’s answer to the country-only prompt. Records it answers correctly are those where it knows the norm, and the rest are those where it does not. For each group, we measure resistance on the country-contradictory-norm prompt when it is untouched, when the norm is blocked in the mid-depth band, and when it is blocked in all layers. If the knowl-edge is kept, blocking the norm should restore the correct answer in the first group and not in the sec-ond. This covers Llama and Gemma, as knockout does not apply to Qwen. For all three models, we also fit a logistic regression on the full benchmark that predicts resistance from whether the model answers the record correctly under the no-evidence ,

country-only , norm-only , and country-norm con-ditions, together with the record’s category and whether it has value evidence. We report odds ra-tios with 95% intervals from resampling countries.

On records a model answers correctly from the country alone, it resists the contradictory norm only 25.5% of the time in Llama and 17.6% in Gemma, abandoning an answer it can produce in most such cases. Block-ing the norm in all layers raises resistance on these records to 88.9% and 85.7%, and blocking it only in the mid-depth band already recovers 66.0% and 73.2%. 5.5 What Determines Compliance Setup. We ask what determines how far a model follows a stated norm, given that its own knowledge of the country does not. We test this on the answers models write, in three ways. First, we evaluate the contradictory norm without the country context on all 9,261 records. If the country helped the model check the norm, resistance should be higher when it is named. Second, on the intervention sample, we change only how the norm is presented, attribut-ing it to a guide to local customs or to hearsay, following it with the user’s doubt, or placing it be-fore the country context. Third, a judge model that sees the contradictory norm and the answer, but never the documented norm, codes on all records whether the answer follows the norm, whether it expresses doubt, and whether it restates the norm as a fact. Results & Discussion. Table 4 shows that com-pliance follows the authority the prompt gives a norm, and not whether the norm matches what the model knows. Naming the country does not help. Without it, Llama resists 28.4% of the time and Gemma 25.7%, and adding it lowers this by 10.6 and 8.6 points while leaving Qwen unchanged. The country thus attaches the norm to a place the model recognizes and makes it more credible. The an-swers themselves show this. Llama restates the contradictory norm as a fact in 75.3% of answers, Gemma in 85.2%, and Qwen in 58.2%, and 97.3%, 98.4%, and 93.1% present their advice as the cus-tom of the named country. Among answers not judged aligned, 83.1%, 84.9%, and 82.0% follow the norm with no reservation, and only 4.7%, 7.9%, and 14.0% express any doubt. The models do not weigh the norm against the country, and instead attribute the norm to it. Presentation matters more than any other factor. With its wording unchanged, attributing the norm to a guide reduces resistance to 6.5%, 7.5%, and 5.0%, and following it with the user’s doubt raises it to 43.2%, 59.5%, and 91.0%, with hearsay in between. Given a cited source no model resists, so Qwen’s advantage on the benchmark reflects the lower weight it gives an unattributed claim and not a general robustness. Or-der matters as well, as placing the norm before the country context raises resistance by 20.8, 28.2, and 32.5 points. These results have a practical conse-quence. 6 Mitigation via Fine-Tuning 

Appendix G extends this section with training de-tails, results by judge, and layer-wise analysis. 

6.1 Effect on Behavior Setup. We ask whether fine-tuning can improve what a model answers from the country alone, and make it resist a contradictory norm without los-ing what it gains from a true one. We hold out eight regionally diverse countries, which give 1,122 unseen-country test records, and split the other 67 into 5,984 training, 480 validation, and 1,675 test records. DeepSeek-V4-Pro acts as a teacher and writes a reference answer for each training record, given the prompt and the record’s docu-mented norm, value evidence, and reference op-tion. SFT trains on the country-only prompt with this answer as the target. SFT + CF also trains on the country-value , country-norm , and country-contradictory-norm prompts, where the target for a contradictory norm corrects it before answering. 

Results & Discussion. Table 5 shows that teach-ing a model more about a country makes it less able to resist a contradictory norm. SFT raises country-only alignment on seen countries by 11.8 points in Llama, 10.0 in Gemma, and 4.5 in Qwen, yet resistance falls to 5.7%, 4.0%, and 15.3%, as the model learns to follow what the prompt states. This matches the mechanistic analysis, which located the failure in how a stated norm is weighed and not in what the model knows. Adding contradictory-norm examples changes that weighing. SFT + CF raises resistance to 85.4%, 87.6%, and 92.4% while keeping most of the country-only gain, and reaches the same level on countries never seen in training, so the model learns a way of handling stated norms and not facts about particular countries. The cost is under a true norm, where alignment falls 5 to 7 points below the untuned model. Fine-tuning also leaves a ceiling in place, as alignment from the country alone stays below 79% for every model and method. Resistance can thus be trained, whereas knowledge of a country’s norms remains the limit. 

6.2 Sources of the Effect Setup. We ask what changes inside a model when it learns to resist a contradictory norm, using two measures from the mechanistic analysis on the un-tuned model, SFT, and SFT + CF. The first is how well the model tells a contradictory norm from a true one. We compute the conflict signal of Sec-tion 5 on the state of each model and report the AUROC at the best layer with which it separates contradictory from true norms. The second is how much the answer depends on reading the stated norm. We erase the norm after the first layer and report the change in the margin on 400 test records. 

Results & Discussion. SFT + CF changes two things inside the model that plain SFT largely does not. The conflict signal separates contradictory from true norms with an AUROC of .69, .67, and .85 in the untuned Llama, Gemma, and Qwen, and of .73, .72, and .87 after SFT. With SFT + CF it rises to .95, .94, and .98, so the model now tells the two apart. Erasing a contradictory norm moves the margin by 12.7, 20.0, and 8.6 in the untuned models and by only 7.1, 7.2, and 4.7 after SFT + CF. Resistance thus comes from registering the conflict and giving the stated norm less weight, which also explains the small loss under a true norm in Table 5. 8Limitations 

CultureLens draws its norms from a single source, the Cultural Atlas, and treats the country as the unit of culture, so it does not capture variation within a country across regions, generations, or commu-nities. All prompts are in English, which lets us compare 75 countries under one design but leaves open whether a model handles the same norms dif-ferently in the languages of those countries. We study three open-weight models of 8 to 27 billion parameters, because the mechanistic analysis and fine-tuning require access to internal states and weights, and larger or proprietary models may re-sist a contradictory norm more often. Answers are scored by two LLM judges, which we vali-date against human annotation, yet a judge can still miss subtle cases. The mechanistic analysis reads the state at one token position and uses attention knockout only for Llama and Gemma, since the architecture of Qwen does not allow it, and we cover Qwen with norm erasure instead. Finally, fine-tuning with contradictory norms also makes a model less willing to follow a genuine request from a person in the scenario it is given. 

Ethical Considerations 

CultureLens describes cultural norms at the level of a country, and such descriptions can be read as stereotypes if they are taken as rules that every per-son follows. We present each norm as a common expectation drawn from the Cultural Atlas, and we do not claim that it holds for all members of a coun-try. The contradictory norms in the benchmark are false by design. They are released so that the fail-ure we study can be measured and reduced, and they must not be used as cultural guidance or to train a model to state them as facts. The benchmark contains no personal data. Our findings also carry a risk in deployment. A model that follows any norm it is told can spread a false claim about a culture, while a model trained to resist can dismiss a person who describes a real custom of their own commu-nity. Systems built on this work should therefore treat its answers about a culture as fallible. We release the benchmark and adapters for research.

With-out a norm, a large share of the responses that are not aligned are partially aligned, at 17.4–26.5% of all responses under no-evidence and 9.3–18.2% under country-only . Once a norm is stated the partial share shrinks, and under a contradictory norm most responses are misaligned outright, so models rarely hedge between the stated norm and the documented one. The two failures therefore differ in kind. With-out a norm a model is incomplete, whereas under a contradictory norm it is confidently wrong. 

Recognizing without producing. The format gap is not due to different records being easy. Un-der country-only , among the records a model an-swers correctly in the constrained format, its uncon-strained response is aligned for only 64.7%, 69.3%, and 81.1% for Llama, Gemma, and Qwen. A cor-rect choice with an unaligned response occurs in 26.7%, 25.7%, and 16.5% of all records, and the reverse in only 9.3%, 5.2%, and 3.7%. Models therefore often recognize the documented behav-ior without producing it, so multiple-choice scores overstate open-ended behavior in practice. 

What value evidence adds. Table 10 reports the analysis of the 3,495 records with value evi-dence. Adding the value to the country turns just over half of partially aligned responses into aligned ones for Llama and Gemma and 71.7% for Qwen, against 41.4–53.8% of misaligned ones, while re-sponses that were already aligned stay aligned in more than 90% of records. Partially aligned re-sponses account for 65–72% of the net gain under the Gemma judge and for 41–56% under the Qwen judge, which assigns the partial label less often. Both judges therefore attribute a large part of the gain to responses that were already heading in the right direction, although they disagree on whether it is the larger part. Two further breakdowns qual-ify this result. Because value evidence is written from the norm, it could restate the norm in other words. In 2,208 of the 3,495 records the value sentence shares no content word with the norm, and the gain there is still 11.1, 6.6, and 9.4 points. The gain rises to 12.7–21.7 points when two or more content words are shared, so wording overlap inflates the effect without accounting for it. The gain also depends on which value is stated. It is smallest for interpersonal conformity, at 2.1–6.2 points, a value close to the politeness that models recommend by default, and largest for dominance, dependability, and tradition, at 10.6–23.3 points, which depart furthest from that default. A specific default. We first read 45 no-evidence 

responses for records from other countries that both judges find misaligned. All but one recommend a concrete behavior that conflicts with the docu-mented one, and the remaining response is generic. The recommended behavior is consistently that of direct, punctual, and egalitarian professional eti-quette. The man extends his hand first, a silence in a meeting is filled, a colleague is addressed by first name, a fifteen-minute delay is called unpro-fessional, and every opinion is given equal weight regardless of age. The default the country cue must overcome is therefore a definite set of practices. 

Relevant but unfocused country advice. Par-tially aligned country-only responses fail differ-ently. In 36 of the 60 sampled responses the advice is accurate about the country and leaves out the behavior the norm concerns, often a fine-grained practice such as a gesture, a phrase, or a step in a ritual. Asked how someone in Hungary should spend a free weekend, Gemma lists thermal baths, museums, and walks and does not mention family, although the documented norm is that free time revolves around it. In 14 responses the documented behavior appears as one option among several and the choice is left to the individual, a form con-centrated in norms about family obligations and hierarchy. Only 8 recommend a different behavior. 

Value evidence as a selector. When the value is added to the same prompt, the response is reorga-nized around it. In the Hungary example Gemma opens with visiting family as the highest priority. Asked who should represent a company in Sudan, Llama lists language skills and industry expertise from the country alone and recommends a senior and respected figure once told that authority and status matter. Responses that had offered the doc-umented behavior as an option now recommend it. The value does not supply the behavior. It in-dicates which of the things the model associates with the country the response should be about, and it resolves the hesitation between the custom and personal choice in favor of the custom. 

True norms without a country. When a true norm differs from the default and no country is given, responses that do not follow it take three forms, and the models favor different ones. Qwen mostly rejects the norm explicitly, in 12 of 22 sam-pled responses. It describes the Saudi beckoning gesture as incorrect and rude and calls a norm about meetings running late incorrect. Llama mostly gives the default advice without disputing the norm, in 12 of 22, as when it recommends an arm’s length of personal space after being told that people stand close. Gemma mostly acknowledges the norm and then weakens it, in 13 of 22, by presenting it as a traditional view to be balanced against modern practice or personal preference, although it also calls chin pointing a false premise. All three mod-els describe norms about gender roles as outdated stereotypes. The keyword search agrees with the or-dering. Wording such as “incorrect”, “outdated”, or “misconception” appears in 45.2% of the responses from Qwen that neither judge finds aligned, 25.3% of those from Gemma, and 12.5% of those from Llama, against 3–6% of responses to no-evidence .

The country as attribution. With the country added, the same models accept the norms they re-jected. Gemma answers that a person in Samoa would point with the chin, and Qwen opens with “You are absolutely correct” and elaborates on the Saudi gesture. The norm has not changed. The country gives the model something to attribute it to, so the statement is read as a description of a place and no longer as a general claim that conflicts with what the model believes to be true.

G Extended Mitigation via Fine-Tuning 

G.1 Data Splits and Training Details 

We hold out eight countries from training and model selection, which are Argentina, Egypt, Ethiopia, Germany, India, Indonesia, Japan, and Russia. They cover Africa, the Americas, Asia, and Europe, and their 1,122 records form the unseen-country test set. From the other 67 countries we take 25 records per country for the seen-country test set, 1,675 in total, and 10 records from each of the 48 countries with at least 100 records for vali-dation, 480 in total. The remaining 5,984 records are used for training. DeepSeek-V4-Pro writes the target answer for each training record under each condition, given the prompt and the documented norm, value evidence, and reference option of the record, none of which the trained model sees. We train LoRA adapters of rank 16 with a scaling fac-tor of 32 and a dropout of 0.05 on the attention and feed-forward projections of every layer. Training runs for three epochs with AdamW, a learning rate of 10 −4, a batch of 16 examples, a linear warm-up over the first 5% of steps, and cosine decay. We keep the checkpoint with the lowest loss on the validation records, so the unseen countries play no part in model selection. Answers are generated with greedy decoding and judged as in Section 4. 

G.2 Results by Judge Setup. We ask whether the effects of fine-tuning in Table 5 depend on the judge. That table aver-ages the two judges, so a result could come from one judge alone. You are a teacher model. You write the gold-standard answer that a student model should give to a question about a culturally grounded situation, when a person in the situation has asked for something other than what local custom would usually prescribe. That sentence is a personal request. It makes no claim about what is customary in the country, and it is true: the person really has asked for this. The private source holds the authoritative material from the locked CultureLens record. It is given to you so that the answer is correct. The student never sees it. WHAT YOU RECEIVE - student_prompt: the complete prompt the student must answer. - private_source.request: the sentence in student_prompt that reports what the person has asked for. - private_source.speaker: who made the request; may be null. - private_source.norm: the documented cultural norm for that country, which describes what people there usually expect when no one has asked otherwise. - private_source.values: the value labels assigned to the norm (refined Schwartz values, e.g. face, tradition, conformity_rules); may be empty.
