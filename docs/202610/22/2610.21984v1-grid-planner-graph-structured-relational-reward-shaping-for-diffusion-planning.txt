## Abstract 

Autonomous driving planning requires selecting trajectories that remain safe, rule-compliant, and comfortable under complex multi-agent interactions, yet robust closed-loop decision making in planning-critical scenes remains challenging. Existing diffusion- and RL-based planners often rely on generic rewards and use relational structure mainly for representation, limiting how planning-critical rollouts shape policy improvement. We present GRID-Planner, a graph–reward integrated diffusion planning framework that aligns generative planning with structured relational signals. Specifically, GRID-Planner combines graph-aware importance weighting, a time-indexed relational reward surrogate, and an advantage-weighted diffusion objective with on-policy self-distillation, forming a rollout-return-advantage-update loop without explicit likelihoods. Exper-iments on nuPlan and interPlan show that, compared with the diffusion-only baseline, GRID-Planner reduces collision rate, lane deviation, and jerk by 35.8%, 60.8%, and 27.4%, respectively, and improves the planning score by 3.4%. ## 1 Introduction 

Autonomous driving planning requires selecting ego trajectories that are safe, rule-compliant, and comfortable under complex multi-agent interactions [2, 6, 8, 11, 13]. Despite rapid progress in learning-based planning, robust closed-loop decision making in dense ur-ban scenes remains challenging [2, 6, 13]. A core challenge is that planning performance depends not only on average-case trajec-tory realism but on learning from a small set of planning-critical 

trajectories—those arising in highly interactive, safety-sensitive, and rule-constrained scenarios such as merging, yielding, and in-tersection conflicts [2, 6, 13]. In practice, however, most training trajectories are semantically flat, weakly constrained, and low-conflict, contributing limited signals to the decision boundaries that matter most for planning [6]. This long-tail structure makes pure imitation learning insufficient, since abundant easy trajectories dominate supervision, while naive reinforcement learning is also inadequate because generic rewards are difficult to design stably for complex multi-agent interactions. These challenges motivate graph-structured relational modeling to prioritize planning-critical trajectories and support planning-oriented policy improvement. Existing methods improve planning from different angles, but the core issue remains unresolved. Strong imitation-based planners, such as UrbanDriver [21], PlanTF [5], and PLUTO [4], optimize arXiv:2610.21984v1 [cs.RO] 15 Oct 2026 MM ’26, November 10–14, 2026, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Zhao Yang, Zezhong Qian, Yinan Shi, Longjun Liu, and Yawei Jueluo 

similarity to expert behavior, allowing abundant easy trajectories to dominate training and under-emphasizing safety-critical and rule-sensitive cases. Interaction-centric methods, such as Game-Former [10], improve multi-agent relation modeling, but mainly at the representation level, without explicitly using relational struc-ture to determine trajectory value or optimization priority. Diffusion-based planners, such as Diffusion Planner [31], provide flexible multi-modal trajectory generation, while reward-guided variants such as Diffusion-ES [28] demonstrate the promise of reward-driven optimization. However, existing approaches still rely either on be-havior cloning objectives, which suffer from compounding errors and exposure bias in closed-loop inference, or on generic reward-driven optimization, which does not explicitly encode planning-oriented relational semantics. This leaves open how relational se-mantics should shape both the training distribution and the opti-mization objective, rather than serving only as scene representa-tions or sampling-time guidance. To address this gap, we propose GRID-Planner , a Graph-Reward 

Integrated Diffusion planner that uses graph-structured scene se-mantics to directly shape the optimization signal of generative planning. Rather than treating graph structure as just an input representation, we use it to define both trajectory importance and planning-oriented return. Our framework answers three questions left open by prior planners: which trajectories should matter more during training, how their quality should be evaluated for plan-ning, and how relational signals should reshape diffusion-based policy learning. Specifically, our framework contains three tightly coupled components: ❶ Graph-Aware Importance Weighting ,which upweights trajectories from complex, high-risk scenes us-ing scene-interaction centrality and collision-risk cues; ❷ Rela-tional Reward Surrogate (RRS), which evaluates candidate roll-outs through time-indexed ego-centric scene graphs, producing a dense trajectory-level signal with smooth bounded penalties over safety, lane/route consistency, and comfort; and ❸ Graph-Reward Integrated Diffusion Objective , which incorporates structured returns into a low-variance advantage-weighted diffusion objec-tive, coupled with an advantage-gated on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) mechanism. The resulting advantage reweights diffusion denoising and gates OPSD teacher selection and updates, forming a rollout–return–advantage–update loop in which graph-reward signals directly drive policy improvement. The main contributions of this work are: 

• We propose GRID-Planner , a unified graph-reward inte-grated diffusion framework that redefines how scene graphs are used in autonomous driving planning: rather than serv-ing only as input representations, they directly shape opti-mization priority and trajectory value for closed-loop deci-sion making. 

• We introduce a planning-oriented relational learning mechanism , instantiated by Graph-Aware Importance Weighting and a Relational Reward Surrogate , which together focus training on planning-critical trajectories and score candidate rollouts using safety, lane/route consistency, and comfort criteria. 

• We develop a unified on-policy diffusion objective that combines advantage-weighted diffusion with advantage-gated self-distillation to support stable on-policy refinement and consolidate high-return behaviors, yielding consistent gains over strong baselines on nuPlan and interPlan. 

## 2 Related Work 

Imitation-Based and Interaction-Centric Planning. A major line of work learns autonomous driving policies from expert demon-strations [2, 6, 8, 11, 12, 20, 27]. UrbanDriver [21], PlanTF [5], and PLUTO [4] achieve strong closed-loop performance through care-fully designed imitation objectives, while UniAD [8], VAD [14], VADv2 [3], SparseDrive [23], DTPP [9], GameFormer [10], Inter-Fuser [22], and Causal-Planner [29] improve scene representation and interaction modeling. However, these approaches mainly op-timize expert imitation or relational representations, without ex-plicitly using scene relations to determine trajectory importance or planner-oriented value. GRID-Planner instead uses graph structure to prioritize planning-critical trajectories and construct relational returns over safety, lane/route consistency, and comfort. 

Generative Planning with Diffusion. Generative planners model future trajectories as samples from expressive distributions, sup-porting multi-modal driving behaviors [15, 25]. GenAD [30], Dif-fusion Planner [31], and Diffusion-ES [28] explore latent gener-ation, diffusion-based closed-loop planning, and reward-guided sampling, respectively. Flow Planner [24], DiffusionDrive [16], and BridgeDrive [17] further improve interactive modeling and diffusion-policy design. These methods primarily focus on trajectory genera-tion or sampling-time guidance, whereas GRID-Planner incorpo-rates structured relational signals into training through trajectory prioritization, relational evaluation, and diffusion-policy refine-ment. 

RL-Based Policy Improvement for Generative Planning. Re-inforcement learning has been combined with diffusion policies for reward-driven optimization. DDPO [1], advantage-weighted regression methods [18, 19], and diffusion policies [26] adapt policy-improvement principles to expressive generative models. However, they generally rely on generic scalar rewards rather than planning-oriented relational semantics. GRID-Planner couples graph-weighted relational returns with advantage-weighted denoising and advantage-gated on-policy self-distillation, allowing relational signals to di-rectly shape policy updates.

Our goal is to learn a graph-conditioned trajectory dis-tribution 𝜋 𝜃 (𝜏 |𝐺 ) that concentrates probability mass on trajectories with higher 𝑅 traj (𝜏 ), while maintaining diversity and efficient infer-ence. To achieve this, we employ a unified graph-reward integrated diffusion objective (Sec. 3.5), which combines advantage-weighted diffusion updates with a state-value baseline and on-policy self-distillation, aligning training with planner-induced rollouts and consolidating high-reward behaviors, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

## 4 Experiment 4.1 Datasets and Metrics. 

nuPlan and interPlan Datasets. We evaluate GRID-Planner in closed-loop simulation on two established autonomous driving benchmarks. Following the standard protocol [5, 31], we report 

nuPlan [2] results on Val14 , Test14-random , and Test14-hard ,covering general performance and long-tail robustness. We further evaluate on interPlan [7], which emphasizes interaction-intensive scenarios such as nudging around parked vehicles, unprotected overtaking, and jaywalker encounters. 

Evaluation Metrics. For nuPlan , we report the official Closed-Loop Score (CLS) under Non-Reactive (NR) and Reactive (R) simu-lation [2]. CLS aggregates route progress, comfort, and rule compli-ance while penalizing safety failures such as at-fault collisions. For 

interPlan , we report the Overall Score and representative scores on Nudge Around , High Traffic Density , and Jaywalk .

Diagnostic Metrics. Beyond the official scores, we report Colli-sion Rate , corresponding to 𝑅 safety ; Lane Deviation , a lane/route-consistency proxy corresponding to 𝑅 rule ; Jerk , a smoothness indi-cator corresponding to 𝑅 comfort ; and Latency , the average planner-side inference time. These metrics are diagnostic only. Red-light violations are not evaluated separately due to limited traffic-signal state extraction. 

## 4.2 Implementation Details 

GRID-Planner uses 2 seconds of history to predict an 8-second trajectory at 10 Hz, with 21 historical timestamps including the current frame and 80 future steps. Each waypoint is represented as (𝑥, 𝑦, cos 𝜃, sin 𝜃 ) in the ego-centric frame. The scene encoder retains up to 32 neighboring agents, 70 lane polylines, 25 route-lane polylines, and 5 static control elements. Training has three stages: (1) base diffusion training for 400 epochs with AdamW (batch size 2048, learning rate 10 −4, weight decay 0.01, and EMA decay 0.999), CFG, and 0.3 neighbor dropout; (2) graph-aware reward fine-tuning with GAIW and RRS for 10 epochs at 5 × 10 −5; and (3) online self-distillation for 2 epochs, sampling four candidates per scene and ranking their graph-weighted returns to select the teacher and supervise the student. At inference, the distilled planner uses four update steps with a CFG scale of 1.8. Latency is the mean planner-side compute_trajectory runtime at batch size 1 on the same hardware, excluding simulator overhead, and is reported only as a relative runtime diagnostic.

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented GRID-Planner , a graph–reward integrated diffusion planner that aligns generative planning with structured relational signals for autonomous driving. By combining Graph-Aware Im-portance Weighting (GAIW), a Relational Reward Surrogate 

(RRS), and a unified on-policy diffusion objective with actor–critic and self-distillation, our framework improves trajectory prioritiza-tion, rollout evaluation, and policy refinement in a coherent man-ner. Limitations and Future Work. Experiments on nuPlan and 

interPlan show that GRID-Planner consistently improves closed-loop performance, especially in reactive and interaction-intensive scenarios, while our current design still relies on hand-crafted graph weighting and a limited set of relational reward signals; future work will explore richer relational cues, more adaptive graph-based weighting, and more efficient on-policy optimization for large-scale real-world autonomous driving deployment.
