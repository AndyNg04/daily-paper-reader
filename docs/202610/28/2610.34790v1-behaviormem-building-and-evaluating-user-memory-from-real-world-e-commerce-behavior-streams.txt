## Abstract 

User memory helps personalized systems build on their under-standing of users over time. In e-commerce, users convey their preferences and needs through both explicit language and implicit interactions with products. We study memory built from logs of these interactions, examining how it can remember concrete ev-idence and established interests, and use that evidence to infer 

underlying needs and preferences that inform judgments beyond well-observed product domains. Existing memory methods primar-ily target language-based interactions, and evaluations offer limited real behavioral data and limited separation of these capabilities. This leaves open how to jointly support and separately evaluate remembering and inference from behavioral logs. We formulate the problem of continual behavioral user memory around these joint requirements and develop a dedicated evaluation framework and memory method to address them. BehaviorMemBench is a benchmark built from real e-commerce logs covering 1,194 users and 17,462 behavioral batches over more than three years, with automatic future-behavior labels and a task-independent memory interface to separately evaluate remembering and inference under a common protocol. BehaviorMem is a memory design that or-ganizes explicit behavioral facts and inferred user characteristics across long-term and short-term scopes in a two-axis state, with evidence-grounded formation, continual maintenance, and query-independent readout. Experiments across two builders and two task readers show that BehaviorMem leads the evaluated agentic memories on remember and infer, and reveal different method rank-ings across the two capabilities and competitive statistical baselines. Validation on a public dataset further supports its usefulness for downstream tasks based on behavioral histories.

## 1 Introduction 

Long-term personalization in LLM-based systems, such as personal agents, search engines, and recommendation systems, requires per-sistent user understanding. User memory accumulates and reuses preferences, circumstances, and goals across interactions to support relevant services as users’ needs change [5, 47]. In e-commerce, this knowledge comes from interactions with products and ser-vices, where users convey preferences and shopping needs through both explicit natural language and implicit behavior . Through product inquiries and customer-service conversations, users can directly state their requirements, preferences, and circumstances. By contrast, browsing, favorites, add-to-cart actions, and purchases show what users engage with without directly explaining why. Their underlying preferences and needs must therefore be inferred from product characteristics and patterns across interactions. Memory construction from explicit natural-language interac-tions has been extensively studied [5, 20]. However, implicit behav-ioral logs provide evidence of preferences and needs that users may not articulate in language. These signals are dispersed across prod-ucts and visits, requiring observations to be interpreted together over time. Making this evidence useful for later personalization calls for a persistent, updatable source of user information that carries relevant history into future interactions. We therefore focus on constructing user memory from behavioral logs to preserve evidence of users’ choices and develop a reusable understanding of the preferences and needs behind them. Past behavior can inform later personalization. However, be-havioral logs record what users did without directly explaining their preferences or needs. Using such evidence therefore calls for memory to remember what users did and infer what their choices imply about them beyond remembering. ## 3.2 Data Collection and Statistics 

We construct BehaviorMemBench from Taobao’s user behavior logs, spanning February 2023 to June 2026. These logs record browsing, clicks, favorites, add-to-cart actions, and purchases with timestamps and product metadata. Following the visit-merging procedure in Appendix A.1.1, we retain 1,194 of 2,000 randomly sampled users with at least 50 valid visits. A batch becomes eligible to close after 50 visits or 120 days and closes at the next inter-visit gap exceeding 15 minutes. This combines activity and elapsed-time limits while avoiding a split within a browsing session. The resulting corpus contains 913,271 visits, 17,462 batches, and 16,268 history-to-future transitions; the summary statistics in Appendix A.2 characterize variation across users. 

## 3.3 Benchmark Framework

We select normalized L2 categories as prediction targets: this inter-mediate level captures recurring interests across items and provides a common evaluation target independent of a specific downstream task. ## 4 BehaviorMem 

BehaviorMem targets continual e-commerce behavioral logs, where preferences and needs are implicit in product interactions. Its two-axis design preserves concrete facts to remember past choices and uses evidence-grounded abstraction to infer reusable user characteristics, maintaining long-term tendencies and short-term needs as new behavior arrives. Figure 2 summarizes this design.

## 5 Experiments 5.1 Experimental Setup 

Dataset. We use BehaviorMemBench as the primary bench-mark to evaluate remembering and inference independently of specific downstream tasks. Its construction and statistics are de-tailed in Appendix A. To cross-check the effectiveness of memory methods on downstream tasks, we evaluate them on the public 

MemoryCD benchmark in Section 5.5. 

Baselines. We include statistical methods to assess what raw behavioral records and simple summaries can support, general-purpose agentic memories to examine whether generic memory mechanisms transfer to behavioral streams, and personal agentic memory to compare designs tailored to user modeling. Implemen-tation details are given in Appendix C.2. 

• Statistical methods. Frequency Profile ranks categories by engagement and recency, and Template Profile renders behavioral aggregates in a fixed template. Long Context 

serves as a raw-history control by retaining recent interac-tions. 

• General-purpose agentic memories. A-Mem [40] orga-nizes linked memory notes, Mem0 [5] maintains extracted facts, and LightMem [7] stores compact long-term entries. 

• Personal agentic memory. Persona Tree [45] maintains a hierarchical persona representation. 

Implementation and evaluation. Methods process history chronologically and expose query-independent readouts. Agentic builders use Qwen3-32B [41] or Qwen3.6-35B-A3B [23]; Qwen3-32B and Qwen3.7-Plus [24] task readers evaluate the outputs using shared candidate pools and prompts within each reader setting.

## 5.4 Discussion 

Overall averages can hide differences across users and targets. 

We therefore examine performance by breakdown users: 

User activity. Figure 4 groups users by total batch count, a proxy for activity, and compares both builders under a Qwen3-32B task reader. For the Qwen3-32B builder, remember-pool recall is highest in the least-active group across all five agentic methods, while infer-pool recall does not improve consistently with activity. BehaviorMem’s remember-pool recall exceeds A-Mem’s in every group; their infer-pool recalls are close, with A-Mem slightly ahead in the two most-active groups. Activity thus reveals variation in relative support for inference that overall rankings can obscure.

## 6 Conclusion 

We study user memory from real, continual e-commerce behav-ioral logs, where preserving observed choices provides the foun-dation for inferring the preferences and needs behind them. This setting calls for joint support for remembering and inference : keep-ing established user information available and making behavioral evidence useful beyond well-observed product domains. We intro-duce BehaviorMemBench to assess these capabilities separately through automatically derived future-behavior labels and a com-mon, task-independent protocol. We also propose BehaviorMem ,which jointly maintains behavioral facts and revisable user charac-teristics through a two-axis state, evidence-grounded abstraction, continual maintenance, and query-independent readout. Across two builders and two task readers, BehaviorMem leads evaluated agentic memories on both recalls. Ablations show complementary roles for observed facts and inferred characteristics, while statistical baselines remain competitive. Validation on a public dataset further supports its downstream utility. 

## 7 Related Work 

Memory for LLM Agents. Memory enables LLM agents to move beyond stateless interactions by retaining and reusing past informa-tion to support reasoning and adaptation. Early studies represent memory as natural-language records of experience and manage them across hierarchical tiers [14, 20, 21]. Later research distills interactions into structured entries and exposes relations among them [5, 10, 38, 40]. Among recent efforts, entries are consolidated by shared topic and linked through multiple types of relations [7, 13, 18, 29, 31, 33]. Together, these advances give agents symbolic structure and scalable access, all grounded in explicit interactions such as conversations. BehaviorMem, in contrast, builds memory from implicit behavioral records such as purchases and clicks, which are noisier and less self-descriptive. 

User Modeling in E-commerce. User modeling supports per-sonalized search and recommendation in e-commerce by capturing shoppers’ preferences from their behavioral histories. Traditional methods encode behavior sequences into latent user representations

BehaviorMem: Building and Evaluating User Memory from Real-World E-Commerce Behavior Streams 

through attention-based interest modeling over historical actions [3, 9, 15, 16, 22, 30, 42, 46, 48, 49]. Recent LLM-based approaches instead express user preferences in language and preserve this un-derstanding as user memory for subsequent interactions. Some approaches construct user memory by distilling diverse behavioral patterns into natural-language descriptions of users’ multiple inter-ests [6, 25, 26, 28, 34, 36], while others revise it through interaction feedback and collaborative signals across users [1, 4, 17, 43]. Be-yond this, BehaviorMem combines structured organization of user inferences and their supporting events with temporal management of memory revision and current availability. 

Evaluation of User Memory. User memory evaluation exam-ines what memory retains and how well it supports an under-standing of the user. Existing benchmarks test recall and reasoning over long-term interactions [2, 19, 32, 37] and memory processing under incremental information streams [11]. User-focused evalua-tions assess evolving profiles inferred from synthetic activity logs [12, 39], personalized task performance [27], and the alignment of inferred interests with user engagement [8]. StreamProfileBench uses future user-authored content to label interests in streaming evaluation, distinguishing historically seen from newly appearing interests [35]. Building on these directions, BehaviorMemBench combines continual memory construction from real-world implicit behaviors with automatically derived labels from future transac-tion categories. Its history-aware candidate pools provide separate operational measures of remembering and interest inference.

## A BehaviorMemBench Details A.1 Data Collection Pipeline 

Data processing produces observations 𝐵 1, . . . , 𝐵 𝑇 independently of evaluation targets. Evaluation construction then labels each history-to-future transition; memory construction and readout never access its candidates. 

A.1.1 Part I: Data processing. Taobao interaction logs span 2023-02-03 to 2026-06-23. Of 2,000 randomly sampled users, 1,194 have at least 50 valid visits. Events contain timestamps, actions, item text, category paths, brand, shop, price, and product attributes. Consecutive same-item events with gaps of at most 30 minutes merge into one visit; 𝐴 → 𝐵 → 𝐴 remains three visits. Metadata comes from the first event. Four counters retain entry clicks, de-tailed browsing (14 action types), favorites/cart actions (6 types, including buy-now intent), and completed purchases. Cart removals, favorite cancellations, and refunds are dropped rather than sub-tracted. A batch becomes eligible at 50 visits or 120 days from its first visit and closes at the next inter-visit gap exceeding 15 minutes. This balances sparse and active users without splitting an ongoing session. 

A.1.2 Part II: Evaluation construction. Prediction uses normalized L2 categories within the L1–L2–leaf taxonomy; placeholder and un-suitable categories are removed. Next-batch favorites, cart actions, purchases, or qualifying deep browsing supply positives; shallow clicks alone do not. 

Pool assignment depends only on preceding history. Remember positives are seen categories or unseen categories within frequently visited L1 domains. Infer positives are unseen categories in lightly observed domains (related), or in unvisited domains linked to his-tory by a fixed semantic group (new). Unsupported categories are excluded. 

Distractors match the pool’s historical relation. Remember uses unseen siblings under positive L1 domains (cluster), seen categories under other domains (history), and eligible random fill. Infer uses unseen siblings anchored to historical interests in lightly observed domains (history-conditioned) and random fill; new-domain dis-tractors have unvisited L1 domains in semantic groups represented in history. All distractors exclude every category in the next batch and are deduplicated; infer distractors also exclude seen categories. Each retained positive requires three legal distractors. Infeasible groups are trimmed from their deterministic tail until this ratio holds. Pools are constructed once with deterministic randomness, shuffled, and frozen across methods and readers.

## A.3 Readout and Task-Reader Protocol 

Methods in each reader block share candidate pools, prompts, and decoding settings. Fixed readouts fill common description/tree slots, with fixed placeholders for unused slots. Remember and infer use separate calls on the same state. Candidates show zero-based in-dices, L2 names, and shared short annotations; method identity, labels, and L1 parents are hidden. Empty positive pools are skipped. The following task-reader template is translated from Chinese; the same selection format is used for both pools. 

> You are a user-profile analysis system. Based on the user profile, select the categories that best match the user’s interests. Use only the profile description and profile tree. ### Profile description: {description} ### Profile tree: {tree} ### Candidate pool B ({pool_size} candidates) {pool} select {n} **Output format**: ## Selection Pool B: 0,2,4 - Indices start from 0 - Select {n} from pool B - Separate the indices with commas; do not explain

The nine metrics follow Section 3.3. Exposure normalizes se-lected distractors by family size. Each metric skips empty denom-inators, averages valid transitions within users and then across users, excluding users without valid transitions. Cohorts are de-fined per reader; each builder–reader configuration uses common valid transitions. 

## B BehaviorMem Implementation Details B.1 Per-Batch Execution 

F1 renders behavior; F2 derives candidate Claims; F3 places them; F4 updates state; F5 projects readout. T1/T2 use closed slots and T3/T4 use open Claims, merging into one tree before F5. An independent append-only visit ledger records F1 facts and never enters the Claim tree or upstream model prompts. 

## B.2 State Representation 

Explicit long-term memory aggregates categories, brands, at-tributes, and engagement with recency decay; short-term memory retains item activity from the latest three batches. Implicit types are demographics (T1), social relations (T2), interests (T3), and situational state (T4), organized by type–domain–attribute paths. T1/T2 use fixed registry paths with at most one current value; T3/T4 reuse or create paths and allow multiple Claims. Products, brands, values, and assertions cannot name paths. Claims store text, confidence from 1 to 5, and first-seen/last-confirmed endpoints with dates, key facts, and visit references. T1/T2 are continuous and bypass Dormancy; T4 is non-continuous. Continuous T3 Claims represent longer-term tendencies; non-continuous T3/T4 Claims represent stage-specific needs. Dormant Claims remain stored but hidden from readout. 

## B.3 Formation: The Evidence-Distance Ladder 

Formation follows behavior → recurring commonality → attended theme → reusable characteristic → demographic/social attribute. A shared call derives the first two abstraction levels. Two arms then use the same themes: T3/T4 stop at a free-text characteristic; T1/T2 continue to a value copied from a closed candidate set. Each level uses only the immediately preceding level as evidence. Stored terminals retain behavioral references, not a materialized parent chain. The following abridged formation prompt is translated from Chinese; D0–D3 denote the four abstraction levels above. 

> You are an e-commerce behavioral inference system. ## Shared stage Input: {behavior log} Derive D0 behavioral commonalities from observed events only, preserving Visit references and merging repeated behavior toward the same object or theme. Derive D1 objects or themes of attention from D0 only. ## T1/T2 arm Input: {D1}, {closed-set registry} Derive D2 from D1 only, then copy one allowed D3 value verbatim. ## T3/T4 arm Input: {D1}, {open registry} Derive a reusable free-text D2 characteristic from D1 only. Do not introduce behavior absent from the preceding level.

T1/T2 already have registry paths. The following abridged place-ment prompt applies only to T3/T4. 

> You are a user-memory placement system. Input: {current T3/T4 registry}, {T3/T4 candidate Claims} Perform placement only. For every candidate, select exactly one type, domain, and attribute, preferring an existing path whenever it fits. Do not add, delete, split, merge, or rewrite candidates. Do not alter Claim, confidence, or evidence. Return one decision for every input index.

## B.4 Maintenance 

T1/T2 use deterministic updates. A repeated value takes the higher of the existing and incoming confidence scores and advances con-firmation; a conflicting value replaces it only with strictly higher confidence. An existing value at the confidence ceiling of 5 is there-fore retained under this rule. Coupled slots compare their strongest confidence and retain unmentioned values only if consistent. De-terministic constraints remove invalid existence/count, partner, life-stage, and occupation combinations. 

T3/T4 operate within one path: Keep preserves, Add accepts a distinct Claim, Update revises the same characteristic, and Retire requires direct contradiction. The abridged instruction accounts for each input exactly once and forbids cross-path merging; evidence endpoints are recomputed from validated sources. Invalid responses fall back to keeping old and appending new Claims. Unobserved continuous paths remain Active; non-continuous paths may become Dormant and reactivate with renewed support. Sustained absence can trigger T3 continuity review. The availabil-ity instruction treats absence as insufficient counter-evidence and defaults to keeping a path Active when uncertain. ## B.5 Readout 

F5 deterministically emits Active Claim text, confidence, and stable/stage-specific scope. Separately, it ranks ledger-derived long-term aggregates and recent item activity by engagement and recency. These explicit facts and inferred Claims share a query-independent readout; F5 calls no model.

## C.1 Dataset and Model Settings 

Data and reader rules follow Appendix A; model assignments fol-low Section 5.1. Activity groups keep batch-count ties together: 1–6, 7–11, 12–16, 17–21, and at least 22 batches. Within each builder and pool, activity analysis uses transitions common to five agen-tic methods and user macro-averaging; target-familiarity analysis likewise uses common valid transitions within each configuration. 

## C.2 Baselines and Adaptation 

Methods process histories chronologically into native query-independent representations, without candidate access during construction. 

Long Context renders dated visits with titles, categories, prices, and engagement, retaining recent lines within 8,000 to-kens. Frequency Profile ranks the top 30 L2 categories using click/browse/favorite-or-cart/purchase weights 1/2/3/5 and 0 .9𝑘 

decay for visits 𝑘 batches earlier. Template Profile uses the same weights in fixed category, price, engagement, and title-fragment slots; new categories first occur within three batches, and inactive categories are absent for at least four. For A-Mem , Mem0 , and LightMem , one batch becomes one user message. Readout enumerates recent stored entries within 8,000 tokens. A-Mem exposes generated context and keywords, excluding raw notes. Mem0 lists current facts by creation time. LightMem disables pre-compression and topic segmentation and forces extraction each batch, evaluating this configured pipeline rather than its full efficiency-oriented setup. 

Persona Tree applies add/update/delete/no-op operations to its fixed hierarchy. The complete schema remains available during construction; only readout prunes empty leaves and uses an empty description placeholder. We evaluate state construction without MemListener training, query-dependent routing, or retrieval fusion. 

## D MemoryCD Experimental Details D.1 Dataset and Tasks 

MemoryCD [44] uses Amazon Reviews 2023 product-review histo-ries containing item information, review text, and observed ratings. We consider Beauty, Books, Electronics, and Home and Kitchen. In the single-domain setting, memory uses the target-domain history preceding the final three interactions, which are reserved for testing. In the cross-domain setting, memory combines inter-actions from Beauty, Books, and Electronics, and testing uses the final three Home and Kitchen interactions for a 323-user cohort. 

## D.2 Baselines and Implementation 

Methods use the adapters in Appendix C.2 under a shared readout token budget. Agentic builders and task readers use Qwen3-32B or Qwen3.6-35B-A3B; non-parametric baselines need no LLM builder. 

## D.3 Evaluation Protocol 

For item ranking , the reader ranks benchmark-provided candi-dates containing one held-out positive and negatives. HR@10 mea-sures whether the positive ranks in the top ten; NDCG@10 also rewards higher placement. For rating prediction , the reader pre-dicts a score on the 1–5 scale; MAE measures average absolute error, and RMSE weights larger errors more heavily. Single-domain results average metrics equally across four domains. Tables 3 and 5 report both readers, with N@10 denoting NDCG@10.
