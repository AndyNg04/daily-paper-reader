## Abstract 

Expressive robot motion positively impacts human-robot inter-action by improving user engagement, likability, or task perfor-mance. We present a novel approach to automatically generate and modulate complex, expressive full-body gesture sequences from amultimodal (text, audio, video) context description. Based on a uni-fied mathematical implementation of the Principles of Animation using Dynamic Movement Primitives, we use multimodal foun-dation models to generate such sequences along with parametric motion variations that are highly context-aware. This method ex-tends the state of the art in terms of flexibility and generality by being interpretable, composable, and working across different robot morphologies. Moreover, we integrate the system into a continuous control framework and leverage knowledge distillation to learn amuch smaller model, significantly improving token efficiency and system latency. Results from a user study with a human-like plat-form indicate that participants judged our system’s motions to be better aligned with the interaction context than motions produced under a non-modulated or API-level condition. We also demon-strate how the system generates expressive motion for robots with different kinematics, showcasing its versatility. Paper webpage: https://gen-mod-expressive-motion-behavior.github.io/.

Therefore, the design and use of expressive robotic body motion are critical for achieving natural and effective communication with socially interactive robots. Key questions include how movements are shaped, selected, and timed, the extent of their expression, and their integration with speech, context, and the course of interac-tion. This paper tackles several of the above questions, namely how body motion can be represented to facilitate learning, execution, and modulation – i.e., context-sensitive variations of the same mo-tion –, and how basic gestures can be automatically selected, timed, and varied such that interlocutors perceive the robot’s behavior as aligned with the interaction context. 

The variety of expressive human body motion appears to be open-ended. One way to tackle this complexity practically is cat-egorizing gestures into communicative functions such as deictic, iconic, or symbolic, and reusing standardized sets of pre-recorded gestures [39 ]. By providing a small set of exemplars for each func-tion, a robot can cover many real-world situations without the need to generate joint-level trajectories on the fly. We build on such motion datasets and adopt recent work [9] that employs Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) to represent, modulate, and compose basic gestures into expressive body language. robot manipulation, DMPs offer properties highly relevant for this task. As shown in [9], they provide a unified implementation of the Principles of Animation and enable modulation of key principles through a low-dimensional, interpretable parameter set. This al-lows animators – and large language models – to stylize full-body motion along the well-established dimensions of the Principles of Animation across different kinematic configurations. They are also online adaptive in the sense that gestures are parametrized by a static or dynamic goal in space towards which gestures are executed. 

However, unlike [9], which was limited to the “how” in generat-ing expressive body language, we focus on the “what” and “when” and automate the generation process given the multimodal descrip-tion of a context. To this end, we integrate multimodal foundation models into a continuous control framework, leveraging their ex-tensive world knowledge to explore novel possibilities for social robot motion design. This also extends prior work that used only large language models and simpler motion representations [23 ]. Specifically, our contributions are: 

• A system for automated context-aware modulation and com-position of basic gestures, represented as Dynamic Move-ment Primitives (DMPs), into expressive body language. The method is robot-agnostic with respect to kinematics and integrates multimodal context inputs, including text, image, and audio. 

• A method that applies knowledge distillation, a technique to transfer the knowledge of a large, computationally expensive foundation model to a smaller, more efficient model. The results are reduced inference cost and significantly smaller system latencies. 

• A user study with a human-like robot that confirms the validity of the approach over a non-modulated and API-level motion condition, as well as qualitative experiments that demonstrate the system’s ability to generate expressive body language across robot morphologies using a manipulator and a four-legged robot. 

## 2 Related Work 

## 2.1  Expressive Motion Behavior Generation 

Prior work has extensively explored the generation and use of gestures and expressive body language in HRI, see [7] for a survey. The literature can be divided into planning and generation methods. The former denotes the high-level task of selecting and composing basic movements into expressive motion behaviors, the latter is the lower-level task of synthesizing, learning, or executing basic movements, e.g., at the joint trajectory level. 

High-Level Motion Behavior Generation: Early works intro-duce methods for high-level behavior generation based on manually designed, rule-based state transition systems. Kato et al. [15 ] point out that such systems require extensive understanding and classifi-cation of human activities, behaviors, and other input modalities for valid and reasonable state transitions. In [3], the authors focus on the ease of use of such systems, while later works also deal with the formal verification of expressive rule-based behaviors [33 ] or the application of a manually designed behavior system, e.g., for the Pepper robot [20 ]. 

As the manual design of rule-based behaviors is tedious and counterintuitive, follow-up works focus on semi-automatic and automated creation of behavioral decision systems. The authors of [32 ] synthesize formalisms from traces of instruction, and later provide modification options [34 ] and user-friendly instruction authoring [35 ]. In order to handle the complexity of multi-modal context inputs automatically, the method proposed in [11 ] fits aBayesian network to model multi-modal behaviors. Recent works utilize deep neural networks with reinforcement learning [13 ] and time-series information [26 ] to trigger gestures during interaction. With the goal of general context understanding, some works also utilize Large Language Models (LLMs) to guide the behavior of the robot. In [18 ], the authors introduce a framework that first derives motion policies from a large-scale human motion gener-ation model, and then employs a language model to determine whether a new task aligns with an existing policy and to refine it accordingly, thereby enabling efficient policy reuse and natural language-based specification of complex motion behaviors. Based on scenario descriptions, Mahadevan et al. [23 ] generate API-level programs with a multi-stage LLM system for context understanding and code generation. Only by providing robot-specific information and a library of available API calls the system can generate expres-sive behaviors for various robots. Similarly, LLMs can enable the relation of a target expression with the available hardware using motion parameters. [36 ] shows results for a quadruped robot by providing the available degrees of freedom as parameters of the robot, along with descriptions that describe a possible effect. 

For high-level behavior generation, we utilize general context understanding to alleviate the limitations of manual and rule-based methods in terms of flexibility. However, instead of simple calls to the robots’ actuators [23 ] or limitation to specific morphologies [36 ], we generate sequences of complex whole-body gestures based on apre-recorded library represented as Dynamic Movement Primitives [14 ]. This enables the generation of cross-morphology context-aligned expressive motion, while providing the additional feature of risk management of gestures that might be perceived as political, obscene, or intensive by simply excluding them from the library. 

Low-Level Motion Representations: In robotics, the challenge of low-level motion generation mirrors that of character animation, where the design of trajectories must balance physical feasibility with expressive qualities that communicate intent, emotion, or style to a human observer. Important works [19 ], and tools like Aldebaran Choreographe utilize trajectory shaping with keyframe interpolation using splines or polynomials. The method provides considerable flexibility at the cost of laborious manual design. Later, methods for motion representation like Dynamic Movement Primi-tives [14 ] for imitation of demonstrated motion or the probabilistic extension [31 ] are proposed. While offering smooth execution capa-bilities in their basic forms, they lack flexibility towards expressive robot motion generation. 

By mapping the latent space of a variational autoencoder to the Valence Arousal Dominance (VAD) space, the method proposed in [24 ] can adjust input motion to align with targeted VAD states. The method is limited to a single kinematic structure on which the model is trained. In [29 ], motion is adjusted by modulating along the Principles of Animation in the latent space of an encoder-decoder structured model for human avatars. in [9] also relies on the representation space of the Principles of Animation, but encodes them in a physics-based model, which is decoupled from learned kinematic structures and enables on-line modulation. The authors of [1] show that also reinforcement learning based systems can be trained for behavioral adjustments based on multi-objective reward functions. The model uses reward weights that can be tuned to modulate motion online. 

Towards lively and autonomous non-verbal interaction, Christen et al. [6] propose a diffusion-based system that generates controls conditioned on a nearby human’s pose and a target expressivity. The system is trained with expert data for a single robot. Some works also directly leverage LLMs to either adapt [43 ] or generate [12 ]sequences of joint states to enable context-dependent expressivity. In both works, the model is provided with an understanding of the human’s kinematic structure with differing levels of detail. 

Compared to these works, which lack dynamic consistency [12 ], modulate with limited flexibility [24 ], or use latent space grounding [1, 29 ], our method combines context-aware expressive adaptation with physically grounded motion execution and modulation. To this end, we extend our past work [9] for fully automatic, online adaptive modulation parametrization. 

Integrated Continuous Control: Frameworks for continuous planning and control enable the deployment of robots for interac-tion by ensuring both timely responses to cues in active scenarios [38 ] and lifelike behavior when idle [2]. 

Generally, and as shown in prior works, both [22 ] and [4] pro-pose methods that combine a selection of perception and cognition models with action planning to continuously handle user inputs and react when necessary. Oleinikov et al. [30 ] further introduce a hierarchical controller that operates at different frequencies, al-lowing a robotic arm to incorporate contextual human interaction information into motion planning at both high and low levels. Cur-rent works face ongoing challenges in combining general context understanding, flexibility, and fast reaction to achieve continuous control with expressive motion behavior. Our method addresses this by picking up the hierarchical control idea and condensing modularity with general understanding. 

## 2.2  Co-Speech Gestures and Motion 

Although our work does not address Co-Speech motion generation, we review related methods as they tackle similar challenges, es-pecially with respect to selection and timing of gestures to enable synchronized behavior. 

Co-speech gestures can be coordinated with speech by either adapting the gestures to the spoken content or adapting the speech to the planned gestures [7]. Adapting gestures to speech involves timing motions to align with specific words, which can be done manually when the utterance is predetermined [41 ]. More precise alignment can be achieved by pre-rendering speech to calculate word durations and combining this information with the robot’s motor capabilities [27 , 37 ]. Contextual factors such as accented syl-lables can be factored in to influence timing [25 ]. The alignment of speech with gestures can be realized by matching relevant speech units with related gesture units [16 ]. As reported in [28 ], learning-based methods trained for human avatars can leverage available 

data to generate co-speech gestures. These can incorporate multi-modal temporal [5], semantic [17 , 42 ], or spatial grounding [8]. 

For robotics applications, this positions our work to share simi-lar goals with co-speech motion research, namely, context-aware generation of expressive behavior. While we do not focus on syn-chronizing multiple output modalities, the temporal grounding in the phase variable of the DMP could enable such coordination. 

## 3 Method 

In this section, we present our approach for the automatic gener-ation of expressive, context-sensitive motion behavior. First, we describe the method for generating motion sequences that capture expressive behavior. Then, we detail how these sequences are mod-ulated to ensure that the executed movements convey expressivity aligned with the context. Finally, we explain the integration of these components into a flexible and efficient framework that supports continuous operation. See  Figure 1 and  Figure 4 for example results of our method and  Figure 2 for the overview of the system. 

Interaction context is inherently multimodal, comprising a vari-ety of spatial, social, and environmental cues that together shape how a robot’s response is perceived as normative or appropriate. To achieve high levels of context sensitivity, robotic systems must in-tegrate such cues in the form of verbal, auditory, and visual stimuli. Thus, we define the input to our system to be a multimodal context description using text, audio, and video, as well as sensory inputs that arrive in real time. We denote this as the robot’s interaction context  C = {𝑟 Text , 𝑟 Video , 𝑟 Audio , 𝑟 RT }. Real-time inputs include, for example, spatial positions from a visual person tracker or object detector. 

## 3.1  Sequence Generation 

Context alignment critically depends on the choice of actions, their execution order, style, and timing. The literature suggests that efficient interaction is often based on a set of essential movements and gestures [10 ]. In line with these findings, our method generates motion sequences from pre-learned robotic skills that represent appropriate actions for various interaction scenarios. The main advantage of this approach is the utilization of complex, whole-body motions that already possess inherent communicative meaning while abstracting the underlying robot kinematics. This enables robots with arbitrary morphologies to utilize complex interaction gestures for effective communication and bridges the embodiment gap by providing a hardware-agnostic mapping between generative models and physical robot execution. 

Given an interaction context  C, we address the open-ended problem of generating context-aligned motion sequences using multimodal foundation models. The inference process follows athree-stage procedure designed to ensure broad adaptability by decoupling high-level sequencing from specific robotic constraints: First, using  C, the model is prompted to generate a contextually appropriate action sequence. The sequence is then mapped against the robot’s capabilities to verify execution feasibility, and finally, the system queries the skill library to synthesize motion behavior that best aligns with the social context under the robot’s kinematic constraints. ## 3.2  Sequence Modulation 

While the behavioral plan  𝜋  determines the sequence of actions (the “what”), different contexts can necessitate meaningful variations in execution. In human-to-human interaction, for example, a greeting differs significantly depending on social factors, such as whether the individual was recently seen or if it is a long-awaited reunion. Although the underlying motion sequence may remain the same, its stylistic execution, or modulation, must vary to remain contextually appropriate (the “how”). 

To this end, we build on past work [9], where we introduced Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) to implement the Princi-ples of Animation in a mathematically unified formulation. We briefly summarize the theoretical approach and operationalization of the individual principles, and then show how we extend it with automatic context-aligned parametrization (see [9] or the supple-mentary material for more details).

## 3.3  System Overview 

With the goal of autonomous and fluent interaction in real scenes, we propose a system for continuous and efficient operation using a hierarchical control structure based on a timeline. To decrease reaction times, we employ a knowledge-distilled multimodal foun-dation model tailored to generate expressive motion behavior with low inference times. Building on the methodological foundation above, we show how the system can be abstracted from specific robots to remain adaptable and flexible across varying capabilities, skills, and morphologies. 

Timeline Control: Using a timeline aligns naturally with the structure of the generated modulated sequences and leverages the inherent sequencing capabilities of DMPs. Transitions between two DMPs occur smoothly, as each DMP’s state is governed by adynamical attractor system. Consequently, the timeline allows for continuous progression through the sequence by linking individual primitives to produce seamless motion. In practice, the timeline is implemented as a first-in-first-out (FIFO) queue, from which motion primitives are retrieved to determine the currently active primitive. For continuous actuation of the robot, the progressing state of the currently active primitive provides the joint control commands to move the robot. 

The hierarchical control strategy of our system operates at dif-ferent frequencies. At a high level with lower frequency, the popu-lation of the timeline follows two governing cases: The first case is reactive to incoming interaction context  C and adds the individual motion primitives of a generated modulated sequence  𝜋 M to the timeline for sequential execution. In this case, the timeline queue is first cleared before appending the new sequence, to allow for fast reaction. In an extreme case, and towards even faster reactions, the currently active primitive can be directly replaced, without finishing execution and directly transitioning to the first primitive of the new sequence. In the second case, during idle operation, idle motion primitives are appended and executed to ensure continuous control of the robot. The idle motion primitives are randomly se-lected from a separate library of idle gestures, which can be a subset of the primary gesture library  𝑟 𝑦  or contain low-intent motion, or even static joint commands. At a lower level, and decoupled from the timeline population, the hierarchical control system enables continuous motion adjustments to modulate the executed motion based on relations between the DMP and real-time data. This mod-ulation is executed at control frequency in an online fashion. When a detected person in the scene changes their location, the robot can adapt and maintain its orientation and gesture target during execu-tion by updating the DMP goal state  𝑔 . Notably, idle movements can also be continuously modulated to track a person or to induce random subtle movements using  𝑝 rand .

Knowledge Distillation and Generation Inference: Since re-action time is critical for natural and fluent interaction, we optimize our generation method using knowledge distillation to fine-tune asmall, efficient multimodal foundation model. This approach relies HRI ’26, March 16–19, 2026, Edinburgh, Scotland, UK Hielscher et al. 

on supervised training with data from a larger model, which gener-ally produces superior results and enables condensing multi-step inference pipelines into a single step. We leverage both aspects, i.e., our training set consists of traces generated by the multi-step pipeline using the large model. Specifically, our training data stems from diverse traces generated from a context dataset consisting of over 100 contextual descriptions  C – similar to the ones we present in the experiments section – and specific data  R for three different robots. The resulting fine-tuned foundation model takes the same inputs and produces the same outputs as the multi-step pipeline, but consumes fewer input and output tokens, as intermediate steps are cut. Generally, and this applies to both the single call of the fine-tuned model as well as the multi-step pipeline, we utilize struc-tured I/O interfaces to ensure that the inference output adheres to the required form, which is especially important with respect to system robustness. 

Cross-Morphology: The system is designed to be robot agnos-tic, enabling its use across different platforms. To operate, the sys-tem requires the following inputs: Capabilities : A textual description of the robot’s motion-related abilities. For example, a human-like robot can move its head, arms, and torso, while a quadruped can move its legs and torso. Kinematics : The robot’s kinematic structure, provided as a URDF file. Movements : A collection of existing joint trajectories for gestures or other task-specific motions and inter-action skills. Parameter Limits : Bounds for individual modulation parameters. While defaults are often sufficient, the limits can be customized to fine-tune modulation. 

Based on these inputs, a preprocessing module computes robot-specific data  R, necessary for system operation, in two steps: First, joint trajectories are encoded to DMPs, forming the motion primi-tive gesture library. Second, the URDF file is analyzed to construct a kinematic map and compute the global axes of each joint. This in-formation allows the preprocessing module to identify valid source-target joint pairs for the Follow Through principle, which must reside within the same kinematic chain and be either directly connected or separated by a single condition joint. Axis alignment within the chain is verified based on the configuration of the condition joint. 

## 4 Experiments 

The evaluation of our method is carried out in multiple experi-ments that address different aspects of the system. Specifically, we conduct a user study to investigate the subjective perception of context alignment of the generated motion behavior and compare our method against an API-level baseline condition. Additionally, we analyze the inference times of the generation process to validate that our approach of knowledge distillation achieves a cut in system reaction times. Further experiments aim to show the handling of multimodal context descriptions as well as the integration of robots with different kinematic structures into our system. 

In our experiments, we use the following generation conditions: 

Ours : Our system, which automatically generates and modulates gesture sequences. The multimodal foundation model is a version of OpenAI’s gpt-4.1-nano, which is learned with knowledge distil-lation from Ours (multi) as introduced below. Ours (non-mod) : An ablation of our system, which automatically generates gesture se-quences that are not modulated. API-level : A baseline condition that 

automatically generates behavior programs from an API library of the robot. This is a reimplementation of the method proposed in [23 ]. Ours (multi) : Our method but with the multi-step pipeline, which interfaces gpt-4.1. 

For the majority of our experiments, we deploy the Pepper robot, with a motion primitive library of over 30 complex and expressive whole-body gestures, for e.g., waving, pointing, or explaining. 

We use the following context descriptions for different scenarios between the robot and an interaction partner: 

Context 1 “A person approaches and greets the robot, then asks for directions and an explanation. The robot knows that the desti-nation is to its left. The robot’s energy level is very low.” 

Context 2 “A person familiar to the robot approaches from its right side. The robot hasn’t seen this person in a long time, and the person is clearly excited to meet the robot again.” 

Context 3 “A person walks past the robot. The person is busy and in a hurry.”

We conducted a user study, for which we recruited 38 partici-pants - 26 male, 12 female - aged between 21 and 84 from mailing lists and university information boards. 8 participants did not finish the survey, and their partially filled questionnaires are not regarded in the evaluation. The study consists of multiple scenarios, which appear in randomized order and are divided into two questionnaire parts. In both, participants watch videos of the human-like robot 

Pepper and answer questions related to the videos. All videos show both the robot and the silhouette of the robot’s interaction partner. 

Study - Experiment 1: For the first part of the study, we gen-erate expressive robot motion behavior using the conditions Ours ,

Ours (non-mod) , and API-level . The API-level condition is provided with a library of API calls to move the arms, the head, and the torso of the robot, as well as functions for scene understanding, like getting the yaw and pitch angles of humans in the scene. 

The questionnaire follows a within-subject structure, where videos of all three conditions appear in randomized order. For each video, the participants rate how well the shown motion behavior aligns with the context that describes the scenario on a 7-point linear scale. With this experiment, we study the hypothesis H1: 

The perceived alignment between context and expressivity is rated higher for non-modulated sequences than for API-level behaviors , as well as the hypothesis H2: The perceived alignment between context and expressivity is rated higher for modulated sequences than for non-modulated sequences .

Study - Experiment 2: In the second part of the study, we compare the generated expressive motion behavior from the two conditions Ours and Ours (multi) .

The questionnaire shows videos of both conditions in random-ized order, and participants are asked to rate which of the two conditions aligns better with the given context description. A con-tinuous bipolar slider ranging from -100 to 100 is used. The extremes (-100 and 100) indicate that one of the two conditions is perceived as much better aligned, while the center (0) marks equal alignment. ## 5 Results and Discussion 

## 5.1  Quantitative Results 

Results - Study - Experiment 1: Data is analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA to assess the effects of experimental factors on the collected ratings. Following the significant effect for the generation conditions (𝑝 < 0.001 ), paired-samples t-tests, with Bonferroni correction applied, are used for specific comparisons to investigate our hypotheses. The results of these post-hoc tests show a significant difference between Ours and Ours (non-mod) 

(𝑝 < 0.001 ), and between Ours (non-mod) and API-level (𝑝 < 0.001 ). The results are visualized on the left in  Figure 3. Our hypotheses 

H1 and H2 are supported. 

Results - Study - Experiment 2: To avoid false statements based on averaging factors, we conduct per-context non-inferiority tests using a one-sided t-test with a non-inferiority margin of  10  %

of the slider range. The results indicate significant non-inferiority of the generation condition Ours compared to Ours (multi) with strong statistical evidence (𝑝 < 0.01 ) for Context 1 and very strong statistical evidence (𝑝 < 0.001 ) for Context 2 and Context 3. The 

respective rating means are  3.86  %, 16 .73  %, and  7.56  % in favor of Ours . The results are visualized in the center of  Figure 3. Our hypothesis H3 is supported. 

Results - Experiment 3: We evaluated system reaction times across Context 1–3 . The knowledge distillation model (Ours ) av-eraged  2.40  s, 2.66  s, and  2.22  s per context respectively, with an overall mean of  2.39  s using an average of 2161 tokens. The multi-step pipeline (Ours (multi) ) averaged  14 .32  s, 16 .66  s, and  10 .26  s,with an overall mean of  12 .87  s using an average of 4969 tokens. This corresponds to a 5.4 × speedup, or an  81 .5 % reduction in reac-tion time, demonstrating substantially lower system latency with the knowledge distilled model. The results are visualized on the right in  Figure 3.

## 5.2  Qualitative Results

For the multimodal version of Context 2, including an image of a person who appears happy together with the voice recording, the generated motion closely matches the one solely based on the text description, on both levels, the expressivity and the generated parameter values. This confirms that multimodal inputs that match or reinforce the context produce the same resulting motion. 

## 5.3  Discussion 

The results of our experiments show how our method enables the context-aware generation and modulation of expressive motion behavior both quantitatively and qualitatively. Specifically, the eval-uation of our user study supports all our hypotheses with strong (𝑝 < 0.01 ) or very strong (𝑝 < 0.001 ) statistical evidence. The eval-uation, while not showing an effect on the ratings from context alone, does show statistical evidence (𝑝 < 0.05 ) of an effect of the context on the generation conditions. However, the effect size is quite small with  𝜂 2 = 0.0918  compared to that of the generation condition alone (𝜂 2 = 0.447 , 𝑝 << 0.001 ), but can still be seen in the plotted alignment rating, especially for Context 1. These findings can possibly be grounded in individual perception from participants that reached out using our contact information, stating comments like the expectation of a robot to still function normally even if its energy reserves are low (Context 1), or embodiment problems with lacking hand opening or closing, which is not possible with our application of the Pepper robot. Also notable is that the collected preference ratings actually show a slight preference towards the knowledge distilled model. ## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we present a system to automatically generate ro-bot motion behavior from a multimodal description of an inter-action context. Using multimodal foundation models, our method sequences and modulates whole-body gestures, encoded as Dy-namic Movement Primitives, to align the robot’s expressivity to the context. With the integration into a timeline-based continuous 

controller that leverages knowledge distillation and is adaptable to robots with different kinematics, the approach extends the state of the art by providing more generality, interpretability, efficiency, and flexibility. In a user study, participants rated the motions pro-duced by our system as better aligned with the interaction context than those generated under a non-modulated or API-level motion condition. Further, participants did not perceive a difference in con-text alignment when using the distilled model, which has a system latency reduced by  81 .5 % on average. Our qualitative experiments show the adaptability of the system to different robots as well as the handling of multimodal context inputs, including images, text, and sounds. Future extensions could include higher-level cognitive com-ponents, such as an episodic memory or a theory-of-mind model, to integrate continuous perceptions into a coherent and persistent context representation, as well as extending the system to verbal outputs combined with synchronized co-speech gestures.
