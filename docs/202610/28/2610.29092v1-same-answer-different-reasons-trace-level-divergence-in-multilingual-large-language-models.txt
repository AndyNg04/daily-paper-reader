Abstract 

Multilingual large language models are in-creasingly deployed in settings where visible reasoning traces support verification, edu-cation, and safety auditing, yet standard evaluations typically measure only final-answer agreement or holistic response simi-larity. As a result, they establish outcome consistency without determining whether users across languages receive compara-ble supporting evidence. We introduce a trace-level divergence framework that compares visible reasoning traces as struc-tured sequences, decomposing agreement into structural similarity, order consistency, and holistic similarity, with pair-specific calibration to enable comparison across lan-guages. Across three reasoning benchmarks, seven languages, and six frontier LLMs, the natural baseline—embedding each whole response and comparing the two by cosine— systematically overstates agreement: 52 .1% –

67 .3% of examples in the top quartile of holistic similarity are not in the top quar-tile of structural similarity. This dissocia-tion is stable across sentence encoders and step segmentations, and blind bilingual an-notation studies in two English-anchored language pairs show that human judgments align with structural similarity rather than with the holistic baseline. A same-language control shows that the level of divergence is language-specific: two English traces for paraphrases of the same question agree more closely than an English trace and its counterparts in other languages. Our re-sults show that answer-level and holistic evaluations can substantially overestimate the consistency of multilingual explanatory artifacts, motivating trace-level evaluation for reliable multilingual deployment.

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increas-ingly deployed in settings where they produce not only final answers but also visible reason-ing traces intended to justify those answers. These traces are consumed by users, educators, auditors, and process-supervision systems to understand, verify, and improve model behav-ior (Wei et al., 2022). As multilingual LLMs become widely deployed, an implicit expecta-tion follows: semantically equivalent questions posed in different languages should produce not only compatible answers but also broadly comparable supporting explanations. In this paper, we study the visible chain of thought emitted by the model, not its hidden internal computation. A chain of thought need not faithfully expose the model’s internal reason-ing process (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and we make no claim that it does. Rather, we study the explanatory artifact that downstream users, verifiers, and chain-of-thought monitors directly observe and act upon; our claims concern what that arti-fact contains in each language, not whether it reflects the computation behind it. Existing multilingual evaluations largely mea-sure outcome consistency : whether translated prompts produce the same or correct final an-swer (Shi et al., 2022; Xuan et al., 2025). More recent approaches compare complete responses using holistic semantic similarity, but still col-lapse an entire reasoning trace into a single representation. As a result, two multilingual responses may be judged equivalent even when they expose different intermediate claims, as-sumptions, problem decompositions, or exe-cution order. Some of these differences may simply reflect natural linguistic variation; oth-ers may represent substantively different sup-1arXiv:2610.29092v1 [cs.AI] 21 Oct 2026 porting evidence. Existing evaluations cannot distinguish between them. We therefore argue that multilingual systems should also be evalu-ated for evidentiary consistency : whether users receive comparable supporting evidence for the same conclusion regardless of the language they ask in. To address this problem, we introduce a trace-level divergence framework that compares multilingual reasoning traces as structured se-quences rather than as single holistic responses. Our framework aligns step-structured chains of thought using multilingual sentence embed-dings and decomposes cross-lingual trace consis-tency into three complementary metrics: struc-tural similarity S, measuring preservation of reasoning content; order consistency τ , measur-ing preservation of reasoning order; and holistic similarity H, a whole-response baseline. Be-cause multilingual sentence embeddings exhibit language-pair-dependent similarity floors and ceilings, a pair-specific calibration rescales sen-tence similarities onto a readable scale within each language pair before aggregation. We evaluate the proposed framework on three reasoning benchmarks, seven languages, and six frontier LLMs spanning four model families. We find that whole-answer simi-larity systematically overstates agreement be-tween multilingual reasoning traces. Across all model–dataset evaluations, holistic similarity remains consistently high ( H = 0 .770 –0.857 ), while structural similarity is substantially lower (S = 0 .502 –0.629 ), with order consistency lower still ( τ = 0 .307 –0.470 ), even when both lan-guage outputs produce the same verified-correct answer. Rank-based analyses reach the same conclusion: within each model–dataset evalua-tion and language pair, 52 .1% –67 .3% of exam-ples in the top quartile of holistic similarity are not also in the top quartile of structural similar-ity. Finally, blind bilingual annotation studies in two English-anchored language pairs show that human judgments of reasoning-trace equiv-alence align with structural similarity rather than holistic similarity, indicating that whole-response embeddings systematically overesti-mate agreement in the explanatory artifacts multilingual users actually observe. Further analyses locate the divergence. In a same-language control, where each question is paraphrased in English and run through the identical pipeline, two English traces agree more closely than an English trace and its coun-terparts in other languages in every cell tested, although the holistic–structural dissociation it-self also appears within English. Within a trace, alignment is weakest at the step stating the shared answer, and divergence grows on harder items. Throughout, we read low structural or order similarity as divergence, not failure. Our contributions are threefold. First, we for-mulate multilingual evaluation as the problem of measuring the consistency of visible reason-ing traces, complementing traditional outcome-based evaluation. Second, we introduce a cali-brated trace-level framework that decomposes agreement into content preservation, execution order, and holistic similarity. Third, through a comprehensive empirical study, we show that answer-level and holistic evaluations system-atically overstate the consistency of multilin-gual explanatory artifacts, motivating trace-level evaluation for reliable multilingual deploy-ment. 

2 Problem Formulation 

We study whether multilingual LLMs produce consistent visible reasoning traces when given semantically equivalent prompts in different languages. Our goal is to evaluate two com-plementary notions of consistency: (i) whether multilingual prompts produce the same final answer, and (ii) whether they produce com-parable supporting explanations. The former captures outcome consistency ; the latter cap-tures trace consistency . This section formalizes both notions and introduces the calibration re-quired to compare multilingual reasoning traces across language pairs.

3 Method 

Given two visible reasoning traces produced for semantically equivalent prompts in different lan-guages, our framework measures whether they preserve the same step-level content and order-ing. The pipeline first parses each response into steps (§ 3.1), calibrates multilingual sentence similarities for each language pair (§ 3.2), and constructs a step-pair similarity matrix through two-level greedy alignment (§ 3.3). This matrix yields the structural similarity score S and the order-consistency score τ (§ 3.4). We compute the holistic baseline H separately over complete responses (Appendix B.6). 

3.1 Step Parsing 

We segment each response using line-initial step headers. The parser first matches the prompted English pattern Step n:, followed by its equiva-lents in the seven evaluated languages, a plain numbered-list pattern, and finally paragraph boundaries. The same ordered rule set is ap-plied to every language, and parsing terminates at the final-answer line. Each non-empty step is further divided into sentences using a Unicode-aware regular expression. No LLM is used during parsing. Appendix B.1 provides the complete parsing hierarchy and validation de-tails.

4 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate on three multilingual reasoning benchmarks: MMLU-ProX (Xuan et al., 2025), StrategyQA (Geva et al., 2021), and 

SciBench (Wang et al., 2024). Each run uses seven languages (Arabic, English, Span-ish, Hindi, Russian, Vietnamese, and Chi-nese), yielding 21 unordered language pairs per model–dataset evaluation. The benchmark suite spans distinct reasoning regimes: MMLU-ProX tests multiple-choice academic reasoning, where answer options and domain knowledge constrain the response; StrategyQA tests yes/no commonsense reasoning, where premise interpretation and implicit world knowledge are central; and SciBench tests quantitative STEM reasoning, where traces often combine formula selection, unit conversion, and numeri-cal computation. This spread lets us distinguish dataset effects from model-specific behavior un-der fixed metric definitions. We evaluate six models from four fami-lies: Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5, DeepSeek-V3.2, GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B, and Qwen3-32B, sampling 500 problems per dataset–model run. Trace metrics are reported only after the answer- and trace-validity cas-cade; one-language-correct cases contribute only to ∆acc (Appendix C.1). Metrics follow § 3, using LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022) for step scores, BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024) for H, and soft Kendall-τ sharpening power 3. Table 1 reports problem-level 95% 

bootstrap confidence intervals (2,000 resam-ples), resampling each question’s 21 language pairs jointly. Appendices C.3, C.4, D.8, and D.5 provide filtering details, audits, and ablations. 

5 Results 

Our evaluation asks whether final-answer agree-ment implies trace agreement across languages (§ 5.1), whether whole-answer similarity can substitute for step-level alignment (§ 5.2), whether structural, order, and holistic simi-larity provide complementary signals (§ 5.3), whether the divergence is specific to crossing a language (§ 5.4), where in a trace it appears and what a low score signals (§ 5.5), and whether the resulting metrics are robust and agree with human judgment (§ 5.6). Appendix D provides the supporting re-sult details: pair-level tables (Appendix D.2), cross-dataset and cross-model patterns (Ap-pendix D.3), qualitative verified traces (Ap-pendix E.1), and rejected-sample audits outside the primary verified-correct set (Appendix E.2). 

5.1 Correct Answers Still Hide Trace Divergence 

We first test whether agreement on the final answer implies agreement in the emitted rea-soning trace by restricting to language pairs where both answers are verified correct. If mul-tilingual reasoning were invariant, such pairs would exhibit high structural similarity S, high order consistency τ , and only a small gap be-tween the holistic score H and the step-level score S.However, across the evaluations in Table 1, H

remains consistently high ( 0.770 –0.857 ), while 

S is substantially lower ( 0.502 –0.629 ) and τ

lower still ( 0.307 –0.470 ). The same pattern holds on MMLU-ProX : across six 500-sample evaluations (21 language pairs), S=0 .513 –0.569 ,

τ =0 .348 –0.469 , and H=0 .787 –0.800 .Taken together, these results contradict the invariance hypothesis. Even when answers agree, reasoning traces diverge: responses re-main globally similar as full strings (high H)while differing in step content ( S) and, more markedly, in step ordering ( τ ), particularly on 

MMLU-ProX .

5.3 Structural Coverage and Order Are Non-Redundant

S, τ , and H is necessary by examining whether the three metrics capture distinct variation. If a single trace-level score were sufficient, then

6 Related Work 

Multilingual reasoning evaluation. Chain-of-thought prompting elicits step-by-step rea-soning (Wei et al., 2022), but multilingual eval-uations largely stop at outputs. Shi et al. (2022) measure multilingual CoT accuracy and Barua et al. (2025) extend this to long-form reasoning, while benchmarks such as MMLU-ProX (Xuan et al., 2025) and metrics like RankC (Qi et al., 2023) assess consistency at the level of final answers. Qi et al. (2025) instead control the language a model thinks in and measure the resulting accuracy cost, which is complemen-tary along a distinct axis: they vary the trace language and observe correctness, whereas we hold correctness fixed and measure how far the traces diverge. None of these localizes diver-gence within a trace. 

Trace-level reasoning evaluation. 

ROSCOE (Golovneva et al., 2023) scores reasoning chains at step level in monolingual settings, and we extend that perspective to the cross-lingual case, where similarity must be calibrated per language pair. ROSCOE is not an available baseline here: it scores a single chain against a reference to judge that chain’s quality, whereas we compare two equally valid traces whose quality is held fixed by the validity cascade, and its monolingual encoders would have to be replaced by amultilingual one—reducing the comparison to the uncalibrated step cosines our calibration exists to correct (Appendix C.4). Concurrent work (Zhao et al., 2026) studies cross-lingual transfer of rationales, but without step- or order-level alignment. 

Faithfulness of the visible trace. A sep-arate line of work asks whether an emitted chain of thought reflects the computation that produced the answer: models can rationalize a predetermined answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Lan-ham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and conse-quential computation can leave no interpretable trace in the output tokens at all (Baherwani et al., 2026). Our measurement is orthogonal to this question: we compare two emitted traces and make no claim that either exhausts inter-nal computation. The visible trace is nonethe-less what verification, education, and chain-of-thought monitoring pipelines consume (Korbak et al., 2025), so our claim concerns a monitor’s input, not its faithfulness: whatever a monitor extracts from a trace, it extracts from a differ-ent artifact in each language. This needs only the emitted text, so the framework also applies to closed-weight models. 

Multilingual embeddings and alignment. 

We use LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022) for sen-tence embeddings and BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024) for holistic similarity. Our alignment ex-tends embedding-based matching (Zhang et al., 2020) to sentence- and step-level structure, and uses a soft Kendall-τ inspired by continuous relaxations of permutations (Mena et al., 2018; Grover et al., 2019). Appendix B.8 gives a detailed comparison with prior step-level met-rics. 

7 Conclusion 

We show that agreement on final answers does not imply agreement in the reasoning traces a model emits across languages: holistic sim-ilarity can remain high even when step-level structure and order diverge. The consequence is at the level of the artifact a user reads: for the same correct answer, the supporting evidence a reader is given can depend on the language they asked in—steps another reader never sees, or the same steps in a different order—more than on how the question is worded within one language. It appears in every one of the 21 lan-guage pairs we evaluate, including the typologi-cally closest. By decomposing trace consistency into structural similarity S, order consistency 

τ , and holistic similarity H, our framework reveals complementary axes that single-score evaluations obscure and locates where traces part. The rank-based finding is stable across sentence encoders and step segmentations, and small blind annotation studies on one model’s 9traces in two English-anchored language pairs are consistent with it. Because the dissocia-tion also appears between two English traces, the decomposition extends to process-level con-sistency beyond cross-lingual settings, includ-ing across paraphrases, prompts, and decoding strategies. 10 Limitations 

Visible traces, not internal computation. 

A chain of thought need not be a faithful record of the computation that produced the answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025), and we link the divergence we measure to no internal quantity. The visible trace is what verification, education, and audit-ing pipelines consume, and that is the scope of our claims. Activation-level analysis would ad-dress faithfulness directly, but it would exclude closed-weight models, including two of the six we evaluate. 

Ranks, not levels. What survives an encoder change is rank structure rather than absolute scale (Appendix G), and per-pair calibration aligns only the endpoints of the similarity scale. We therefore compare S and τ by rank within a fixed encoder and read absolute cross-pair val-ues as descriptive only. The one cross-pair com-parison of levels we draw, the same-language control of § 5.4, is paired within question and its calibration exposure is measured, but it re-mains a level comparison. Ceiling estimates additionally rely on LLM translation, whose co-dependence with the evaluated model we bound in Appendix D.1. 

Scope of the evidence. The human valida-tion covers two language pairs, both including English, with 60 verified-correct trace pairs each, all from one model; agreement is mod-erate, which attenuates rather than inflates the reported correlations. We do not estab-lish that the metrics track human judgment for other models or for pairs without English. The same-language control covers two of the six models, about 100 questions per benchmark, and one paraphrase per question, and it does not resample an identical prompt at non-zero temperature, which would isolate decoding vari-ation; its effect is unanimous in direction but varies about threefold in size across cells. Step boundaries are verified automatically and by a manual spot check too small to bound the residual error rate (Appendix B.1.1). 

Divergence and outcomes. The association between low S and shared wrong answers rests on 225 pairs from two language pairs and is largely carried by item difficulty (§ 5.5). We draw no causal conclusion from it, and we do not show that divergence impairs verification, comprehension, or safety; that would require a study with human readers. 

Ethical Considerations 

Data and annotation. Questions come from public reasoning benchmarks and the traces are model-generated, so no personal or sensitive data is involved. Annotators were bilingual speakers informed of the purpose of the study, and no personal data was collected from them; Appendix F.7 reports recruitment and consent in full. 

Artifact release. We will release the eval-uation code under a permissive open-source license. It builds on publicly released models and benchmarks—LaBSE, BGE-M3, and 

paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 ,together with MMLU-ProX , StrategyQA ,and SciBench —and our use and redistri-bution are consistent with their respective licenses. We redistribute no benchmark data and no annotation labels. 

Intended use. The framework diagnoses whether a system reasons the same way in dif-ferent languages; it does not certify reasoning quality. Scores are relative within a fixed en-coder and language pair and should not be used to rank languages, to gate access to a service, or as a training target—traces can be made to agree without any improvement in the under-lying reasoning. Because all pairs in the main analyses are verified-correct, a low score there marks a different route to the same answer, not a wrong one; its association with shared errors supports prioritizing human review, not detecting errors. 

Fairness. Equal accuracy can mask unequal traces, so users who read a model’s explanation may receive systematically different support depending on the language they use.

12 A The Necessity of Per-Language-Pair Calibration 

The trace-divergence measure Dtrace relies on cross-lingual sentence similarities that must be comparable across language pairs. In practice, raw cosine similarities violate this requirement because they conflate semantic relatedness with language-pair-specific properties of the embed-ding space. These differences manifest as pair-dependent similarity floors and ceilings (re-ported in Appendix C.4), making raw cosine scores unsuitable for direct comparison. We therefore normalize sentence similarities using pair-specific calibration before constructing the trace-level metrics. Formally, the structural and order metrics are built from the sentence-level cosine

B Supplementary Method Details 

B.1 Step-Parsing Details 

Each response is segmented using the following ordered fallback hierarchy: 1. the prompted English line-initial pattern 

Step n:; 2. line-initial equivalents of the step marker in the seven evaluated languages; 3. a plain line-initial numbered-list pattern; and 4. paragraph boundaries. Parsing terminates when the final-answer marker is encountered. Empty blocks are dis-carded, and each remaining step is divided into sentences using a Unicode-aware regular expres-sion. The same ordered rule set is applied in every language, and the parser records which tier was used for each response. No LLM or language-specific semantic parser is invoked. We distinguish two notions of parser qual-ity. Coverage asks whether a response yields a non-empty sequence of steps. Boundary fi-delity asks whether the extracted step count agrees with the explicit step markers in the raw output. The corresponding diagnostics by language, model, and dataset, together with a manual spot check of the recovered boundaries, are reported in Appendix B.1.1. 

B.1.1 Parser Validation 

The automatic marker-count comparison evalu-ates whether the implementation faithfully re-covers the boundaries explicitly emitted by the model. It does not establish that those model-provided boundaries constitute the uniquely correct semantic segmentation of the trace. We therefore also read the parsed output manually, inspecting roughly 20 randomly drawn traces per model–dataset cell against the correspond-ing raw responses. In every trace inspected, the recovered boundaries matched the segmen-tation a reader would impose, and we found no case of a step being merged or split against the evident structure of the trace. This au-dit is small: manual boundary inspection is costly, and we did not extend it to a sample large enough to bound the residual error rate. We report it as a spot check supporting the 13 automatic diagnostics rather than as an inde-pendent measurement of boundary fidelity.

B.2.3 What Calibration Does and Does Not Establish 

The floor anchors the calibrated scale at an em-pirical neutral level between unrelated content. The ceiling anchors it at an empirical estimate of the encoder’s attainable similarity between semantically equivalent content for the same language pair. Calibration therefore removes pair-dependent drift at these two endpoints. This procedure does not establish that an intermediate calibrated value has exactly the same semantic interpretation across different language pairs. Translation quality and embed-ding geometry may vary nonlinearly between the estimated endpoints. Accordingly, pooled absolute values should be interpreted descrip-tively. The paper’s central comparison is less de-manding: examples are ranked within a fixed model–dataset–language-pair cell. For a fixed embedding space, any strictly monotone pair-specific rescaling preserves this ordering except where clipping creates ties. The rank-based comparison therefore does not require interme-diate calibrated values to be globally semanti-cally identical. 

B.2.4 Rank Invariance of the Headline Statistic 

The rank comparison reported in §5.2 is the share of pair–question instances in the top quar-tile of H that do not fall in the top quartile of S,14 with both thresholds taken within a single eval-uation and language pair. Within such a cell the calibration of Eq. (4) is a fixed affine rescal-ing followed by clipping to [0 , 1] . It is monotone, so it preserves the ordering of instances in the cell and leaves the quantile thresholds at the same instances; the greedy alignment of §3.3 selects matches by argmax and is likewise in-variant. The only way calibration could move the statistic is by clipping distinct values onto a shared endpoint and creating ties. H is an uncalibrated cosine, so any movement would fall entirely on S.The residual tie effect is nil in practice. Re-computing the statistic on the uncalibrated scale—recovered by inverting the stored per-pair floor and ceiling—reproduces the cali-brated value on all three Claude Opus 4.6 eval-uations, with every difference below 10 −4 (Ta-ble 5). B.2.5 Ceiling-Percentile Sensitivity 

We assess the stability of the estimated ceil-ings by varying the self-translation percentile while holding the sampled translation pool fixed. This analysis measures sensitivity of the endpoint estimator rather than sensitivity to a change in encoder. 

B.2.6 Translator Independence 

The ceiling translator can, in some evaluations, belong to the same broad model family as the model whose traces are being analyzed. We test whether this creates a family-specific cali-bration artifact by re-estimating the language-pair ceilings with an independent-family trans-lator and recomputing the metrics; per-pair values and rank correlations are reported in Appendix D.1. The experiment tests the de-pendence of the estimated calibration endpoints on the translation model. It does not imply that machine translation is equally reliable for every language, particularly for lower-resource languages.

B.5 Controlled Metric Checks 

We use controlled perturbations to verify that the metrics respond to their intended dimen-sions. Removing a reasoning step should re-duce structural similarity, because one trace no longer covers all content in the other. Rewrit-ing a step without altering its semantic content should preserve structural similarity, subject to the multilingual encoder’s robustness. Di-viding one reasoning move into multiple steps, or combining adjacent moves, should be toler-ated by the many-to-one correspondences in the structural score. Permuting otherwise un-changed steps should leave structural similarity relatively stable while reducing order consis-tency. Appendix D.7 reports S, τ , and H for each perturbation family. These checks estab-lish dimension-specific metric behavior under known transformations; they complement, but do not replace, validation on naturally gener-ated multilingual traces.

denote the whole-response encoder. We define 

H(ra, r b) = cos( ψ(ra), ψ (rb)) . (16) Unlike S, the holistic baseline does not align individual steps or identify where two traces differ. Unlike τ , it does not explicitly mea-sure preservation of step order. It serves as a comparison against the common practice of representing an entire response with a single embedding. 

Why BGE-M3 for H and LaBSE for 

S. Step-level structural similarity S uses LaBSE (Feng et al., 2022) because it is the standard cross-lingual sentence encoder and individual reasoning steps comfortably fit its 256-token context. The whole-trace baseline H

instead uses BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024) be-cause LaBSE’s 256-token limit truncates a sub-stantial fraction of our reasoning traces (espe-cially on SciBench and StrategyQA , where a single verified trace frequently exceeds 1,000 tokens), and in preliminary tests the resulting LaBSE-on-truncated-trace cosines were uninfor-mative as a holistic signal. BGE-M3’s 8K-token window covers the entire trace without trunca-tion in our evaluations, which is the property a holistic baseline is meant to provide. Adopting LaBSE on both sides would therefore not iso-late the aggregation effect from H−S; it would replace the encoder asymmetry with a trun-cation artifact whose magnitude depends on dataset-specific trace-length distributions. We accordingly do not interpret the absolute magni-tude of H−S in Table 1 as a calibrated distance, and rely on the scale-free rank comparison in Figure 1 for the qualitative claim that holistic similarity overstates structural consistency. 

B.7 Interpretation of Structural and Order Scores 

The two primary trace-level metrics capture complementary properties. 

High S, high τ . The traces contain similar step-level content in a similar order. 

High S, low τ . The traces contain similar reasoning moves but arrange them differently. 

Low S, high τ . The subset of content that can be aligned appears in a consistent order, but substantial content is absent or unmatched. 

Low S, low τ . The traces differ both in step-level content and in the ordering of their matched components. Neither metric determines whether a differ-ence is harmful. They identify and decompose observable differences in the visible explana-tory artifacts, which can then be inspected by human or automated evaluators. 

B.8 Relation to Prior Step-Level Metrics 

We compare against prior step-level metrics to clarify which parts of the framework are reused and which are specific to cross-lingual rea-soning. The method recomposes BERTScore-16 style greedy matching (Zhang et al., 2020), ROSCOE-style step alignment (Golovneva et al., 2023), multilingual sentence embed-dings (Feng et al., 2022), and soft ordering ideas from continuous sorting (Mena et al., 2018; Grover et al., 2019). The main difference is that our alignment is calibrated per language pair before ag-gregation. Prior step metrics typically use raw within-language cosine scores, while cross-lingual traces require a pair-specific unrelated-sentence floor and self-translation ceiling so that a score for, for example, en-es has the same interpretation as a score for hi-zh .The second difference is that we report struc-ture and order separately. A single alignment score can hide whether two traces contain differ-ent reasoning moves or contain similar moves in a different sequence; S and τ are kept separate so these two kinds of divergence remain visible.

29 F Human Validation of the Trace-Divergence Metrics 

This appendix gives the protocol, sampling pro-cedure, and full statistics behind §5.6. 

F.1 Protocol Task. Each item presents an English source question, the final answer (both languages reached the same verified-correct answer), and two reasoning traces labeled Trace A and Trace B. Annotators judge whether the traces reach the answer by the same reasoning. Three fields are recorded per item: • equivalence , the primary variable: Same 

/ Partially different / Substantially differ-ent , coded ordinally as 2/1/0.• diff_* , a multi-label difference taxonomy: wording, granularity, missing content, dif-ferent strategy, different order. • material , binary: would the difference change what a reviewer concludes when using the trace to verify the answer? A bi-nary item yields a more stable per-stratum rate at this sample size than a Likert scale. 

Blinding. Annotators never see S, τ , H, or any stratum assignment; scores live only in a hidden key. The assignment of English and non-English traces to Trace A and Trace B 

is randomized per item to remove positional bias. Every item is labeled independently by at least two annotators, with a shared calibration subset labeled by all four. Annotators do not confer during labeling. 

Annotators. Four bilingual annotators per language pair, disjoint across the two studies. §F.5 reports a shared-anchor control establish-ing that the two groups do not differ in labeling threshold. 

F.2 Sampling 

Both studies draw from Claude Opus 4.6 out-puts pooled over MMLU-ProX , SciBench ,and StrategyQA , restricted to pairs where both languages are answer-correct and both traces parse into at least two steps, with du-plicate question text removed. The EN–ES eligible pool contains n = 1 ,178 pairs. Items are sampled crossed on H and S: both metrics are split at pool-relative terciles and items are drawn from every cell of the resulting 

3 × 3 grid, with a round-robin over datasets inside each cell. This matters for interpretation. Stratifying on S alone while holding H high would suppress H’s variance by construction, so a null correlation for H would be partly guaranteed by the design rather than measured. Sampling crossed avoids that: both metrics span their natural ranges, and any asymmetry between them is a property of the metrics rather than of the sample. Table 20 confirms the design achieved this. Sample standard deviations closely match the pool for both metrics in both studies, and the sample correlation between H and S reproduces the pool value ( 0.25 against a pool 0.254 for EN–ES), so the design induces no dependence between the two axes.

F.4 Comparison with an LLM-as-Judge Baseline 

A natural alternative to a structural metric is to ask a strong model directly whether two traces reason the same way. We ran that baseline on the 60 English–Chinese pairs of the initial annotation round, giving the judge (GPT-5.6) the identical rubric our annotators used, both traces, and no similarity scores. The structural metric predicts human-judged equivalence more closely than the judge does. On these items ρ(equiv , S ) = 0 .419 (p = 0 .004 )and ρ(equiv , τ ) = 0 .413 (p = 0 .005 ), while the judge reaches ρ = 0 .235 (p = 0 .12 , n.s.) and the holistic baseline ρ = 0 .129 (n.s.). The judge also agrees with the human consensus less than the annotators agree with one another (pairwise agreement 0.576 against 0.633 ), and entering it as a fifth coder halves ordinal Krippendorff’s α,from 0.462 to 0.237 : relative to the consensus it behaves as an outlier rather than as a sub-stitute for it. Its errors are systematic rather than noisy—exact agreement with the human majority is 0.422 but within-one agreement is 

0.956 , so it is nearly always adjacent on the ordinal scale. The failure mode is the one the framework targets. The judge never applied the Substan-tially different label at any point, and its mean rating does not vary monotonically across S

strata. On high-H / low-S pairs it rates as 

Same or Partially different items that annota-tors unanimously call substantially different— for instance a multiple-choice item answered correctly in both languages where one trace eliminates every option exhaustively and the other identifies the answer directly ( S = 0 .46 ,

H = 0 .80 ; human Substantially different , judge 

Same ). We state the strength of this comparison pre-cisely: the confidence intervals for S and for the judge overlap, so the defensible claim is that S

is at least as faithful to human judgment as the judge and, unlike the judge, statistically signif-icant on this sample. Beyond faithfulness, S

is deterministic, localizes divergence to specific steps, and separates content from order at no per-call cost.

We reproduce the instructions given to anno-tators. The guide opens by stating that no similarity score of any kind will be shown and that only the reasoning is to be judged. 

What is being labeled. Each item gives an English source question, the final answer (both languages reached the same verified-correct an-swer), and two reasoning traces labeled Trace A 

and Trace B , one English and one non-English, with the side assignment randomized per item. Annotators read both and decide whether the traces reach the answer by the same reason-ing , recording three fields per item with fixed allowed values. 

Field 1: equivalence (the primary label, ex-actly one value). • Same — the two traces use the same rea-soning: same key claims, same logical path. Pure wording or translation differences still count as Same .• Partially different — largely the same rea-soning, but with a real difference in one part: a step present on one side is miss-ing or merged on the other, or one sub-argument differs, while the overall path is recognizably the same. • Substantially different — the traces rea-son differently: different strategy, different decomposition, different premises or evi-dence, or different logical order of the core steps. A reader could not treat one as a faithful stand-in for the other. 

Field 2: diff_* (difference type; mark each that applies, skipped entirely when 

equivalence is Same ). diff_wording :only phrasing or translation differs. 

diff_granularity : same content, split into more, fewer, or uneven steps. diff_missing :a reasoning step or claim present on one side is absent on the other. diff_strategy :a genuinely different solution approach or argument. diff_order : the same core steps occur in a different order. diff_other : with a free-text description. 

Field 3: material (binary). Would the differ-ence change what a human reviewer concludes when using the trace to verify the answer or understand how it was reached? If the traces are the same or differ only cosmetically, No . If a reviewer auditing one trace would reach a materially different understanding of why the answer holds than from the other, Yes .

Rules of thumb. The guide instructs anno-tators to translate mentally and compare logic rather than surface tokens, noting that two traces in different languages will never be word-identical and that this alone is not a difference; to disregard correctness, since both answers are already verified correct, and to judge only whether the reasoning is the same rather than whether one trace is better; to pick the more different label when torn between two and add a note; and not to discuss items with other annotators while labeling, so that judgments remain independent. 

Language check. Annotators are asked to 32 flag any trace written in the wrong language— one that should be in Spanish but is actually in English, or the reverse—with a fixed token in the notes field, then label the item as normal. This is requested particularly of the EN–ES annotators, because English and Spanish share the Latin alphabet and a reversion that an automatic script-level check cannot see is im-mediately obvious to a bilingual reader. 

Calibration. All annotators for a study first label a shared calibration subset. After those are complete the group meets briefly to com-pare judgments and settle the wording of edge cases, and the remaining items are then la-beled independently. Annotators returning for the EN–ZH top-up batch were instructed that the labels and their definitions were unchanged and to apply exactly the standard they had used previously, without re-reading their ear-lier files to match specific judgments, since the two batches are pooled in analysis. 

F.7 Annotator Recruitment and Consent 

Annotators were unpaid bilingual volunteers from the authors’ academic and professional networks, based in Asia and North America, all adults with professional or native proficiency in both languages of the pair they annotated. Par-ticipation was voluntary and could be stopped at any time; annotators were told the purpose of the study and how their labels would be used, and consented on that basis. The burden was under thirty minutes per annotator for English– Chinese and under ninety minutes for English– Spanish. No personal data was collected, and none is retained in any released artifact. We did not seek ethics-board review: the study collects no personal or sensitive data, involves no decep-tion or intervention, and asks adult volunteers only to compare model-generated text derived from public benchmarks—a minimal-risk pro-tocol under our institutional framework. 

G Encoder Sensitivity 

Methodological concern. S and τ are com-puted from sentence embeddings, so a natural question is how much of the reported H−S con-trast is a property of reasoning traces and how much is a property of the encoders. Two dis-tinct worries sit inside this. First, H and S are produced by different encoders in the main ex-periments (BGE-M3 and LaBSE respectively), so the gap between them could be an artifact of that asymmetry. Second, the framework might require a strong encoder to work at all, which would limit its usefulness. 

Setup. We recompute S and τ from scratch under two alternative step encoders, on three Claude Opus 4.6 evaluations at full retained size ( StrategyQA n = 451 , MMLU-ProX 

n = 431 , SciBench n = 362 ), across all 21 

language pairs. The per-pair floor and ceiling are re-derived for each encoder rather than reused, since calibration constants estimated for one embedding space do not transfer. The alternatives are chosen to probe the two worries separately: • BGE-M3 (568 M parameters, 1024 dimen-sions, retrieval-contrastive objective) is the encoder H already uses, so running S on it removes the H–S encoder asymmetry entirely. This is the only sound direction for that swap: placing LaBSE on H would truncate whole traces at its 256 -token limit, whereas individual steps fit comfortably in both encoders. • paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 

(118 M parameters, 384 dimensions, paraphrase-distillation objective) is a four-to five-fold reduction in scale from LaBSE (471 M) and a third training objective. Its 128 -token limit is not binding here, because the encoding unit is sentences within steps rather than whole steps: median sentence length is 56 characters in English and 28 in Chinese, with 99 th percentiles of 237 and 124 .

Validation of the reimplementation. Be-fore comparing encoders we re-ran the identical pipeline with LaBSE, the encoder used in the main experiments, and recovered the cached val-ues (Spearman 0.959 on S, mean |∆S|= 0 .0044 ,top-quartile mismatch unchanged). This appendix gives the design and full results of the same-language control summarized in §5.4. 

H.1 Design The paraphrase condition. Every reported 

S and τ in the main evaluation compares traces across a language boundary, so the level of divergence alone cannot distinguish an effect of changing language from ordinary variation in generated reasoning. The control adds an eighth condition, en para : each English question is rewritten as a meaning-preserving English paraphrase and answered under the same sys-tem prompt, decoding (greedy, temperature 0,no reasoning mode), parser, retention cascade, and metric settings as every other condition. en para receives the same appended language instruction as a translated condition, naming English, so that the only uncontrolled difference between the pairs (en , en para ) and (en , ℓ ) is the language of the output. The paraphrases are produced by Claude Opus 4.6 with the prompt below, which asks for as little surface overlap with the original as the language allows: a para-phraser left to itself restates a question nearly verbatim, which would collapse the condition onto the source and measure nothing. 

Question paraphrase: system prompt 

> You are rewriting exam questions into different wording in the same language. Produce a paraphrase that a reader would judge to ask exactly the same thing, while sharing as little surface wording with the original as the language allows. Change sentence structure, word order, and vocabulary. Prefer different but equivalent phrasings and constructions. Preserve exactly: the meaning, the scope, every number, unit, symbol, variable, equation, named entity, and the specific quantity or decision the question asks for. Do not make the question easier or harder, do not add or remove information, do not add context, hints, or commentary, and do not answer the question. Return only the rewritten question text.

Question paraphrase: user message 

> Language: English (unchanged) Rewrite the following question in different wording, in the same language. Share as little surface wording with the original as possible while asking exactly the same thing. Original question: {question}

Calibration of the same-language pair. 

Floors are estimated within this run for all 28

Same-language ceiling: system prompt 

> You are a careful sentence rewriter. Rewrite the given sentence in the same language using different wording, preserving its meaning, technical terms, symbols, and numbers exactly. Change word choice and sentence structure where the language allows it; do not return the sentence unchanged. Do not add, omit, explain, or reformat anything. Return only the rewritten sentence.

Comparison and reporting rule. For each question, ∆S is S(en , en para ) minus the mean of S(en , ℓ ) over the English-anchored cross-lingual pairs available for that question. Pair-ing within question matters here because ques-tion identity carries far more of the variance in S than language pair does. The compari-son is against English-anchored pairs because those are the pairs that share the English trace with (en , en para ). The comparison set, the per-question pairing, and the reporting of the result whichever way it fell were fixed before the run, as were decision thresholds for MMLU-ProX 

(0.030 ) and SciBench (0.015 ), set from the size 35 of the floor-induced bias measured on stored ar-tifacts before any paraphrase was generated: an advantage for (en , en para ) below the threshold would have been read as no detectable differ-ence. StrategyQA was added to the run after the thresholds were set and has none.

We rank pairs within their own evaluation and lan-guage pair, so that differences in mean sim-ilarity between cells do not stand in for the ordering a reviewer would use, and review the least similar first.
