## Abstract 

Disease diagnosis is a key application of large language model (LLM) agents in healthcare. However, existing medical agents are primarily trained and evaluated with static question-answering data, providing limited opportunities to learn active information acquisition, examination decision-making, and dynamic clinical interaction. To address this limitation, we present ClinVerse , an interactive, real-data-grounded multi-agent environment designed for training and evaluating medical diagnostic agents. Built on 

ClinPatient , a scalable dataset comprising 150k diagnostic cases derived from real-world electronic health records, ClinVerse sup-ports coordinated interactions among six specialized clinical roles. Specifically, a doctor agent starts from an initial patient complaint, progressively gathers clinical evidence through patient interviews and examination requests, and ultimately produces a diagnosis. For each interaction trajectory, a rule-based scoring framework quantifies information acquisition, examination ordering, and di-agnostic accuracy. These trajectory-level scores serve as reward signals for agent training while also enabling scalable evaluation. In experiments, we evaluate various general-purpose and medical-domain models and show that nine self-evolution methods across three categories consistently improve agents’ diagnostic capabilities within ClinVerse. We further analyze the interplay among model capability, environment feedback, and self-evolution methods 1.

## 1 Introduction 

To alleviate the shortage of healthcare resources, medical agents driven by large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being integrated into modern clinical practice, with assisting diagnosis emerging as one of their most important applications [6, 28]. To ensure their reliability in real-world clinical settings, extensive research has been devoted to evaluating and advancing their diag-nostic capabilities [8, 32, 35]. As shown in Table 1, early studies predominantly assess and train medical agents within static single-turn question-answering

(QA) formats derived from medical licensing exams [9, 20, 40, 48]. This paradigm provides medical agents with pre-defined clinical information and requires them to directly formulate a diagnosis, offering standardization and efficiency in model training and eval-uation. However, in real-world clinical practice, physicians must actively gather information through multi-turn patient consulta-tions, coordinate with laboratory and radiology departments to request diagnostic tests, and progressively refine their diagnostic hypotheses based on newly acquired evidence [2, 12]. This funda-mental discrepancy highlights that static QA tasks primarily assess medical agents’ diagnostic reasoning capabilities while overlooking their information acquisition capabilities, thereby failing to fully capture the dynamic and interactive nature of clinical diagnosis. These limitations motivate a shift from static QA toward inter-active simulations of real-world clinical environments that better reflect real-world multi-role interactions. Within such environ-ments, medical agents can continuously improve their diagnostic reasoning capability and information acquisition capability, through iterative interaction, feedback, and self-evolution. Compared with static QA, this shifts the training paradigm from taking medical exams to undergoing clinical residency training .Despite this promising vision, constructing interactive clinical environments remains challenging. The first challenge is how to prevent information leakage while supporting open-ended clinical interactions . During patient simulation, granting simula-tors access to complete clinical records may inadvertently expose ground-truth diagnoses or unrevealed examination results, under-mining the authenticity of clinical interactions. Moreover, when agents request examinations absent from electronic health records (EHRs), existing environments [4, 16, 27] often return "unavailable" or "normal", potentially exposing missingness patterns that agents can exploit as diagnostic shortcuts. These issues highlight the diffi-culty of preserving controlled information access while enabling flexible clinical interactions. The second challenge is how to provide reliable trajectory-level feedback for evaluating and improving medical agents .

Unlike static QA tasks that primarily assess final-answer correct-ness, interactive clinical diagnosis involves multi-turn trajectories of information acquisition, examination decisions, and diagnostic reasoning, requiring evaluation of both the clinical decision-making process and its outcomes [23, 35]. However, expert-based evalua-tion, as adopted in MIRA [5], is costly and difficult to scale, while LLM-as-a-judge approaches [4, 14] may introduce model-dependent biases and sensitivity to prompting strategies [36, 47]. To address these challenges, we propose ClinVerse , an interac-tive, EHR-grounded multi-agent environment designed for training and evaluating medical diagnostic agents. To achieve scalability and open-endedness while preserving clinical grounding, we develop an EHR-to-environment transformation framework that con-verts real-world clinical records into interactive diagnostic environ-ments. Specifically, an automated data construction pipeline enables the scalable construction of ClinPatient , a dataset comprising 150k EHR-derived patient cases, reducing dependence on extensive manual curation. We further introduce a dual-layer patient simula-tor with a self-check mechanism that separates patient-observable information from latent clinical states, mitigating unintended dis-closure of privileged evidence. Meanwhile, missing examination results in EHRs are addressed by ClinLab , a trained physiological-state predictor that infers clinically plausible results. To provide scalable and reproducible trajectory-level feedback, we develop a rule-based evaluation framework grounded in structured clinical evidence and ICD-10 diagnosis labels. Rather than relying on subjective judgments of entire conversations, this framework evaluates interaction trajectories using deterministic rules across three complementary dimensions: information acquisi-tion, examination coverage, and diagnostic accuracy. These metrics characterize both the evidence acquisition process and the result-ing diagnostic outcomes, providing consistent feedback for agent evaluation and subsequent self-evolution. Built upon these components, ClinVerse coordinates six spe-cialized roles to simulate the core workflow of clinical diagnosis, where a doctor agent, coordinated by the environment controller, progressively acquires patient information through multi-turn con-sultations with the observable patient simulator, requests exam-ination results from the laboratory physician supported by the latent patient simulator, and ultimately formulates a diagnosis. The resulting interaction trajectory is then evaluated by a judge. Consequently, ClinVerse can serve as both an evaluation plat-form and a training environment for medical agents. We evaluate nine general-purpose and medical-domain agents, identifying their strengths and limitations in interactive diagnosis. We further inves-tigate multiple self-evolution methods across three levels, demon-strating that agents can improve their capabilities by learning from interaction trajectories generated within ClinVerse. Beyond perfor-mance improvements, our analyses reveal that evidence acquisition remains a major bottleneck, while collecting more information does not necessarily lead to better diagnoses, highlighting the impor-tance of evidence prioritization and integration. We also examine how trajectory effectiveness depends on the capabilities of source and target models, and whether learned skills transfer to other clinical environments. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We introduce ClinVerse , a realistic diagnostic environment that supports open-ended multi-turn interactions among six specialized roles and enables both the self-evolving and evalua-tion of medical agents. 

• We develop a scalable pipeline for transforming EHR data into diagnostic cases and use it to construct ClinPatient , a dataset containing 150k patient cases. We design a dual-layer patient simulator with a self-check mechanism and train a physiological-state predictor to infer missing examination results. 

• We demonstrate the effectiveness of ClinVerse as a training environment and validate its reliability as an evaluation plat-form, and further analyze the interplay among the environment, model, and self-evolution methods..

## 2 Setup of ClinVerse 

As illustrated in Figure 1, ClinVerse is a realistic clinical diagnostic environment grounded in real-world EHR data. It comprises six specialized roles that interact freely throughout the consultation, enabling the doctor agent to progressively gather evidence and produce a final diagnosis. The resulting interaction trajectories are evaluated using three categories of rule-based criteria and can subsequently be utilized for both model assessment and training.

## 2.2 Six Roles in ClinVerse 

To simulate realistic clinical interactions while maintaining con-trolled information access, ClinVerse comprises six specialized roles: an observable patient simulator, a latent patient simulator, a laboratory physician, a doctor agent, an environment controller, and a diagnostic judge. As illustrated in Figure 1, the doctor agent inter-acts with the patient simulators and laboratory physician through

the environment controller, progressively acquiring clinical evi-dence before submitting a final diagnosis. The diagnostic judge evaluates the resulting interaction trajectory, providing feedback for agent evaluation and subsequent self-evolution. 

Observable Patient Simulator. Existing patient simulators [13, 27] often grant a single model access to the complete patient record, increasing the risk of information leakage under leading or adversar-ial queries. To address this issue, we adopt a dual-layer patient sim-ulation architecture that separates patient-observable information from latent clinical states. The observable patient simulator is re-stricted to patient-reportable information, including demographics, chief complaints, subjective symptoms, medical history, and person-ality traits, while excluding ground-truth diagnoses, clinician-only records, and unrevealed examination results. A self-check mecha-nism further verifies each generated response against the patient’s accessible information and underlying case evidence, revising or rejecting privileged, unsupported, or premature disclosures. Validly revealed information units are recorded for subsequent trajectory-level evaluation. 

Latent Patient Simulator. Complementing the observable pa-tient simulator, the latent patient simulator maintains the complete EHR-derived case state, including the ground-truth diagnosis, lon-gitudinal medical history, physiological measurements, and exami-nation results. It does not interact directly with the doctor agent but provides case-specific clinical information to authorized roles, par-ticularly the laboratory physician. By separating patient-reportable evidence from privileged clinical records, the dual-layer architec-ture preserves the partial observability of clinical diagnosis and mitigates information leakage. The effectiveness of this design, together with the self-check mechanism, is evaluated in Section 6.1. 

Laboratory Physician. The laboratory physician handles phys-ical examination requests, laboratory tests, and imaging examina-tions issued by the doctor agent. As illustrated in Figure 1, each request follows a two-stage process: if the corresponding result is recorded in the original EHR, the laboratory physician retrieves it from the latent patient simulator; otherwise, it invokes ClinLab ,a trained physiological-state predictor, to infer a clinically plausible result from the case context and available observations. This design avoids directly returning missingness responses for unrecorded examinations, as in prior environments [4, 5], thereby supporting flexible examination requests while reducing the risk of exploiting systematic missingness as a diagnostic shortcut. Both recorded and predicted results are standardized into structured reports, including measurements, units, reference ranges, and abnormality indica-tors when applicable. The training and evaluation of ClinLab are detailed in Appendix I and Section 6.2, respectively. 

Doctor Agent. The doctor agent is the primary role evaluated and trained within ClinVerse. Starting from a limited initial pa-tient presentation, it progressively acquires clinical evidence and formulates diagnostic hypotheses through multi-turn interactions. Its action space comprises three types: patient inquiry, exami-nation requests, and final diagnosis submission . Examination requests cover physical examinations, laboratory tests, microbiolog-ical tests, and imaging studies. At each turn, the doctor selects an action based on the accumulated interaction history, reflecting the sequential process of clinical evidence acquisition and diagnostic reasoning. The consultation terminates when the doctor submits a final diagnosis or reaches the interaction limit. 

Environment Controller. The environment controller serves as the central coordinator of clinical interactions, connecting the doctor agent with the supporting roles while enforcing information access boundaries. It validates the doctor’s actions, routes patient inquiries to the observable patient simulator and examination re-quests to the laboratory physician, and returns the corresponding observations to the doctor. Throughout the consultation, it main-tains the interaction history and records information disclosure and examination events as hidden metadata for subsequent evalua-tion. This centralized coordination ensures that the doctor obtains clinical evidence only through authorized interactions, preserving the partial observability and consistency of the diagnostic process. 

Diagnostic Judge. The diagnostic judge evaluates the complete interaction trajectory without participating in the consultation. Us-ing the ground-truth case information and interaction metadata maintained by the environment controller, it applies deterministic scoring rules across three dimensions: patient information collec-tion, examination coverage, and diagnostic accuracy at different levels of disease granularity. These scores provide reproducible feed-back on both evidence acquisition and diagnostic outcomes, sup-porting systematic agent evaluation and subsequent self-evolution. The scoring criteria are detailed in Section 2.4. 

## 2.3 Interaction Flow 

As illustrated in Figure 1, each diagnostic episode begins with the environment controller initializing an EHR-derived case from Clin-Patient and providing the doctor agent with a limited initial pre-sentation. At each turn, the doctor selects a clinical action based on the accumulated interaction history, which is validated and routed by the environment controller. Patient inquiries are directed to the observable patient simulator, which generates self-checked responses based on patient-accessible information. Physical exami-nation and test requests are handled by the laboratory physician, which retrieves recorded findings from the latent patient simulator or invokes ClinLab to predict unavailable results. The returned observations are incorporated into the interaction history, allowing the doctor to progressively update its diagnostic reasoning and determine subsequent actions.

## 3.1 From EHRs to a Diagnostic Dataset 

We construct ClinPatient from structured records in SICdb [30] and MIMIC-IV v3.1 [10], together with clinical narratives from MIMIC-IV-Note [11]. These sources are integrated into admission-level datasets, SICdb-Ext and MIMIC-IV-Ext, by aggregating demo-graphics, ICD diagnoses, medications, laboratory and microbiology results, radiology records, and clinical narratives extracted from discharge summaries. We retain only admissions with sufficient evidence, remove diagnosis-revealing text, and preserve only the 

Findings section of radiology reports. The primary diagnosis is normalized to ICD-10 and stored as the hidden ground-truth label 

𝑦 , while the processed records provide the acute clinical context and retrievable examination evidence E without exposing clinician-only information to the observable patient simulator. The statistics of ClinPatient are shown in the Figure 2. 

## 3.2 Data Augmentation 

To ensure the realism of the environment, we model patients along three complementary dimensions, including patient profile, clin-ical status, and social attributes. Because real-world admission records often lack social attributes of patients, we augment each case with a conditionally generated Synthea 3 profile containing patient-reportable information, including prior conditions, long-term medications, allergies, immunization history, and social his-tory. Generation is conditioned on age, sex, and secondary ICD diagnoses, while excluding the primary diagnosis to prevent label leakage. Inconsistent profiles and events occurring after admission or revealing the diagnosis are removed. Each case is also assigned a Big Five personality profile along the OCEAN dimensions to control conversational behaviors such as information disclosure, response conciseness, and anxiety expression [22]. Personality affects only dialogue generation, while all clinical observations and diagnos-tic targets remain grounded in the original EHR, enabling richer patient simulation without introducing diagnostic shortcuts.

## 3.3 Information Unit Extraction 

To support controlled information disclosure and trajectory-level evaluation, we transform long content into a structured set of atomic information units, following prior work [29]. Specifically, we extract facts from demographics, the history of present illness, and other patient-related contents, and normalize it into a set U.Each unit is assigned a semantic category, an importance label, and a stable identifier. The subset revealed in the initial presenta-tion is denoted by U0 ⊆ U , while the remaining units must be acquired through interaction with the observable patient simulator. Examination-derived evidence, including physical examination, lab-oratory, microbiology, and radiology findings, is stored separately in E and accessed through the laboratory physician. This separa-tion preserves role-specific information boundaries and provides structured supervision for the scoring functions in Section 2.4. 

## 4 Experiments 

We envision ClinVerse as an interactive clinical consultation envi-ronment that supports both the evaluation and training of medical agents. In this section, we empirically assess its effectiveness for training and evaluation through a series of experiments. 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Metrics. We evaluate doctor agents using five rule-based metrics introduced in Section 2.4. All metrics are computed on 100 cases randomly sampled from a held-out test set with 5 independent runs. 

Models. We evaluate doctor agents from diverse model fami-lies and parameter scales, including general-purpose and medical-domain models such as Lingshu-32B [42], Baichuan-M2-32B [34], and the HealthGPT-Pro series [19]. ## 4.2 ClinVerse as an Evaluation Platform 

As presented in Table 2, we evaluate agents’ performance across the full consultation process, covering patient information elicitation, examination ordering, and evidence integration for final diagnosis. GPT-5.6-Luna achieves the strongest overall performance among the evaluated agents, yet a clear gap remains relative to the full-information reference, indicating that effective evidence acquisition is still a major bottleneck in interactive diagnosis. The five metrics further reveal distinct behavioral profiles that cannot be captured by final diagnostic accuracy alone. For example, Baichuan-M2-32B performs well in identifying relevant and abnormal examinations but does not consistently convert this advantage into more accurate diagnoses, suggesting limitations in evidence integration or clinical reasoning. In contrast, Lingshu-32B achieves comparatively strong diagnostic performance despite rarely requesting examinations, implying a greater reliance on prior knowledge or initially avail-able information. By separately evaluating information seeking, examination decisions, and diagnostic inference under controlled information boundaries, ClinVerse exposes where each doctor agent succeeds or fails throughout the consultation process. 

## 4.3 ClinVerse as an Evolution Environment 

Beyond evaluating static agents, we further investigate how three categories of adaptation and evolution strategies improve Qwen3-32B within ClinVerse. As shown in Table 2, these methods pro-duce distinct capability profiles. MemRL-online mainly improves Information Collection Rate and Diagnosis Subtype Accuracy, al-though its information-collection performance exhibits substantial variance, whereas MemRL-offline yields more balanced gains in examination coverage and diagnosis. Dynamic Cheatsheet achieves the strongest overall adaptation results, substantially improving Test Order Coverage, Abnormal Test Coverage, and both diagnostic metrics despite collecting less patient-reported information. This pattern suggests that adaptation can improve diagnosis by shifting evidence acquisition toward more informative examinations rather than uniformly increasing the amount of collected evidence. All three categories improve diagnostic performance, but their efficiency differs. Online Self-Evolution achieves larger diagnostic gains using 1,000 optimization trajectories than Offline Adaptation using 5,000, indicating substantially greater experience efficiency. Recursive Self-Improvement provides more limited gains under the same setting, suggesting that optimizing the update mechanism introduces additional search and credit-assignment demands that are not necessarily compensated for by the available feedback. 

## 5 Further Analysis 5.1 Info Acquisition & Diagnostic Convergence 

Figure 3(a–b) shows that information acquisition is concentrated in the early consultation turns and then exhibits clear diminish-ing returns, whereas diagnostic agreement generally continues to increase as the interaction proceeds. Stronger models achieve both higher information coverage and more consistent diagnoses, suggesting that effective evidence acquisition facilitates diagnos-tic convergence. Notably, agreement can continue improving af-ter information collection has largely plateaued, indicating that later turns may help models confirm and integrate existing evi-dence rather than acquire new facts. The occasional fluctuations in agreement further reflect evidence-driven revision of intermediate hypotheses during the consultation. 

## 5.2 Temporal Analysis of Clinical Actions 

To analyze interaction dynamics, we visualize the turn-level action distributions of three doctor agents. As shown in Figure 3(c), all models exhibit a common coarse-grained workflow: early turns focus on patient inquiry, middle turns increasingly involve physical examinations and laboratory tests, and final diagnoses dominate toward the end. However, the transitions vary across models. GPT-5.6-Luna and Qwen3-32B shift relatively smoothly between action types, whereas Baichuan-M2-32B shows more abrupt changes, sug-gesting less stable long-horizon planning. These results show that ClinVerse captures not only final performance but also the tem-poral organization of clinical decision-making.

## 5.5 Cross-Methods Improvement 

We further investigate how self-evolution methods affect mod-els of different scales. As shown in Figure 4, all methods yield positive gains, with performance progressively approaching the full-information reference. Improvements in information collec-tion capability, however, are not always accompanied by gains in diagnostic accuracy. For Qwen3-32B, stronger information collec-tion generally correlates with higher diagnostic accuracy, whereas GPT-5.6-luna can achieve better diagnostic performance with less collected information. This indicates that a substantial portion of patient information may be redundant or weakly relevant to di-agnosis, and that self-evolution can help models focus on more informative evidence while reducing unnecessary information ac-quisition. We also observe a scale-dependent pattern for the L3 RSI method. It provides larger gains for Mid-tier models, while weaker and stronger models benefit less, consistent with the trend reported by Lin et al. [18].

## 7 Conclusion 

We propose ClinVerse, a scalable real-data-grounded multi-agent environment for medical diagnostic agents. Built on ClinPatient, it supports realistic interactions, flexible examinations, and rule-based evaluation of evidence acquisition and diagnosis. Experiments vali-date the effectiveness of ClinVerse as an evolution environment and its reliability as an evaluation platform. 

## Limitations and Ethical Considerations 

This study uses only de-identified clinical data from SICdb [30], MIMIC-IV v3.1 [10], and MIMIC-IV-Note [11], accessed through PhysioNet under the required credentialing and Data Use Agree-ment procedures. We used these data solely for research and made no attempt to identify patients or institutions. Raw EHRs were trans-formed into structured admission-level cases, and doctor agents accessed only de-identified, role-specific information. Any released artifacts will comply with original licenses and will not include raw records or re-identifiable information. 

## Generative AI Usage 

Generative AI tools were used only in an assistive capacity dur-ing manuscript preparation and implementation. Specifically, LLM were used for language polishing, grammar and typographical-error checking, and assistance with drafting, debugging, and document-ing research code. All AI-assisted text and code were reviewed, revised, and verified by the authors.

## A Related Work A.1 Diagnostic Decision-Making Evaluation 

Existing evaluations of medical agents can be broadly divided into static and interactive paradigms. Static benchmarks typically formu-late diagnosis as a question-answering task using cases derived from medical licensing examinations [9, 20], textbooks [7], or EHRs [40], with answer accuracy serving as the primary metric. These bench-marks are effective for measuring medical knowledge and diagnos-tic reasoning under fixed evidence, but they provide limited insight into how agents acquire information or make sequential clinical decisions. More recent benchmarks [14, 17, 41] therefore introduce multi-turn consultation settings in which agents interact with sim-ulated patients and other clinical roles, enabling evaluation of both diagnostic outcomes and the decision processes that produce them. Evaluating such interaction trajectories, however, remains chal-lenging. MIRA [5] relies on case-specific rules and expert review to assess clinically meaningful behaviors, achieving high evaluation fi-delity at substantial annotation cost. Other systems [4, 41] improve scalability by using LLM-based judges to score interactions across multiple dimensions, but their results may vary with the evaluator model, prompt design, and scoring criteria. In contrast, ClinVerse adopts a rule-based evaluation framework that derives reproducible scores directly from hidden case states and recorded interaction events, covering information collection, examination ordering, and diagnostic accuracy without requiring per-case expert annotation or model-based judging. 

## A.2 Interactive Clinical Environments 

Recent studies [1, 3, 15, 21, 24, 25, 38] have explored interactive clinical environments for evaluating medical agents beyond static question answering. Agent Hospital [14] constructs a flexible hospi-tal sandbox by synthesizing patients and clinical roles from external knowledge sources, but its fully synthetic design may compro-mise clinical fidelity. In contrast, AIHospital [4] and SDBench [27] ground patient simulation in cases collected from online resources or medical literature, providing more realistic clinical scenarios while remaining limited by the scale, completeness, and examina-tion coverage of the source cases. AgentClinic [31] introduces a multimodal clinical environment with four interacting roles, en-abling patient consultations, examination requests, and diagnos-tic decision-making across diverse clinical scenarios. However, its text-parsed interaction protocol and reliance on predefined case findings, with potentially normal responses for unavailable ex-aminations, limit structured clinical interactions and examination flexibility. MIRA [5] further improves evaluation fidelity through expert-curated cases and fine-grained, case-specific assessment criteria, but the associated annotation burden limits scalability. Overall, existing environments face a trade-off among interaction flexibility, clinical grounding, and scalable evaluation. ## B Training Methods Used in Experiments 

We adapt doctor agents within ClinVerse using nine methods. Most of the methods keep the backbone LLM frozen and improve agent behavior through external skills or memories derived from interaction trajectories. 

Trace2Skill [26] collects successful and failed clinical trajec-tories from a frozen doctor agent and analyzes them separately to extract reusable behaviors and causally grounded corrections. These trajectory-level lessons are converted into local skill patches, then hierarchically deduplicated and consolidated into a coherent clinical skill directory. The resulting skill is directly supplied at in-ference time without model updates or per-case memory retrieval. 

SkillRL [39] distills successful trajectories into effective decision patterns and failed trajectories into counterfactual lessons describ-ing errors and preferable actions. It organizes this knowledge into a hierarchical SkillBank containing general clinical principles and task-specific guidance for stages such as history collection, inves-tigation, and diagnosis. Our parameter-free adaptation retrieves relevant skills as contextual guidance while omitting the SFT and reinforcement-learning updates of the original framework. We addi-tionally apply the full SkillRL training pipeline to Qwen3-8B, with details and results reported in Appendix C. 

MemRL [46] represents each clinical episode as an intent – ex-perience – utility triplet in an external memory bank initialized from offline rollouts. At inference time, it first recalls semantically similar experiences and then re-ranks them using learned utility, reducing the influence of relevant-looking but unhelpful memories. Environmental feedback subsequently updates the utilities of re-trieved experiences and may add new memories, enabling online adaptation without changing model weights. 

MedAgent-Zero [14] maintains separate Medical Record Li-braries and Experience Bases for the examination, diagnosis, and treatment stages. Successful cases are stored as validated exam-ples, whereas failed cases are reflected upon and summarized into transferable corrective principles. For each new clinical decision, similar records and failure-derived guidance are jointly retrieved to

support reasoning, allowing both repositories—and thus the doctor agent—to improve continually without parameter updates. 

Dynamic Cheatsheet [33] gives a black-box LLM a persistent, self-curated memory that accumulates concise strategies, code snip-pets, and corrections across test-time interactions. After each case, a curator examines the current memory, query, and attempted so-lution to add useful generalizations and revise or prune unreliable entries; the revised cheatsheet then conditions subsequent cases. This enables online adaptation without ground-truth labels, hu-man feedback, or changes to model parameters, while limiting the context growth caused by appending the full interaction history. 

ACE [45] treats the agent context as an evolving playbook and separates adaptation into generator, reflector, and curator roles. The generator acts using the current playbook, the reflector extracts generalizable lessons from the resulting trajectory and natural exe-cution feedback, and the curator integrates compact delta updates before semantically deduplicating overlapping entries. We use its online setting so that later consultations receive the refined play-book; these localized updates preserve useful clinical detail and mitigate the brevity bias and context collapse associated with re-peatedly rewriting the entire context. 

Self-Taught Optimizer [44] begins with an LLM-powered im-prover program that proposes multiple revisions to an input pro-gram, evaluates them with a task-specific utility function, and re-turns the best candidate. The seed improver is then applied to its own source code, allowing utility feedback to produce a stronger update procedure whose emergent search strategies can include beam search, genetic algorithms, and simulated annealing. The improved improver is subsequently used to optimize agent solu-tions, yielding a form of recursive self-improvement in which the scaffolding evolves while the backbone LLM remains frozen. 

Meta Context Engineering [43] formulates adaptation as bi-level optimization that co-evolves context-engineering skills and the context artifacts produced by those skills. At the meta level, agentic crossover synthesizes a revised skill from the task specifi-cation and the history of prior skills, executions, and evaluations; at the base level, an agent executes that skill to construct con-text as flexible files and code, including both static resources and query-dependent operations such as retrieval and filtering. Itera-tive evaluation selects improved skill–context pairs, allowing both what the doctor agent learns and how it organizes and updates that knowledge to evolve without modifying the backbone model. 

MedRSI [37] enables recursive self-improvement by transform-ing diagnostic failures into new clinical capabilities while keeping the backbone LLM frozen. It prioritizes failures according to their potential clinical consequences and identifies missing capabilities through trajectory analysis and reflection. These capability gaps are addressed through automated code generation, tool composition, or task-specific model training, producing new clinical tools for measurement, prediction, and evidence integration. To ensure reli-able adaptation, MedRSI adopts a fast-discovery, slow-registration mechanism, where candidate tools are evaluated across subsequent patient cohorts and incorporated into a persistent tool registry only after demonstrating sustained diagnostic improvements without increasing clinical risk. ## C Effectiveness of ClinVerse for GRPO Training 

To further examine whether ClinVerse can support parameter-updating reinforcement learning, we adapt the GRPO stage of 

SkillRL to the ClinVerse interaction and reward interface and optimize an SFT-initialized Qwen3-8B doctor agent for 30 steps. During training, the agent conducts multi-turn clinical encounters and receives the rule-based ClinVerse score as its reward, allow-ing the policy to learn directly from its information-gathering and diagnostic decisions. As shown in Table 7, SkillRL-GRPO consistently improves exam-ination decisions and diagnostic performance, indicating that the reward signals provided by ClinVerse can effectively guide policy optimization. Notably, the trained model achieves subtype-level diagnostic performance close to the Full Info reference, despite op-erating under the original partially observable setting. Meanwhile, the decrease in information collection suggests that the optimized policy relies more on examination-based evidence and diagnos-tic inference rather than extensive patient inquiry. These results demonstrate that ClinVerse not only provides effective training signals for GRPO but also enables fine-grained analysis of how optimization reshapes clinical decision-making behaviors.

## H Diagnosis with an Oracle ICD-10 Block 

We further investigate fine-grained diagnostic reasoning under an oracle block-conditioned setting, where models are provided with complete patient information, examination results, and the ground-truth ICD-10 block as a hard constraint. The task is to predict the corresponding three-character diagnostic category within the given block, and predictions violating the constraint are considered incor-rect. As shown in Table 11, oracle block information substantially improves subtype-level diagnosis across models, indicating that coarse disease localization accounts for a major portion of diag-nostic errors. Nevertheless, considerable performance gaps remain between models, demonstrating that fine-grained category selec-tion requires additional clinical reasoning and constraint-following ability beyond access to the correct diagnostic region. The con-sistent ranking with the unconditioned full-information setting further suggests that stronger models maintain advantages even when the search space is narrowed by oracle information. 

## I Training Detail of Physiological-state Predictor 

Training. We construct the training set from laboratory measure-ments in MIMIC-IV-Ext and split samples by hospital admission to prevent admission-level context leakage between training and eval-uation. For each eligible measurement, we apply a leave-one-out masking strategy, where the target laboratory result is removed while all remaining clinical information, including demographics, history, physical examination, diagnoses, medications, microbiol-ogy results, and hospital course, is preserved. The model receives the masked test name, category, unit, and reference range, and is trained to predict only the missing result value. This formulation simulates real scenarios where requested examinations are absent from the source EHR while preventing direct target copying. To emphasize clinically meaningful cases, all abnormal measurements are retained and normal measurements are subsampled. We fine-tune a Qwen3.5-2B backbone with LoRA under the causal language modeling objective. During training, system, user, and padding to-kens are assigned the ignore index −100, ensuring that only the generated laboratory result contributes to the loss. For samples exceeding the context limit, inputs are truncated while preserving the target response.

## K ClinLab Validity Analysis 

To investigate whether ClinLab-generated examination results af-fect diagnostic reliability, we conduct two additional experiments examining the robustness of diagnostic performance to predicted values and missing examination results. 

Robustness to ClinLab predictions. We randomly replace 10% and 20% of available EHR examination values with ClinLab predic-tions and evaluate the resulting diagnostic performance. As shown in Table 12, both category and subtype accuracy remain stable across different replacement ratios, suggesting that the introduc-tion of ClinLab-generated values does not substantially compromise overall diagnostic performance. 

Impact of missing examination results. We further compare ClinLab-based prediction with explicitly returning null for un-available examinations. As shown in Table 13, removing ClinLab leads to higher diagnostic accuracy, particularly at the category level. One possible explanation is that explicit missingness provides additional cues about the underlying diagnosis, which agents may exploit rather than relying solely on acquired clinical evidence. These results suggest that diagnostic performance is relatively robust to ClinLab predictions, while the treatment of missing exam-inations can influence evaluation outcomes. Nevertheless, stable ag-gregate accuracy does not rule out individual cases where incorrect predicted values affect diagnostic reasoning. Further provenance-aware analysis is needed to distinguish decisions supported by ob-served EHR evidence from those influenced by ClinLab-generated results.

## L Subgroup Robustness Analysis 

We evaluate Qwen3-32B across patient-disjoint cohorts sampled from MIMIC-IV-Ext, covering different genders, age groups, and disease categories. As shown in Table 14, diagnostic accu-racy is relatively stable across age groups despite variations in information collection and examination coverage.
