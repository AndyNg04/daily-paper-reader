## Abstract 

Web systems increasingly predict users’ decisions from what users say, yet when interests partly conflict, users may promise to cooper-ate to win the other side’s cooperation and then not do so. Systems that rely on such promises, such as sales or service agents deciding what to offer next, may grant concessions to users who then do not keep their promises, at a cost to the party each agent repre-sents. We test how LLMs combine users’ statements with decision records to predict users’ decisions, using 1,260 rounds in which 42 users played a repeated prisoner’s dilemma-style game with five agents, each prompted to express a dominant Big Five trait, and could talk with the agent before every decision. Across eight open-weight LLMs, predictions from decision records separated cooperation from defection well (AUC = .80), and adding dialogue

## 1 Introduction 

Web systems predict what users will do next. Recommendation and ranking have long drawn these predictions from past behavior, such as clicks and purchases [7, 9, 20]. Conversational interaction adds a more direct source, because users often say what they will do before they act. As LLM-based agents enter Web platforms, users talk with sales and service agents that represent another party and then decide whether to accept an offer or keep an agreement [21, 30]. When a user tells such an agent “I will accept this offer,” the dialogue appears to contain the answer to the prediction task. User modeling has built the means to use such language. Con-versational recommenders model users from the current dialogue [8, 11, 36]; LLMs predict individuals’ choices from interviews [18], from earlier choices in psychology experiments [1], from play in persuasion games [22] and after conversations [19], and stand in for users when conversational agents are evaluated [15, 33–35]. Of the language available to a predictor, a user’s own statement about the predicted action is the most direct evidence. A statement fixes the action the user names, but not how likely the user is to take it. This gap matters most in mixed-motive interactions, where both sides gain from cooperation while their interests partly conflict: there a statement expresses a plan and also seeks to influence the other side. Promises raise cooperation in experimental games, yet about a quarter of them are broken [2, 27], and people broke more than half of their cooperation agreements with LLM agents [29]. A predictor therefore has to decide how far a statement should move its prediction, above all when the statement is inconsistent with the user’s earlier decisions: ignoring it can miss real changes of plan, and relying on it heavily can overestimate how often it is kept. Overall accuracy does not reveal which error a predictor makes: a predictor that relies on statements gains accuracy on kept state-ments and loses it on broken ones, and the two can cancel. Whether dialogue improves prediction must therefore be judged against what users do later, separately for kept and broken statements. A statement is also made to a particular counterpart, and the action is taken toward it. A counterpart’s conduct and persona change people’s decisions [3, 14, 29], and how people respond to an agent’s personality depends on their own: people were more attracted to a computer whose personality matched theirs [16], and human–AI teamwork depended on how the human’s and the AI’s traits were paired [13]. Users’ decisions may therefore change with the agent’s persona and with how that persona matches the user’s own traits. A predictor that is accurate over all agents together can still be biased with particular agents or user–agent pairs. Existing studies cannot settle either issue, as their data were collected for other questions. In choice-prediction studies, users receive messages from a bot [22], talk without stating the predicted action [19], or play one-shot games with no record of earlier behav-ior [6]. User-simulator evaluations score generated conversation turns, so no later action can contradict what a simulated user says, and they compare responses to different assistants after pooling over users [15]. No study links a user’s statement to that user’s earlier decisions, the counterpart, and the later action. We therefore ask three research questions. 

• RQ1: How do LLM predictors combine users’ stated decisions, what users say they will do in the current round, with the record of earlier decisions, and is their reliance on statements supported by how often statements are kept? 

• RQ2: Do users’ decisions and word-keeping vary with the agent persona, and do the predictions vary in the same way? 

• RQ3: Do users’ decisions vary with their own personality, mea-sured as trait compatibility , the user’s score on the agent’s defin-ing trait, and do the predictions vary in the same way? Answering them requires statements and actions recorded sep-arately, repeated decisions that form a record against which to compare a statement, and counterparts that change while actions and payoffs stay the same. A repeated prisoner’s-dilemma-style game with communication before each decision meets these require-ments. We use the Personality-1260 dataset [31], in which 42 users played six rounds with each of five GPT-4o agents, each prompted to express a dominant Big Five trait, talking with the agent before every decision (Figure 1a). Eight open-weight LLMs predicted each decision from 19 combinations of the decision record (both players’ earlier decisions, the deeds ) and the dialogue (what users said, the 

words ). We evaluate predictions separately for kept and broken statements and for those inconsistent with users’ earlier decisions, measure the effect of one inserted statement, and compare them with users’ decisions across agents and trait compatibility. Predictions from the decision record separated cooperation from defection well (AUC = .80), and adding dialogue brought no de-tectable gain. Adding the round’s dialogue left accuracy on rounds with a stated decision almost unchanged (.73 to .74), because it raised accuracy where users kept their statement (.76 to .95) and lowered it where they broke it (.65 to .17; Figure 1b, left). When users stated a decision they had not yet chosen in that game, they kept it at a rate of .40, while the predicted probability of keeping it rose from .13 without the dialogue to .69 with it: the predictions moved beyond what the statement warranted. Users cooperated in 70.6% of rounds with the Agreeableness agent and 54.5% with the Extraversion agent and kept more of their statements with the for-mer; predicted cooperation was 64.5% and 63.1% (Figure 1b, right). Users also cooperated more as trait compatibility rose, whereas the predictions barely changed and fell increasingly below users’ cooperation. Pooled over agents, the prediction bias was near zero. This paper makes four contributions: (1) An evaluation design that ties a predictor’s use of dialogue to separately recorded actions, split by whether statements were kept, whether they were inconsistent with users’ earlier decisions, and the agent the user faced. (2) Evidence that eight LLM predictors relied on stated decisions beyond how often they were kept. (3) Evidence that the predictions missed how users’ behavior varied with the agent persona and trait compatibility, and that pooled metrics concealed both errors.

(4) Design implications for dialogue-based user modeling: how much weight a user’s statement receives should depend on the user’s earlier decisions and on the agent, and predictions should be checked against what users later do with each agent. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Prediction from Dialogue and History 

Studies of decision prediction have used behavioral history, con-versational information [24], or both. Nay and Vorobeychik [17] model cooperation in repeated games using the game structure and both players’ previous actions. In language-based persuasion games, Shapira et al. [22] find that LLM-generated data support bet-ter human-choice prediction when they capture history-dependent behavior beyond the sentiment of the messages users receive. Dia-logue can also provide context without revealing the user’s intended choice: Pilli and Nallur [19] predict decisions after cognitively de-manding conversations, but replacing users’ replies with random text changes accuracy little. In these settings, dialogue provides context for a choice without containing the user’s statement of that choice. Gonzalez-Fernandez et al. [6] examine such statements more directly by predicting strategic choices from pre-play communica-tion. In their three games, GPT-5 achieved higher mean accuracy than human forecasters, but was much less accurate for defection than cooperation. Their one-shot settings, however, provide no repeated decision history against which a current statement can be assessed. Repeated interactions allow a further question: does a predictor interpret a current statement in light of the speaker’s earlier choices and record of keeping statements? 

## 2.2 Stated Decisions as Behavioral Evidence 

A stated decision can be informative without guaranteeing that the stated action will occur. Promises can encourage trustworthy behav-ior [2], and their wording matters: conditional or implicit statements in a high-stakes game show were associated with less cooperation than explicit, unconditional ones [26]. Yet even expressed agree-ment can precede a different action. In online donation dialogues, some participants who agreed to donate subsequently gave less or nothing [28]. We use stated decisions to include expressions of intended action beyond explicit promises, without assuming that they will be kept. Recognizing that statements are informative does not ensure that an observer uses them appropriately. Chen and Houser [4] found that human observers distinguished promises from empty talk, but overestimated promise keeping and could not reliably distinguish kept from broken promises. This separates two questions: whether statements predict behavior, and whether a forecaster’s confidence in them matches observed behavior. 

## 2.3 Counterparts and User-Agent Pairing 

The behavior against which these forecasts are assessed also de-pends on the counterpart. de Melo et al. [5] found that emotional displays altered cooperation with agents using the same decision strategy. In conversational negotiation, agents’ willingness to com-promise affects whether people reach agreements [3]; with LLM counterparts, both cooperation and agreement keeping vary across personas [29]. These findings make the agent’s conduct part of the context for predicting a user’s next action. Responses to that conduct can further depend on the user. Per-sonality similarity increased attraction to computers in Moon and Nass [16], whereas Isbister and Nass [10] found a preference for complementary interactive characters. More recently, Ju and Aral [13] found that human and AI personality pairings affected team-work and performance. We examine how the user’s score on the agent’s defining trait relates to decisions and prediction bias, with-out assuming that higher compatibility implies greater cooperation. For prediction, the question is whether forecasts reproduce such contextual differences. ConvApparel tests whether simulated con-versational responses reproduce real users’ reactions to assistants of differing quality, finding that data-driven simulators adapt more faithfully than a prompted one [15]. We bring this evaluation ques-tion to separately recorded actions after dialogue, comparing fore-casts with behavior across agent personas and user-agent pairings. 

## 3 Experiment Design 

We study offline prediction on recorded games: for each round, a predictor receives material recorded before the user’s decision and outputs the probability that the user cooperates. The agent played with the user; the predictor is a separate model, run afterwards, that only forecasts the user’s recorded decision. 

## 3.1 Users, Agents and Game 

We use the Personality-1260 dataset [31]. Forty-two participants from a Chinese university (21 female; mean age 22.1) completed the Chinese BFI-44 [12] and played one game with each of five agents in random order. In each of six rounds per game, user and agent may talk freely; then both decide whether to cooperate or defect, the agent’s decision being revealed only after the user’s. Mutual cooperation pays 2 points each, defecting against a cooperator 3 (the cooperator 0), and mutual defection 0 each. Talking was optional, and the dialogue was empty in 276 of the 1,260 rounds. Each agent is GPT-4o prompted to be extremely high in one Big Five trait, to maximize its score, and to show the trait in dialogue and decisions without naming it. GPT-4o expressed personas set to the high end of a trait more faithfully than other models [23], and blind experts rated each agent 4.53–4.80 of 5 on its trait [31]. We call the agents by their agent persona : Openness (O), Conscientiousness (C), Extraversion (E), Agreeableness (A), Neuroticism (N). Users cooperated in 64.5% of rounds and agents in 77.7% (Appendix F). 

## 3.2 Stated Decisions 

A stated decision is what the user said in the round’s dialogue about their own decision in that round. Two researchers independently labeled each dialogue, shown without the decision, as stating coop-eration, stating defection, or no stated decision . Inter-rater agree-ment was high ( 𝜅 = .97), and disagreements were resolved through discussion (Appendix E). Statements were cheap: in games 2–5, 96% of stated decisions announced cooperation, 27.6% of stated cooperation was followed by defection, and 47.6% of all defections came right after a stated cooperation.

## 3.5 Statistical Analysis 

Rounds are nested in games and games in users, so uncertainty comes from a user bootstrap: 50,000 draws of 42 users with replace-ment, each bringing all their rounds. We report percentile intervals and two-sided bootstrap 𝑝 -values, Holm-corrected within each fam-ily of tests displayed together (Appendix H); a model difference counts as significant only if it has the same sign under each letter order. For RQ3 we estimate the least-squares slope of a game-level outcome on compatibility, pooling agents, without correction. 

## 4 Results 4.1 RQ1: Words and Deeds 

Finding 1: Predictions from earlier decisions separated co-operation from defection better than predictions from dia-logue, and adding dialogue to earlier decisions brought no detectable gain. Decisions alone ( 𝑅 ) reached AUC = .80 [.76, .85], dialogue alone ( 𝐷 ) .70 [.64, .76], and both ( 𝑅 + 𝐷 ) .79 [.75, .83] (Fig-ure 3a, b). Adding a decision record to any level of dialogue raised AUC in all 12 comparisons (+.04 to +.18, all 𝑝 Holm < .05), whereas within each non-empty level of decisions, the conditions with no dialogue, 𝐷 𝑜 , 𝐷 𝑐 and 𝐷 did not differ from one another (0 of 18 comparisons). The pattern largely held in the 646 rounds with a stated decision (11 of 12 and 0 of 18; Figure 3c, d). Even dialogue from other games ranked current decisions above chance ( 𝐷 𝑜 : .61): what users say carries information about their decisions, which the decision record largely already provides. The stated decision itself ranks poorly: with 96% of statements announcing cooperation, the statement separates cooperation from defection at AUC = .55. 

Finding 2: Similar overall accuracy concealed opposite changes; adding the round’s dialogue raised accuracy where users kept their statements and lowered it where they broke them. Adding the current-round dialogue to the current-game decisions changed accuracy on rounds with a stated decision from .73 to .74. Because kept rounds outnumber broken ones, the gains offset the losses: the dialogue moved the errors onto the rounds in which users broke their word, which held nearly half of all defections. Finding 3: When users’ stated decisions were inconsistent with their earlier decisions, the predictions overestimated how often they kept them. Evidence that users had broken earlier statements was only partly used. When users stated a decision they had already chosen earlier in the game, the round’s dialogue raised the predicted word-keeping probability from .65 to .90, against an observed .73 (Figure 4b). When they stated one they had not yet chosen (53 rounds, 20 users), the prediction from the current-game decisions alone was .13 and rose to .69 with the dia-logue, while users kept their word in .40 [.25, .58] of these rounds: the prediction moved from .27 below the observed rate to .30 above it (both 𝑝 Holm < .001). The statement was informative, since users kept it far more often than predicted with-out it, and the predictions gave it more weight than its keeping rate supported. Evidence that a user’s words had failed moved the predictions by only eleven percentage points. 

Finding 4: One sentence stating a decision moved the pre-dictions more than statements went with actions in users’ behavior. In rounds 2–6 without a stated decision, one inserted sentence moved agreement with the recorded decision from .75 to .98 when it matched that decision and to .08 when it did not (Figure 4d). Relative to swapping the user’s previous decision, the in-serted cooperation sentence moved the log-odds .63 to 2.60 times as much across models (mean 1.34); in users’ behavior, the coefficient of a stated cooperation was .35 [.11, .68] times that of the previous decision. The difference was positive for all eight models (mean +.99 [.65, 1.38], 𝑝 Holm < .001; significant for six). The first ratio mea-sures a response to an input change and the second an association in behavior; their comparison shows that the predictions moved with statements more than users’ actions went with them. 

## 4.2 RQ2: Agent Persona 

Finding 5: Users cooperated and kept their word most with the Agreeableness agent and least with the Extraversion agent. How often they stated a decision did not differ de-tectably. Users cooperated in 72.2% [63.5, 80.6] of rounds with the Agreeableness agent and 56.0% [46.4, 65.1] with the Extraversion agent (+16.3 [6.7, 25.8], 𝑝 Holm = .008; Figure 5a) and on average kept 82.4% versus 68.1% of their stated decisions (+14.3 [4.7, 24.5], 

𝑝 Holm = .022; Figure 5b). No other pair differed after correction, nor did any pair in how often users stated a decision. The difference is not explained by the agents’ own cooperation alone: the Neuroti-cism agent cooperated least (67.5%), yet users cooperated with it descriptively more than with the E agent (65.1% vs. 56.0%).

## 5 Discussion 5.1 Grounding Statements in Behavior 

A user can state an action unambiguously while leaving substantial uncertainty about whether it will occur. When users named an action they had not previously chosen, adding the current-round di-alogue moved mean predicted fulfillment from below the observed rate to above it (Finding 3). Returning to the history-only condition would leave the original underestimation unresolved. The central issue is therefore how far dialogue revises a forecast and whether that revision is supported by subsequent behavior. The insertion experiment establishes sensitivity to a stated decision; naturally occurring statement-action pairs provide the behavioral reference needed to assess that sensitivity. Human observers have also overes-timated promise keeping [4]. Our repeated setting exposes a further difficulty: even when earlier unfulfilled statements became avail-able, predictions remained above observed cooperation (Finding 3). An action log alone cannot reveal whether an earlier statement was kept, and the earlier dialogue contains the statements without their outcomes. We therefore added to the prompt a record of what the user had said in each earlier round and what they chose (Appen-dix D). For statements inconsistent with the user’s earlier decisions, the predicted word-keeping probability fell from .69 to .40, the observed rate; after an earlier broken statement, predicted cooper-ation fell from .60 to .40 (observed .29). Adding the earlier-round dialogue instead lowered them only to .60 and .49 (both differences from the record 𝑝 Holm < .001). Linking each earlier statement to its outcome corrected most of the overestimation in Finding 3. A sales or service agent that decides whether to grant a concession on a user’s promise could therefore keep, for each user, a record of what they said and then did, and weigh a new promise against it. 

## 5.2 Modeling Users with Their Counterparts 

The behavioral evidence itself depends on the interaction. Users cooperated and fulfilled statements at different rates across agent personas (Finding 5), and cooperation was associated with trait compatibility (Finding 7). A user representation that absorbs these choices into a general cooperation tendency risks losing informa-tion about the counterpart involved. Compatibility also showed no detected association with statement fulfillment: willingness to cooperate and the reliability of a stated decision remain distinct outcomes. These results support evaluating a user’s action in rela-tion to the counterpart and interaction history, without treating a single game as evidence of a stable personal disposition. Making that context available is only one part of the problem. The main persona comparison already supplied the counterpart’s earlier actions and dialogue. Adding the user’s BFI scores or the compatibility score reduced the estimated compatibility-related bias slope by roughly one quarter (Finding 8). The remaining point estimates were negative, although their intervals included zero. The added information therefore helped, while the extent of the remain-ing error is uncertain. Richer user descriptions require validation of how they change predicted behavior; their presence in a prompt provides no assurance that its forecasts reproduce the relevant be-havioral differences. The contrast with the word-keeping record (Section 5.1) suggests that a predictor uses added information best when it states the relation to be predicted: the record linked each statement to its outcome, whereas the trait scores left the predictor to infer how a trait shapes behavior toward a particular agent. A platform deploying agents with different personas should therefore calibrate predictions per persona instead of reusing one user profile: a user who keeps agreements with one agent may not with another. 

## 5.3 Evaluating Errors That Averages Conceal 

Two different cancellations made overall performance appear re-assuring. Gains on fulfilled statements offset losses on unfulfilled statements, while positive and negative mean biases across per-sonas nearly canceled (Findings 2 and 6). A different mixture of fulfillment outcomes or counterparts could therefore change aggre-gate performance even with unchanged within-group performance. Likewise, AUC measures discrimination between cooperation and defection, leaving the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed rates to be checked separately. Together, these results show how a model can perform well on aggregate measures while missing relationships that matter within particular interactions. Evaluation should consequently connect forecast changes to independently recorded actions under identifiable interaction con-ditions. Splitting by subsequent fulfillment locates errors retro-spectively. Testing whether those errors are recognizable before a decision requires groups defined from available evidence, such as a statement inconsistent with the user’s earlier decisions or an earlier unfulfilled statement. Comparisons across counterparts and levels of compatibility then test whether predictions reproduce contextual variation in behavior. These checks can also guide evaluation of action-generating user simulators, extending the concern with re-sponsiveness to different assistants in ConvApparel [15]. Matching a pooled cooperation rate alone would provide little evidence that a simulator reproduces users’ responses to a particular agent. Before an LLM predictor or user simulator is deployed, its accuracy should therefore be reported separately for kept and broken statements and for each agent, as a pooled score can look adequate while it fails on the users and agents that matter most.

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Work 

We studied 42 Chinese university students in short, low-stakes games and eight zero-shot predictors using Chinese prompts with reasoning disabled. Generalization to other populations, longer re-lationships, and Web tasks needs validation. All counterparts used GPT-4o; persona prompts changed language and decisions, con-founding personality expression with behavioral reciprocity. Com-patibility uses one trait; analyses are exploratory and uncorrected. Some subgroups were small, including 53 rounds from 20 users. Prompt edits measure model sensitivity, while statement-action associations do not identify causal effects. Future work should inde-pendently vary agent language and decision policy, and test history-aware or counterpart-conditioned predictors on held-out users and interactions across prompts, reasoning settings, and trained models. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present an empirical evaluation of how LLMs combine dialogue and behavioral history to predict users’ decisions. Using recorded mixed-motive games with 42 users and five agent personas, we examined eight open-weight predictors through infor-mation ablations, statement insertions, and analyses of trait com-patibility. The predictors overestimated the fulfillment of stated decisions and understated behavioral variation associated with agent persona and trait compatibility. Nearly unchanged accuracy on stated-decision rounds and near-zero mean bias pooled across agents obscured these mismatches. Supplying explicit personality information modestly reduced the estimated compatibility-related bias slope. Together, these findings show why dialogue-based user models need evaluation that connects their use of language and interaction context to subsequent actions. We hope this work sup-ports user models that connect what people say to how they have acted and to the counterpart they face, while preserving uncertainty about choices they have yet to make.

## E Stated-Decision Labels 

Two researchers independently read each round’s dialogue without seeing the user’s decision and assigned a label based on the user’s words, using the agent’s words only to understand what the user was responding to. The five coding categories were: explicit cooper-ation (the user says they will cooperate), implicit cooperation (the user accepts the agent’s proposal to cooperate or clearly commits without naming the decision), defection (the user says or threatens that they will defect, including in jest), vague (the user discusses the decision without committing, or only asks the agent), and none (the user does not discuss their own decision this round, including talk about earlier rounds only). If the user changed their statement, the last statement counted. Across the 1,260 rounds, the final resolved labels comprised 594 explicit cooperation, 197 implicit cooperation, 30 defection, 116 vague, and 323 none. Inter-rater agreement was 

𝜅 = .81 for the five-class coding scheme and 𝜅 = .97 after collapsing explicit and implicit cooperation into stated cooperation , retaining defection as stated defection , and merging vague and none into no stated decision . All disagreements were resolved through discussion. 

## F Descriptive Statistics 

Users cooperated in 64.5% of the 1,260 rounds, falling from 82.4% in round 1 to 42.4% in round 6. Agents cooperated in 77.7% of rounds: O 78.2%, C 81.3%, E 72.2%, A 89.3%, N 67.5%. After removing decision-submission records, the dialogue was empty in 276 rounds; in the other 984 rounds users spoke 2.4 turns on average. Users’ mean BFI-44 scores (1–5) were O 3.47, C 3.13, E 3.10, A 3.76, N 2.95. Because game order was random, the number of users who met each persona in games 2–5 ranged from 30 (O, A) to 39 (C). Overall, users kept 74.3% of their stated decisions (74.0% of stated cooperation and 83.3% of stated defection).

## I Ethical Considerations 

We reuse Personality-1260, whose collection received Institutional Review Board approval and written informed consent, reconfirmed after debriefing; participants received US$10 [31]. The dialogues contain no identifying information, and our predictors ran on local servers. An unfulfilled statement can reflect a changed decision and should not be used to label a person dishonest. Better prediction and persona selection also create risks of manipulative targeting. Ap-plications should disclose agent roles and data use, preserve users’ ability to revise their choices, and obtain authorization separately from any forecast of what a user might do.
