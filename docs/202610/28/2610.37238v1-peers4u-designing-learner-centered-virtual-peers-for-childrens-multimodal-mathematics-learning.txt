1 Introduction 

In early mathematics, it is crucial for children to ground their understanding of abstract concepts and operations in concrete experiences [5, 64]. For example, children can conceptualize place value by bundling ten individual objects into a group, drawing corresponding quantities, and connecting these actions to written numerals. Such multimodal learning through multiple representations, including manipulating, drawing, writing, and speaking [11, 12, 52, 53], helps children develop deeper conceptual understanding and representational fluency [25, 62]. Learning with peers offers natural opportunities to enrich this process [18, 96]. Peers often bring diverse solution strategies across different representations [113], exposing one another to unfamiliar ways of thinking and sparking curiosity [23]. Through interaction, they can further explain their reasoning, compare alternative approaches, exchange ideas, and challenge or build on one another’s understanding [27, 85]. The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has opened new possibilities for introducing virtual peers into Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITSs). However, many recent virtual-peer systems target older learners or other learning domains [56, 102, 106], with peer interactions primarily centered on text- and voice-based conversation [97, 104, 112]. This conversational paradigm fits poorly with the visual and manipulable needs of early mathematics learning [53, 63]. Furthermore, little is known about how young children would participate and learn in an open virtual-peer environment. To bridge this gap, we explore how to design a multimodal virtual peer-learning system for early mathematics. Specifically, we investigate: How can virtual peers be designed to support children’s multimodal mathematics learning, and how do children participate in the resulting peer-learning interactions? 

We first conducted a formative study consisting of exploratory probe sessions with 8 children and interviews with 5 experienced teachers. We identified four interrelated design challenges: (1) children’s mathematical peer communication needs to be grounded in shareable multimodal solution artifacts; (2) peers need to be sufficiently different from the learner to provide new perspectives while remaining understandable; (3) children vary substantially in how they prefer to participate; and (4) open peer interaction alone does not naturally produce a coherent learning progression. These findings informed four corresponding design goals (DGs). Guided by these goals, we designed and developed Peers4U , a multimodal virtual-peer ITS for early mathematics learning, as shown in Figure 1. In Peers4U , after independently completing multimodal solutions to an open-ended problem, the learner is presented with a personalized learning group of 4 peers based on the learner’s demonstrated mathematical understanding. Each peer possesses a diverse set of solution artifacts whose multimodal construction processes can be inspected. The interaction follows a mixed-initiative design [36]: the learner can engage with peers to discuss specific artifacts and author and share new solutions at any time, while virtual peers can likewise respond, proactively initiate topics, and advance discussions along a pedagogical trajectory. Peers4U 3To characterize children’s peer-learning experiences and evaluate the system design, we conducted an exploratory user study with 22 children across different peer-composition conditions and a post-hoc evaluation with 2 experts. 

Peers4U ’s learner-centered topic orchestration received high pedagogical appropriateness ratings (4.69/5), significantly outperforming a persona-centered baseline. Furthermore, children spontaneously authored new artifacts to teach peers, demonstrate newly inspired ideas, or improve and share their own strategies. Children produced significantly more additional solutions when learning with a Leading Group, while those in a Mixed Group showed significantly greater willingness to learn with the same peers again. We also identified how different types of peer messages were associated with children’s likelihood of responding. We further discuss broader implications for designing multimodal virtual peers that children can both learn from and teach. Our contributions are threefold: 

• We identify core challenges of introducing virtual peers into children’s mathematics learning and derive four DGs spanning modality mediation, peer heterogeneity, flexible initiative, and progressive orchestration. 

• We present the design and implementation of Peers4U , instantiating an artifact-mediated multi-peer learning paradigm that combines mixed-initiative interaction with learner-centered pedagogical orchestration. 

• We provide empirical findings revealing children’s interaction patterns in multimodal virtual peer learning, and outline transferable design implications for virtual peer learning systems. 

2 Related Work 2.1 Theoretical Foundations: Multiple Representations and Peer Learning in Mathematics 

Mathematics learning is fundamentally a process of cognitive development. Rooted in Embodied Cognition, children construct abstract concepts through physical manipulation and visual perception [6, 64]. Theories such as Bruner’s Enactive-Iconic-Symbolic (EIS) framework [11, 12] and Lesh’s translation model [53] emphasize the necessity of practicing and translating across multiple representations—including concrete manipulatives, pictorial drawings, spoken language, and written symbols [63, 66]. Engaging in these translational activities allows children to develop Representational Fluency and achieve a deep conceptual understanding [25, 62]. Consequently, multimodal interactive learning systems inherently align with this pedagogical need, which forms the foundational rationale of our system. Social learning environments naturally complement this need for multi-representational learning [107], where peers bring diverse strategies and varying representational choices [23]. Drawing on Social Learning Theory [3], children can effectively acquire new strategies by observing and imitating peer behaviors. Furthermore, exposure to such heterogeneous peer strategies often triggers Socio-Cognitive Conflict, compelling children to reflect on their own reasoning and ultimately facilitating deeper knowledge internalization [18, 69]. Consequently, peer learning paradigms are widely adopted in pedagogical practice [16, 100, 101]. Specifically, Reciprocal Peer Tutoring—where children alternate between tutor and tutee roles—yields mutual learning gains through preparation and explanation [22, 24, 99], while cooperative learning enhances academic achievement and strengthens social support [37, 41]. Recently, advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have made virtual peers a reality, providing a unique opportunity to offer highly controlled and tailored peer interactions. Building on this, our research explores how to effectively design such virtual peers to facilitate multimodal mathematics learning for children. 4 Zheng et al. 

2.2 Multimodal Interactive Systems for Mathematics Learning 

Grounded in the theories outlined above, mathematical understanding hinges on multiple representations. Existing HCI research seeks to move beyond abstract symbols by externalizing mathematical relationships into directly perceptible, manipulable objects to scaffold learning. One line of research combines physical environments with digital feedback to contextualize the mathematical learning experience. For instance, Tangible Tens [70] and Bricksmart [57] leverage tabletop block play to visually represent quantitative relationships and spatial structures; Balance Board Math [93] and MOVES-NL [1, 17] map embodied action onto symbolic forms such as graphs and number lines. Extended Reality (XR) environments, such as ARMath [42], Mathland [44], and MaR-T [9], further augment mathematical inquiry with immersive visualizations. While grounding math learning in embodied cognition provides strong intuition, these systems remain bound by the inherent properties of physical artifacts, constraining their applicability to particular mathematical topics. Beyond physical interfaces, digital systems can cover broader learning scenarios. They can support learners’ un-derstanding of multiple interconnected representations [74, 81], using on-screen virtual manipulatives to bridge concreteness and abstraction [61, 82]. Specifically, Fractions Tutor dynamically integrates graphical models with num-ber lines [73, 74], and MathWorlds links graphs, animations, and equations [78]. Yet, these systems are predominantly designed for solo learning and lack built-in peer-interaction mechanisms, missing opportunities for the collision of different perspectives. While OpenCD further captures speech alongside these modalities, it is designed for diagnostic assessment rather than interactive learning [113]. To overcome the limitations of solo learning, we aim to introduce virtual peers embedded within a multimodal interactive environment, fostering rich cognitive friction and learning opportunities. 

2.3 Virtual Peers in Learning Environments 

Since their emergence, LLMs have been widely adopted for role-playing to generate anthropomorphic dialogue and behavior [68, 88]. Building on this, educational research employs LLMs to simulate students [40, 65], laying the groundwork for virtual peers and complex social learning dynamics. The HCI community has long focused on the design of virtual peers [13, 72, 87]. Applications span from collaborative child–agent co-reading [102] to systems like Your Thoughtful Opponent and ArgueMate, which induce cognitive conflict through disagreement and debate [45, 104]. Research has also explored robots as physical companions [8, 35, 67, 79]. A separate line of work has investigated teachable agents—where students learn by teaching AI—to enhance learning outcomes, foster metacognitive skills, and boost student engagement [39, 59, 77, 80, 110]. In more social learning settings, virtual peers can participate alongside human learners [60]. For instance, PeerGPT joins children’s small-group collaboration, while Saya takes part in classroom activities as an “additional student” [56, 97]. Beyond introducing a single peer, some studies employ multiple virtual peers to create richer social learning environments, such as MAIC [111], ClassMeta [58], among others [60, 94, 106, 112]. However, these virtual peers primarily communicate through conversations (text/voice), which constrains their use in children’s mathematics learning, where interaction often involves richer forms of engagement. Thus, how young children interact with and learn alongside virtual peers, and how such interactions can be facilitated in early mathematics, remain underexplored. Peers4U 5

3 Formative Study 

To inform the design of our system, we conducted a two-part formative study consisting of exploratory probe sessions with children and semi-structured interviews with elementary mathematics teachers. We synthesized evidence from both parts into four design goals (DGs). 

3.1 Study Rationale and Design 

We specifically focused on the early stages of mathematical and social learning—spanning late kindergarten to early primary school. Mathematics at this stage is strongly grounded in concrete and visual representations. Rather than relying solely on verbal explanations, children at this stage commonly express and develop mathematical ideas through manipulating objects, drawing, writing, and speaking. This setting therefore provides a particularly relevant context for examining how virtual peers might participate in and support multimodal mathematical learning. Our formative study combined two complementary perspectives. First, we used an exploratory probe to observe how children attended to, interpreted, and interacted with peers’ multimodal mathematical solutions. Second, we interviewed experienced teachers to contextualize these behaviors within classroom practice and to identify pedagogical opportunities, risks, and desirable forms of peer interaction. 

3.2 Part 1: Child Probe Sessions 

Probe. We developed an exploratory probe that intentionally kept the social behaviors of virtual peers relatively simple while providing a rich and open multimodal solution space. The probe was intended to elicit how children and teachers expected peers to participate in such a space and what forms of interaction they found useful or problematic.

Children first selected a mathematics task and created one or more solutions using multiple modalities, including manipulating virtual objects, drawing, writing, and verbal explanation, using a multimodal authoring workspace described in prior work [113]. After submitting their own solution, children were presented with a gallery of peer 6 Zheng et al. solutions, as shown in Figure 2. They could replay the multimodal process of each solution and chat with the associated LLM-driven virtual peer via voice input and text-to-speech (TTS) output. Artifacts included flawed solutions where peers actively asked for help. The peer-solution repository was constructed from an existing dataset described in OpenCD [113], with its reuse approved under the applicable IRB protocol. All data were de-identified prior to use, and audio responses additionally underwent voice conversion to reduce speaker identifiability. The mathematics tasks are listed in Appendix A.1; details of the peer-solution presentation order and the LLM configuration of the virtual peers are provided in Appendix A.2. 

Participants. We recruited 8 children (4 boys and 4 girls; age 𝑀 = 6.7 years, 𝑆𝐷 = 0.61, range = 5y11m–7y5m) spanning the final year of kindergarten through first grade (4 K, 4 G1). Recruitment was conducted via personal accounts on RedNote for in-person sessions in a Chinese city. Parents registered their children voluntarily, and we filtered excess applicants solely to balance gender and grade. Following IRB approval and parental written consent, children participated voluntarily and were informed that they could pause or withdraw at any time. They received certificates as gifts upon completion. Participant details are provided in Appendix A.3. 

Procedure. Each child participated in an approximately 60-minute, in-person session using the probe on a 13-inch iPad with an Apple Pencil. The probe included nine mathematics tasks across three categories (Appendix A.1). Children could freely choose tasks, complete one or more solutions, browse peer solutions, inspect their multimodal construction processes, and interact with the corresponding virtual peers. We did not explicitly tell children beforehand that the peers were AI-driven; children could nevertheless infer this from occasional imperfections in the interactions. The sessions followed a semi-structured observation protocol: as children navigated the system, the researcher occasionally probed their willingness to engage in specific interactions, prompted them to articulate their feelings and thoughts, and observed their spontaneous behaviors. Children could work on multiple tasks during the session, and the researcher occasionally varied the peer-solution presentation configuration to elicit user reactions across different social contexts (Appendix A.3). 

3.3 Part 2: Teacher Interviews 

Participants. We recruited 5 experienced elementary school mathematics teachers from different schools through personal networks. They had 7–17 years of teaching experience ( 𝑀 = 11 .2, 𝑆𝐷 = 4.15), including 3–17 years of experience teaching Grades 1–2 ( 𝑀 = 7.2, 𝑆𝐷 = 5.67). Participants received 200 RMB (approx. $30 USD) for the 1.5-hour interview. See Appendix A.4 for detailed teacher profiles. 

Procedure. We conducted semi-structured interviews centered on three themes: (1) the role and dynamics of peer interaction in real-world early math classrooms; (2) their professional critique and design suggestions after the demonstration of our technology probe; and (3) their interpretations and pedagogical attributions of the specific child behaviors we observed in Part 1. 

3.4 Data Collection and Analysis 

Throughout the study, we collected screen recordings, video footage, and raw interaction logs from the children’s sessions, alongside audio recordings from the teacher interviews. All audio was transcribed verbatim. We conducted a team-based codebook thematic analysis [10]. The child-session and teacher-interview data were first analyzed separately. For the child-session data, screen recordings and video footage were synchronized and analyzed together, using segments containing a coherent expression or behavior as analytic units. Two researchers independently open-coded a subset of each dataset (three child sessions and two teacher transcripts) and developed codebooks. One Peers4U 7researcher coded the remaining data, while the second reviewed the coded segments. Within each dataset, related codes were grouped into candidate themes and reviewed against the full data. Finally, the researchers compared and integrated themes across the two datasets, connecting children’s observed behaviors with teachers’ pedagogical interpretations. 

3.5 Findings and Design Goals 

Overall, the probe successfully elicited rich interactions. Children selected an average of 4 .0 ± 1.2 tasks, initiated chats around 5 .5 ± 4.1 peer artifacts, and sent 19 .6 ± 16 .5 messages. Through our observations of children’s interactions with the probe and interviews with expert teachers, we identified four core challenges and derived corresponding Design Goals (DGs) for integrating virtual peers into multimodal early math learning. 

Finding 1: Peer interaction around mathematics extends beyond language to multimodal artifacts . A key challenge is that text or speech alone could not accommodate how children wanted to communicate mathematical ideas. In our probe sessions, children often sought to share or demonstrate their own multimodal solutions, making solution artifacts part of the “language” through which they observed, questioned, explained, and responded to one another. The dynamic solution process drew particular attention, and children frequently directed their questions to specific actions within it (S2, S4, S5, S8). For example, S4 noticed the sequence of a peer’s actions, asking, “Why did you drag 8 squares first, and then 8 circles?” Teachers similarly emphasized the importance of making mathematical thinking visible (T1, T4). As T4 explained, young children often need to “see it while listening” in order to follow an explanation. Solution artifacts also became a natural medium for interacting with peers. When attempting to respond or explain, some children wanted to directly share or demonstrate their own solutions rather than rely on verbal explanation (S1, S2, S5). Peer solutions could also prompt further creation: S4 and S7 voluntarily proposed returning to the canvas to create another solution after viewing others’ approaches. Children sometimes even used their solutions for self-presentation, proudly telling peers how many methods they had created (S4, S6). 

⇒ DG1: Ground peer interaction in shareable multimodal mathematical artifacts. Peer interactions should revolve around mathematical solutions rather than purely conversational text. Artifacts should serve not only as initial assessments but also as an ongoing medium of communication. The system should allow children to revisit, share, and discuss artifacts during interaction, as well as create new solutions. 

Finding 2: Useful peers must be different enough to inspire, yet close enough to understand . A second challenge lies in calibrating peer heterogeneity. Across all eight children, overly simple or similar solutions were often quickly skipped. Conversely, unfamiliar methods sparked curiosity, with 7 children expressing a desire to communicate upon seeing a novel solution. However, when the cognitive gap was too large, children sometimes misinterpreted the methods or incorrectly judged them as wrong (S2, S4, S5). Teachers therefore noted that peer materials must provide an “intermediate ladder”, guiding children from understandable methods toward unfamiliar ones (T2, T4). We also observed the unique pedagogical value of peers presenting less developed or flawed solutions. Six children showed a strong willingness to help when peers explicitly asked for assistance, and re-explained their own thoughts while helping (S1–S6). S1 eagerly claimed, “Of course I want to help, I’ll be the little teacher!” Some children even transferred strategies they had just learned from another peer (S1, S3). Teachers noted that explaining to others can force children to reorganize their reasoning and expose gaps in their own understanding (T2, T3). Consequently, different peer levels serve distinct purposes: more advanced peers inspire, similar peers provide relatability, and struggling peers offer opportunities for correction and confidence-building (T2–T5). 8 Zheng et al. 

⇒ DG2: Construct heterogeneous peers relative to the learner. The system should dynamically assign a group of peers with diverse mathematical approaches and cognitive levels, tailored to the user’s initial understanding. This heterogeneity must balance inspiration with approachability, deliberately providing diverse opportunities for observation, explanation and learning-by-teaching [24, 27, 99]. 

Finding 3: Children’s participation styles vary substantially, requiring flexible interaction initiative . Athird challenge is the substantial variation in how children prefer to participate in peer learning. A rigid interaction paradigm—whether primarily system-led or learner-led—is unlikely to work well for all children. Interaction logs revealed a participation divide. On average, some children sent over 10 messages per task (S3, S6), while others sent no more than 1 message per task (S7, S8). S7 even explicitly rejected a peer’s request for help and later explained, “I don’t want to talk to them anymore because I want to go to the next problem faster.” T5 pointed out that while peer presence encourages some children to express themselves, it can make others more silent, thus for introverted learners, “listening while other peers ask questions” was also an effective and necessary form of participation. 

⇒ DG3: Support flexible interaction initiative and participation roles. 

The system’s conversational agency must be flexible, as reflected in prior learning systems that combine learner-and agent-initiated interaction [106, 111]. Virtual peers should be able to naturally respond to user inquiries, but also proactively initiate topics, move the discussion forward, or gracefully conclude them when the user is passive. This allows children with different participation styles to fluidly shift among the roles of leader, responder, or observer. 

Finding 4: Open Peer Interaction Lacks Focus Without Progressive Pedagogical Orchestration . Finally, young children may lack the metacognitive capacity to independently navigate multiple peers, representations, and mathematical approaches. An open peer environment does not by itself produce an effective learning trajectory. As T3 emphasized, “Learning is not just about interaction. . . it must have clear pedagogical objectives.” Teachers outlined a consistent progression for productive peer learning. First, children must form their own initial ideas before being exposed to peers (T1, T5). Subsequently, peer materials should be introduced progressively—starting from relatable, easier methods to increasingly diverse representations (T1, T2, T4). Furthermore, T1 noted that after exploring divergent methods, there must be a convergent process where children are guided to compare differences, analyze commonalities, and extract mathematical essences. This suggests a tension between social openness and pedagogical structure : peer interaction should feel exploratory, while its underlying progression still needs to be organized toward specific learning objectives. 

⇒ DG4: Orchestrate peer interaction through cognitively progressive learning topics. The system must act as an “invisible director”, dynamically orchestrating learning topics around the learner’s current understanding. The discussion of solution methods should follow the developmental trajectory of mathematical cognition [30, 89], and the types of discussion topics should deepen progressively according to cognitive levels [50]. 

4 System Design 

In this section, we present the design of Peers4U based on the four DGs derived from our formative study. Together, they shaped a holistic interaction paradigm for Peers4U .Peers4U 9

4.1 System Overview 

For each task, the learner first independently constructs their own solutions. This serves multiple purposes: it ensures independent mathematical thinking, provides materials for subsequent peer interaction, and allows the system to estimate the learner’s current mathematical understanding. Following this, the learner enters a virtual “classroom”, as shown in Figure 3, where the system has tailored a personalized learning group (DG2). Mirroring real-world small-group dynamics, this group consists of four virtual peers, each equipped with multiple multimodal solution artifacts. Learners can review their peers’ problem-solving processes, engage in group or private chats, directly discuss specific artifacts, and create and share new artifacts at any time (DG1). Furthermore, peers can proactively initiate learning topics to engage the user and move the discussion forward (DG3). These topics are diverse, unfold iteratively, and are dynamically orchestrated based on the learner’s performance, progressing from shallow to deep following pedagogical strategies (DG4). Ultimately, these mechanisms aim to foster deep participation, effective peer collisions, and an orderly deepening of conceptual understanding. Below, we detail the system’s design. 

4.2 Multimodal Artifact Creation and Peer Interaction (DG1) 

DG1 requires the interface to facilitate natural, multimodal interactions centered around mathematical artifacts. To support children’s mathematical expression and learning through multiple representations, Peers4U allows learners to create solutions using a variety of modalities, including virtual manipulatives [61, 82], drawing, writing, and speaking, as shown in Figure 3b. We adopted the multimodal workspace introduced in OpenCD [113] for this purpose. After completing their initial solutions, learners enter the “Learning Group” page (Figure 3a). The four peers and their associated solutions are presented together for an overview. Learners can open the chat panel to participate in group discussions, or inspect a peer’s solutions, replay their complete multimodal construction process, and communicate privately with that peer (Figure 3d). Beyond text or voice chat, students can return to the workspace at any point to author new content, and send their entire multimodal creation process to peers. Naturally, students can also forward other peers’ solutions into the chat for discussion, a capability shared by the virtual peers as well. 

4.3 Personalized Peer Construction (DG2) 

DG2 requires the system to dynamically assemble an appropriate peer group for each learner. Specifically, the system needs to estimate the learner’s current mathematical understanding, determine heterogeneous but appropriate cognitive profiles for multiple peers, and assign each peer a set of solutions consistent with that profile. 

4.3.1 Artifact Analysis and Cognitive Mapping. A learner’s cognitive level can be estimated from their initial solution artifacts. The system must comprehend the full multimodal problem-solving process and map it to task-specific mathe-matical competencies. Following DG2, we distinguish two broad peer roles relative to the learner: Leading Peers and Struggling Peers .Leading Peers are assigned valuable strategies that the learner has not yet demonstrated, providing opportunities to encounter new perspectives. Struggling Peers contain more incomplete or flawed solutions, creating opportunities for correction and learning-by-teaching. Peers within the same role can also differ in their specific cognitive profiles. The system supports flexible combinations of these roles; the configurations used in our user study are described in § 5.2. Once the peers’ cognitive profiles are determined (Figure 4b), the system assigns solutions that both support these personas and provide useful materials for interaction (Figure 4c). Motivated by prior work showing both the benefits and cognitive demands of learning from multiple solution methods [31, 75], we bounded each group’s solution pool around 10 artifacts, with at least 2 per peer. Using the preprocessing results described above, the assignment procedure prioritizes solutions according to their cognitive fit with the target peer, similarity to existing solutions and modality coverage. This produces peer portfolios that are consistent with the intended cognitive profiles while remaining diverse in strategy and form. 4.4 Learning Topic Initiation and Progression (DG3, DG4) 

Rather than allowing each virtual peer to independently pursue a persona-driven agenda, Peers4U coordinates peer behaviors around the learner’s learning progression to achieve learner-centered interaction. To fulfill this, we design the interaction mechanism from two dimensions: adaptive conversational agency and progressive topic orchestration. 

4.4.1 Adaptive Conversational Agency via Implicit Interaction. DG3 requires the coordination to remain flexible: peers should respond when the learner takes initiative, while also being able to proactively drive the discussion when appropriate, consistent with mixed-initiative interaction principles [36]. However, rigid system interventions can easily interrupt the child’s active learning process. We operationalize this through implicit interaction : using the user’s operational silence as an implicit contextual cue to dynamically modulate conversational agency [15, 86]. The system continuously monitors the learner’s activity stream and triggers generation through a conversation control flow shown in Figure 5. When the learner sends a chat message, the system directly invokes the Reactive Agent 

for a response. For other activities, the system differentiates the user’s state: if the user is engaged in high-initiative tasks such as recording speech, constructing an artifact, or replaying a solution process, the system classifies the state as Engaged and remains silent to avoid interruption. Conversely, after the learner remains Idle for a period, the system treats this as an opportunity for a lightweight proactive intervention. It invokes the Topic Agent to initiate a new topic or the Follow-up Agent to advance the current discussion. Both the Reactive and Follow-up Agents can close the current topic when the topic’s goal has been reached or has expired. Implementation details are provided in Appendix B.3. Through this mechanism, children who prefer to lead can do so without interruption, while those who participate less actively can still receive timely invitations to engage, fostering an elastic and natural group chat rhythm. 5 User Study 

We conducted a user study to answer the following research questions: 

RQ1: How do children engage in and experience multimodal peer learning with Peers4U ?

RQ2: How does peer composition influence children’s engagement and experience? 

RQ3: How pedagogically appropriate are the learning topics generated from children’s actual interactions? We conducted an exploratory, in-person, between-subjects study with children to address RQ1 and RQ2, and a post-hoc evaluation with two expert teachers to answer RQ3. 

5.1 Children Participants 

We initially recruited 24 children, while two withdrew, and their incomplete records were excluded. The final sample comprised 22 children (11 girls and 11 boys; age 𝑀 = 7.00 years, 𝑆𝐷 = 0.67, range = 6y0m–8y2m, denoted as U1–U22). The study took place during the summer break; 11 children had completed their final kindergarten year and 11 had completed Grade 1. Participant details are provided in Appendix C.1. Recruitment was conducted via personal accounts on RedNote for in-person sessions in a Chinese city, with parents registering their children voluntarily. Because learning ahead of the school curriculum is common in this context, parents reported their child’s actual mathematics progress. We excluded applicants who had already completed the Grade 2 curriculum to minimize ceiling effects, and filtered excess applicants solely to balance gender and grade. Following IRB approval and parental written consent, children participated voluntarily and could pause or withdraw at any time. Each child received a small toy and a certificate as gifts upon completion. 14 Zheng et al. 

5.2 Study Setup 

We employed a between-subjects design comparing two configurations ( 𝑛 = 11 each): Condition A (Mixed Group) 

included two Leading and two Struggling Peers, providing a balanced cognitive distribution; Condition B (Leading Group) included four Leading Peers, maximizing exposure to inspiring strategies. In both conditions, peer cognitive profiles and solution artifacts were personalized based on the learner’s initial solutions. Peer appearance, screen position, and voice were randomized for each participant, with two male and two female peer characters per group.

All sessions used a 13-inch iPad Pro with an Apple Pencil, as shown in Figure 6. We selected Subtraction with Regrouping as the learning task and prepared three task tiers corresponding approximately to entry into Grade 1, the first semester, and the second semester. Each tier paired a focal learning task with a different but comparable post-test task (Appendix C.2). The artifact repository for these tasks was assembled from the OpenCD dataset [113], our formative study, and researcher-authored supplements, with details provided in Appendix C.2; all artifacts were preprocessed using the pipeline described in § 4.3.1. 

5.3 Procedure 

Each child first completed a guided tutorial covering the multimodal workspace, solution replay, and chats. Then the researcher suggested a task tier based on the child’s grade and parent-reported progress; the child could confirm or adjust this choice. Participants were then assigned to one of the two conditions to balance task tier and gender. Children then completed one full learning session in Peers4U . Their independently authored initial solutions served as the pre-test and as the input for personalizing the four peers. Subsequently, they explored peer solutions and interacted with the peers. The system explicitly notified the user when planned learning topics were completed, though children were free to continue interacting if they wished. Children could also choose to end the lesson or decline any remaining procedures at any time. After the lesson, children completed a questionnaire, which provided a short break from problem solving and then authored solutions to the paired post-test task. A brief structured interview concluded the session. The researcher remained present during the formal task, but assistance was strictly limited to reading text for children who needed it, correcting speech-recognition transcripts, and answering basic interface queries. 5.4 Data Collection and Analysis 

We collected camera and screen recordings, along with system logs capturing the complete pre- and post-test solution processes, additional solutions created during learning, chat messages, and interaction events. The post-study ques-tionnaire captured perceptions of individual peers, social pressure, and comparisons with regular classes. Most ratings used a five-point pictorial Smileyometer [33]. The interview probed perceived learning, preferred solution strategies, experiences of giving and seeking help, and liked or disliked aspects of the experience. The complete instruments are provided in Appendix C.3. We combined descriptive analyses of logs and questionnaires with structured coding of messages, multimodal artifacts, and interviews. Messages and artifacts were coded using researcher-defined schemes, and structured interview responses were analyzed question by question using qualitative content analysis. Between-condition outcomes were compared using two-sided exact Mann–Whitney 𝑈 tests, while message-level response comparisons used user-stratified exact permutation tests. Coding procedures and statistical details are provided in Appendices C.4 and D.1. 

5.5 Post-hoc Expert Evaluation 

To assess the pedagogical quality of system-initiated topics, we conducted an expert evaluation examining (1) whether topic messages were pedagogically appropriate, (2) whether our learner-centered orchestration produced more appro-priate topics than a persona-centered agent, and (3) why topics were sometimes inappropriate. 

Evaluation Dataset. We evaluated all 157 topic message points from U1–U22. Each paired the learner-centered message delivered during the user study with a persona-centered counterpart generated from the same preceding interaction context. The latter initiated topics from a peer’s own cognitive state and interaction history, representing a vanilla agent-driven approach to peer participation. Further details are provided in Appendix C.5.1. 

Expert Participants. We recruited two expert mathematics teachers from different primary schools. E1 had 28 years of teaching experience, including 15 years teaching Grades 1–2; E2 had 29 years, including 5 years teaching Grades 1–2. 

Procedure. Using a custom replay interface (Figure 12; Appendix C.5.2), the experts independently and blindly rated the paired topics. After calibration, they reviewed the remaining sessions independently, and reconciled rating differences exceeding one point, while briefly explaining each inappropriate judgment to the researchers (Appendix C.5.3). During independent review, the experts agreed exactly on 65.0% of topics and differed by at most one point on 97.0%; the linearly weighted Cohen’s 𝜅 was 0.466. Appendix C.5.3 provides a distribution-sensitive interpretation. 

Measures. Experts rated the overall pedagogical appropriateness of each topic—considering both its instructional value and its fit with the learner and ongoing interaction—from 1 ( very inappropriate ) to 5 ( very appropriate ). A rating of 3 or below required a reason, provided through predefined tags or written feedback. After reconciliation, the two ratings were averaged to obtain the final message-level appropriateness score. For the reasons for inappropriateness, we retained all distinct reasons identified by either expert, interpreting them with reference to the experts’ tags, comments, and consensus-meeting explanations.

7 Discussion 

Drawing on our findings, we discuss two central questions for designing virtual peers in children’s mathematics learning: how peer interaction takes shape, and how it becomes productive for learning. Beyond these, we discuss how artifact-mediated peer learning may extend to broader educational contexts. Peers4U 21 

7.1 Supporting Peer Interaction through Multimodal Artifacts and Flexible Participation 

For young children, both the medium of peer interaction and the available modes of participation shape how accessible and meaningful that interaction becomes. Our findings show how multimodal solution artifacts can ground interaction in observable mathematical thinking, while flexible participation can accommodate children who prefer to initiate, respond, or observe. Children’s emotional experiences also emerged as an important design consideration. 

7.1.1 Multimodal Solution Artifacts as the Medium of Peer Interaction . Existing virtual peers are often designed first as “conversational agents”, treating mathematical content merely as information exchanged through dialogue [60, 112]. Our findings suggest that for early mathematics learning, this relationship may be reversed: concrete mathematical solutions can themselves become the primary medium of interaction, with language revolving around these observable, authorable artifacts. Indeed, the act of authoring an artifact is, in itself, a form of communication [21, 34]. In Peers4U , children did not merely observe peer solutions; they directed their inquiries and explanations at specific actions, symbols, and strategies embodied in those solutions. More importantly, exposure to peer methods frequently elicited children to imitate, adapt, or completely author new solutions, and then bring these new artifacts back into the interaction. Consequently, mathematical ideas can flow across artifacts through a continuous cycle of observation, explanation, imitation, modification, and sharing [49, 91]. Supporting multimodal mathematical peer learning is thus not simply a matter of adding whiteboards, images, or videos to a chat interface; it requires elevating mathematical artifacts to become the primary shared objects of social interaction. This perspective also opens a broader design space. Children need not always start from a blank canvas. Future systems could allow them to directly annotate, remix, or build upon a peer’s existing solution, thus making comparison and strategy transfer easier to enact. Likewise, virtual peers need not be restricted to statically pre-assigned solutions. As interaction unfolds, peers could also “author” new artifacts, either through real-time AI generation or dynamic retrieval from a solution repository. Such capabilities could strengthen the symmetry of action between learners and peers [20], while further encouraging children to engage in mathematical construction and practice. We therefore argue that the “peer-like” quality of mathematical virtual peers need not rely only on conversational styles, personas, or anthropomorphic avatars [87]. It can also stem from a symmetry of mathematical agency: both children and peers can author, share, explain, and respond to one another’s solutions. Such artifact-mediated peer interaction turns mathematical representations from static learning resources into evolving objects of social interaction, grounding peer learning more directly in concrete mathematical thinking. 

7.1.2 Flexible Participation across Initiating, Responding, and Observing . Our findings highlight multiple ways for children to participate in peer learning. Some frequently initiated questions and shared ideas, others primarily responded to explicit invitations, while still others preferred to observe. The critical design question, therefore, is whether the interaction lets children engage in ways that fit their preferences. First, virtual peers can adjust the form of interaction to alter the threshold for participation. Our results showed that specific invitations directed toward the learner were more likely to receive responses than open, generalized group questions. When initial topics went unanswered, progressively direct and specific follow-ups brought some previously silent children into the discussion. Effective proactive peer behavior therefore depends on modulating the directness and openness of questions to provide accessible entry points. At the same time, observational participation can itself be a meaningful form of engagement [3, 71]. For children who are reluctant to speak, observing questions and explanations exchanged among other peers may also support vicarious learning [95]. 22 Zheng et al. Second, the timing of system-initiated interaction matters. While proactive peer messages can stimulate participation, they can also compete for children’s attention and interrupt ongoing mathematical activity [105]. For this reason, 

Peers4U employs a strategy of implicit interaction [15, 86]: the system remains silent when the learner is engaged in active learning behaviors, and gradually takes greater initiative only after a sustained period of operational silence. This design also highlights a broader challenge: because operational silence is inherently ambiguous, learner-centered initiative should be grounded in continuous awareness of the learner’s ongoing activity [55], rather than treating silence uniformly as disengagement. This suggests that an ideal peer-learning system should personalize interaction through joint models of learners’ mathematical cognition and social interaction styles [7, 54]. Systems could estimate a learner’s initiative tendency, response habits, and topic preferences from prior interactions [83]. For relatively passive children, a system might allow longer periods of observation, avoid repeated prompting, and offer clearer, lower-threshold invitations; for highly active children, it might reduce interventions and preserve more space for self-expression. In this way, different learners could first engage through modes that feel comfortable; over time, the system could gently nudge them toward more diverse forms of participation, helping them take fuller advantage of varied reciprocal learning opportunities, as we will discuss later. Together, these findings position flexible participation as a central design principle. Learner-centered virtual peers should adapt the threshold and timing of their interventions to each child’s ongoing activity and interaction style. 

7.1.3 Children’s Emotional Experiences in Virtual Peer Learning . Because young children are still developing emotion regulation [84, 90], virtual peer learning can introduce complex emotional dynamics alongside pedagogical opportunities. In our study, U9 frequently exhaled deeply, showed tension, and responded only passively; he relaxed only after the lesson ended, spending an extended time carefully inspecting the peers’ artifacts. U11 communicated normally at first, but grew increasingly impatient as peer responses and audio continued to appear, eventually covering his ears, stating “I don’t want to listen anymore,” and asking to stop. U15 initially engaged actively in explaining and creating solutions, expressing delight when a peer used the same method. However, when that peer later praised a different approach, U15 sent a blaming message, became angry, and stopped responding across several consecutive topics. She rejoined the chat only after telling her peers that she was angry because “they all said things that did not support her”. These cases highlight the need for peer-learning systems to respond appropriately to children’s potential emotional reactions. First, systems should enhance affect-awareness for young users through in-system interactions [2] and external signals such as tone of voice, facial expressions, gaze, and body movements [43, 108], provided privacy is preserved [4]. Second, systems should adapt the timing, frequency, and content of messages—for example, by enabling peers to affirm children’s emotions [28], perform relational repairs [19], or acknowledge the value in children’s solutions. Together, affect-aware sensing and interactional adaptation can help young children remain safe and engaged in virtual peer learning. 

7.2 Turning Multi-Peer Interaction into Productive Mathematics Learning 

Creating rich opportunities for peer interaction does not guarantee that learning will automatically occur. Our research highlights that translating interaction into learning depends on at least three interconnected factors: whether the system creates reciprocal learning oppurtunities, whether children can recognize the mathematical value of peers’ methods, and whether interactions are orchestrated into a cognitively progressive learning process. Peers4U 23 

7.2.1 Enabling Reciprocal Learning through Heterogeneous Peers . The value of heterogeneous peers extends beyond presenting solutions at different levels; it lies in providing learners with cognitively complementary peers, thereby creating reciprocal learning opportunities [22, 27, 47]. Leading Peers can introduce novel strategies that push the learner’s boundaries, whereas Struggling Peers can provide opportunities to explain, correct, and help. Crucially, these roles need not be rigidly fixed. A Struggling Peer may still possess a basic strategy that the learner has not used, while a Leading Peer may ask the learner for clarification or assistance on a particular issue. Virtual peers can therefore support a dynamic form of reciprocal learning . The direction of learning—“who learns from whom”—can shift fluidly across different methods and topics. This differs from a typical teachable-agent paradigm, where the child is always cast as the tutor [14, 39]. Here, the contributory role emerges locally and temporarily within a holistic peer relationship of mutual learning, preserving the benefits of learning-by-teaching without permanently positioning participants as stronger or weaker. This flexibility represents a practical advantage of virtual peers. Physical peer groupings are constrained by children’s existing abilities and social relationships, whereas virtual groups can be tailored to an individual learner and adjust their roles as interaction unfolds. Heterogeneity can thus become a dynamic pedagogical resource within an enduring reciprocal relationship. 

7.2.2 Making Virtual Peers’ Expertise Recognizable to Children . Designing virtual peers with greater mathe-matical expertise does not ensure that children will recognize it. Although every peer in Condition B was designed as a Leading Peer, children did not perceive these peers as having higher ability. Their judgments of peer competence may not be based solely on the mathematical value of the artifacts, but also on social signals such as help-seeking behaviors, question frequency, and conversational style. For instance, some children interpreted peers’ repeated pedagogical questions as a sign of ignorance, concluding that they themselves were the more knowledgeable learners. This gap matters pedagogically: a novel method can influence learning only when the learner recognizes its value. This may be particularly challenging for younger children, who may rely on their own familiar methods and lack the metacognitive skills to actively compare and adopt new strategies [38, 92]. Systems should therefore construct cognitively useful peers while also making their expertise and learning value perceptible and interpretable to the child. One possible direction is to leverage richer social and pedagogical signals to help learners identify content worth attending to [29, 51, 98]. This could involve having other peers explicitly endorse a valuable method, introducing lightweight facilitator guidance, or designing clearer expertise cues. Future designs could also integrate teacher-led instruction organically with peer learning, allowing teachers or facilitators to provide scaffolding at critical moments. Yet a strong authoritative presence may undermine the reciprocal and equal character of peer interaction. Balancing horizontal peer equality with vertical pedagogical authority remains a crucial challenge for future system design. 

7.2.3 Orchestrating Peer Interaction along a Cognitive Progression . Pedagogical orchestration remains neces-sary for moving peer interaction toward deeper mathematical learning. Young children may not spontaneously move from understanding a method toward analyzing, comparing, and transferring it, nor identify common mathematical structures across multiple representations—all of which requires appropriate guidance [46, 75, 76]. An underlying cognitive progression can guide open peer interaction from casual social exchange toward deeper cognitive activity. This also explains the limitations of allowing virtual peers to initiate topics independently based on their own personas. As our expert evaluation demonstrated, persona-centered topic generation can produce naturalistic, socially plausible peer-to-peer messages, yet such messages frequently fail to provide a clear participatory role for the learner or align with the learner’s current level of understanding. 24 Zheng et al. Multi-peer learning systems therefore need to maintain both social openness and pedagogical structure . The interaction can feel open, equal, and emergent, while the system orchestrates topics along a cognitive trajectory, moving from observation and comprehension toward application, comparison, evaluation, and eventually, creation and transfer. Such orchestration can evolve from predefined sequences of learning activities [48, 103] into a dynamic process that adapts to the learner’s evolving understanding. This more adaptive form can preserve the openness of peer interaction while guiding children toward deeper conceptual understanding and strategy transfer. 

7.3 Extending Artifact-Mediated Peer Learning to Broader Educational Contexts 

Although this study focuses on children’s mathematics, artifact-mediated peer learning may extend to broader ed-ucational contexts. In more advanced mathematics, learners still construct, interpret, and connect representations to understand abstract concepts[26]. Science learning likewise relies on dynamic representations that make abstract relationships observable. For example, Augmented Physics turns static textbook diagrams into interactive simulations of topics such as optics, circuits, and kinematics [32]. Such representations could become shared artifacts that learners and virtual peers manipulate and discuss together. Scaling this paradigm, however, requires artifacts that are both credible and cognitively diverse. During development, we found that directly generating multimodal problem-solving processes with LLMs often produced overly polished and mature artifacts, missing the irregularities, partial errors, and subtle diversity of human reasoning. Similar limitations have been reported in LLM-based student simulation [109]. We therefore assembled Peers4U ’s artifact repository from authentic child-produced artifacts and researcher-authored supplements. This approach was manageable for early mathematics, but constructing comparable repositories may become a bottleneck in domains where authentic artifacts are more costly to obtain or produce. 

8 Limitations and Future Work 

First, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the small sample of 22 children aged 6–8, recruited from one Chinese city, and by our focus on one mathematics topic. The single, researcher-present laboratory session may also amplify novelty effects and cannot reveal how participation, peer relationships, or interest evolve over time. Longitudinal deployments across populations, topics, and everyday learning settings are needed. Second, our immediate pre–post task and small between-subjects conditions provide limited evidence of learning effectiveness. Without a learning control condition or delayed measures, we cannot establish durable learning gains or transfer. The persona-centered baseline was evaluated offline by two experts and never entered children’s interactions; an online comparison is required to understand its effects on participation and learning. Finally, the pedagogical value of peer profiles and artifact assignments produced by our VLM analysis and hand-crafted rules remains unvalidated, while expert-identified low-quality artifacts call for stronger curation. We also held group size and total artifacts constant at four peers and approximately ten artifacts, leaving their effects on social presence and cognitive load unknown. Future work should validate and adapt peer construction, group size, and artifact exposure to support productive learning.

A.2 Probe Implementation 

Peer-solution configurations. To expose participants to different forms of peer heterogeneity, we implemented three configurations for presenting peer solutions: (1) a developmental configuration that followed the developmental progression of the underlying mathematical strategies; (2) a diversity configuration that maximized variation among the presented artifacts; and (3) a researcher-selected configuration that enabled targeted presentation of unusual, incomplete, or flawed solutions. In the developmental configuration, the probe mapped the child’s submitted solutions to a task-specific cognitive graph adapted from OpenCD [113], prioritized artifacts near the child’s next developmental steps, and updated its estimate as artifacts were viewed. The diversity configuration represented solutions by their mathematical behavior features and selected artifacts dissimilar to both the child’s solutions and previously presented artifacts. These two configurations initially displayed four artifacts and added two when the researcher requested more. In the researcher-selected configuration, flawed solutions were marked as needing help and opened with a short, pre-scripted request for assistance before the conversation began. 

Virtual-peer interaction. The probe used one artifact-grounded Chat Agent based on Gemini 3 Flash Preview (temper-ature 0.7). For each turn, it received the associated task, the peer artifact’s complete multimodal construction process and analysis summary, and the conversation history, and produced a concise reply from a peer persona aligned with the mathematical understanding reflected in that artifact. Children’s speech was transcribed with Volcengine streaming ASR, and peer replies were streamed as speech using ElevenLabs’ eleven_v3 model and a childlike voice. To further de-identify the original children’s narration during artifact replay, its pitch was shifted.

B System Design B.1 Artifact Analysis Agents 

Artifact preprocessing used the two VLM agents described in the main text. The Solution Artifact Comprehension Agent received the complete multimodal construction process and produced a concise description of the solution. The Behavior Detection and Cognitive Mapping Agent compared that process with the task-specific behavior rubrics and mapped the detected mathematical behaviors to competencies in the cognitive graph. When a behavior fell outside the predefined rubrics, this analysis included an additional outlier-diagnosis call to identify the relevant competencies. These outputs supported peer-profile construction and artifact assignment. 32 Zheng et al. 

B.2 Personalized Peer Construction 

Peer-profile construction. The system used a topological enumeration algorithm over the task-specific cognitive graph to generate prerequisite-consistent states for Leading and Struggling Peers, and sampled the required number of each according to the group composition. Struggling Peers were also allowed to master selected foundational competencies that the learner had not yet demonstrated, creating opportunities for reciprocal tutoring. 

Artifact assignment. Artifacts were assigned in three passes. First, the system covered the learner’s mastery boundary, intermediate learning paths, and target competencies with artifacts compatible with each peer profile. Second, it added role-consistent incomplete or flawed artifacts to support correction and learning-by-teaching. Third, it supplemented the portfolios so that each peer had at least two artifacts, relevant terminal competencies were represented, and the group contained ten artifacts overall at least. When several assignments were possible, the priorities were candidate scarcity, cognitive fit, distributing repeated competency coverage across peers, balancing portfolio sizes, and novelty in mathematical behavior and modality. Figure 11 provides two additional construction examples: a Mixed Group for the task of representing 17 (Figure 11a) and a Leading Group for an addition task (Figure 11b). 

B.3 Adaptive Conversational Agency 

Activity intensity. To estimate interruption cost from interface behavior, the activity monitor grouped client events into four intensity levels. No-intensity events included opening or closing a chat panel and completing a replay; low-intensity events included navigating among peers, artifacts, and panels or pausing a replay; high-intensity events included listening to a message, playing or seeking within a solution replay, and changing a chat attachment; and extreme-intensity events included composing, recording, or sending a message and actively creating or submitting an artifact. These levels controlled how long the system continued to treat the learner as engaged: more intensive activity prolonged the waiting period, and extreme activity could cancel a pending proactive message. 

Proactive follow-ups. During an ongoing group topic, sustained silence could trigger a pickup follow-up , in which a peer re-entered the discussion with a more accessible invitation grounded in the current topic. Before the learner first responded, the system allowed up to two pickup follow-ups; after a response, it allowed one further pickup. If the learner continued to remain silent after the available pickups—or during a private topic, where pickups were not used—an end follow-up produced a brief, concluding peer message without posing another question and closed the topic. Follow-ups were withheld while the relevant chat channel contained an unread message, preserving an opportunity for the learner to inspect the existing messages first. 

Message-generation agents. The formal system used four message-generation agents, summarized in Table 7. The three chat agents used interaction-specific context and output constraints, while the Topic Agent followed the learner-centered orchestration described in the main text. All four used Gemini 3.6 Flash with a temperature of 0.

C.3 Post-study Questionnaire and Interview 

The child-facing questionnaire used labeled five-point pictorial faces adapted from the Smileyometer [33]; labels were customized to each construct while retaining an ordered negative-to-positive response. The researcher read or clarified items when needed without suggesting an answer. 

Peer liking. Children rated each peer from strongly dislike to strongly like and then ranked the four peers from most to least liked. 

Perceived mathematics level. Children rated each peer’s mathematics level from very low to very high, ranked the four peers, and placed themselves within that ranking. 

Social pressure. Children rated four statements: “After seeing their solutions, I felt less capable than them”; “After our discussion, I felt less capable than them”; “During the discussion, I worried that my method was poor”; and “During the discussion, I was reluctant to share my ideas”. Each item used five labeled responses from endorsement to the opposite position. 

Experience. Children compared learning this way with regular classes on two five-point scales: much more relaxed to much more tiring, and much more interesting to much more boring. The structured interview followed a common set of questions aligned with the questionnaire; the researcher used brief clarification prompts to elicit reasons or concrete examples. The questions were: (1) Did you learn any new methods, and where did they come from? (2) Which of your own and the peers’ methods did you like most? (3) Did you help peers, and do you like teaching peers? (4) Did you ask peers for help, and do you like doing so? (5) How did this experience compare with the ways you usually learn mathematics? and (6) Which parts did you like or dislike? 

C.4 Analysis of Child-study Data 

Message analysis. For learner messages, two researchers first discussed an initial set of coding dimensions and independently coded all messages from four children. They then reconciled their interpretations and finalized a codebook covering content domain (mathematical learning, peer-interaction coordination, or off-task conversation) and conversational role (initiating, responding, or continuing the learner’s own prior message). Each response was additionally linked to the specific prior message it answered. One researcher applied the codebook to the remaining learner messages, after which the second researcher reviewed the complete coding. For the larger set of non-learner messages, the researchers defined a categorical codebook covering learning relevance, question versus non-question, open- versus closed-ended questions, and the target of group-chat questions (another peer, the whole group, or the learner). Given the volume of these messages, their comparatively standardized and explicit system-generated language, and the direct binary or three-way classification criteria, researchers used GPT-5.6-sol 36 Zheng et al. to apply these codes before they reviewed the resulting classifications. Channel, topic, and follow-up sequence were obtained directly from system metadata. A group-chat topic was eligible for the follow-up response analysis when the learner had not yet contributed to that topic, or when the learner’s first message was coded as a response linked to a message within that topic. 

Artifact analysis. One researcher initially coded the pre- and post-test artifacts. Both researchers then jointly reviewed every artifact from the beginning and coded its representational forms, calculation strategies, and correctness based on the mathematical behavior features synthesized in OpenCD [113]. Comparing each child’s two artifacts yielded three non-exclusive forms of positive change: adopting a new calculation strategy, introducing a new visual representation, and improving correctness. For additional artifacts created during learning, we aligned camera video, screen recordings, and system logs to reconstruct the surrounding interaction. We coded typical events preceding creation, actions immediately following it, and content relationships to the child’s own or peers’ earlier artifacts and chat suggestions. 

Interview analysis. Because the interviews followed a shared set of structured questions, we conducted question-guided qualitative content analysis: responses were organized by interview question and recurring explanations and examples were grouped within each question. Quotations reported in the paper were translated from Chinese into English. 

Quantitative analysis. Log-derived behavioral measures and questionnaire responses were summarized descriptively. Between-condition comparisons used two-sided, tie-aware exact Mann–Whitney 𝑈 tests, with Cliff’s 𝛿 as the effect size. Comparisons of response rates across message characteristics used two-sided, user-stratified exact permutation tests to account for messages nested within children. Full test specifications and results are reported in Appendix D.1. 

C.5 Post-hoc Expert Evaluation 

C.5.1 Evaluation Dataset and Persona-centered Baseline. At each intervention point, the learner-centered candidate was the message delivered during the user study. The persona-centered candidate was generated using the interaction state immediately before that intervention, including the task, available artifacts, and conversation history. We implemented the persona-centered baseline as two sequential LLM calls. The first selected the peer most likely to initiate a topic based on the peers’ cognitive profiles, available artifacts, recent participation, and the current conversational context. The second prompted the selected peer to generate a topic from its own perspective, jointly determining the message and attached artifacts from its profile and relevant chat history. Both calls used Gemini 3.6 Flash with a temperature of 0. 

C.5.2 Review Interface and Rating Instrument. The custom interface (Figure 12) replayed each session chronologically. At each intervention point, experts were provided with the conversation history up to that point. They were then presented with two candidate topic messages in a randomized order, with the condition identities hidden. The interface displayed each message’s sender, channel, and attached artifacts. Experts independently rated both messages before the actual subsequent trajectory was revealed for the next turn. Each topic was rated for its overall pedagogical appropriateness within the displayed context. The five-point scale ranged from 1 ( very inappropriate ) to 5 ( very appropriate ). For ratings of 3 or below, experts were required to select at least one predefined reason or provide a written explanation. The predefined tags included: too difficult for the learner, too easy for the learner, limited learner participation, inconsistent with the peer’s cognitive profile, potentially misleading, and repetitive. > Fig. 12. Expert-review interface. The interaction history preceding a topic appears above the divider, while one blinded candidate topic appears below it. Experts rated each candidate on a five-point appropriateness scale and could select reason tags or enter a comment.

C.5.3 Review Procedure. The review followed a three-phase protocol: (1) Onboarding and Calibration. We first introduced the review task, rating scale, reason tags, and replay interface. The experts then independently reviewed 3 participant sessions. Subsequently, they met with the researchers to compare their judgments and discuss their interpretations of the scale and tags. The researchers clarified rating criteria and procedural questions, while the experts resolved discrepancies in the calibration cases. This established shared rating criteria for the remaining sessions. (2) Independent Review. The remaining 19 sessions contained 133 intervention points, yielding 266 candidate messages. To ensure blind review, both experts rated the candidate messages before viewing the subsequent trajectory at each intervention point. The mean absolute difference between the two experts’ pre-consensus ratings was 0.387. Because 85.5% of the individual ratings were 4 or 5, the concentrated marginal distributions may partly explain the moderate weighted 𝜅 despite high raw agreement. (3) Consensus Meeting. After both independent reviews were completed, the experts used a comparison view showing their scores, selected tags, and comments side by side. They jointly revisited every candidate message for which their scores differed by more than one point and adjusted their ratings until the difference was no greater than one point. During this meeting, they also briefly explained to the researchers each message they regarded as inappropriate. The two resulting ratings were then averaged to obtain the final score for each message. For the reasons for inappropriateness, all distinct reasons identified by either expert were retained. The researchers interpreted and organized these reasons 38 Zheng et al. with reference to the experts’ selected tags, written comments, and meeting explanations. The final analysis utilized these scores and reasons across all 22 participant sessions.

Both comparisons included 22 children, Peers4U 39 of whom 20 contributed nondegenerate permutation information. Wilson 95% CIs are message-level descriptive intervals and do not account for within-child clustering. 

• Questions versus non-question messages. Questions received responses in 187 of 442 eligible cases (42.3%, 95% CI 

[37 .8% , 47 .0% ]), compared with 52 of 275 non-question messages (18.9%, 95% CI [14 .7% , 24 .0% ]). The response-rate difference was 23.4 percentage points ( 𝑝 = 2.58 × 10 −13 ). 

• Closed- versus open-ended questions in group chat. Closed-ended questions received responses in 74 of 183 eligible cases (40.4%, 95% CI [33 .6% , 47 .7% ]), compared with 48 of 174 open-ended questions (27.6%, 95% CI 

[21 .5% , 34 .7% ]). The response-rate difference was 12.9 percentage points ( 𝑝 = .0105).
