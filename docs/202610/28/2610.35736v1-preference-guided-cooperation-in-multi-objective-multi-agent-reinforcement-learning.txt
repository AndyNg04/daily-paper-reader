## Abstract 

Multi-objective multi-agent reinforcement learning (MOMARL) addresses agent coordination and objective trade-offs simultane-ously while optimising overall system performance. Existing work tends to treat multi-agent cooperation and multi-objective optimi-sation as largely independent components of a learning problem, which amounts to an exorbitant complexity except in trivial cases. We study here whether the structure provided by multiple objec-tives can itself be exploited to facilitate cooperation, rather than being treated as an additional optimisation challenge. We formulate MOMARL at both the team and individual levels and introduce objective-wise counterfactual credit assignment to evaluate each agent’s contribution across the team objectives. We propose Meta-Gradient Informed Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MIRL), with static (MIRL-S) and dynamic (MIRL-D) vari-ants. By incorporating meta-gradient information, MIRL enables agents to learn how to prioritise objectives individually while col-lectively optimising the system-level objective, allowing the multi-objective structure to directly guide cooperation. Experiments on a multi-objective multiple particle environment show that our meth-ods improve Pareto-front quality over baselines, with the dynamic variant achieving the strongest performance. 

## Keywords 

Multi-agent reinforcement learning, Multi-objective reinforcement learning 

## 1 Introduction 

Many real-world decision-making problems naturally involve both multiple decision makers and multiple objectives. For example, au-tonomous vehicles [30], drones [15], smart homes [37], and collab-orative industrial systems [26] require several autonomous entities to interact with a shared environment while simultaneously con-sidering different and potentially conflicting performance criteria. These requirements motivate two important extensions of conven-tional reinforcement learning. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) addresses decision-making with multiple simultaneously interacting agents, where the learning problem is complicated by non-stationarity caused by other agents simultaneously interacting with the environment and adapting their policies, partial observ-ability due to agents having access to only local information, and the need to develop effective coordination among agents. Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL), in contrast, extends reinforcement learning beyond the optimisation of a single scalar objective to problems involving multiple, potentially conflicting objectives. Rather than searching for one optimal policy, MORL commonly aims to approximate a Pareto front consisting of poli-cies that represent different trade-offs among objectives, allowing solutions to be selected according to different preferences. Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MO-MARL) brings these two dimensions together, providing a more comprehensive modelling framework for complex decision-making environments in which both interactions among multiple decision makers and trade-offs among multiple objectives must be consid-ered. Although MARL and MORL have each developed into sub-stantial research areas, their intersection remains comparatively under-explored. Recent work has gradually extended the scope of MOMARL through dedicated deep learning methods [14], standard-ised benchmarks [7, 10], and more flexible formulations of agent utilities and preferences [20]. However, these existing approaches have largely treated the multi-objective component as an additional optimisation dimension built on top of the multi-agent problem, rather than as a source of structure that can itself inform and shape the broader process of multi-agent learning. In this work, we take a different perspective on the MOMARL formulation and demonstrate that the multi-objective structure itself can be exploited to facilitate multi-agent cooperation. We aim to provide agents with a higher-level adaptive capability, allowing them to adjust how they contribute to the team according to the current cooperative situation. To realise this capability, we pro-pose a bi-level preference formulation with a team preference and learnable individual preferences. Since these individual preferences influence how the policy itself is updated, learning them naturally forms a learning-to-learn problem. We therefore derive a novel meta-gradient mechanism that adapts the individual preferences according to how the resulting policy updates affect subsequent team-level performance. Building on this framework, we introduce Meta-Gradient Informed Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MIRL), a novel algorithm that enables agents to adapt their learn-ing across multiple objectives while remaining aligned with the system-level objective. Our main contributions are as follows: (1) We formulate cooperative MOMARL from both team and in-dividual levels, where a team preference defines the desired system-level objective trade-off and learnable individual preferences pro-vide adaptive guidance for individual behaviour. (2) We introduce objective-wise counterfactual credit assignment, which evaluates each agent’s contribution separately across the team objectives and provides objective-specific learning signals. (3) We derive a novel meta-gradient preference adaptation for-mulation, treating individual preferences as meta-variables that shape policy learning and optimising them according to their effect arXiv:2610.35736v1 [cs.MA] 26 Oct 2026 on subsequent team-level performance. We develop a static vari-ant, MIRL-S, and a dynamic variant, MIRL-D, which further adapts preferences according to the current cooperative context. (4) We conduct comprehensive experiments in Multi-Agent Parti-cle Environment. The results demonstrate that our methods consis-tently improve Pareto-front quality over a range of baselines, with the dynamic variant achieving the strongest overall performance.

## 2.3 Pareto Optimality in MOMARL 

In multi-objective optimisation, the concept of optimality differs from the single-objective case. Typically, no single solution simulta-neously maximises all objectives due to inherent trade-offs. Without any additional information about the user’s preference, there may therefore be multiple possibly optimal solutions. Thus, in MORL, Pareto optimality is introduced as a solution concept for possibly op-timal policies. This concept extends naturally to the multi-objective multi-agent setting.

## 3 Related Work 

In recent years, deep Reinforcement Learning has shown great promise in solving complex decision-making problems, enabling sig-nificant advancements across a wide range of applications [19, 31]. In real-world scenarios, however, problems often feature multiple, potentially conflicting objectives and involve multiple agents inter-acting in a shared environment, which raises challenging problems in the RL domain. A growing number of studies have investigated these challenges from different perspectives. We introduce relevant works in the following. MORL extends RL methods to handle multiple, potentially con-flicting objectives, and existing approaches can be broadly grouped into single-policy methods and multi-policy methods for approxi-mating the Pareto front. Single-policy approaches typically convert the multi-objective problem into a single-objective task using a predefined preference or weighting scheme, including linear scalar-ization [4, 38], and more advanced scalarization functions such as Chebyshev methods [39], hypervolume-based approaches [46] and the addition of concave terms to rewards [22]. Multi-policy MORL strategies approximate the Pareto front by learning a diverse collection of policies. One direction is developing single models conditioned on preferences [1, 18]. Further techniques for gener-ating policy sets involve Generalised Policy Improvement (GPI) for sample-efficient learning [3] or the development of transfer-able policy components using representations like successor fea-tures [2]. Besides these approaches, several studies have explored the structural relationships among Pareto-optimal solutions from other perspectives, such as navigation along the Pareto set [44], exploitation of regularities in policy parameter space [42], and pol-icy manifolds [25, 41] to improve the efficiency of Pareto-front reconstruction. MARL considers learning problems involving multiple inter-acting agents. In this paper, we focus on the domain of coopera-tive MARL, where several agents must cooperate to accomplish a shared task. Independent learning is one of the simplest MARL paradigms, in which each agent receives local observations and applies a reinforcement learning algorithm independently [35]. Al-though such approaches can be effective in simple settings [6, 11], they suffer from non-stationarity because other agents are simul-taneously interacting with the environment and adapting their policies [13]. The centralised training with decentralised execu-tion (CTDE) framework has therefore widely adopted to exploit additional global information during training while retaining decen-tralised policies at execution time, as in MADDPG [21], MAAC [16], and MAPPO [45]. Credit assignment is another major challenge in cooperative MARL. When agents share a team reward, it can be difficult to determine the contribution of each individual agent to the overall outcome, potentially leading to the lazy agent problem, where poorly contributing agents may still receive high rewards due to the successful behaviour of their teammates. To address this issue, value-decomposition methods such as VDN [33] and QMIX [28] decompose the joint value function into agent-level utilities, while COMA [9] uses a counterfactual baseline to eval-uate each agent’s contribution to the team outcome. Cooperative MARL has also explored mechanisms for facilitating coordinated behaviours, including learned communication [8, 32] and emergent role specialisation [40]. MOMARL studies multi-agent decision-making problems with multiple, potentially conflicting objectives, where interactions among agents and trade-offs among objectives must be consid-ered jointly. Early studies mainly addressed application-specific problems, often reducing multiple objectives to a scalar learning objective, as in cooperative traffic signal control [17], or considering independent agent learning, as in smart-home energy control [37]. Later work moved beyond application-specific formulations to-wards more general learning mechanisms and conceptual analyses for MOMARL. Potential-based reward shaping and difference re-wards were investigated to address credit assignment in cooperative multi-objective settings [23]. A systematic utility-based analysis further characterised multi-objective multi-agent decision-making problems according to their reward structures, utility functions, and corresponding solution concepts [27]. More recently, dedicated deep MOMARL methods have been developed. MO-MIX [14] extends value-decomposition methods to the multi-objective setting, using preference-conditioned value functions to learn cooperative policies for different objective trade-offs. The development of standardised benchmarks has also supported broader evaluation of MOMARL methods, with MOMAland [7] providing a diverse benchmark suite and MOSMAC [10] introducing sequential multi-objective tasks with longer temporal horizons. Recent work has further considered continuous state and action spaces through MOMA-AC [5], and heterogeneous utility functions across agents [20]. The above works have explored the MOMARL problem from different perspectives, but have mainly considered multiple objec-tives from the perspective of system-level optimisation. Compara-tively less attention has been paid to how multiple objectives can shape the learning and cooperation of individual agents. We move beyond a solely system-level view by incorporating an individual-level learning-to-learn perspective into MOMARL, providing agents with the adaptive capability to adjust how they contribute to the cooperative task. From this perspective, rather than viewing the multi-objective component only as an additional optimisation diffi-culty, we claim that the structure provided by multiple objectives can itself be exploited to facilitate multi-agent cooperation.

## 4.2 Objective-Wise Counterfactual Credit Assignment 

In cooperative MARL, a shared team reward alone does not directly reveal how much each agent’s action contributes to the resulting team performance. Counterfactual Multi-Agent Policy Gradients (COMA) [9] addresses this problem by comparing the value of the actual joint action with a counterfactual baseline in which one agent’s action is varied while the actions of the other agents are kept fixed. Within the MOMARL structure formulated above, an agent needs to assess not only its overall contribution to the team, but also how it contributes to each of the team objectives. We therefore extend counterfactual credit assignment to preserve this objective-wise structure, representing the contribution of each agent as a vector rather than immediately reducing it to a single scalar quantity. ## 4.3 Static Meta-Gradient Informed Preference Adaptation 

The objective-wise counterfactual credit developed above provides each agent with separate estimates of its contribution across the team objectives. The next question is how these objective-wise contributions should be combined when updating the individual policy. Rather than fixing this combination manually, we aim to let each agent learn how its individual preference should guide its learning so that the resulting behaviour better contributes to the overall team performance. This naturally leads to a learning-to-learn problem. In addition to learning the policy itself, the agent must learn how its learning sig-nal should be constructed from the objective-wise credit available to it. Inspired by meta-gradient reinforcement learning [43], we develop a meta-gradient preference adaptation mechanism that al-lows each agent to adapt its individual preference according to how that preference affects subsequent team performance. Specifically, we treat the individual preference as a differentiable meta-variable that controls how the agent’s objective-wise credit is combined during its policy update. ## 5 Experiments 5.1 Experiment Setup 

Benchmark Problems. We conduct experiments using the Multi-Agent Particle Environment (MPE) [21], which is a widely used RL benchmark. We extend the original MPE to a multi-objective setting by retaining the original task reward as the first objective and introducing a second objective that captures the energy consumed during interaction. We evaluate the proposed methods and baselines on four cooperative tasks: Spread , Formation , Line , and Reference .The first three tasks involve three agents with different spatial coordination requirements, while Reference involves two agents and additionally requires communication for successful cooperation. Full environment definitions are provided in Appendix B.1. 

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate the quality of the resulting Pareto fronts using three standard metrics: Hypervolume (HV), Expected Utility (EU), and Sparsity (SP), following the formalism in [12, 47, 48]. HV measures the overall quality and coverage of the Pareto front, EU evaluates performance across different objective preferences, and SP measures the distribution of solutions along the front. Higher HV and EU and lower SP indicate better performance. Since SP is scale-dependent and less directly related to decision quality, we report it mainly as a complementary metric to assess coverage diversity. More details can be found in Appendix B.2. 

Baselines. As dedicated MOMARL algorithms remain relatively limited, we consider five baselines of increasing complexity: DTDE-PPO , CTDE-PPO-V , CTDE-PPO-Q , MO-COMA , and MO-MIX . DTDE-PPO represents the fully decentralised setting, where each agent independently learns its policy using PPO [29] network. Given the surprisingly strong performance of PPO previously observed in co-operative MARL [45], we further extend MAPPO to the MOMARL setting under CTDE and consider two variants, CTDE-PPO-V and 

CTDE-PPO-Q , using centralised 𝑉 - and 𝑄 -critics, respectively. MO-COMA extends COMA [9] to the multi-objective setting and pro-vides counterfactual credit assignment for individual agents across multiple objectives. It also serves as an ablation baseline for evalu-ating the additional preference-adaptation mechanisms introduced in our methods. We additionally compare against MO-MIX [14], a dedicated MOMARL algorithm based on multi-objective value decomposition. All methods except MO-MIX operate in continuous action spaces. Since MO-MIX only supports discrete actions, it is evaluated using a discretised version of the action space. Further implementation details are provided in Appendix B.3.

CTDE-PPO-Q generally performs better than CTDE-PPO-V, sug-gesting that conditioning the critic on the joint action provides a more informative learning signal for coordinating individual ac-tions. It is worth noting that these PPO-based methods constitute strong rather than trivial baselines. PPO-based methods have previ-ously demonstrated surprisingly strong performance in MARL [45], and the variants here retain this strong policy-optimisation back-bone while extending it to the multi-objective setting. MO-MIX exhibits a different behaviour from the other methods. As shown in Figure 4, it often produces relatively dense groups of solutions, which is also reflected by its low SP values, but these solutions tend to occupy a more limited region of the objective space and result in substantially lower HV and EU. One possible reason is that MO-MIX is a single preference-conditioned network, which may be more sensitive to hyperparameter choices and can be more difficult to train. Under the same overall interaction budget, a single model must represent policies across the full preference space, and this shared representation may, to some extent, compromise performance. In addition, MO-MIX operates on discrete actions and therefore requires discretisation of the originally continuous MPE action space, which may further limit its achievable performance. MO-COMA achieves substantially stronger performance than the PPO-based baselines across all tasks, showing that objective-wise counterfactual credit assignment alone already provides an effective learning signal for cooperative MOMARL. Its performance is particularly strong on Formation and Line , where it is already close to both MIRL variants. On Spread and Reference , however, the MIRL methods provide clearer improvements over MO-COMA. We analyse these differences further in the ablation study. 

## 5.3 Ablation and Further Analysis 

To isolate the contribution of the proposed meta-gradient informed preference adaptation mechanism, we compare the ablation base-line MO-COMA with static MIRL-S and dynamic MIRL-D methods. The benefit of preference adaptation varies with the coordina-tion requirements of the task. On Spread , MIRL-S improves over MO-COMA, and MIRL-D provides a further improvement. This task requires agents to continuously adjust their relative positions and contributions during interaction, making the ability to adapt individual preferences to the current cooperative context particu-larly useful. On Reference , both MIRL-S and MIRL-D improve over MO-COMA, while the difference between the two MIRL variants is comparatively small. In this task, the cooperative requirement, par-ticularly the communication component, is relatively stable and less dependent on the changing cooperative situation. The additional flexibility of dynamic preference adaptation therefore provides a smaller advantage. By contrast, on Formation and Line , MO-COMA already achieves performance very close to the two MIRL variants. These tasks have relatively simple and stable coordination require-ments, and objective-wise counterfactual credit assignment alone appears sufficient to produce strong cooperative behaviour, leaving less room for additional gains from preference adaptation. 86 Conclusion 

In this work, we show that the multi-objective structure can be exploited to facilitate multi-agent cooperation, rather than be-ing treated solely as an additional optimisation challenge in MO-MARL. Building on this novel perspective, we formulate team-level and individual-level preferences to introduce adaptive agent-level guidance while preserving the system-level objective trade-off. To enable preference-guided cooperation across agents, we derive objective-wise counterfactual credit assignment together with a meta-gradient informed preference adaptation mechanism, provid-ing a principled way to learn how individual preferences should shape policy updates. These developments lead to MIRL, with static (MIRL-S) and dynamic (MIRL-D) variants, which achieve strong Pareto-front performance across the benchmark tasks, with MIRL-D showing the best overall results. Our ablation results further demonstrate that meta-gradient informed preference adaptation provides additional benefits beyond counterfactual credit assign-ment, particularly when cooperation requires greater adaptability.

## B.3 Training Details 

Training budget and preference handling. All methods are trained using a fixed budget of 5 × 10 6 joint environment steps for each environment and random seed. We use 3 random seeds for each environment, an episode horizon of 𝐻 = 25, and a discount factor of 𝛾 = 0.99. Except for MO-MIX , all methods use linear scalar-isation and are trained separately for 𝐾 = 21 uniformly spaced team preferences with an interval of 0 .05, with the total training budget divided evenly across these preferences. For the PPO-based baselines, the vector-valued reward is scalarised using the fixed team preference. In our methods, the scalarisation weights 𝝎 team 

represent the user-specified team preference and define the de-sired system-level objective trade-off, while the learned individual preferences are used within the agent learning process. MO-MIX ,in contrast, uses a single preference-conditioned network trained with the complete 5 × 10 6-step budget, with the requested team preference provided directly as an input and training preferences sampled using the same interval of 0 .05. 

PPO-based baselines. We consider three PPO-based baselines with progressively richer information available during training. DTDE-PPO uses fully decentralised training and execution, with each agent learning independently from its local observation and scalarised reward. CTDE-PPO-V introduces a centralised state-value critic during training, while CTDE-PPO-Q instead uses a cen-tralised joint-action critic. All three methods retain decentralised actors during execution and use the same training budget and pref-erence settings described above. Their main hyperparameters are summarised in Table 2.

## D Limitations 

The learned individual preferences provide a potentially inter-pretable description of how different agents emphasise the underly-ing objectives, and our current results show that these preferences adapt differently across agents and cooperative contexts. However, the present benchmark tasks are not designed to cleanly separate distinct behavioural tendencies or cooperative roles, which limits our ability to quantitatively relate changes in the learned prefer-ences to specific changes in agent behaviour. A more systematic analysis would therefore require environments in which such be-havioural tendencies can be independently identified and measured. This represents an interesting direction for future work, particu-larly for studying whether the learned preferences can provide a meaningful and interpretable characterisation of agent roles and contributions. Our current evaluation is also limited to two-objective coopera-tive tasks with relatively small agent populations. Extending the analysis to larger multi-agent systems and higher-dimensional ob-jective spaces would provide a broader assessment of the scalability and generality of the proposed approach. 14
