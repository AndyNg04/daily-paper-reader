# BE-CTDE: Blockchain-Enabled Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning with Anomaly Detection and Token Economics 

Chih-Wei Huang, Hsin-Chieh Lee, Yun-Chen Yu, Ibrahim Althamary, Rongxing Lu, and Phone Lin 

Abstract —Multi-agent reinforcement learning supports cooper-ative decision-making in Internet of Things systems, but strategic deviations can disrupt coordination and undermine confidence in the training process. This paper proposes Blockchain-Enabled Centralized Training and Decentralized Execution (BE-CTDE), which uses blockchain as an active training-management layer. A behavioral verifier compares executed trajectories with shared-policy recommendations; its flags drive a token mechanism com-bining slashing, burning, rewards, and compensation. Retained balances determine each agent’s contribution to the global policy update, connecting auditable behavior to learning influence. We derive a one-round incentive condition that accounts for action-dependent compensation and the payoff from deviation, and a finite expected exclusion-time bound under explicit conditional detection and accounting assumptions, showing perfect detec-tion is unnecessary. An exact finite-population capacity bound characterizes the limits of the population-relative detector. In multi-agent path finding experiments, BE-CTDE outperforms a state-of-the-art learning-based path planner with substantially higher success rates under attack, even when adversaries form the majority. It also outperforms partially defended baselines in all configurations, with collision rates up to 35% lower than under detection without economic consequences and up to 43% lower than under conventional stake slashing. Together, these results characterize how behavioral verification and token feedback can support accountable cooperative learning. 

Index Terms —Anomaly detection, blockchain, incentive mech-anism, Internet of Things (IoT), multi-agent reinforcement learn-ing (MARL), smart contracts, token economics.

Achieving high performance, security, and reliability in such resource-constrained, interactive settings remains challenging, particu-larly amid data tampering, privacy breaches, and adversarial disruption [3]. To address decision-making complexity in these settings, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has emerged as an essential paradigm in which agents optimize resource scheduling and actions through continuous learning over shared information [4], [5]. A widely adopted framework is Centralized Training and Decentralized Execution (CTDE), which derives global insights from centralized training while preserving the distributed execution that real-world IoT latency and connectivity constraints demand. Learning-based multi-agent path finding (MAPF) commonly adopts this frame-work [6], as does the state-of-the-art SCRIMP planner [7]. The traditional training process of the CTDE global model introduces critical vulnerabilities. These include untraceable data that hinder transparency, inherent weaknesses in the cen-tralized architecture such as lack of accountability and a single point of failure [5], and policy disobedience where agents may execute malicious actions [8]. In open IoT deployments, these weaknesses are exacerbated by the broader threat landscape facing autonomous AI agents, including data poisoning, model evasion, and behavioral manipulation [9]. Purely on-device training, conversely, loses the global context cooperative tasks require. What is needed is a training framework that distributes management of the learning process for transparency and fault tolerance without sacrificing the coordination benefits of centralized training. Blockchain can provide a replicated management layer [10], [11], in which commitments to model updates and interactions support an auditable coordination history [12], [13]. Commit-ments bind recorded inputs; they do not establish sensor truth or the correctness of off-chain inference. Such deployments commonly span several operators with no single party that all of them trust, which is the setting in which a replicated ledger repays its overhead. Trust is then bounded rather than elim-inated, since the shared coordination infrastructure is trusted while the agents that participate in training are not. Blockchain alone, however, does not address policy disobedience, because it preserves submitted interaction records yet cannot determine arXiv:2610.34121v1 [cs.MA] 25 Oct 2026 2

whether recorded behavior reflects genuine cooperation or adversarial interference. Closing this loop requires behavioral verification and incentive alignment inside the training process itself, complementing recent advances in adversarial detection for cooperative multi-agent systems [14], [15]. Several architectures have been proposed at the convergence of blockchain and AI. Early frameworks pioneered decentral-ized machine learning on simulated blockchains, using token incentives to validate data quality [16]–[18]. Subsequent work added incentives for data and compute sharing on real chains but largely overlooked behavior verification and adversarial mitigation during training [19], [20]. In blockchain-integrated MARL specifically, recent efforts explore on-chain coordi-nation for multi-object tracking, path planning in cognitive vehicular networks, and resource management for blockchain-based federated learning in IoT [21]–[23]. The most di-rectly comparable framework, LearnChain [24], makes MARL model updates traceable, but its record is compiled after the fact, and an agent logged as deviant still contributes to the shared model exactly as a compliant one does. Across this literature the ledger observes training rather than participating in it, so the step from detecting misbehavior to withholding its influence over the learned policy remains open. The gap is therefore not auditability but consequence, because evidence of how an agent actually behaved is not carried back into the update that trains the shared policy. We close that loop with Blockchain-Enabled Centralized Training and Decentralized Execution (BE-CTDE), in which blockchain is an active training-management layer. The contributions are:  

> •

Closed-loop BE-CTDE. On-chain coordination and off-chain learning form an auditable training cycle: behav-ioral verification updates economic state, economic state determines aggregation weight, and insufficient balance ends an agent’s training contribution.  

> •

Detection and aggregation. A framework-agnostic ver-ifier compares executed trajectories with shared-policy recommendations using a task-specific residual. The cou-pled token mechanism applies slashing, burning, rewards, and compensation, while retained balances weight the per-agent learning contributions. This makes detected deviations economically consequential without requiring gradient-space similarity between heterogeneous agents.  

> •

Conditional theoretical guarantees. We derive the one-round incentive comparison with action-dependent com-pensation and deviation utility. Exclusion in finite ex-pected time needs no perfect detector, only history-uniform detection above a threshold, at most 1/3 in our setting even with full compensation, and the excluded adversary then contributes no new gradient. An exact ca-pacity result makes the threshold hold for every retained population.  

> •

Implementation and MAPF evaluation. A private Ethereum/Solidity implementation evaluates BE-CTDE across population scales, adversary ratios, and obstacle densities. BE-CTDE achieves higher success rates under attack than the state-of-the-art SCRIMP planner and, in every configuration, lower collision rates than partially defended baselines, by up to 35% relative to detection without economic consequences and up to 43% relative to conventional stake slashing. A. Blockchain Foundations for IoT 

Blockchain replicates an append-only, cryptographically linked ledger across nodes, replacing trust in a central opera-tor with consensus-backed immutability [11], so transactions and model-update commitments can be audited by every participant when multiple IoT operators share infrastructure without a common trusted server. Proof of Work relies on energy-intensive computational competition, whereas Proof of Stake (PoS) uses stake-based validator selection; their energy requirements differ [25]. Ethereum also supports deterministic smart contracts executed by the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) [26], encoding deposits, verifiable artifacts, rewards, and penalties as an automated trust layer. BE-CTDE uses a permissioned Ethereum management layer to coordinate training while neural-network execution remains off-chain. 

B. AI and Blockchain Integration

IoT-focused work primarily addresses federated learning, with privacy-preserving data sharing [27], blockchain resource management [23], privacy plus incentives for the Internet of Vehicles [20], and reputation-weighted token incentives for IoT ecosystems [28]. C. Byzantine-Resilient Multi-Agent Learning and Incentive Design 

Robust gradient aggregation offers a different defense. Krum [32] replaces averaging with a selection rule based on the n−f −2 nearest gradients and provides convergence under Byzantine workers; coordinate-wise median and trimmed-mean rules attain optimal statistical rates [33]. Both families assume an honest majority, so their guarantees do not extend to the majority-adversary setting evaluated in Section VI. These methods identify adversaries through statistical devia-tion in parameter space, assuming honest gradients concentrate around a common direction. In MARL, however, agents face heterogeneous states, observations, and goals, so honest gra-dients need not concentrate. BE-CTDE instead evaluates each executed trajectory against the shared policy recommendation, a behavioral reference meaningful across distinct local sub-problems. Work specific to cooperative MARL includes Li et al.’s Bayesian Adversarial Robust Dec-POMDP [34], in which Byzantine agents are latent types and honest agents maintain beliefs about peers. This approach is theoretically rigorous but requires extensive belief tracking and supplies no economic mechanism for sustained honest behavior. BE-CTDE takes a complementary path: trajectory-level verification changes ac-counting state and gradient weights, with removal conditional on crossing an exclusion threshold. BARD-MARL combines policy-graph evidence with Bayesian trust signals to diagnose compromised learned com-munication [35]. Wu and Klabjan study blockchain-based federated learning with loss-based softmax aggregation and a convergence result in a convex setting [36]. C. Threat Model 

The intended deployment separates participating agents from a permissioned training coordinator and execution ledger. Up to M < N agents can deviate from policy actions [8], [34]; the remaining agents sample or execute the prescribed policy. The model assumes authentic physical observations and an honest training coordinator. The permissioned ledger provides an auditable coordination history under its consensus assumptions [11]. 

Adversarial capabilities and objective. Attackers can re-place their sampled actions with persistent blocking, occu-pation of another agent’s goal, or repetitive motion [40]. Their objective is disruption rather than token profit. Attacker identities are used to define the adversarial conditions and evaluate outcomes; the behavioral decision rule operates on trajectory evidence. Exclusion removes an agent’s contribution to training; it does not physically remove the agent from the environment. Consequently, learning-layer containment does not guarantee that honest goals remain physically attainable. 

Trust and scope. The global policy is computed by an honest off-chain learner. A policy hash commits to its bytes, not to correct inference, and a transaction signature authenticates the submitting controller, not independent agent observations. The experiments assess learning under behavioral attacks; network consensus faults and validator incentives are out-side this evaluation. Sybil attacks, fabricated sensor records, gradient-inversion attacks, collusion channels, and concealed nonstationary attacks are outside the evaluated threat model; see Section VII.

A. Smart Contract Execution Pipeline 

The architecture separates the blockchain coordination layer from the SCRIMP learner. The contracts correspond to the stages in Fig. 2 and provide persistent records at their interfaces. BatchDataStorage receives sampled interac-tion records (oi,t , a i,t , r i,t , m i,t −1), associated with the agent, episode and policy version. The OutputHead , TDError ,

TrainingGAE and PolicyLoss interfaces anchor pol-icy/value outputs, temporal-difference residuals, advantages and policy-update information, respectively. Their names iden-tify the computations being recorded; neural inference and optimization remain off-chain. At episode end, MaliciousDetection uses recom-mended and executed behavior to form the cumulative residuals and population-relative flags of Section IV-B. 

IncentiveMechanism consumes those flags, applies de-posit forfeiture, rewards, compensation and burning, and up-dates balances and eligibility. This state determines the weights for the subsequent policy update. Keeping the detection result and its economic consequence in the same episode sequence prevents an update from combining flags from one round with balances from another. Module boundaries organize access to shared state; they do not, by themselves, establish a gas advantage over a single-contract implementation. 

B. On-Chain and Off-Chain Function Allocation 

Observation encoding, Transformer communication, the re-current output head, temporal-difference and generalized ad-vantage estimation, PPO losses, gradient computation and the final token-weighted update run in the learner. General neural computation is unsuitable for direct execution in the EVM [26]. The coordination layer instead records the behav-ioral evidence, detector outputs, economic state and policy commitments needed to associate an update with its inputs. A policy hash identifies a particular parameter snapshot, while interaction commitments identify the records used for an episode. This supports checking whether two participants refer to the same data and model. A hash is not a proof that the committed model produced an action, that a submitted loss or gradient was calculated correctly, or that physical telemetry is truthful. Such checks require the corresponding records and an agreed observation source. Likewise, a signed submission authenticates its sender without establishing the accuracy of its contents. These distinctions separate tamper-evident coordination from behavioral verification. Tuple submission can overlap off-chain computation, but the learner must use a consistent policy version and economic state. An update that depends on an unsettled balance transi-tion must wait for the required state; asynchronous submission alone does not establish an end-to-end latency bound. 

C. Solidity Implementation and Geth Architecture 

The software stack comprises Solidity 0.8.0 contracts, Geth 1.13.14 execution nodes, and a Python 3.9.21 learner connected through Web3.py 7.4.0 and HTTP-RPC. Truffle provides contract compilation/deployment interfaces and the application binary interface used for submissions and state queries [41]. The intended placement is a private Ethereum network coordinating authorized participants. Execution-client configuration, validator operation and the chosen finality pol-icy are separate deployment concerns; the module description does not establish consensus performance. Contract arithmetic represents numerical quantities using scale 10 6, giving six decimal places for scaled values. D. Workload and IoT Placement 

With K sampling boundaries and N agents, interaction collection produces N K agent records. At a 32-step interval and a 256-step episode limit, K ≤ 8. Under one submission per sampled record, collection plus separate detection and incentive calls requires at most 8N + 2 requests for those operations. This count excludes deployment, deposit handling, additional policy-stage commitments and retries; it is not a measurement of the complete transaction workload. Record volume scales with N K , and balance accounting scans the participating agents. Gas consumption, block capacity and communication delay additionally depend on contract storage, payloads and network configuration. For a multi-operator automated guided vehicle fleet, coor-dinator nodes can host the learner and blockchain interfaces while vehicles execute the transferred policy locally. Recom-mended and measured vehicle positions supply the behavioral comparison. The chain coordinates training and its economic consequences, rather than serving each navigation decision. Deployment suitability therefore depends on both trustworthy telemetry and device-specific control requirements; neither an edge-control timing budget nor a network-throughput claim follows from the MAPF task results. 9

BE-CTDE makes blockchain an active training-management layer for cooperative learning. A trajectory residual links exe-cuted behavior to an auditable token mechanism, and retained balances set each agent’s contribution to the shared policy update, so behavioral verification, economic accountability, and learning influence operate within one CTDE workflow. Experiments on multi-agent path finding show that BE-CTDE succeeds far more often under attack than the state-of-the-art SCRIMP planner, including with an adversarial majority, and consistently collides less than variants that only flag deviations or that burn forfeited stake instead of redistributing it.
