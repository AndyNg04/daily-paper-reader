## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are increas-ingly used to support research idea generation and scientific discovery. Multi-agent debate offers a collaborative approach to this task by bringing together diverse perspectives to de-velop research ideas. However, a key challenge is how to equip agents with distinct and mean-ingful expertise to guide their contributions. In-spired by how researchers draw on accumulated research experience during collaboration, we introduce I NSIGHT AGENTS , a framework that constructs literature-grounded agent expertise by distilling insights from graphs of related papers. Guided by insights that trace evolv-ing research approaches, reveal underlying as-sumptions, and identify unresolved challenges, within a line of research, agents jointly iden-tify a research problem and propose, challenge, and refine potential approaches through debate to develop a research proposal. We evaluate INSIGHT AGENTS against three research idea generation frameworks on 100 NLP research topics through pairwise comparisons by two LLM judges. In both online and offline knowl-edge settings, I NSIGHT AGENTS achieves the highest average score across the five evaluation dimensions under both judges. 

## 1 Introduction 

Recent research frameworks use large language models (LLMs) to automate multiple stages of sci-entific work, from literature review and idea de-velopment to experimentation and paper writing (Yamada et al., 2025; Schmidgall et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025). The quality of an initial research idea can shape subsequent research outcomes (Si et al., 2025). We therefore focus on generating research ideas grounded in relevant literature through multi-agent collaboration. ## 2 Related Work 

Literature-grounded research idea generation 

Prior work has explored different ways of ground-ing research idea generation in scientific literature. SciMON, ResearchAgent, and AI Scientist-v2 re-trieve relevant papers to inform idea generation and refinement (Wang et al., 2024; Baek et al., 2025; Yamada et al., 2025). Other methods organize lit-erature into chains or graphs to capture research developments and relationships among scientific knowledge (Li et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). While retrieval-based methods typically do not explicitly trace how re-search evolves, structure-aware methods primarily use these structures to guide idea generation, rather than explore how different lines of research might challenge or complement one another. Multi-agent research ideation Multi-agent de-bate allows LLM agents to exchange and critique ideas, encouraging diverse perspectives and iter-ative refinement (Du et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024). Existing scientific collaboration frame-works introduce agent diversity through functional roles or domain-specific personas (Ghafarollahi and Buehler, 2025; Ueda et al., 2025), researcher profiles (Su et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025a), or dif-ferent knowledge backgrounds (Yu et al., 2025b). However, these forms of diversity do not equip agents with distinct and topic-specific expertise grounded in prior research findings and assump-tions. In contrast, I NSIGHT AGENTS initializes each expert with insights distilled from a different line of research, providing a concrete and literature-grounded basis for proposing and challenging ideas during debate. ## 3 Method 

INSIGHT AGENTS transforms insights distilled from graph-structured literature into agent exper-tise for research idea generation. As shown in Fig-ure 1, the framework comprises two components: graph-based expertise construction and multi-agent debate. 

3.1 Constructing Agent Expertise Paper graph construction We construct a paper graph G = ( V, E ) to organize related literature for insight distillation. The seed node v0 is either a user-specified paper or one paper retrieved for the input topic. Starting from v0, we expand each node v through Semantic Scholar’s recommenda-tion API, which returns a one-hop candidate set 

C(v) of papers similar to v. Among new candi-dates C(v) \ V , an LLM selects papers relevant to both the topic and v, favoring distinct methods, set-tings, or failure modes. Candidates already in V are linked directly to v, while selected new papers are added to V and connected to v through new edges in E. We orient recommendation links from older to newer papers by publication date. We recur-sively expand newly added nodes for three rounds, yielding up to three expansion levels beyond v0.The resulting graph collects related papers to the topic for methodological comparison and insight distillation. 

Insight distillation Given the paper graph G =(V, E ), we prompt an LLM to jointly distill three research insights I = {I1, I 2, I 3} using the paper titles and abstracts stored in its nodes and the rela-tionships between papers represented by its edges. The LLM first groups papers by the type of re-search they pursue, such as measurement, training, or dataset construction, and selects three distinct lines of work, each supported by at least two papers. For each line, it synthesizes recurring challenges, the evolution of research approaches, underlying assumptions, and unresolved tensions into an in-sight Ik. Each insight is grounded in a supporting 3subgraph Gk = ( Vk, E k) and a set of findings Fk,retaining specific methods, benchmarks, and re-ported results linked to their source papers. 

Expertise formation For each research insight 

Ik, we initialize an expert agent Ak with a system prompt containing the insight, its supporting find-ings Fk, and the titles and abstracts of papers in 

Vk. The insight provides the agent with a perspec-tive on a line of research, while the findings and source abstracts ground this perspective in specific methods, benchmarks, and reported results. During debate, the agent is instructed to reason from this perspective while drawing on its broader knowl-edge to propose and evaluate research approaches. 

3.2 Research Idea Generation through Multi-Agent Debate 

The debate involves three roles: expert agents, a moderator, and a critic. Each expert agent Ak

draws on its insight Ik and supporting findings to identify research problems, propose approaches, and evaluate the shared proposal. The moderator coordinates the discussion and integrates expert contributions into a common draft. To encourage critical examination rather than premature consen-sus (Pitre et al., 2025), the critic acts as an inde-pendent reviewer, identifying weaknesses in the current draft. It receives the draft and the latest discussion summary, without access to the full de-bate history, and provides feedback to guide sub-sequent expert revisions. Together, these roles de-velop one research proposal through the following three stages. 

Stage 1: Problem selection Each expert pro-poses a research problem based on its insight. The moderator consolidates these proposals into three anonymous candidates, which are shuffled before the experts rank them independently. Rankings are aggregated using Borda scoring (Young, 1974), with 3, 2, and 1 points for first, second, and third place, respectively. The moderator summarizes the highest-scoring problem for subsequent debate, choosing among the tied candidates only when nec-essary. 

Stage 2: Proposal development and debate For the selected problem, experts develop a shared pro-posal through three types of rounds: proposal, re-finement, and convergence. In the proposal round, each expert proposes an approach informed by its expertise and, where useful, broader knowledge.

The moderator identifies the strongest direction supported across the proposals, combining comple-mentary approaches, and produces an initial draft. The critic then reviews the draft for scientific con-tribution, soundness, consistency, feasibility, and clarity. In subsequent refinement rounds, experts re-spond to the critic’s feedback, proposing improve-ments to the method, claims, or evaluation. The moderator updates the draft and summarizes re-solved, unresolved, and newly raised issues. The critic reviews each revised draft and identifies is-sues to be addressed in the next round. In the final convergence round, experts address remaining concerns and state their final positions without introducing new research directions. 

Stage 3: Final synthesis After the debate, the moderator integrates the latest draft and the experts’ final positions into a final research proposal. It re-tains the selected research problem, incorporates the methods the experts agreed on during debate, and reports unresolved concerns as limitations. The final proposal specifies the problem and motivation, method, experiment, anticipated novelty, and po-tential impact. 

## 4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Pipeline Setup Knowledge settings We evaluate I NSIGHT A- 

> GENTS

under two knowledge settings that reflect different ways of accessing prior research: retriev-ing papers on demand from an evolving body of lit-erature and exploring a fixed and structured knowl-edge resource. In the online setting, we dynamically construct a paper graph using the Semantic Scholar API, as described in Section 3. In the offline setting, we use a preconstructed scientific contribution graph (Jansen, 2026) and map its contribution nodes to 4their source papers, preserving the prerequisite re-lationships between contributions as paper-level connections. Table 2 summarizes the key differ-ences between the two settings. Both use the same insight distillation, expertise formation, and debate procedures, while graph retrieval and expansion differ. Evaluating both settings allows us to ex-amine whether I NSIGHT AGENTS remains effective across different knowledge sources and graph struc-tures. Appendix B details the offline graph retrieval process and the evidence provided to agents. Since the settings differ in their underlying cor-pora, graph construction procedures, edge seman-tics, and seed selection, we treat them as two con-figurations of I NSIGHT AGENTS rather than a con-trolled comparison of knowledge sources. 

Topic selection We evaluate our framework on the same set of 100 NLP research topics in both knowledge settings. To construct the candidate topic pool, we randomly sample papers from the 2025 main proceedings and available Findings vol-umes of ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, and COLING. We use GPT-5.6 Luna to derive research topics from their titles and abstracts, exclude unsuitable source papers (e.g., surveys), and remove semantic duplicates. From the resulting pool, we randomly select 100 evaluation topics spanning 20 research areas. Appendix A describes the sampling, abstrac-tion, filtering, and deduplication rules and reports the area distribution. 

Generation configuration We use GPT-5.6 Luna for insight extraction and all debate roles in INSIGHT AGENTS . Each topic is processed by three insight-based experts, a moderator, and a critic. Problem selection uses two rounds, followed by three debate rounds and a final rewrite that pro-duces one research proposal. Graph construction parameters and generation budgets are provided in Appendix C. Prompt templates are provided in Appendix H. 

4.2 Baselines 

We select baselines from the frameworks in Table 1 that have public code and can generate ideas for a given topic, either from the topic itself or from a single seed paper. Three frameworks meet both criteria: ResearchAgent (Baek et al., 2025), AI Scientist-v2 (Yamada et al., 2025), and Chain of Ideas (Li et al., 2025). AI Scientist-v2 and Chain of Ideas take the topic directly; ResearchAgent takes the seed paper selected by I NSIGHT AGENTS 

in the online setting, with the topic text added to its problem prompts. The multi-agent frameworks in Table 1 do not meet these criteria. VirSci forms a research team that chooses its own topic, Sci-Agents generates hypotheses from keywords in a fixed concept graph, and IDVSCI has no public code. Adapting them to a given topic would change their task, so we do not include them as baselines. We evaluate all three baselines using the same 100 research topics and generation model (GPT-5.6 Luna) as I NSIGHT AGENTS . We use the official im-plementations with default pipeline settings, except for the adaptations detailed in Appendix D. Since we compare complete pipelines, we do not control for generation costs across methods. 

4.3 Evaluation Protocol LLM evaluation We adopt pairwise evaluation, as prior studies suggest that LLM judges align more closely with human judgments when com-paring outputs than when assigning absolute scores (Liusie et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). Following Chain of Ideas (Li et al., 2025), we evaluate re-search ideas along five dimensions: novelty, sig-nificance, feasibility, clarity, and expected effec-tiveness. Table 39 provides detailed definitions of these dimensions and the evaluation prompt. We use GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 4.8 as judges, with the same evaluation prompt across methods, and report their results separately. For each topic, the judge compares every pair of meth-ods and selects a winner or a tie on each dimen-sion, with a brief rationale. To reduce position and stylistic biases, we evaluate each pair in both pre-sentation orders and instruct the judge to focus on content rather than surface features such as length and formatting (Feuer et al., 2025). 

Human–LLM agreement To assess agreement between human and LLM judgments, two NLP researchers independently evaluate proposals on 10 research topics selected based on their famil-iarity with the topics. For each topic, annotators rank the four proposals from I NSIGHT AGENTS (on-line setting) and the three baselines along the same five evaluation dimensions, allowing ties. Anno-tators receive the same criteria and instructions as the LLM judges, and method identities are hid-den during annotation. We derive pairwise prefer-ences from these rankings and measure both inter-annotator and human–LLM agreement. 55 Results 

Main results We aggregate pairwise judgments into Elo ratings for each evaluation dimension, knowledge setting, and judge, with equal weights across the five dimensions for the average Elo. Fig-ure 2 presents the comparative evaluation results. INSIGHT AGENTS achieves the highest average Elo in all four combinations of knowledge set-ting and judge, followed by AI Scientist-v2. Both judges rank I NSIGHT AGENTS first in novelty, sig-nificance, and expected effectiveness under both settings. Feasibility represents the main trade-off, with AI Scientist-v2 ranking first and I NSIGHT AGENTS sec-ond across both settings and judges. Appendix E.2 further examines the judges’ rationales for this gap in the online setting. Clarity rankings vary by judge: GPT-5.6 Sol ranks I NSIGHT AGENTS first, while Claude Opus 4.8 favors AI Scientist-v2, placing INSIGHT AGENTS second in both settings. Detailed Elo ratings and direct pairwise comparison scores are provided in Appendix E. The consistent results across both settings sug-gest that I NSIGHT AGENTS can effectively leverage different forms of literature-grounded knowledge without relying on a specific graph construction procedure. 

Human–LLM agreement Figure 3 shows that INSIGHT AGENTS also achieves the highest aver-age Elo on the human-evaluated topics, with the largest margins over AI Scientist-v2 in novelty, sig-nificance, and expected effectiveness. The two methods receive similar ratings for clarity and fea-sibility, with I NSIGHT AGENTS slightly ahead on clarity and AI Scientist-v2 on feasibility. These results support the main pattern of the LLM evalu-ation on this subset. We compare human and LLM judgments on INSIGHT AGENTS –baseline pairs using two mea-sures. Exact agreement requires both judges to select the same winner or both declare a tie. Non-contradiction counts all cases except those where the two judges prefer opposite methods, includ-ing cases where both select the same winner, both declare a tie, or one declares a tie while the other se-lects a winner. Figure 4 shows that opposing prefer-ences account for only 7.3–10.0% of human–LLM comparisons, yielding non-contradiction rates of 90.0–92.7%, close to the 93.3% between the two LLM judges. Exact agreement is lower (63.7– 65.0%), with most disagreements involving ties rather than opposing preferences. Human anno-tators show a similar pattern, with tie–preference disagreements (46.0%) exceeding exact agreement (44.0%). Appendix E.4.2 provides confidence in-tervals and dimension-level results.

6.2 Generation Process Analysis Evidence diversity and use We first examine how literature-grounded expertise is distributed and used across 100 online topics. Among 300 within-topic expert pairs, 289 share no supporting papers, indicating largely non-overlapping evidence sets. In the first proposal round, 299 of 300 expert pro-posals reference at least one paper from their own supporting sets, with an average of 4.3 papers per proposal. In 67 of 100 topics, the final proposal’s 

builds on field references evidence from at least two experts. These statistics characterize how distinct sources of evidence enter the proposal development process. 

Case study: Diversity of research insights We examine the topic Personalized dialogue genera-tion through evolving user-specific memory to il-lustrate the distinct research perspectives distilled from the same paper graph. Expert A ( Memory Utilization Evaluation ) focuses on the gap between 7retrieving user memories and appropriately inte-grating them into responses (Sumida et al., 2026). Expert B ( Evolving Memory Architecture ) empha-sizes how memory systems represent evolving evi-dence, including temporal changes, contradictions, and contextual validity (Choi et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Yacoubi et al., 2026). Expert C (Continual Personalization Alignment ) focuses on the challenges of adapting to evolving user pref-erences while avoiding irrelevant personalization, preference narrowing, and sycophancy (Wang et al., 2026). These insights provide complementary per-spectives on evaluating memory use, representing evolving memory states, and regulating personal-ization. 

Case study: Expertise-guided debate and refine-ment To illustrate how experts interact during de-bate, we examine one of the issues they discussed: how to control the influence of user memory on generated responses. Expert C initially proposed an influence budget to regulate the effects of uncer-tain preferences. Expert B challenged the reliance on confidence alone, arguing that memory influ-ence must also account for lifecycle states, such as whether a memory is current, superseded, or context-specific. The initial draft combined these perspectives but left the influence budget loosely defined. In response, the experts jointly refined this mechanism: Expert A specified four discrete influence levels, Expert B reinforced their lifecycle constraints, and Expert C proposed applying these interventions to a shared candidate pool before gen-eration. The revised draft incorporated these con-tributions, turning the qualitative influence budget into explicit and testable operations.

## 7 Conclusion 

We introduced I NSIGHT AGENTS , a framework for research idea generation through multi-agent de-bate. Its key feature is constructing agent expertise from insights distilled across related papers, giving each agent a distinct research perspective grounded in supporting findings. Evaluations on 100 NLP re-search topics show that I NSIGHT AGENTS achieves the highest average Elo among the compared meth-ods under both LLM judges in both online and offline knowledge settings. Our findings suggest that how agents acquire and use scientific knowledge is an important consider-ation in automated research. Literature-grounded expert debate could serve as an ideation component in larger automated research workflows, connecting prior research to collaborative idea development. 8Limitations 

Our evaluation covers only NLP research topics, leaving the applicability of I NSIGHT AGENTS to other scientific disciplines untested. Moreover, since I NSIGHT AGENTS starts from a predefined topic and builds expertise from existing literature, its ability to independently discover emerging or entirely new research directions remains limited. INSIGHT AGENTS generates research proposals without executing the proposed experiments or in-corporating experimental feedback. Our evaluation therefore assesses the quality of research ideas be-fore execution, rather than whether they lead to meaningful scientific findings in practice. Evalu-ating ideas through experimental execution would provide stronger evidence of their research value. Finally, our evaluation relies primarily on LLM judges. Although we include human evaluation to assess agreement with LLM judgments, it covers only a small subset of topics. LLM-based assess-ments may not fully reflect expert judgments of research quality. 

## Ethical considerations 

Our framework relies on LLMs to synthesize scien-tific literature and generate research proposals. Al-though the proposals are grounded in prior research, they may contain unsupported claims, inaccurate interpretations of existing work, or ideas that are not genuinely novel. These proposals have not been experimentally validated and require expert review before use. Our evaluation also relies primarily on LLM judges, which may favor particular writing styles or types of proposals. 11 Appendix A Topic Selection 

We construct the topic pool from the ACL Anthol-ogy’s 2025 main proceedings for ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, and COLING, together with the ACL, EMNLP, and NAACL Findings volumes. We ex-clude demonstrations, tutorials, student research workshops, and industry tracks, and require an ab-stract of at least 200 characters. This yields 8,207 eligible papers, from which we uniformly sample 600 without replacement. Research-area labels are used to inspect coverage after sampling, without imposing area quotas. GPT-5.6 Luna derives one topic from each sam-pled paper’s title and abstract. The model is in-structed to express the underlying research prob-lem in at most 12 words, without naming specific models, datasets, or methods. Papers without a suit-able research problem may be rejected, including surveys and resource releases that pose no research question. The finalized run rejects 10 surveys and 4 resource papers, leaving 586 candidate topics. We embed the topics with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and process them in the original sampling order. A topic is removed if its cosine similarity to any previously retained topic exceeds 0.85. This removes 30 duplicates, leaving 556 unique topics. We randomly sample 100 topics from the dedu-plicated pool for evaluation and use the same topics for all methods and both knowledge settings. In the same call that derives a topic, GPT-5.6 Luna also assigns the source paper one of the 22 ACL Rolling Review research areas. Table 4 reports the area distribution. The 100 topics span 20 of these areas, and no area contributes more than 12 topics.

## C Implementation Details 

Graph construction Given an input topic, we use GPT-5.6 Luna to select a seed paper from recent Semantic Scholar search results, prioritizing topical relevance and representativeness of the research area. Starting from the seed, we retrieve up to 20 related candidates per expanded paper and retain up to 5, 3, and 2 new papers per parent across three expansion levels. This gives an upper bound of 51 papers, including the seed. The graph is passed to a single call that extracts three insights, each supported by at least two papers. 

Generation budgets The configured output al-lowances are 8000 tokens for insight extraction, 2500 per expert speech, 4000 per critic review, and 6000 for the final proposal rewrite. For reasoning models, the client adds a 2000-token reserve to each output allowance to accommodate reasoning tokens.

Title. Lifecycle-Aware Counterfactual Control of Memory Influence Continued on next page 

21 Generated output: Final research proposal (continued) 

Problem and motivation. Can a personalized dialogue system determine which evolving user-memory evidence is currently justified, whether it applies to the present request, and how strongly it should change the response? A memory may be current, historical, context-specific, superseded, unresolved, or irrelevant. The appropriate behavior may therefore be silent personalization, explicit grounding, confirmation, abstention, or no behavioral change. Existing evaluations often reduce personalization to retrieval accuracy, answer correctness, or explicit memory mention. This misses useful silent grounding and cannot distinguish missing evidence from a system that retrieved the right evidence but gave it unjustified authority. The proposal studies whether lifecycle-aware evidence states and auditable influence control can produce more useful and less harmful personalization in recommendation, planning, and decision-support tasks. 

Method. The system constructs a lifecycle-aware evidence unit from retrieved memories. It represents whether claims are current, historical, context-specific, superseded, unresolved, or irrelevant, together with provenance and supporting or conflicting relations. A lightweight controller receives the current request, this evidence unit, and a no-memory shadow response. It selects an action—silent use, explicit grounding, confirmation, or abstention—and one of four discrete influence levels. Level 0 prevents memory from changing ranking, membership, or plan constraints. Level 0.25 permits reranking only among options that already satisfy the explicit task constraints. Level 0.5 permits a soft preference or constraint while retaining at least one viable alternative. Level 1 permits filtering options or materially changing a plan only when the evidence is a confirmed current constraint, such as an explicit recent statement or concordant evidence meeting a preregistered rule. If level 1 is predicted without confirmation, the system downgrades to level 0.5 or asks for confirmation. The controller applies the intervention to a common candidate pool or structured plan representation; the generator then receives only that representation, not the retrieved evidence or controller action, and verbalizes it with deterministic decoding. Confirmation produces only a clarification question, explicit grounding must accurately mention the relevant evidence, and abstention uses the no-memory response. Influence is measured primarily through changes in option ranking, membership, retained alternatives, and plan constraints, and secondarily through textual grounding, usefulness, and naturalness. This makes memory influence an auditable intervention rather than a latent score or a proxy based on memory mention. 

Experiment. The primary study will evaluate structured recommendation and planning cases drawn from eligible examples in MemUse, When Users Don’t Ask: Benchmarking Context-Driven Memory Retrieval in Conversational Agents, UTILMEM: Benchmarking Evidence Utilization in Long-Term Conversational Memory, and PRAGMA: Evaluating Personalized Guidance with Memory Alignment in Lifelong Conversations. A preliminary audit will determine whether these resources contain sufficient suitable instances; if not, the natural-data component will be reported as exploratory rather than presented as representative. The benchmark will have two separated components. The controlled component will contain approximately 50 cases for each of five conditions: supersession, contextual coexistence, contradiction, stale distractors, and irrelevance. These labels are known by construction, but cases with the same lifecycle state will vary in request risk and applicability so that state classification alone cannot solve the task. The natural component will use unmodified or minimally transformed histories where possible: 300 cases for development and calibration and 100 held-out cases for final evaluation. Three annotators will label each case’s lifecycle state, acceptable action set, maximum permitted influence, and whether the correct behavior is no change. They will not label system outputs. Three blinded raters will independently judge held-out outputs against the same case and its no-memory reference for useful improvement, harmful degradation, grounding accuracy, and naturalness. Agreement will be reported before adjudication, and mixed-effects or paired-bootstrap intervals will account for case and rater clustering. All systems will use the same request, history, retrieved evidence, candidate pool or initial plan, prompt template, model, decoding settings, and random seed. The no-memory and memory branches will differ only in the prescribed intervention, and verbalization will receive only the resulting structured representation. Baselines will include no memory, aggressive top-k retrieval, lifecycle retrieval without influence control, influence control without lifecycle relations, a confidence-only controller, and random action or budget policies calibrated on development data to match abstention and mean influence. The primary estimand is the held-out paired difference in useful influence and harmful influence between the complete method and the strongest matched control. The preregistered success criterion is at least a 0.10-point improvement in useful influence without more than a 0.05-point increase in harmful influence on the rating scale. Additional measures are lifecycle-state accuracy, action-set accuracy, influence-level accuracy, no-change accuracy, missed and unnecessary confirmation, and explicit-grounding correctness. A result supporting the claim must show higher useful and lower harmful behavioral change at matched abstention and average influence, with lifecycle ablations failing especially on supersession, contextual coexistence, and contradiction cases. A null result will be interpreted narrowly as non-improvement for this controller, benchmark, and task domain. If the primary result holds, a follow-up study can test longitudinal authority decay and recommendation-induced self-confirmation; these are not claims of the present paper. 

Novelty. The proposal makes memory’s permitted behavioral influence—not retrieval, explicit mention, or binary utilization—the central object of study. It combines lifecycle-aware evidence reconstruction with a discrete, auditable policy over how much that evidence may change a recommendation or plan. The no-memory shadow response exposes silent adaptation, while fixed intervention levels make behavioral changes attributable and testable. The design also treats justified no-change behavior as a first-class outcome and controls for improvements caused merely by retrieving more evidence, abstaining more often, or changing prompt format. Builds on. MemUse: Moving Memory Evaluation from Direct QA to Natural Integration in Long-Term Human-AI Conversation (2026) reports that a system can perform well on direct fact questions while naturally referencing very few corresponding facts, and that natural integration better tracks satisfaction; this motivates evaluating behavioral change and silent grounding rather than explicit memory mention. Potential impact. If successful, the work will give personalized-dialogue research a measurable distinction between possessing a justified memory and granting it justified behavioral authority. It will help separate missing evidence, incorrect lifecycle interpretation, excessive influence, ignored evidence, and generation failures, while supporting useful personalization without treating every retrieved fact as an instruction. Its fixed interventions and matched controls provide a practical evaluation and control layer for public-model systems under university-lab compute. If it does not improve human-rated usefulness and harm at matched abstention and influence levels, the result will show that this lifecycle-aware controller does not improve personalization under the tested conditions, or that generation and user modeling remain the dominant bottlenecks; it will not by itself establish that memory authority is generally invalid.

Pairwise research idea evaluation 

Reading note. Proposal 0 and Proposal 1 are the final ideas in the current presentation order; they are exchanged for the reverse-order judgment. The five criteria appear directly in the prompt. Effectiveness is reported as expected effectiveness in the paper. Braced descriptions in the response format specify required outputs, not missing input values. 

User message 

You are a judge in a competition. You have to decide which idea is better. The idea0 is: [Proposal 0] The idea1 is: [Proposal 1] The topic is: [Research topic] Which idea do you think is better? Judge each criterion separately. Here are your evaluation criteria: 1. Novelty: Which idea makes the more substantive original intellectual contribution: an insight, hypothesis, problem formulation, method, or explanatory connection? A meaningful combination of familiar techniques can be original when their interaction enables new understanding or capability. Mere assembly, complexity or renaming is not novelty. Do not favor or penalize an idea because it is easier to implement. Support duplication claims with identifiable prior work when known; acknowledge uncertainty rather than inventing references. 2. Significance: Which central contribution, if established, would teach the field more or address a more consequential bottleneck? Consider changes in understanding, useful capabilities and what others could build on, including focused studies and informative negative results. Judge supported potential value independently of execution difficulty. Breadth and ambitious language alone do not establish significance. 3. Feasibility: Which idea has the more credible path for an academic group to implement its core mechanism and test its central claim? Distinguish essential requirements from optional extensions and manageable adjustments from fundamental barriers. Adjustments must preserve the intellectual contribution; do not invent missing solutions or weaken the claim. Lower cost or shorter duration alone should not decide between two credible plans. Compare essential resources, assumptions and unresolved dependencies; novelty and ambition do not excuse an infeasible plan. 4. Clarity: Which idea more clearly connects its central contribution, mechanism, assumptions and proposed test? Require the details needed to assess that connection, not an exhaustive implementation recipe. Do not favor simpler scope or a longer checklist of datasets and metrics over a precisely explained intellectual contribution. Penalize undefined concepts and missing reasoning, not conceptual ambition or routine implementation choices left open. 5. Effectiveness: Which idea’s mechanism and evaluation more credibly deliver its stated research objective and produce justified knowledge? A hypothesis test can be effective even when it refutes the hypothesis; a claimed improvement still needs a plausible mechanism for achieving it. Prefer tests that distinguish the claim from credible alternatives and support the conclusions actually sought. Length, number of sections, bullet density, formatting polish, and confident tone are NOT evidence of quality on any criterion. Prefer the idea that is specific and straight-to-the-point over one that is longer or more elaborately formatted. - Do not reward claims you cannot verify. Citations, numbers, or assertions about prior work that you cannot check count neither for nor against the idea; they never raise novelty or effectiveness on their own. - Keep the criteria orthogonal. Judge each criterion only against its own definition: do not lower feasibility because an idea is unoriginal, and do not raise novelty because an idea is well written. - Every idea has limitations. Only limitations that bear on the criterion you are currently judging should affect that criterion. - State of the art is not required. Impact may come from efficiency, generalizability, interpretability, robustness, or fairness, not only from beating a leaderboard. Note: Avoid any position biases and ensure that the order in which the responses were presented does not influence your decision. DO NOT allow the LENGTH of the responses to influence your evaluation, choose the one that is straight-to-the-point instead of unnecessarily verbose. Be as objective as possible. (very important!!!)
