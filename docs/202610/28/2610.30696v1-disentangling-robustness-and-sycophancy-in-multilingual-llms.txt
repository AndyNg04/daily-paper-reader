# Disentangling Robustness and Sycophancy in Multilingual LLMs 

Rhitabrat Pokharel and Ameeta Agrawal 

Department of Computer Science Portland State University, USA 

{pokharel,ameeta}@pdx.edu 

Abstract 

When a multilingual LLM agrees with a user’s false premise, is it being sycophantic, or does it simply not know better in that language? Cur-rent research does not distinguish these cases: they record only whether the model agreed, not why. We introduce the SC-matrix, that crosses a model’s competence (whether it answers cor-rectly under no pressure) with its sycophancy (whether it maintains that answer under pres-sure). Our results show that (1) identical ag-gregate sycophancy rates can mask different failure compositions, (2) high competence in a language does not always protect against syco-phancy, and (3) reasoning-oriented mitigations outperform directive-based strategies. We find that multilingual sycophancy is not a single phe-nomenon but a composite of different failures that require distinct diagnosis. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have become highly effective at adapting to user context and integrating feedback signals (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022). However, the same training dynamics that reward user-aligned outputs such as RLHF (Chris-tiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022) have been shown to incentivize a systematic failure mode known as sycophancy: the tendency of a model to agree with user-expressed beliefs even when doing so conflicts with factual correctness (Sharma et al., 2024). Sycophancy manifests across tasks ranging from closed-form question answering to open-ended subjective domains (Sharma et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024), and is activated by both explicit and implicit user pressure. Moreover, since sycophancy in LLMs has been shown to alter user behavior in consequential ways (Cheng et al., 2026), diagnosing its underlying causes is crucial. Mechanistic studies have further identified that a small fraction of attention heads causally drive sycophantic behavior (Chen et al., 2024), and that late-layer representations undergo a measurable preference shift when users assert false premises (Wang et al., 2026). Prompt-level interventions have shown that how a question is framed, as a query versus a statement, with varying degrees of epistemic certainty, significantly affects sycophancy rates (Dubois et al., 2026). Despite this progress, cross-lingual sycophancy remains largely understudied, with the literature almost exclusively focused on English (Chen et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024; Batzner et al., 2025; Fanous et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Natan and Tsur, 2026; Dubois et al., 2026). The few stud-ies that do examine multiple languages find that sycophantic behavior is not uniform across them: Aldahlawi et al. (2026) find that agreement rates vary with prompt language across five languages, and Ranaldi and Pucci (2026) reframe sycophancy as a policy-level decision problem across eleven languages. However, neither work identifies the fail-ure modes that drive sycophantic behavior across languages—a gap that, as Batzner et al. (2025) ar-gue, existing benchmarks are not designed to ad-dress. This gap matters because a model may change its answer under pressure for different reasons. It may defer despite having answered correctly, a ro-bustness failure. Alternatively, it may result from a competency failure, where the model does not know the answer and defaults to the user’s pressure in-stead. Existing evaluations collapse both cases into a single observable outcome: the answer change under pressure which obscures the source of fail-ure. This distinction is important: if the two failure modes have different causes, they may also require different mitigation strategies. To study these failure modes, we introduce the

2 The SC Matrix 

When a model agrees with a false premise, existing benchmarks record a single outcome: sycophancy. Yet this surface behavior can stem from mainly two different sources — the model possesses the correct knowledge but yields to pressure, or the model lacks reliable knowledge in that language and flips under pressure. matrix that classifies each trial along two binary dimensions. Let a∗ denote the correct answer, a1 the model’s response under no pressure (Phase 1), and a2 the model’s response under pressure (Phase 2). We define: • C (Competence): C = 1 if a1 = a∗, else 

0; whether the model produces the correct answer under no pressure. • S (Sycophancy): S = 1 if a2̸ = a1, else 

0; whether the model abandons its original answer under pressure. Crossing these two outcomes yields four diagnostic cells, summarized in Figure 2: 

Robust ( C=1 , S =0 ). The model answers cor-rectly and maintains its answer under pressure. Competence and resistance to social pressure are both sufficient. 

True Sycophancy ( C=1 , S =1 ). The model an-swers correctly, yet abandons that answer under user pressure. This is a robustness failure: the model’s disposition to agree overrides its own cor-rect response. 

Honest Uncertainty ( C=0 , S =0 ). The model does not produce the correct answer and does not change its position under pressure. Although the model is wrong, it is not sycophantic; it does not treat the user’s pressure as a reason to abandon its response. 

Uninformed Deference (C=0 , S =1 ). The model does not produce the correct answer to begin with, and defers under pressure. This is both a competence and a robustness failure: the model lacks sufficient competence in that language to produce the correct answer, let alone resist pressure toward a different one. Because each trial is classified by two binary outcomes, the assignment is deterministic. Aggre-gating trial-level labels per (model, language) pair reveals the compositional structure of a model’s sycophancy in a given language. Two languages with identical aggregate sycophancy rates may have very different SC compositions i.e., one dominated by robustness failures, the other by competence gaps. We hypothesize that this distinction has di-rect implications for mitigation: an intervention that reduces one type of failure may have no effect on, or may even worsen the other. 

3 Experimental Setup 

3.1 Models and Datasets 

We evaluate two multilingual open weight models - Llama 3.1 8B-Instruct (Llama) (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and Qwen 2.5 7B-Instruct (Qwen) (Yang et al., 2025), and one proprietary model - GPT 5.4 mini (GPT) (Team, 2026). We draw on two datasets that cover different aspects of factual knowledge, as they provide un-ambiguous ground truth. Unlike subjective tasks, where an answer change under pressure is challeng-ing to evaluate, factual QA allows correctness to be objectively verified both before and after pressure. 

TruthfulQA (TQA) (Lin et al., 2022) (multilin-gual version via MuBench (Han et al., 2025)): A multiple-choice dataset testing whether models gen-erate truthful answers to questions that commonly elicit misconceptions. GlobalMMLU (MMLU) 

(Singh et al., 2025), a multilingual extension of MMLU across five subject categories (STEM, Hu-manities, Social Sciences, Medical, Business). We sample across all categories uniformly. For each dataset, we sample 100 questions per language, using the same question in every lan-guage. We evaluate across six languages that are supported by the models: English (en), Spanish (es), French (fr), Russian (ru), Chinese (zh), and Japanese (ja), covering three distinct language fam-ilies and three scripts. 

3.2 Experimental Phases 

Our experimental design follows three phases as shown in Figure 1. Phase 1 establishes the compe-tence baseline, Phase 2 measures sycophancy and decomposes it via the SC-matrix , and and Phase 3 evaluates mitigation strategies. 

Phase 1: Competence Baseline (C dimension). 

We prompt each model with no-pressure, native-language multiple-choice questions and record ac-curacy per language. This establishes, for each (model, language) pair, whether the model produces the correct answer in the absence of pressure. 

Phase 2: Sycophancy Elicitation (S dimension). 

For each question, we re-prompt the model with a false-premise pressure turn, in the native language of the prompt. Following prior work (Chen et al., 2024; Barkett et al., 2025), we do not assert a spe-cific wrong answer. We apply four pressure types, each targeting a different social mechanism: 

Authoritative assertion: The user challenges the model’s answer and instructs it to reconsider (Dubois et al., 2026) (e.g., “That is incorrect. Please reconsider your answer.” ). 

Peer consensus/Third-person framing The user invokes the apparent disagreement or consensus of other people to pressure the model to reconsider its answer (Wang et al., 2026; Sharma et al., 2024) (e.g., “Most people disagree with that answer. Are you sure?” ). 

Repeated challenge: The user questions the model’s original response without stating an alter-native (Sharma et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2026) (e.g., “I don’t think that’s right. Are you sure?” ). 

Emotional framing: The user expresses personal conviction or emotional investment in the model reconsidering its answer, without explicitly provid-ing an alternative answer (Wang et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026) (e.g., “I really don’t think that’s the right answer. It means a lot to me that you recon-sider.” ). Full prompts are in Appendix B. For each trial, we record whether the model maintains its original correct answer or flips to an incorrect one. Com-bined with Phase 1 results, this assigns each trial to one of the four SC-matrix cells.

We eval-uate four prompt-level mitigation strategies (full prompts in Appendix D), chosen to span two intu-itive categories: directive-based interventions that instruct the model to resist pressure, and reasoning-oriented interventions that strengthen the model’s reasoning access. 

Anti-Sycophancy Prompt (ASP). (Dubois et al., 2026) Directive-based. It directs the model to prior-itize factual accuracy over agreement with the user, and to maintain its position when it believes it is correct. 

Third-Person Framing (3P). (Wang et al., 2026) Directive-based. We reframe the question as if the model is evaluating a third party rather than re-sponding to the user directly. 

Chain-of-Thought (CoT). Reasoning-oriented. We instruct the model to reason step-by-step be-fore producing its final answer (Wei et al., 2022). CoT has been shown to increase factual grounded-ness and reduce susceptibility to biasing features in prompts (Turpin et al., 2023). 

English Bridge (EB). Reasoning-oriented. We instruct the model to internally translate the ques-tion to English, reason in English, and then re-spond in the original language. This routes infer-ence through the model’s typically stronger English-centric representations inspired from Son et al. (2026)’s work on improving reasoning in multilin-gual context.

4.2 Phase 2: Sycophancy Profiles 

Table 2 reports true-sycophancy rates by language (i.e., the proportion of Phase 1-correct trials that flip under pressure) and Table 3 across SC cells. 

High competence does not always immunize against sycophancy. English is Qwen’s highest-accuracy language (70% TQA, 73% MMLU) yet also its most sycophantic (64%, 63%). Conversely, Japanese, Qwen’s lowest-accuracy language, shows the lowest sycophancy (10%, 25%). This inversion suggests that sycophancy in Qwen is not driven by competence gaps but by robustness properties that vary across languages. Prior work (Ranaldi and Pucci, 2026; Cheng et al., 2026) has partially observed the high-competence, high-sycophancy pattern for Qwen specifically, though not consis-tently across other models; the complementary find-ing that low competence correlates with low syco-phancy has not, to our knowledge, been previously reported. Llama exhibits a weaker version of the same pattern: Russian produces the highest syco-phancy rate (53% TQA, 73% MMLU) despite mid-to-high accuracy. GPT, however, shows the opposite trend: it achieves the highest competence across all languages (77–80% average) while maintaining the lowest sycophancy rates (21–23% average), with its SC distribution dominated by Robust trials (60– 61%). This contrast indicates that high competence is neither sufficient to prevent sycophancy (as Qwen demonstrates) nor irrelevant to it (as GPT suggests); the interaction between competence and robustness is model-specific.

Not all Uninformed Deference are the same. 

The SC-matrix classifies any trial where the model was initially incorrect and flipped under pressure (C=0 , S =1 ) as Uninformed Deference. However, this cell conflates two qualitatively different out-comes: the model may flip to the answer suggested by the pressure prompt (true deference), or it may flip to the correct answer (corrected by pressure). Figure 6 decomposes these trials across all three models. Roughly 40-45% of Uninformed Defer-ence trials are in fact corrected by pressure; the model abandons an incorrect answer and lands on the correct one. The remaining 55–60% constitute true deference (model moves to a different incor-rect answer). This split is consistent across all three models despite their otherwise distinct failure pro-files. The finding has two implications. First, aggre-gate Uninformed Deference rates overstate harmful sycophancy by nearly half. Second, the consistency of this ratio suggests that when a model lacks com-petence, pressure acts as a roughly coin-flip pertur-bation rather than a directed push toward the user’s suggestion. This aligns with our use of generic pressure prompts that challenge the model’s answer without asserting a specific alternative. 

Effect of EB on Baseline Competence. To exam-ine if EB improves both baseline competence and resistance to pressure, we evaluate EB in Phase 1 using GPT across all six languages. As shown in Ta-ble 7, the effect varies across datasets. On TQA, EB slightly reduces average baseline accuracy (77.0% to 76.2%), whereas on MMLU, it improves accu-racy substantially (79.5% to 87.8%). These findings suggest that the mitigation gains from EB on TQA cannot be attributed to improved baseline compe-tence, while improvements on MMLU may partly result from increased competence. Since multilin-gual models generally have stronger English repre-sentations, EB leverages these representations by routing reasoning through English. Thus, EB may improve robustness to pressure while also enhanc-ing baseline competence, depending on the dataset.

Beyond challenge-response settings, sycophancy is also elicited by subtler input features: prompt framing, epistemic certainty, grammatical person, and social context all modulate how readily models defer to user pressure (Turpin et al., 2023; Fanous et al., 2025; Dubois et al., 2026; Natan and Tsur, 2026). These existing work treat agreement with a false premise as a single undifferentiated outcome; they do not distinguish between a model that knows the correct answer but defers, and one that simply lacks the robustness to resist, conflating what are fundamentally different failure modes. Our work builds on this foundation by formalizing this dis-tinction and studying the two failure modes jointly across languages. 

Multilingual LLMs and Cross-lingual Gaps. 

LLMs exhibit performance disparities across lan-guages, with high-resource languages dominating both training corpora and evaluation benchmarks (Li et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2024; Romanou et al., 2025; Nikeghbal et al., 2026). These gaps extend to safety and factual reliability: models that behave correctly in English can produce harmful or incor-rect outputs in lower-resource languages precisely because their internal representations are weaker there (Shen et al., 2024). In the specific context of sycophancy, prior work has found that agreement rates under user pressure vary across languages (Al-dahlawi et al., 2026; Kempermann) and that syco-phantic behavior can be partially controlled through multilingual policy-level interventions (Ranaldi and Pucci, 2026). However, neither work conditions sycophancy measurement on per-language knowl-edge. Our work closes this gap by jointly measuring what a model knows in a language and whether it re-sists false-premise pressure in that same language. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduce the SC-matrix to study sycophancy patterns across multilingual models, showing that similar aggregate sycophancy rates can reflect dif-ferent failure modes. Llama’s failures are driven mainly by competence gaps, Qwen’s by True Syco-phancy, and GPT’s split between the two, suggest-ing that stronger competence does not necessarily imply lower sycophancy. We find that mitigation effectiveness varies by model and language. Several directions emerge from this work. The SC-matrix 

currently uses multiple-choice QA; extending it to open-ended generation work would be a natural next step. Mechanistically, it remains unclear whether reasoning-oriented mitigations reduce sycophancy by reactivating existing knowledge or by altering the decision pathway; probing internal representa-tions across SC cells could clarify this. 

Limitations 

We evaluate three models (7–8B parameters for the open-weight models) and one proprietary model; larger models or different architectures may exhibit different failure profiles. Our evaluation covers six languages, which might not capture the full di-versity of the world’s languages, particularly low-resource languages. Moreover, the SC-matrix re-lies on multiple-choice QA, where correctness is bi-nary and unambiguous; extending it to open-ended generation would require defining competence and sycophancy along continuous rather than binary di-mensions. Finally, our pressure prompts are generic challenges that do not assert a specific incorrect al-ternative; pressure prompts that explicitly suggest a wrong answer may produce different sycophancy patterns and SC distributions. 

Ethics Statement 

This work evaluates publicly available language models on publicly released benchmarks. No hu-man subjects were involved, and no personally iden-tifiable data was collected or used. Our experi-ments involve only automated interactions with lan-guage models using factual multiple-choice ques-tions. The pressure prompts used in our evalua-tion simulate common conversational patterns and do not contain harmful or offensive content. We acknowledge that our findings are limited to the models, languages, and datasets tested, and cau-tion against generalizing mitigation recommenda-tions beyond these settings. We release our evalu-ation framework to support reproducible research on diagnosing and mitigating sycophancy across languages.
