Abstract 

Claims about the cognitive capabilities of large language models are typically tested by translating behavioral paradigms into text prompts, bypassing the visual interfaces and time pressure through which human participants actually engage with experiments. At the same time, AI agents can now complete online behavioral tasks and produce human-like data, which raises concerns that online datasets in the social and behavioral sciences are increasingly open to contamination. We introduce CogArena, a benchmark of ten experimental paradigms from the social and behavioral sciences. Agents complete the experiments through a standard web browser, working with the same visual stimuli and response interfaces that online participants use. A three-level scoring procedure evaluates whether the agent completed the task, how accurately it performed, and whether its data show the behavioral signatures each paradigm reliably produces in people. Across four multimodal models, each run ten times per task, we find that finishing an experiment and reproducing its signatures are different things. Many sessions did not finish, and those that did reproduced some signatures reliably and others not at all. CogArena provides the tasks, scoring code, and a public leaderboard for new submissions. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models are increasingly evaluated on tasks drawn from the social and behavioral sciences, with results used to characterize the models’ decision-making, reasoning, and learning capacities [Binz and Schulz, 2023, Coda-Forno et al., 2024, Binz and Schulz, 2024]. These evaluations typically translate behavioral paradigms into natural language prompts. The stimuli, instructions, and response options are rendered as text, and the model’s completions are scored against human behavioral data. While experimentally clean, this approach bypasses the interfaces human participants actually use. When people complete experiments online, they navigate web-based platforms, encode visual stimuli, and respond under time pressure with keypresses or mouse movements. Consider a standard interference task such as the Stroop task. Presented as text alone (“the word RED printed in

blue ink, press the key corresponding to the ink color”), the task tests language comprehension and instruction following. Presented instead as a colored word on screen, with a two-second response deadline and a fixation cross between trials, the task additionally recruits visual perception, selective attention, and response inhibition. The two versions may be testing different things, and neither necessarily includes the other. At the same time, AI agents that can complete tasks on the web without supervision have improved quickly. Recent benchmarks show that they can navigate websites, fill out forms, and carry out multi-step tasks through a browser [Zhou et al., 2024, Yao et al., 2022, Deng et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2025]. For researchers who collect behavioral data online, this is a practical problem. An agent can sign up for a study, pass the attention checks, and submit answers that look like a person’s, and current methods for detecting such respondents do not catch it [Westwood, 2025, Fernandez et al., 2026]. There is also early evidence that agents can reproduce finer-grained signatures of human behavior, such as the shape of a response-time distribution [Van der Stigchel et al., 2026]. What has not been tested is how current models fare with a behavioral experiment as a participant meets it, with a page to operate, stimuli to encode, a deadline on each response, and trials whose meaning depends on the trials before them. Here we introduce CogArena, a benchmark designed to evaluate how AI agents perform behavioral experiments as humans commonly encounter them in online research settings. Rather than scoring answers to text prompts or annotation tasks, CogArena evaluates whether an agent’s behavioral data exhibit the same signatures that humans reliably produce. The benchmark serves two purposes. For researchers studying machine cognition, it tests whether an agent’s behavior on well-studied paradigms resembles human behavior beyond getting the answers right [Shiffrin and Mitchell, 2023, Hagendorff, 2023, Dillion et al., 2023]. For researchers who rely on online data collection, it characterizes which response modalities and signatures agents can and cannot reproduce, and so provides a continuously updated picture of the threats autonomous agents pose to the validity of online behavioral experiments [Westwood, 2025, Van der Stigchel et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026]. This paper makes three contributions. First, we provide an initial release of a curated ten-task benchmark of behavioral experiments spanning learning, risk, social and economic decision-making, memory recall, memory recognition, perception, judgment, foraging, compositionality, and moral decision-making. Each task is adapted from prior work, with parameters and stimuli drawn from published studies and paired with human baselines computed from the source studies’ released participant data wherever those data exist. Second, we introduce a three-level scoring procedure that tests whether the agent completed the task, how accurately it performed relative to those baselines, and whether its data show each paradigm’s behavioral signatures, with every score computed by a fixed statistical test rather than by a language model. We additionally report a chance floor, the score each level assigns to an agent that responds at random, as a reference against which reported scores can be read. To our knowledge, no prior benchmark scores cognitive signatures in interactive, browser-based agent evaluation. Third, as a preliminary evaluation, we report results from four multimodal models, each driven through the browser by the same software (the Browser-Use agent, which converts a model’s output into clicks and keypresses), with ten sessions per model and task. The central finding is that completing an experiment does not guarantee reproducing the signatures that decades of human research have established as defining features of these tasks. Sessions that reach the end of an experiment reproduce some signatures and miss others, the pattern varies sharply across tasks and models, and many sessions never reach the end at all. Section 5.1 gives the evidence for this claim and Section 7 its limits. 1

Alongside this expansion of task domains, researchers have argued for grounding evaluation in verifiable task state rather than in approximate scoring that depends on auxiliary judgments or surface-level cues. The application of cognitive science to artificial systems. A complementary line of research evaluates whether AI systems exhibit human-like cognitive patterns [Shiffrin and Mitchell, 2023, Hagendorff, 2023, Aher et al., 2023, Dillion et al., 2023]. Binz and Schulz [2023] applied canonical cognitive paradigms to GPT-3 through text-based prompting, identifying signatures consistent with model-based reinforcement learning alongside failures in causal reasoning. Follow-up work, Cog-Bench [Coda-Forno et al., 2024], formalized this approach into a systematic framework, testing 35 models across seven experiments through a text API. Subsequent work, including the Psych-101 [Binz et al., 2025] and Psych-201 [Binz et al., 2026] datasets, has aggregated trial-by-trial human behavioral responses across more than 160 experiments, providing large-scale training data for behavioral foundation models. All of these approaches present stimuli in text-based form. In contrast, CogArena evaluates agents in interactive online behavioral experiments, under the stimulus formats, timing constraints, and response requirements under which human behavioral data are typically collected. 

3 The CogArena Benchmark 

CogArena version 1 (v1) consists of ten experiments spanning learning, risk, social and economic decision-making, memory recall, memory recognition, perception, judgment, foraging, composition-ality, and moral decision-making (Table 2). Trial counts range from 16 to 200 per task, and response modalities include keypresses, button clicks, and typed responses. All tasks are implemented as standalone web applications using jsPsych v8 [de Leeuw, 2015, de Leeuw et al., 2023], a JavaScript framework for browser-based behavioral experiments, and include instruction screens, practice trials, and main trial blocks. Each task is adapted from a vetted research codebase, with original stimuli and trial-structure conventions retained wherever the source license permits. Per-task configurations and provenance are recorded in Appendices A and F, including, where relevant, a task’s correspondence to a Psych-101/201 entry.

3.2 Three-Level Scoring Pipeline 

A human participant completing the Stroop task does not merely press the correct keys. They also respond more slowly on incongruent than on congruent trials, known as Stroop interference [Stroop, 1935], slow down after an error, known as post-error slowing [Dutilh et al., 2012], and show less interference after incongruent trials, the congruency sequence or Gratton effect [Gratton et al., 1992]. Accuracy alone misses these signatures. CogArena therefore scores agents at three levels, each more specific than the last. Level 1 asks whether the agent completes the experiment, Level 2 whether it performs accurately, and Level 3 whether its trial data show the signatures that the human literature has established for the paradigm. Level 1: Task Completion. The completion level checks that the agent engaged with the experiment. Five binary checks assess whether trial data were received, whether enough trials were completed 4(at least 80% of those expected), whether the timeout rate was below 20%, whether responses used valid inputs, and whether the experiment reached its end. The Level 1 score is the proportion of these checks that passed.

The Level 2 score is the mean of the metric scores for the task. Across the v1 set the benchmark computes 31 accuracy metrics over eight tasks, 3.9 per task on average (per-task counts in Appendix E). Absolute response time is not among them. It is scored instead through within-session relational signatures at Level 3, for reasons given in Section 6. 

Level 3: Behavioral Alignment. The behavioral alignment level tests whether agent trial data show the cognitive signatures that characterize human performance on each paradigm (e.g., coherence-dependent accuracy in random dot motion or tit-for-tat reciprocity in repeated games). Each task specifies a set of signatures, and each signature defines a statistical test, an expected direction, a significance threshold ( p < 0.05 ), and an importance weight wj . Appendix C describes the four test types, which are exact one-sample proportion tests, paired proportion contrasts, conditional proportion contrasts, and Pearson or Spearman correlations. 4 Methods 

4.1 Platform 

CogArena provides the experiments and the scoring, but not the agent (i.e., the harness). The software that lets a model operate a browser, which we call the scaffold, is supplied by whoever submits to the benchmark. A scaffold takes the model’s text output and turns it into clicks, keypresses, and page navigation, and passes screenshots or page text back to the model. Examples include Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s Computer Use tools, Browser-Use [Browser-Use Contributors, 2024], OpenHands [Wang et al., 2025], and custom code written with a browser-automation library such as Playwright. The platform itself provides the tasks, an interface for starting sessions and submitting trial data, the scoring pipeline, and a public leaderboard. The platform is a web server that hosts the ten jsPsych experiments and accepts requests from agents to start a session, submit trial data, and score the session (Figure 1). An agent starts a session, opens each task’s web page in its browser, and completes the experiment as a participant would. Once every task in a session has been received, the server scores it. All experimental logic (stimulus presentation, response recording, timing, and condition randomization) lives in jsPsych, so the server only stores and scores what the page sends. The platform does not constrain how an agent controls the browser. The model may see the page as a screenshot, as the underlying page text, or both, and any scaffold may be used. When a session is created, the agent reports which scaffold it uses and what the model sees, and these are stored as metadata that can be used to compare models. The setup used in the present study is described in Section 4.3 and in full in Appendix G. A plain-text instruction file (i.e., a SKILL.md) explains how to start a session and complete each task. Response deadlines can be modified for a session, and by default jsPsych will record the response time whether or not a deadline is in force. The platform is a public website on which agents complete the same experiments under the same conditions, and every scored session appears on a shared leaderboard ranked by composite score. 64.2 Reference Agents and Public Submissions 

We provide two agents with the platform. The first, the Random Agent, has no language model at all. Instead, it guesses by pressing a randomly selected valid key on each trial. It serves two purposes. It verifies that the experiments and scoring run end to end, and it establishes the chance floor described in Section 3.2. The second, the Browser-Use Agent, is the scaffold used in this study, and it can drive any model available through the major model providers (Section 4.3). Anyone can also submit sessions from their own agent, and submissions so far include commercial agent products and several custom scaffolds. 

4.3 Study Design 

We evaluated four multimodal models on the ten tasks, all through the same scaffold, Browser-Use [Browser-Use Contributors, 2024]. The models were kimi-k2.5 (Moonshot AI), 

gemini-3-flash-preview (Google), qwen3-vl-235b-instruct (Alibaba), and gpt-5.4-nano 

(OpenAI). All four sit in the inexpensive tier of their providers’ offerings, which we refer to as economy-tier models. Model calls were routed through OpenRouter, a service that provides one interface to many model providers. We define a cell as one model–task combination. Each of the 

40 cells was run ten times, each session with a new random seed for the stimulus sequence, giving 

400 sessions in total. Every session returned Level 1 and Level 3 scores, and Level 2 was defined for eight of the ten tasks. Running each cell ten times lets us report how much scores vary from session to session rather than a single value (Section 5.1). Because all four models are in the economy tier, a low score could indicate either that current multimodal models cannot reproduce a signature or that only less expensive models cannot. To separate these, we also evaluated one state-of-the-art model at the time of writing, gpt-5.6-sol ,on each of the ten tasks using the same scaffold. We report it separately from the four-model panel, since a single session per task is too little to compare with ten (Appendix K). The scaffold determines what the model sees and how its responses reach the page, so our results describe each model together with Browser-Use rather than the model alone. At every step (one model call, producing one or more actions), we recorded whether the model was given a screenshot or only the page text. Every step of all 400 sessions used screenshots, so every model saw the rendered page throughout. The Random Agent, which has no model, reads the page text only. Each session was limited to 1800 s and 500 steps per task, whatever the task’s number of trials (Table 5). Agent sessions were run with the per-trial response deadlines switched off, so that a slow model response could not by itself count as a missed trial. The Random Agent was run ten times per task under the same scoring, giving the chance floor of Section 3.2; all one hundred of its sessions were scored. The full run settings are listed in Appendix G. All results in the paper come from one released version of the tasks and scoring code ( v1.3.0 ), and every number can be recomputed from the released results files (Appendix A).

5.1 The Completion-vs-Signature Gap 

We first ask whether the agents finished the experiments. Completion was far from guaranteed. Only 167 of the 400 sessions passed all five Level 1 checks, and in 124 sessions fewer than half the behavioral signatures could be tested, 83 of them with no testable signature at all. The time and step limits explain little of this. Of the 233 sessions that failed a Level 1 check, 30 ran out of time and 

1 ran out of steps, and 11 were stopped by the scaffold after ten failed actions in a row. The other 

191 ended because the agent declared the task finished when the experiment had not yet ended. The Random Agent, by contrast, completed every session it started (Level 1 = 1 .000 ), and no model matched it; the best, gemini-3-flash-preview , reached 0.936 . This is expected. A script that presses a key on every trial should always complete the study. Finishing an experiment is therefore not by itself evidence of ability. What completion does tell us is whether the agent and the platform worked together well enough to produce data. We next ask whether the sessions that produced data showed the signatures. Over the measurable sessions, agents scored well above chance on both remaining levels. Mean Level 2 was 0.345 against a chance floor of 0.159 , and mean Level 3 was 0.490 against 0.184 . However, that mean conceals large differences between signatures (Table 8). Some were reproduced in nearly every session that could test them. These were above-chance discrimination of phishing from legitimate email ( 90% 

of testable sessions), the preference for saving more people in the moral dilemmas ( 100% ), and cooperating after the opponent cooperated in the prisoner’s dilemma ( 100% ). Others were reproduced in none. These were recognizing previously seen images above chance ( 0 of 25 ), staying longer in a patch when travel is costly ( 0 of 32 ), and faster responses at higher motion coherence ( 0 of 21 ), although for the last of these the scaffold itself is a likely cause, since a single screenshot carries no motion (Appendix G). Completion and signatures came apart in both directions. Agents finished the grid bandit without choosing the tiles their own reward history favored in 44% of sessions, and finished the foraging task without ever showing the travel-cost effect. Even among the sessions that passed every Level 1 check, only 40% of testable signatures were reproduced. Whether an agent completed a task thus says little about whether its data look like a person’s. The repeats showed that a single session can be misleading. In 28 of the 40 cells, the same model on the same task scored zero on Level 3 in some sessions and above zero in others. Across sessions within a cell, the standard deviation of Level 3 had a median of 0.20 . At the level of signatures, 60 of the 139 model–signature combinations passed in some sessions and failed in others. For example, 

kimi-k2.5 on marbles_risk had a composite of 63 .2 ± 24 .8, so one session could have shown strong sensitivity to expected value or none (Appendix J). The model ranking was stable under resampling, but the individual cells were not, so our claims are about the panel as a whole. 8Finally, the state-of-the-art model, gpt-5.6-sol , run once per task, had a higher mean completion score than the panel (Level 1 of 0.960 against 0.750 , trailing the panel only on serial recall) and scored higher on Level 3 ( 0.656 against 0.490 over measurable sessions), but beat the best panel model on only four of the ten tasks. With one session per task we cannot say how much of this would survive repetition, so we take it as a sign that a stronger model can do better, not as an estimate of how much better (Appendix K).

The all-session composite ranking was unchanged under three alternative weightings, 

(0 .10 , 0.30 , 0.60) , (0 .20 , 0.40 , 0.40) , and (0 , 0, 1) . The measurable-session ranking was unchanged under three alternatives to the {0, 0.5, 1} Level 3 grade, which shifted the agent–floor gap by at most 0.021 (Appendix L). New community submissions appear on the shared leaderboard at 

https://cog-arena.vercel.app/leaderboard , with per-level columns and the chance-floor reference always shown. 

6 Discussion 

We built a benchmark on which AI agents complete ten behavioral experiments through a browser, as online participants do, and scored their data for completion, accuracy, and the behavioral signatures each paradigm reliably produces in people. Four multimodal models, each run ten times per task, often failed to finish, and when they did finish they reproduced some signatures and not others, in a pattern that varied across tasks and across sessions of the same task. An evaluation that scores only whether an agent finished, or only how accurate it was, would not have shown this. The result bears on both audiences for the benchmark. For those asking whether AI systems behave like people [Shiffrin and Mitchell, 2023, Hagendorff, 2023, Dillion et al., 2023], it says which human signatures current agents reproduce and which they do not. For those who collect behavioral data online and worry that agents are contaminating it [Westwood, 2025, Van der Stigchel et al., 2026, Zhang et al., 2026], it suggests which measures may still separate people from current agents. A low Level 3 score should not be read as a deficit in ability, because human behavior is not always the best behavior [Ying et al., 2025]. We therefore labeled every signature as either a capacity or a bias. A capacity is a pattern that a normative model of the task also predicts, so that showing it and performing well are the same thing. For example, an ideal observer’s accuracy rises with motion coherence, and a forager that maximizes its rate of reward stays longer in a patch when travel between patches is costly [Charnov, 1976]. Failing a capacity signature is a performance deficit. A bias, on the other hand, is a pattern that departs from the normative benchmark, or a preference that no benchmark prescribes, so an agent that does not show it may be performing as well as people or better. The repeated prisoner’s dilemma illustrates the distinction. The opponent always plays tit-for-tat, so the agent faces a sequential decision problem rather than a game, and over the fifteen rounds shown on screen the policy that earns the most cooperates in every round but the last one. That policy cooperates whenever the opponent has just cooperated, so this signature is a capacity. It also resumes cooperation immediately after a defection rather than retaliating in the next round, so tit-for-tat reciprocity is a bias. In the risky-choice task, expected-utility theory predicts that the risky option will be chosen more often as its probability or payoff rises, so the sensitivity and magnitude signatures are capacities. The theory does not fix how much risk a decision-maker should accept, so choosing the higher-expected-value option, as a risk-neutral decision-maker would, is a preference it permits but does not require. The four moral-dilemma preferences (sparing more people, the young, humans over pets, and the lawful) have no agreed normative standard. The two kinds of signatures gave different results. Over measurable sessions, agents reproduced capacity signatures in 31% of the cases in which they could be tested and bias signatures in 66% 

(with the response-time signatures discussed below set aside, 36% against 66% ). The overall Level 3 score is therefore raised by patterns that good performance does not require, and claims about what agents can do should rest on the capacity signatures. For detecting AI-generated data, the same result cuts the other way. The human patterns that current agents reproduce most reliably are preferences, so screening respondents by whether they show the expected preference will not separate agents from people. For example, every agent session that could be tested preferred saving more lives in the moral dilemmas. 9Response time needs the same care. Agents took up to ten times longer than people to respond (about twelve times, in median response time, on random dot motion), mostly because each response waits for the model to process a screenshot and produce an action. Comparing an agent’s response times with human ones therefore measures how fast the model is served rather than how it decides. What can be scored is relative speed within a session, since an agent can be uniformly slow and still respond faster on easy trials than on hard ones, as people do. The seven response-time signatures in the benchmark test relations of this kind, and agents passed them in 30 of 217 testable cases. Response times did vary within sessions (median coefficient of variation 0.27 to 0.58 across tasks), so the absence of these relations is not because agents responded at a fixed rate. Slow responding may still be a useful cue for flagging AI-generated data in online studies; it is just not a measure of the agent’s decision process. To conclude, CogArena separately measures whether an agent can complete a behavioral experiment and whether its data reproduce the behavioral signatures seen in people. We hope it will serve as a shared benchmark for two increasingly intertwined communities, AI-agent evaluation and online behavioral research. The tasks, scoring procedures, and leaderboard are open to new submissions, so that its picture of current agents can be updated as models change. 

7 Limitations 

First, and most importantly, no human participants have completed our versions of the tasks. Of the 31 

Level 2 baselines, 24 are computed from participant data released by the original studies (Appendix F). Because those participants completed the original experiments rather than ours, a difference between agents and people may partly reflect our implementation rather than the agents. This concern is largest for Level 2, which compares magnitudes, and smallest for Level 3, which compares directions. We therefore make no claim about how agents compare with people completing CogArena itself. That comparison requires human data collected on the platform. The platform already serves the same experiments to human visitors, so collecting those data is a matter of recruitment rather than new software, and it is the first priority for the next release. Second, the models we evaluated are a narrow sample. All four are economy-tier, and even with ten sessions per cell their means have wide intervals. The state-of-the-art model was run only once. Every model ran through the same scaffold, Browser-Use, so differences between models are differences between model-and-scaffold combinations, and the screenshot-removal check shows how Browser-Use uses the screenshots rather than how the models themselves could. Third, the current task set does not test every phenomenon these paradigms are known for. The grid bandit, for example, keeps the number of remaining choices fixed, so it cannot test whether an agent explores more when more choices remain. Signatures and manipulations can be added to each task, and the present set should be read as a first release rather than an exhaustive one.

File layout. Each CogArena task is a standalone web application with a consistent file structure. The entry point is an index.html file that loads jsPsych v8 and the experiment script. The exper-iment logic resides in experiment.js , which defines the full trial sequence including instruction screens, practice blocks with feedback, experimental blocks, and automatic data submission. A

Relation to Psych-101/201. Those corpora informed which paradigms we selected, but the v1 set is not drawn from them. Three tasks share a source study with a Psych-201 entry. These are repeated_games [Akata et al., 2025], moral_machine [Awad et al., 2018], and 

phishing_detection [Singh et al., 2019]. The other seven correspond to no entry in either corpus, which each task records explicitly in its task_config.json . Even for the three, the correspondence is at the level of the source study rather than the implementation, since our implementations regenerate stimuli from a per-session seed and present them in a browser rather than as transcribed text. 

Stimulus randomization. Stimulus randomization is seeded from the session identifier, so each evaluation session produces a unique but reproducible stimulus sequence. In random_dot_motion ,coherence levels are interleaved within each block. In grid_bandit , the locations of high-value tiles and Kraken tiles are regenerated for each block. In marbles_risk , gamble values and probabilities are sampled from the task’s pre-registered grid. In tiny_alchemy , the starting set of base elements and its presentation order are fixed. In repeated_games , the opponent strategy is identical across sessions (tit-for-tat in the prisoner’s dilemma (PD) and an alternating opponent in the battle of the sexes (BoS)), but the state on each trial evolves with the agent’s actions. 

B Behavioral Signatures Evaluated in v1 

Table 3 lists all 39 Level 3 behavioral signatures evaluated across the ten v1 tasks, the statistical test used for each, and the importance weight wj . Per-task literature sources for expected directions are given in Appendix D. Test types and weights map directly to the Level 3 scoring formula (Equation 2). Table 2 lists only scored signatures. Two phenomena these paradigms are known for are deliberately absent. Horizon-dependent exploration in grid_bandit is not operationalized, because the task has no horizon manipulation. Our version of serial_recall also does not predict a U-shaped serial-position curve. It inserts a five-second filler interval, the standard manipulation for abolishing 14 recency [Glanzer and Cunitz, 1966], so scoring recency would test our implementation rather than the agent. Only primacy is scored. The Type column records each signature’s construct_type , which is stored in the task specification rather than assigned after the fact. C (capacity) marks a signature for which a normative model of the task predicts the same pattern people show, so reproducing it and performing well coincide and failing it is a performance deficit. B (bias) marks a signature that departs from a normative benchmark or expresses a preference that no normative benchmark prescribes, so an agent may reasonably not share it. Thirty-three signatures are capacities and six are biases, namely the four moral_machine 

preferences, repeated_games ’s tit-for-tat reciprocity, and marbles_risk ’s above-chance choice of the option with the higher expected value (EV). Each bias signature carries a written rationale in its specification. Over measurable sessions, agents reproduced capacity signatures in 31% of the cases in which they could be tested and bias signatures in 66% , so the overall Level 3 mean overstates their abilities, and claims about ability should rest on the capacity signatures (Section 6). For detecting AI-generated data, both kinds are useful, since a bias that people reliably show separates people from agents whatever its normative status. 

C Statistical Test Descriptions 

Level 3 scoring uses four types of statistical tests. Each takes a per-task signature specification (predictor, outcome, expected direction, and weight) and returns a per-signature score in {0, 0.5, 1},or reports the signature untestable. Implementations are in scoring/analysis_templates/ in the public repository. 

One-sample proportion tests (19 signatures) compare an observed proportion against a chance level with a one-sided exact binomial test, run at the effective sample size neff = n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ) given the series’ lag-1 autocorrelation ρ (clamped at n, so negative autocorrelation cannot add power). They are used for signatures such as above-chance accuracy, Kraken avoidance, and utilitarian preference. The test also computes the smallest p attainable at the given n and chance level. If even a perfect result could not reach p < 0.05 , the signature is reported untestable rather than scored 0.

Paired proportion contrasts (5 signatures) compare binary accuracy or choice rates between two trial subsets within the same session, taking the p-value from a moving-block bootstrap (Appendix H) so that within-session dependence does not inflate significance. They are used for the high-vs-low similarity advantage and the primacy effect in serial recall, the post-vs-pre training learning effect in phishing detection, and above-chance discrimination (hit rate vs. false-alarm rate) in visual recognition and phishing detection. 

Conditional proportion contrasts (2 signatures) compare a binary outcome’s rate between two conditions with a one-sided Fisher exact test. The two cases are cooperation after an opponent’s cooperation versus after a defection (tit-for-tat reciprocity in repeated games) and the stay rate under high versus low travel cost (the residence effect in effort foraging). A constant policy against a deterministic opponent can leave one condition empty. Reciprocity is then not identifiable from the session, and the signature is reported untestable. 

Correlation tests (13 signatures, labeled corr in Table 3) test the sign and significance of a Pearson correlation between two trial-level variables (e.g., the coherence-accuracy psychometric in random dot motion, EV sensitivity in marbles risk, within-block learning in grid bandit), or a Spearman correlation for the seven response-time signatures, which test relative rather than absolute speed. Each signature scores 1.0 if the test reaches p < 0.05 in the expected direction, 0.5 if it is in the expected direction but non-significant, and 0.0 otherwise. The per-task L3 score is the importance-weighted mean over testable signatures (Equation 2). An undefined statistic is untestable when the predictor is constant, since the design never varied, but scores 0 when the outcome is constant, since an agent that answers identically at every level of the manipulation shows no effect.

Above-chance reward ( w = 1 .0) tests whether mean reward exceeds the grid mean of 50, and within-block learning ( w = 1 .0) tests whether observed reward grows monotonically across click index within a block. 

marbles_risk (after Levin and Hart, 2003, Tymula et al., 2013). EV sensitivity ( w = 2 .0) tests whether the probability of choosing the risky urn correlates with the EV difference between the risky and safe options. Above-chance EV-optimal choice ( w = 1 .5) tests whether the agent picks the higher-EV option at rates above chance on trials with a clear EV advantage. The magnitude effect ( w = 1 .0) tests whether the risky option is chosen more often when its payoff is larger, across matched probability levels. 

repeated_games (after Akata et al., 2025 for the repeated PD against tit-for-tat and Cooper et al., 1989 for BoS coordination). Tit-for-tat reciprocity ( w = 1 .5) tests whether the agent’s current cooperate decision tracks the opponent’s previous-round move in the PD blocks. Above-chance coordination ( w = 1 .5) tests whether the agent matches the opponent’s BoS action at rates above chance. Conditional cooperation when the opponent cooperated ( w = 1 .0) tests whether, when the opponent cooperated in the previous round, the agent’s cooperation rate exceeds 0.5 (the v1.1 audit replaced an earlier non-zero-cooperation signature that random play trivially passed). 

serial_recall (after Murdock, 1962, Haridi et al., 2026). Above-chance recall ( w = 1 .5) tests whether overall recall accuracy exceeds the typed-three-letters chance baseline. Similarity-aids-recall (w = 1 .5) tests whether recall accuracy increases with semantic similarity of the cue-target pair. The high-vs-low similarity advantage ( w = 1 .0) sharpens this contrast as a paired proportion test on the extreme similarity buckets. The primacy effect ( w = 1 .0) tests whether pairs studied in the first quarter of the list are recalled better than those studied in the middle. Position bands are defined from the configured list length of 16 pairs rather than the highest position a session reached, so a truncated session cannot relabel positions. Recency is not scored, for the reason given in Appendix B. 

visual_recognition (after Brady et al., 2008, Standing, 1973). Above-chance discrimination (w = 2 .0) tests whether the hit rate on old items significantly exceeds the false-alarm rate on new items, the canonical recognition-memory comparison. Above-chance accuracy ( w = 1 .5) tests overall accuracy against chance. Low false-alarm rate ( w = 0 .5) tests whether the agent avoids the trivial old-bias strategy. 

effort_foraging (after Constantino and Daw, 2015, Bustamante et al., 2023, Charnov, 1976). The marginal value theorem (MVT) says that a forager should leave a patch when its current rate of reward falls below the average rate for the environment. The travel-cost residence effect ( w = 2 .0)tests whether the agent stays in a patch more often under high than under low travel cost between patches, the central MVT prediction. It is scored from the stay rate on every trial rather than from residence times, because agents leave patches too rarely for residence times to be testable. Above-chance MVT match ( w = 1 .5) tests whether per-trial stay/leave decisions match the MVT-optimal prediction. Above-chance total reward ( w = 1 .0) tests whether reward per decision exceeds the random-policy baseline. 

tiny_alchemy (after Brändle et al., 2023). Above-chance discovery ( w = 2 .0) tests whether successful element combinations occur at a rate above the empirical random-agent ceiling. Non-redundant attempts ( w = 1 .0) tests whether the proportion of unique unordered (element_a, element_b) pairs across the session exceeds the random-policy ceiling. The v1.1 audit dropped an empowerment-preference correlation that was firing mechanically for random play (high in-degree base elements were over-sampled by chance) without indicating cognitive selectivity. 17 moral_machine (after Awad et al., 2018). Utilitarian preference ( w = 1 .5) tests whether agents prefer to save the larger group when group sizes differ. Save-young ( w = 1 .0) tests the cross-cultural preference for saving the young over the elderly. Save-human ( w = 1 .5) tests the preference for saving humans over pets, the largest single-factor effect in the original Moral Machine corpus. Save-lawful ( w = 1 .0) tests the preference to save lawful over unlawful crossers ( 0.68 in Awad et al.’s data). An intervention-aversion signature ( w = 0 .5), testing the mild human preference against swerving when outcomes are otherwise equivalent ( 0.47 intervention rate in Awad et al.’s data), was removed from the v1 specification after the power audit found it untestable at n = 4 (Appendix H). No metric scores it now, since moral_machine has no Level 2 metrics (Appendix E). 

phishing_detection (after Singh et al., 2019). Above-chance discrimination ( w = 2 .0) tests whether the hit rate on phishing emails exceeds the false-alarm rate on legitimate emails. The learning effect ( w = 1 .5) tests whether accuracy on the ten post-training trials significantly exceeds accuracy on the ten pre-training trials. Above-chance overall accuracy ( w = 1 .0) tests whether classification accuracy across all sixty trials is above chance. 

E Level 2 Accuracy Metrics in v1 

CogArena v1 evaluates 31 Level 2 accuracy metrics across eight of the ten tasks, 3.9 per task on average. Absolute response time is not among them. It is scored at Level 3 through relational signatures instead (Section 6). The normalization is monotonic in z, which rewards exceeding the human value. That is correct only for a metric on which exceeding the human value is better performance, so Level 2 is restricted to such metrics. Three groups that the submitted version scored here are therefore not scored at Level 2. The five moral_machine proportions (utilitarian, save-young, save-human, save-lawful, and intervention choices) are preferences with no better direction, so the monotonic mapping rewarded an agent for being more extreme than people, and each preference was also counted a second time through its Level 3 signature. The rate of higher-expected-value choices in marbles_risk treats risk neutrality as correct, which expected-utility theory does not, since it leaves the decision-maker’s risk attitude open. Similarly, the stay rate and the two mean residence times in effort_foraging rewarded staying longer, but under the marginal value theorem staying is better only up to the optimum, so these metrics rewarded over-harvesting. Agents stayed on 96% of decisions and scored higher on the stay rate than on any other foraging metric. Whether an agent shares the human direction on each of these patterns is tested at Level 3. As a result, 

moral_machine and marbles_risk have no Level 2 score, and their composite redistributes the Level 2 weight to Levels 1 and 3 in proportion (Section 3.2). Five metrics are scored with the sign reversed, because for them a lower value is better performance. These are the two false-alarm rates, 

grid_bandit ’s Kraken-caught rate, and the two timeout rates. Each metric is normalized against its source-study human baseline (Appendix F) by converting it to a z-score and mapping the z-score through the standard normal CDF to a score between 0 and 1 (Section 3.2). The per-task metrics are listed below, and full specifications, including human baseline means and standard deviations, are in 

tasks/<task_id>/scoring/level2_metrics.json in the public repository. • random_dot_motion (4 metrics) covers overall accuracy and accuracy at low, medium, and high coherence. • grid_bandit (5 metrics) covers mean reward per click, mean reward in safe and in risky blocks, the proportion of high-value clicks (tested against an explicit numeric threshold), and the Kraken-caught rate in risky blocks. • marbles_risk has no Level 2 metrics. Its higher-EV choice rate is tested at Level 3. • repeated_games (5 metrics) covers the cooperation rate in the PD, the coordination rate in the BoS, mean payoff in each game, and the timeout rate. • serial_recall (4 metrics) covers overall accuracy, accuracy at high and at low similarity, and the timeout rate. • visual_recognition (3 metrics) covers overall accuracy, hit rate, and false-alarm rate. • effort_foraging (2 metrics) covers total reward and the MVT-optimal match rate. • tiny_alchemy (3 metrics) covers success rate, novelty rate, and unique-pair rate. • moral_machine has no Level 2 metrics. Its preferences are tested at Level 3. 18 • phishing_detection (5 metrics) covers overall accuracy, pre- and post-training accuracy, hit rate, and false-alarm rate. 

F Human-Baseline Provenance 

Every Level 3 expected direction in v1, and every Level 2 baseline except four targets that we set our-selves, comes from published work rather than from participants completing our versions of the tasks. Because a measured agent–human difference could in principle reflect our implementation rather than the agent, this appendix documents, for each task, the source of the baseline, how firmly it is anchored in that source, and the specific ways our implementation differs from the study the numbers come from. Twenty-four of the thirty-one Level 2 baselines, spanning six tasks, are computed directly from the original authors’ released participant data. Of the other seven, three are visual_recognition 

baselines derived from the source paper’s reported statistics, because no public archive of that study exists. Two are effort_foraging targets set from the design of our own version of the task, because the source’s travel manipulation differs from ours. The last two are timeout-rate targets, which we set ( 0.02 for repeated_games and 0.05 for serial_recall ) rather than measured from participants. Because deadlines were disabled for agent sessions (Appendix G), no agent trial could time out, and these two metrics took the same value in every agent session. The marbles_risk and 

moral_machine rates and the effort_foraging stay rate are also computed from released data, but they are preferences or strategy descriptors rather than accuracy measures, so they are kept as ref-erence values and not scored (Appendix E). The values actually used in scoring are the human_mean 

and human_sd fields of each task’s tasks/<task>/scoring/level2_metrics.json . The source archives ship with the release, together with per-file citations, and scripts/derive_baselines.py 

documents, for every derived value, the archive, condition, and participants it comes from. The Tier column of Table 4 records how each baseline is derived. R (raw data) means the value is computed directly from the original authors’ released participant data, and six tasks carry this tier throughout. D ( effort_foraging ) means the targets are set from the design of our version of the task. A dash marks a task with no Level 2 metrics, whose source supplies reference values only. P (paper-derived) means no public archive exists and the value is derived from statistics reported in the source paper, which applies only to visual_recognition . Earlier versions used author estimates throughout; the current release replaces them with recomputed values wherever source data exist, and 

scripts/derive_baselines.py reproduces every derived value from the archived data. Two consequences follow for interpretation. First, the two levels are unequally exposed to this provenance gap. Level 3 tests the direction of an effect, which transfers across implementations far better than magnitude does. Level 2 converts a raw value into a z-score against a specific mean and standard deviation, so an implementation that shifts the underlying distribution shifts the score even when behavior is unchanged. Level 2 should accordingly be read as normalization against a source-study anchor, not as a comparison against humans on this platform. Second, the direction of the likely distortion is known in at least one case. Our visual_recognition stimuli are compositionally structured and therefore probably easier than the photographs in the source study, so a human completing our version would likely exceed the baseline we score against, which makes agent scores on that task optimistic rather than pessimistic. Collecting on-platform human data for all ten tasks is the planned remedy and would replace every row of Table 4.

What the model saw. With screenshots enabled, Browser-Use gives the model at each step a screenshot of the visible part of the page together with a text list of the page’s clickable and typeable elements. Every step of every evaluated session used this combination; no model was ever reduced to the text list alone. H Signature Trial Counts and Statistical Power 

Level 3 scores each signature from a single session’s trials, so the number of trials feeding a given test bounds what that test can detect. Table 5 gives the trial budget per task and the subset each signature draws on. The spread is wide. The coherence psychometric in random_dot_motion draws on 100 trials, whereas several signatures in moral_machine and phishing_detection draw on a few dozen or fewer, because the trial budget is divided across the dimensions or blocks the signature contrasts. This appendix examines the power of the signature tests under short trial budgets, the sensitivity of the results to within-session dependence, and how many signature observations could not be tested. 

Power. The shortest signatures cannot detect the effects they target. Detecting a preference of the size in Awad et al.’s data for legality ( 0.68 against a chance level of 0.5) at 80% power needs about fifty trials on that dimension, and v1 allocates six. A null on such a signature is therefore weak evidence that the agent lacks the pattern. Dependence. The tests assume independent trials, and agents violate that assumption more than human participants do. A Browser-Use agent carries its own action history in context and readily settles into a repeated policy. Across the archive the median lag-1 autocorrelation is near zero, but the tail is severe. The 90th percentile is ρ = 0 .75 , the maximum is 0.93 , and 23 .5% of proportion tests exceed ρ = 0 .3, against 1.6% for the random agent. We therefore checked Level 3 against a moving-block bootstrap, which imposes no parametric model of the dependence. Contiguous blocks of trials are resampled, so dependence within a block is preserved while dependence across blocks is broken, and the p-value inverts the resulting percentile interval. Re-running the 920 signature tests in measurable sessions this way changed 5.5% of grades, left the model ranking unchanged, and slightly widened rather than narrowed the gap between the agents and the chance floor ( +0 .013 on Level 3). Fixed ten-trial blocks, well above the default length of two to five, changed more grades ( 12 .2% ) and widened the gap further ( +0 .018 ). The all-session composite ranking was unchanged under both block rules. Over measurable sessions only, ten-trial blocks moved qwen3-vl-235b-instruct 

above gemini-3-flash-preview on Level 3 alone; gemini-3-flash-preview still leads on the headline composite under both conventions (Section 5). The comparison also shows that the first-order correction neff = n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ) applied to the one-sample proportion tests over-charges for dependence, since on that test 26 grades rise under the bootstrap and none fall. It showed the opposite for the paired proportion contrast, whose original pooled normal approximation was anti-conservative (median p 2.4× too small). The reported scores therefore take that test’s p-value from the bootstrap itself, and the switch changed four grades across the archive. Neither treatment separates serial dependence from a genuine within-session trend, so a learning curve is penalized like a sticky response. On the whole, then, these corrections are more likely to lower Level 3 than to raise it, although this need not hold for every signature.

K State-of-the-Art Model 

The panel is economy-tier, so a low Level 3 could reflect the price of the models we chose rather than a limit of current multimodal models. To check, we ran gpt-5.6-sol , a state-of-the-art model at the time of writing, once on each of the ten tasks under the same scaffold and scoring. Table 9 gives the per-task comparison against the panel’s per-task means over measurable sessions. Three results are worth separating. The model beat the panel’s per-task mean Level 3 on eight of ten tasks and gained most where the panel was weakest, reaching a perfect Level 3 on 

visual_recognition , on which the panel averaged 0.235 . Against the panel’s best member on each task it led on only four of ten, so it is not distinguishable from gemini-3-flash-preview ,and the two comparisons must be read together. Its most consistent advantage was completion, with 

L1 = 1 .000 on nine of ten tasks against a panel mean of 0.750 over all sessions. A large share of the composite gap therefore follows from finishing experiments that the economy panel abandons rather than from closer alignment. The serial_recall task is the one on which it trailed the panel on completion ( 0.600 against 0.655 ). These values come from a single session per task. The main study shows that Level 3 varies widely across repeated sessions for every panel model, and there is no reason to assume this model is exempt, so none of the per-task differences below should be read on its own. Repeats would be needed before this model could support a claim about any one task. We report it because it indicates how far the panel’s results generalize, not because one session settles anything.
