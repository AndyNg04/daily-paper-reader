Abstract 

Successful tool-use trajectories are not necessarily useful supervision. Even successful interactions may underrepresent multi-step dependencies or contain actions that are unjustified by the information visible to the agent at decision time. We identify two coupled requirements for effective trajectory supervision: compositional coverage, so that important observation– action dependencies are represented, and decision grounding, so that each supervised action is supported by the learner-visible interaction history. We introduce Trajectory2Gym, which constructs executable interactions around under-supported compositional structures and derives supervision only from grounded decisions in their realized executions. Structural-support selection allocates the resulting experience, while parent-normalized supervised fine-tuning preserves the semantic interaction as the unit of training weight. This reframes trajectory generation as the deliberate construction of learnable interaction experience, rather than the collection of successful trajectories. Across five Qwen3 scales, Trajectory2Gym improves Tau1, Tau2, and BFCL at every tested scale, increasing the equal-weight three-benchmark mean by 16.9–29.8 percentage points. The gains are strongest in stateful and multi-turn interaction, where later decisions depend on earlier observations and feedback. Our results suggest that scaling tool-use agents depends not only on collecting more successful trajectories, but on constructing interactions whose dependencies are represented and whose decisions are learnable from the agent’s visible history. 

1 Introduction 

Ask a support agent to cancel two orders and update a third. A competent agent looks up the account; when the email lookup fails, it asks for a name and ZIP code and retries; it reads each order to check it is pending, confirms with the user, and only then cancels and swaps items using catalog-returned identifiers. Each call is simple; what must be learned is their composition. We call such workflows compositional interactions : an observation, user update, or state change determines a later decision. Tool-use agents face this composition constantly [Yao et al., 2023, Schick et al., 2023, Patil et al., 2024, Qin et al., 2024a, Qu et al., 2025], interactive benchmarks make it central to task success [Yao et al., 2025, Barres et al., 2026, Patil et al., 2025], and collected or synthesized trajectories are its standard supervision [Qin et al., 2024b, Zeng et al., 2024]. Recent pipelines show that what interactions are collected matters, yet the prevailing strategy still filters trajectories by task completion or per-call execution [Liu et al., 2024c, 2025, Prabhakar et al., 2025, Xu et al., 2025c], and fine-tuning directly on logged trajectories can even fall below the initialization [Wu et al., 2026a]. Success certifies that an episode ended well, not what it teaches or whether each imitated action was justified by what the agent could see. Two challenges follow. Challenge 1 (compositional coverage): trajectories must exercise cross-step dependencies with repeated support, yet in our pool of 34,772 certified candidates, 40.9% contain at most two supervised decisions and only 10.0% contain a certified decision after an authentic error. Challenge 2 (decision grounding): each target must follow from decision-time visible information; successful API execution alone cannot establish this. A collector may use an unseen identifier, and in Figure 1 the API accepts a stale two-item exchange as readily as the lamp-only exchange finally requested by the user. Our central claim is that what a trajectory teaches is decided not by its outcome, but by the dependencies it realizes and the decisions its visible history can justify . Trajectory2Gym operationalizes this claim in three stages (Figure 2).

(1) Compositional interaction synthesis builds and executes tasks around under-supported interaction paths in native environments, so that information acquisition and recovery are demonstrated (Challenge 1). (2) Grounded supervision 

supervises only decisions certified by visible evidence, execution, and workflow order, keeping authentic errors as recovery context (Challenge 2). (3) Structural-support learning selects trajectories to cover capability paths at a per-scale support depth and trains with parent-normalized supervised fine-tuning (SFT). The stages are coupled: synthesis without grounding would teach the collector’s shortcuts, grounding without synthesis would certify a pool where recovery and discovery stay rare, and selection without both would only re-weight that pool. With plain supervised fine-tuning from each public initialization, the paired gains measure the complete post-training recipe. Data built this way lifts every scale: Trajectory2Gym improves Tau1, Tau2, and BFCL on all five Qwen3 models from 1.7B to 30B, raising the three-benchmark mean by 16.9 to 29.8 points. The lift outweighs model size: the post-trained 4B model surpasses the public 30B-A3B initialization on Tau2 (69.17 versus 28.33), and, against public agent-training checkpoints used as references rather than matched controls, the 8B model has the highest three-benchmark mean. Our contributions are: • We identify compositional coverage and decision grounding as coupled requirements and formalize them as a decision-level supervision mask over agent-visible histories (Section 3). • We introduce Trajectory2Gym, which synthesizes under-supported interactions in native environments and certifies targets from their executed, agent-visible histories (Sections 4.1 and 4.2). • We develop structural-support selection with parent-normalized learning and show that the framework improves all three benchmarks at each of five scales, with controlled comparisons of every stage (Sections 4.3 and 5). 

2 Related Work 

Tool-use benchmarks and training. Single-turn benchmarks test tool and argument selection [Li et al., 2023, Zhuang et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024a]; later suites add nested, multi-hop, and long-context calls [Basu et al., 2025, Ye et al., 2025, Zhong et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2025a]. Interactive benchmarks score whole episodes: multi-turn tool use [Wang et al., 2024b, Liu et al., 2024b, Lu et al., 2025, Guo et al., 2024], τ -bench customer service [Shi et al., 2026], web, computer, and workplace tasks [Zhou et al., 2024, Xie et al., 2024, Mialon et al., 2024, Trivedi et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2025a, Li et al., 2026], and MCP ecosystems [Luo et al., 2025, Mo et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2026c, Wu et al., 2026b]. Our focus is on representing the cross-step dependencies that an executed trajectory actually exercises. Training uses synthesized demonstrations [Parisi et al., 2022, Tang et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2023], cross-environment action traces [Chen et al., 2023,

Trajectory synthesis and curation. Trajectory construction is a data-design problem: verified executable synthesis produces function-calling data at scale [Yang et al., 2025], multi-turn extensions add dialogues, tool graphs, and simulated users [Chen et al., 2025b, Yin et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025b], and web and GUI agents mine tutorials, indirect knowledge, reverse task synthesis, and exploration [Xu et al., 2025b, Ou et al., 2024, Sun et al., 2025, Pahuja et al., 2025]. Capability-oriented pipelines target changing or infeasible intents [Wang et al., 2026d], active visual acquisition [Zhang et al., 2026d], or compositional tool graphs [Lu et al., 2023, Shen et al., 2024]; others scale environments [Hu et al., 2025, Song et al., 2026a, Tu et al., 2026, Fang et al., 2026, Chen et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026b, Tian et al., 2026], rebuild executable workspaces from logged trajectories and re-solve them under hidden tests, or standardize formats [Song et al., 2026b]. Code2Skill derives and verifies reusable procedural skills from source code [Tong et al., 2026]. Executable tool-use synthesis often verifies outcomes or calls. For software-engineering agents, Patches-to-Trajectories curates prefix-grounded, efficient steps using privileged reference patches [Ma et al., 2026]. Curation is complementary: data selection favors quality over volume [Zhou et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2024a], TopoCurate selects by interaction topology [Yang et al., 2026], and imitation learning separates privileged demonstrator knowledge from student observations [Ross et al., 2011, Chen et al., 2020]. Trajectory2Gym combines deficit-guided synthesis in native tool-use environments, policy-visible target eligibility, and structural-support allocation across learner scales.

Construction establishes the entities, prerequisites, and information sources each path needs (a transaction task may identify the customer but require a lookup for the transaction identifier; a recovery variant creates a discoverable native rejection) and binds required values to visible sources (the request, a user exchange, or a tool result), since only such values can become grounded targets. 5.2 Stateful Tool Use (RQ1 to RQ3) 

Trajectory2Gym raises the Tau1 and Tau2 mean by 20 to 41 points at every scale. All five initializations improve on both benchmarks (Table 1); the paired gain is smallest at 1.7B (20.04) and between 35 and 41 points from 4B upward, and supervision changes what a given capacity delivers: the post-trained 4B model reaches 69.17 on Tau2, against 28.33 for the public 30B-A3B initialization, and the post-trained 30B-A3B model reaches 84.17, within 1.2 points of the reported closed-source references, 84.76 and 85.31 (RQ1). Against re-evaluated agent-training checkpoints, our 8B model reaches 74.17 on Tau2, at least 28 points above every one of them, 14B and 32B ones included, and on Tau1 trails only MUA-RL-32B, which our 14B and 30B-A3B models exceed (RQ2). The gain is also broad: across five domains and five scales, 24 of 25 paired comparisons improve, with the largest movement in Tau2 Telecom, which rises from 12.50 to 95.00 at 30B-A3B, and the exception, Tau2 Airline at 1.7B, is unchanged at 20.00 (RQ3). Taken together, standard SFT on decision-grounded compositional data, with no reinforcement learning, makes every initialization from 4B upward a stronger stateful agent than any evaluated checkpoint, RL-trained and 32B ones included (Tau mean 56.70 to 75.47 versus at most 49.52); the margin is widest on Tau2, whose multi-step, state-dependent tasks are where compositional coverage and decision grounding are meant to act. These gains reflect the complete recipe, whose training view also changes with scale (Appendix B.1); the matched ablations of Section 5.4 separate its stages.

More structural support is not automatically better. Performance does not increase monotonically with support depth (Table 4): the three-benchmark mean is highest at the shallowest setting (72.28 versus 70.55 and 69.44) even as trajectories and target tokens continue to grow. The decline is concentrated in Tau2 (82.50 to 75.00), while BFCL remains comparatively stable. The headline schedule was fixed before these runs, so this is a sensitivity test rather than checkpoint selection; because depth also changes data volume, it does not isolate path support from token count. In short, structural support is an allocation problem rather than a simple data-scaling rule, and we set depth per scale by a predeclared schedule (Appendices F.5 and D). The structural-selection control and the depth sweep answer complementary questions. The former changes the distribution of represented structures at matched parent and target-token budgets; the latter increases repetition together with supervision volume. A gain from the first intervention can therefore coexist with a decline from the second. In terms of the construction objective, adding an uncovered path expands breadth, whereas adding another interaction to a covered path increases support. This distinction links the empirical results to the separate coverage and deficit-allocation stages of the selector.

6 Conclusion 

Successful trajectories are the default supervision for tool-use agents, yet success certifies an episode, not what it teaches. This paper has argued that a trajectory’s value lies in properties an outcome filter cannot see, compositional coverage and decision grounding, and Trajectory2Gym builds training data around them: it synthesizes the interactions a pool lacks, certifies supervision one decision at a time, and allocates structural support per scale. Across scales from 1.7B to 30B, standard SFT on the constructed interactions improves tool use, especially on multi-turn and stateful tasks. Matched 8B controls give directional evidence for the four design choices, with the clearest isolated gain from parent normalization. The broader lesson is that success should gate supervision, not license it; that authentic errors belong in context, not in targets; and that support is an allocation, not a quantity to maximize. The resulting training corpus can be viewed at three levels: which interaction structures are available, which decisions are eligible for imitation, and how much optimization mass each complete interaction receives. These levels can vary even when two datasets contain similar numbers of trajectories or target tokens. Treating them separately makes data-construction choices explicit and helps interpret why larger collections do not automatically provide better supervision. For future tool-use training pipelines, this suggests tracking structural coverage and decision eligibility alongside data volume, while keeping the training weight of an interaction independent of its rendering format. Extending decision-level certification to on-policy rollouts and to web, software, and terminal agents is the natural next step. What a trajectory teaches is decided not by its outcome, but by the dependencies it realizes and the decisions its visible history can justify.

A Experimental Protocol  

> A.1 Benchmarks and Metrics

We evaluate Trajectory2Gym on Tau1, Tau2, and BFCL. Tau1 contains 115 Retail and 50 Airline tasks, while Tau2 contains 40 Retail, 20 Airline, and 40 Telecom tasks. For both Tau benchmarks, we report native task success within each domain and macro-average equally across domains. BFCL contains 1,718 examples spanning four Non-live categories, three Live categories, and Multi-turn Base. We use the same three-group Overall aggregation as in the main text, equally averaging the Non-live mean, Live mean, and Multi-turn score.  

> A.2 Training and Evaluation Populations

Training-side interaction construction follows the released data partitions of each environment. Tau1 Retail uses separate training and evaluation populations. Tau1 Airline uses the public 50-task identity set for training-side interaction construction and fresh-dialogue evaluation; we therefore treat it as a seen-task, transductive setting rather than task-disjoint generalization. For Tau2 Retail, Airline, and Telecom, training-side construction uses the official train split and evaluation uses the official test split. BFCL follows its released training-side and evaluation memberships. This provides example-level separation, although semantically related tool behaviors may occur across the two populations.  

> A.3 Comparison Protocol

Our primary comparison pairs each Trajectory2Gym model with its corresponding public Qwen3 initialization. Within each scale, the two models are evaluated on the same benchmark instances with the same agent interface, tool access, decoding configuration, user simulator where applicable, and native scorer. These paired evaluations isolate the effect of the post-training procedure from differences in evaluation conditions. We additionally report public agent-training checkpoints as external references. These models are evaluated on the same benchmark populations and scoring rules, while retaining model-native prompting and tool-call interfaces where necessary. They therefore provide cross-method context rather than matched interventions; full results and model-specific settings are given in Appendix F.1. Closed-source systems in Tables 1 and 2 are included only as contextual references under their reported protocols. The BFCL results for GPT-5.5, Gemini-3.0-Pro, and ToolACE-2-8B follow Table 3 of Gecko; we use the unrefined scores rather than the additional GATS test-time computation.  

> A.4 Training Configuration

All Trajectory2Gym models are initialized from their corresponding public Qwen3 checkpoints and trained for one epoch with full-parameter, assistant-token causal cross-entropy. We use a global batch size of eight rendered examples, bfloat16 precision, AdamW with weight decay 0.01 , a cosine learning-rate schedule with ten warmup steps, and a maximum gradient norm of 1.0. Training is non-thinking throughout, with no preference-learning, process-reward, or reinforcement-learning objective. Among optimization hyperparameters, only the learning rate varies with model scale: 1.1 × 10 −5 for 1.7B, 7 × 10 −6 for 4B and 8B, and 3.7 × 10 −6 for 14B and 30B-A3B. Each learner receives the scale-conditioned training experience defined by the structural-support procedure in Appendix D. 

B Interaction Synthesis and Data Composition  

> B.1 Training Interaction Corpus

We treat each complete realized interaction as a semantic unit of training experience. It preserves the user request, the observations exposed through native execution, and the grounded assistant decisions that can serve as learning targets. Keeping these elements within one interaction preserves the dependency between earlier evidence and later decisions. The 1.7B–14B learners draw from a common corpus of 34,772 certified interactions spanning 2,515 training-side tasks. The 30B-A3B learner draws from an expanded corpus of 35,819 interactions spanning 3,602 tasks. Both corpora use the same compositional representation and selection principle; the expanded corpus broadens the set of available tasks and interaction structures. The selected views contain 11,583, 13,986, 14,819, 15,805, and 19,937 interactions for the 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B, and 30B-A3B learners, respectively, corresponding to approximately 13.0M, 18.0M, 19.4M, 20.6M, and 23.9M supervised target tokens. Task coverage remains fixed at 2,515 tasks for the 1.7B–14B views and expands to 3,602 tasks for 30B-A3B.

The available experience is strongly skewed toward short, direct interactions. Within the common corpus, 40.9% of interactions contain at most two supervised assistant decisions, while only 10.0% contain a grounded decision after an authentic error observation. Thus corpus size alone does not imply adequate exposure to recovery, feedback-conditioned continuation, or other cross-step dependencies. This imbalance motivates explicit control over compositional support.  

> B.2 Compositional Structure Representation

We represent an interaction by the dependencies that connect decisions across time rather than by its sequence of native tool names. A later action may depend on an earlier observation, user update, environment response, or state transition. Examples include reusing a retrieved identifier, revising a decision after a rejection, or satisfying a prerequisite before a later state change. To compare such dependencies across tasks and environments, we use a shared capability vocabulary K that abstracts semantic operations, observations, control-flow events, and interaction horizon. Temporal order over these elements defines a compositional path. The representation therefore captures how decisions depend on one another, rather than the surface API through which they are expressed. For example, retrieving an identifier and later using it in an update instantiates the same information-dependent structure across different tool APIs. Likewise, a rejected action followed by information acquisition and a successful continuation instantiates a recovery structure independently of the backend that produced the rejection. A crucial distinction is between intended and realized structure. A target composition can guide task construction, but it contributes support only when the dependency is actually realized during native execution. A task intended to require recovery, for example, provides no recovery support if the collected interaction never encounters and resolves the relevant obstacle. Structural support is therefore defined by the learner’s realized experience, not by the generator’s specification.  

> Operational encoding.

The released representation uses fixed feature families on the policy-visible, decision-masked interaction. Decision kinds distinguish text, tool calls, multiple calls, and handoffs. Tool access is mapped to read, write, irreversible write, or handoff using fixed name rules. Object classes cover identity, commerce, travel, finance, connectivity, communication, location, and time; argument roles cover identifiers, amounts, temporal values, locations, contacts, status, quantity, and content. Names are tokenized before these dictionary lookups. Multiple matches form a sorted composite class, while unmatched names receive a generic class. Values contribute their types, such as string, number, list, or object, rather than their literal contents. Observation descriptors record return shape and, for object-valued returns, the roles of their top-level fields. Set-valued coverage additionally includes call and argument counts, decision-horizon buckets, adjacent deci-sion/access/object transitions, and an error-recovery indicator. For example, horizon buckets have upper boundaries 

1, 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 , with a final overflow bucket. The capability vocabulary comprises the instantiated labels from these families, rather than a separate label for each native API.  

> A unique path for a complete interaction.

Each supervised assistant decision contributes one descriptor containing its decision kind, ordered call descriptors, subsequent consecutive tool observations, and whether visible error feedback occurred since the preceding assistant decision. Argument-role/type pairs are sorted within a call; calls and observations retain their recorded order. A call also records a bucketed count of nonempty string arguments found in preceding visible non-assistant evidence. This is a representation feature applied after target eligibility has been determined, not a replacement for grounding checks. The canonical path is the ordered sequence of these descriptors, serialized deterministically and assigned a stable key. Thus a trajectory is not assigned to one manually chosen dependency: its complete supervised decision sequence determines its path, while all of its capability atoms remain available for coverage accounting. Exact tool names, argument values, task identities, and benchmark labels are absent from the serialized path. Different native interactions share a path when their descriptors match; changing decision order, observation shape, or recovery context can produce a different path.

An order_id argument contributes identifier/string rather than the particular order number. The order status, returned identifiers, and user confirmations remain in the underlying visible history used to certify actions. The resulting path summarizes the ordered, eligible experience; it does not replace that history or its execution evidence.  

> B.3 Synthesis from Structural Deficits

Synthesis is directed toward compositional structures that remain weakly represented in the current interaction corpus. Given a target path, task construction produces a user request and an initial environment state for which successful completion requires the corresponding dependency. Different dependencies induce different forms of construction. Information-dependent tasks make a value needed later in the workflow obtainable only through earlier visible evidence. Recovery tasks introduce a resolvable obstacle whose cause and resolution are exposed through native feedback. State-dependent tasks require later operations to respect information or state established earlier in the interaction. The resulting task is then executed in the native environment by a collection policy. Observations, state transitions, user responses, errors, and terminal outcomes are therefore consequences of execution rather than annotations inserted by the generator. Collection-side guidance may shape the attempted interaction, but it does not become learner-visible evidence unless it is exposed through the native interface. After execution, the realized interaction is mapped back to the compositional representation. Only structures that actually occurred contribute support, and decision-grounded supervision determines which realized assistant decisions can become learning targets. Subsequent synthesis is then directed toward structures that remain under-supported. Thus intended structure determines where to generate , whereas realized structure determines what experience was actually obtained .

C Decision-Grounded Supervision 

Decision grounding asks whether a realized assistant decision can be reconstructed from the information available to the policy when that decision is made. This differs from execution validity: an action may be accepted by the environment while still relying on information unavailable to the learner. The grounding procedure therefore separates what happened in the collected interaction from which decisions constitute reproducible supervision.  

> C.1 Policy-Visible Evidence

For a decision at step t, grounding is defined relative to the policy-visible history V (H<t ). Only information exposed through the native agent interface can justify the decision. User messages and assistant-visible tool observations may therefore provide evidence, whereas collector-private state and private external operations cannot directly support a target. The relevant notion is information availability rather than textual overlap. A decision is grounded when the information needed to choose it is recoverable from the preceding visible interaction. For example, an identifier known only to the collection policy cannot justify an assistant action even if the resulting call succeeds. If the same identifier is subsequently returned through a visible tool observation, later decisions may legitimately depend on it. The same principle applies to dynamically available tools. When tool discovery is part of the native interface, a tool can support a supervised action only after its identity and usable interface have become visible to the assistant. More generally, arguments referring to users, orders, items, accounts, or other environment entities must be supported by preceding visible evidence. Private events may change the underlying environment without entering the student history. Their consequences become usable only when they are subsequently exposed through an observation available to the assistant. Grounding therefore preserves the distinction between latent environment state and evidence available to the acting policy. These checks implement specific requirements for visible provenance; they do not prove that every admitted action is uniquely determined by its visible history or verify every possible reason for choosing it.  

> C.2 Decision and Workflow Eligibility

A supervised decision must satisfy both execution and information requirements. Execution validity asks whether the action is admissible under the native environment semantics; decision grounding asks whether its choice is supported by the  

> 18 ANT INTERNATIONAL RESEARCH Trajectory2Gym

policy-visible history. Neither condition subsumes the other. This distinction is especially important for successful trajectories. Trajectory-level success certifies an episode outcome, but does not imply that every intermediate action is an appropriate imitation target. A successful action that depends on collector-private information remains ungrounded, while a grounded decision may occur only after several observations or clarifications have accumulated. For tasks with ordered state changes, local eligibility is supplemented by workflow consistency. Required operations must appear in the realized interaction in an order compatible with the task. Information gathering, user clarification, and recovery may occur between those operations, but a rejected or masked action cannot satisfy a required workflow step simply because the trajectory eventually succeeds. The resulting supervision therefore distinguishes three levels: trajectory success , which concerns completion of the interaction; execution validity , which concerns whether an individual action is accepted by the environment; and decision grounding , which concerns whether that action is justified by information available when it is taken. Only decisions satisfying the relevant levels contribute imitation loss. Native environments occasionally provide action-level validation without a corresponding observation. Such validation can authorize the action as a target, but does not create an unobserved action–observation transition.  

> C.3 Failure as Evidence for Recovery

An action need not be a valid imitation target to contribute useful information to the interaction. This is particularly important for recovery. When an assistant action fails, the failed action itself receives no imitation loss. If the action and its authentic error response are visible through the native interface, however, they remain part of the policy-visible history. A later recovery decision can then be supervised from the richer context created by that feedback. The learner therefore observes the consequence of the failed attempt without being trained to reproduce the failure. Repeated actions are treated according to the same principle. A repeated read after a state transition may reveal new information and support a new decision, even when its serialized action matches an earlier call. Likewise, a repeated operation may be required by the task after the environment state has changed. Surface repetition alone is therefore insufficient to identify redundancy. By contrast, a repeated action that contributes no new evidence, state change, or required operation provides no additional compositional support. The distinction is semantic: what matters is whether the repeated action changes the information or state on which subsequent decisions depend. This treatment allows one realized interaction to contain both masked failures and valid supervision for their recovery. Figure 5 provides a concrete example in Appendix G.2.

> D.2 Experience Allocation

Selection balances two complementary objectives: preserving breadth over tasks and compositional structures, and providing repeated support for structures that remain under-represented. We begin from a fixed certified core shared across training views. Selection then proceeds hierarchically. Task coverage preserves completing demonstrations across the available training tasks; structural coverage adds interactions that expose previously uncovered capability atoms and canonical paths; finally, support expansion allocates additional interactions toward paths with positive deficit δs. This ordering prevents repeated exposure to frequent structures from displacing broad task and capability coverage. Within each stage, interactions that provide comparable structural support are distinguished by secondary criteria. Task-level selection favors concise, completing demonstrations, while structural selection favors interactions that add visible capability content with limited redundant supervision. Recovery and branching are retained when they contribute additional interaction structure rather than merely increasing trajectory length. The resulting procedure is deliberately independent of downstream evaluation outcomes. Benchmark identity, evaluation score, teacher identity, and test-task identity are not used as selection features. Likewise, negative log-likelihood under the public initialization does not determine which interactions are retained. Training-set composition therefore emerges from task breadth, compositional coverage, and structural-support deficits rather than from benchmark-specific quotas or difficulty scores. Coverage closes before repeated support is added. Within each support level, the queue is built once, giving each eligible under-supported path an opportunity before advancing to the next level. Allocation stops after the requested levels have been processed. Depth is a support target, not a hard upper bound: retained interactions are never removed to reduce an over-supported path, and candidate availability or the task cap can leave another path below the target. For successive depths on the same corpus, previously selected interactions remain selected. The emitted view is ordered by task, path, and trace key; rendering and parent-normalized training are applied afterward.

> F.3 Matched Component Controls

The main-text ablation in Table 3 reports four matched Qwen3-8B comparisons. Because the interventions operate on different stages of the construction procedure, each comparison has its own matched reference; the resulting gains are therefore not an additive decomposition of the full-method improvement.  

> Targeted expansion.

The reference uses the existing certified interaction corpus, while the proposed condition adds experience synthesized around under-supported structures before applying the same downstream selection and training procedure. This comparison changes candidate supply and therefore does not hold the training-token budget fixed.  

> Decision grounding.

The semantic interactions and policy-visible histories are held fixed, while the set of supervised assistant decisions changes. The comparison therefore isolates whether success-derived targets are filtered by decision-time evidence.  

> Structural selection.

Both conditions draw from the same eligible interaction corpus and retain the same task and capability anchors, 14,819 semantic interactions, and target-token budget. Structural allocation covers 6,360 canonical paths, compared with 5,418 under random surplus selection.  

> Parent normalization.

The selected semantic interactions, rendered examples, target masks, and sample order are held fixed. Only the aggregate optimization mass assigned to each semantic interaction changes.  

> F.4 Structural Coverage under a Matched Budget

The structural-selection control connects experience allocation with structural coverage. At the same budget of 14,819 semantic interactions and matched target-token mass, structural selection covers 6,360 canonical paths, compared with 5,418 under random surplus selection: an increase of 942 paths. The corresponding performance change is concentrated on the stateful Tau benchmarks. As reported in Table 3, structural selection increases Tau1 and Tau2 while slightly decreasing BFCL, yielding a 2.32-point gain in the equal-weight three-benchmark mean. Together, these results suggest that the allocation rule primarily changes which stateful interaction structures are represented, rather than simply increasing the amount of supervision.  

> F.5 Support-Depth Sensitivity

Table 4 varies the structural-support depth at fixed Qwen3-8B. Changing depth alters both the number of retained interactions and the amount of supervised target mass, so this experiment should be interpreted as sensitivity to the resulting training allocation rather than as an isolated causal effect of repeated path exposure. The three-benchmark mean is highest at depth two and decreases as additional support is allocated. Tau2 accounts for most of this decline, while BFCL remains comparatively stable. The result reinforces the role of support depth as an allocation parameter: more repeated support is not automatically better. The support schedule used in the scaling study was specified independently of these sensitivity outcomes.
