# Causal DAG-Guided Reinforcement Learning for Long-Term Physical Activity Maintenance: Using Prior Data to Inform Future Deployment

Abstract 

Digital health interventions can provide scalable, personal-ized support for physical activity (PA), yet sustaining PA over the long-term remains difficult. Because affective experiences with PA are important for activity sustainment, we devel-oped an online reinforcement learning (RL) system for the forthcoming ADAPT factorial trial that personalizes twice-daily activity suggestions aimed at improving affective as-sociations with PA. This setting poses several challenges: participant-specific data are scarce relative to the candidate state space, important states and rewards are partially ob-served, interventions are frequent while the primary outcome is measured weekly, and activity suggestions can both improve PA-related experiences and increase intervention burden. We address these challenges using an expert-specified causal di-rected acyclic graph as an integrated RL design tool, guiding state construction, estimation of partially observed states, de-layed credit assignment, and representation of beneficial and adverse intervention pathways. We further construct a longi-tudinal simulator calibrated from a prior micro-randomized trial to compare candidate RL designs under realistic state evolution, missingness, and participant heterogeneity before deployment. Together, this work illustrates how causal do-main knowledge and prior trial data can make long-horizon, participant-specific RL more practical for digital health inter-ventions targeting sustained behavior change.

## 1 Introduction 

Digital health interventions (DHIs) offer a scalable way to de-liver personalized support for physical activity (PA) (Bi et al. 2024; Klasnja et al. 2019). However, maintaining long-term PA behavior remains difficult: interventions may increase ac-tivity in the short term without necessarily supporting long-term maintenance (Amagai et al. 2022). One potential deter-minant of maintenance is how individuals feel about physical activity. Affective associations with PA—automatic affective reactions to the thought of being active and cognitive atti-tudes about liking or disliking activity—can shape future be-havioral choices (Brand and Ekkekakis 2018; Stevens et al. 2020), and more positive affective associations have been as-sociated with greater subsequent PA participation (Williams et al. 2008; Rhodes and Kates 2015). These findings motivate intervention strategies that target not only the amount of PA performed, but also the affective experience associated with being active (Simonton 2021; Choi et al. 2025). The forthcoming 9-month ADAPT factorial trial, sched-uled to begin in fall 2026, aims to improve participants’ affective associations with PA through a multicomponent DHI. Among participants randomized to the ADAPT-on con-ditions 1, an online reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm (Sutton, Barto, and Barto 1998) will personalize the delivery of activity suggestions at two decision times per day. The RL algorithm uses data from wristband activity tracker, the HeartSteps physical activity mHealth app, and self-reported surveys for learning and decision-making. The primary out-come optimized by the RL algorithm is the core affective ex-perience (CAE) subscale of the Affective Experiences (AF-FEXX) scale (Ekkekakis, Zenko, and Vazou 2021), a weekly measure of how participants feel about being physically ac-tive. Thus, the algorithm must learn when to deliver activity suggestions at frequent decision times in order to improve an outcome that is observed and evolves on a much slower, weekly timescale. Designing and deploying a participant-specific online RL algorithm in this setting raises several interconnected chal-lenges. First, participant-specific data are scarce relative to the potentially large state space. Each participant provides only a limited number of interactions with the RL algo-rithm, while wristband activity tracker, app, and survey data provide many candidate state variables. Learning a high-dimensional decision rule from scratch can therefore lead to high estimation variance and poor performance early in deployment (Liao et al. 2020; Gazi et al. 2026). Second, im-portant state information may be partially observed. Sensor measurements can be delayed or unavailable, and participants may not complete all surveys, resulting in missing or delayed state variables and outcomes (Seewald et al. 2019). Third, more intervention is not necessarily better. Although activity suggestions may improve affective associations with PA, fre-quent or poorly timed suggestions may increase intervention burden or reduce perceived utility, producing adverse effects that may only become apparent later (Nahum-Shani et al.

An effective policy must therefore account for both the potential benefit of an activity suggestion and its possi-ble downstream costs. Finally, the reward is sparse and de-layed. Activity suggestions can be delivered twice daily from Monday through Saturday, resulting in 12 decision times as-sociated with only one weekly CAE reward. This creates a difficult credit-assignment problem: the RL algorithm must learn which earlier actions contributed to the eventual weekly outcome (Arjona-Medina et al. 2019). We use an expert-specified causal directed acyclic graph (DAG) to incorporate domain knowledge into the RL system and address these challenges in a principled manner. The DAG guides four linked design choices. First, it informs state construction by identifying which aspects of the observed history are relevant for future states and outcomes, allowing us to build lower-dimensional state summaries rather than in-clude every available sensor, app, and survey variable (Gao et al. 2025; Xu, Maasch, and Gan 2026). Second, it speci-fies how latent weekly CAE and perceived utility relate to observed survey, sensor, and engagement measures, moti-vating the models used to estimate these states when direct measurements are missing or incomplete. Third, the DAG represents both a beneficial pathway to CAE-assessed affec-tive associations through anticipated affect and a potentially adverse pathway through perceived utility. This allows the RL design to account for both the intended effects of activ-ity suggestions and their possible delayed effects on future engagement and outcomes. Finally, the DAG helps address sparse and delayed rewards by identifying intermediate vari-ables along the pathways from activity suggestions to future CAE, which provide structured information for delayed credit assignment and reward redistribution across within-week de-cisions (Arjona-Medina et al. 2019; Zou et al. 2025). Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 1. We develop a participant-specific online RL framework that uses an expert-specified causal DAG to guide state construction, latent-state estimation, representation of benefit and burden pathways, and delayed credit assign-ment in a sparse-data digital health setting. 2. We construct an ADAPT simulation testbed calibrated using data from a prior micro-randomized trial (MRT). It reproduces the key decision schedule, longitudinal feed-back pathways, and observation processes of the forth-coming ADAPT trial, enabling evaluation of candidate RL variants before deployment. 

## 2 Related Work 

Our work is related to three streams of research: 

Online RL in digital health. Online RL has increasingly been deployed to personalize DHIs, including interventions for physical activity (Yom-Tov et al. 2017; Zhou et al. 2018; Liao et al. 2020; Aguilera et al. 2024), medication adherence (Lauffenburger et al. 2024), chronic pain (Piette et al. 2022), cannabis use (Ghosh et al. 2024), oral health (Trella et al. 2025), and dietary behavior (Gonzalez et al. 2026). Most deployed systems use contextual bandits targeting relatively proximal outcomes, although some account for longer-term effects through engineered state features, reward adjustments, or nonstationary dynamics (Liao et al. 2020; Mintz et al. 2020; Baek et al. 2025). More closely related, Pulick et al. (2026) explicitly model a dynamic engagement state and plan over its future consequences. ADAPT combines several additional long-horizon chal-lenges: multiple within-week decisions share a weekly out-come, key weekly states are partially observed, and ef-fects may propagate across weeks through distinct affective-associations and perceived-utility pathways. 

Causal RL. Causal structure has been used to improve se-quential decision-making by reducing exploration complex-ity (Lattimore, Lattimore, and Reid 2016; Lu et al. 2020; Lu, Meisami, and Tewari 2022), restricting policy search (Zhang 2020), constructing state representations (Huang et al. 2022; Xu, Maasch, and Gan 2026), and estimating long-term treat-ment effects from short-term data (Tran, Bibaut, and Kallus 2024). Closest to our setting, Gao et al. (2025) use an expert-specified DAG to construct Markovian states for multiple decisions sharing a delayed reward, while Gao et al. (2026) use causal structure and belief-state modeling for partially observed states. Causal approaches have also addressed de-layed credit assignment through latent reward redistribution (Zhang et al. 2023) and counterfactual Shapley attribution (Li, Bareinboim et al. 2026). Our work uses a single expert-specified DAG to jointly guide state construction, partially observed-state estima-tion, representation of beneficial and adverse pathways, and mediator-based credit assignment in a participant-specific online RL system. 

Pre-deployment evaluation. Simulation testbeds in-formed by historical data are commonly used to evaluate digital health RL algorithms before deployment(Liao et al. 2020; Trella et al. 2022; Mintz et al. 2020; Ghosh et al. 2024). Related model-based approaches estimate participant dynamics from behavioral-intervention data and use the re-sulting models for closed-loop simulation and robustness analysis (El Mistiri et al. 2023, 2025). More recently, Pulick et al. (2026) used an MRT-calibrated hierarchical dynami-cal model to generate synthetic patients for evaluating long-horizon RL algorithms, while Xu et al. (2025) constructed a simulator from clinical data to evaluate multi-agent RL before an upcoming medication-adherence trial. The ADAPT simulator extends this line of work by model-ing multi-timescale states, mediators, outcomes, and obser-vation processes, enabling pre-deployment evaluation under delayed rewards, participant heterogeneity, and incomplete observations.

The benefit pathway represents the hypothesized effects of an activity suggestion on anticipated affect and physical activity, which may improve CAE at the end of the week. The burden pathway represents the possibility that repeated or poorly timed suggestions adversely affect engagement-related me-diators M Ew, · and perceived utility Ew+1 , with downstream consequences for later CAE-related mediators M Yw+1 ,· and CAE Yw+2 . Because the weekly states evolve recursively, these effects may propagate further into the future. Incorpo-rating summaries of both pathways into the state and value-learning design allows the RL system to account for delayed benefits and more delayed intervention costs rather than op-timizing only proximal responses. 

Temporal structure and delayed credit assignment. Fig-ure 1 highlights two sources of delayed credit. First, 12 within-week w decisions share a single weekly CAE out-come Yw+1 . Second, effects operating through perceived utility Ew+1 require an additional week before affecting CAE Yw+2 . The DAG also identifies two forms of temporal structure that can be exploited for learning. First, Yw and 

Ew form a low-dimensional weekly bottleneck state between consecutive weeks: effects of earlier actions and mediators on later weeks propagate through these weekly states. Second, the within-week mediators provide intermediate information along the pathways from activity suggestions to the weekly states and outcomes. These structures motivate three modifications to reward design. First, the bottleneck-value variant introduces an aux-iliary week-level value function V (bbw, ebw.bew), providing a direct value bridge between within-week decisions and fu-ture weeks. Its Bayesian formulation allows historical MRT data to initialize learning while re-taining randomized exploration as participant-specific data accumulate. ## 4 Pre-Deployment Evaluation 

We use data from the prior ADAPT micro-randomized trial to construct an interactive simulation environment for com-paring candidate RL algorithms before deployment in the forthcoming factorial trial. 

4.1 The ADAPT MRT Data 

The ADAPT MRT closely aligns with the forthcoming facto-rial trial in intervention structure and data collection: activity suggestions are delivered twice daily, weekly surveys mea-sure CAE and perceived utility, and additional information is collected through daily surveys, wristband activity tracker, and app use. Some variables differ across studies; for exam-ple, step count is used as a surrogate for active minutes when the corresponding factorial trial variable is unavailable in the MRT. A complete mapping between MRT and factorial trial variables is provided in Appendix A. We use MRT data available through July 26, 2026. Among 43 participants who completed the 12-week active phase, eight were excluded based on prespecified data-availability criteria involving wearable and survey/CAE measurements. An additional seven were excluded based on prespecified CAE ceiling criteria, because their persistently high CAE made them less representative of the population for whom the RL intervention is intended. This yielded 28 participants for simulator construction. Details are provided in Supplement Section 1. The MRT serves two roles: estimating the data-generating models for the simulator and constructing informative priors for participant-specific RL. During pre-deployment evalua-tion, these priors are constructed in a leave-one-participant-out manner so that the simulated participant’s own MRT data are not used for initialization. 

4.2 Simulation Environment Design 

The simulator generates policy-dependent participant trajec-tories using three interacting components: a CAE-related process, a perceived-utility process, and additional time-varying state variables. We summarize the resulting deploy-ment choices and remaining limitations. 

CAE-related process. This process includes weekly CAE 

Yw, weekly short CAE eYw, daily anticipated affect M Y,AA w,d ,and post-decision next-four-hour step count M Y,SC w,d,t . We model these variables with Bayesian hierarchical linear mod-els that partially pool across participants to stabilize estima-tion and retain participant heterogeneity. 

Perceived-utility process. This process includes latent weekly perceived utility Ew, weekly survey completion Jw,weekly app-usefulness and helpfulness measurements Uw, 1

and Uw, 2, wristband activity tracker wear M E,F W w,d , daily sur-vey completion M E,P J w,d , and next-four-hour app page views 

M E,P V w,d,t . We jointly model these variables with a participant-specific state-space model, treating Ew as a latent state in-ferred from its observed mediators and emissions. 

Additional time-varying state variables. The simulator also models recent step count CSC w,d,t , recent interaction with activity suggestions CAS w,d,t , and daily activity status CAD w,d .These processes are estimated separately for each participant using ridge-penalized regression. To carry temporal dependence remaining in the fitted-model residuals into the simulator, we generate participant-specific residual sequences with an AR(1)-style bootstrap based on the fitted residuals. For alternative testbed variants, we retune the fitted en-vironment so that mean STE equals Cohen’s conventional small (0.2) and large (0.8) values (Cohen 2013), by strength-ening the burden and beneficial pathways, respectively. Full model specifications and simulation procedures are provided in Appendix E. 

4.3 Experiments and Results 

We conduct two sets of experiments. We first validate the simulator against observed MRT trajectories and then use it to compare candidate RL variants under the planned 36-week factorial trial setting. RL performance is evaluated by cumulative CAE over the study horizon. 

Simulator Validation We assess simulator fidelity by comparing simulated and observed MRT trajectories. For each participant and process, we calculate the proportion of observed MRT measurements that fall within the pointwise 95% simulation interval across 100 simulated trajectories. We then average these proportions equally across the 28 participants. For perceived-utility-related mediators, the re-ported value is the average across app page views, Fitbit wear, and daily survey completion. ## 5 Discussion 

This work develops a causal-DAG-guided RL system for the forthcoming ADAPT factorial trial and evaluates candidate designs in an MRT-calibrated simulator. 

5.1 Path to Deployment 

The pre-deployment experiments inform the final RL config-uration. We select a cross-week discount factor of γ = 0 .9

and use potential-based shaping combined with learned re-ward redistribution to provide denser within-week learning signals. Because participant-specific data are limited early in the study, the deployed algorithm will be initialized using 

Figure 2: Cumulative 36-week CAE for each policy relative to never send, paired by experiment and participant draw. Panels are the three testbed environments. Dots are means over 200 simulated trials of 75 participants; error bars are 95% confidence intervals. informative priors estimated from all eligible ADAPT MRT participants. Deployment requires safeguards for missing or delayed data and failed weekly updates. Each decision uses only information available at that time; later-arriving measure-ments are incorporated only into subsequent state estimation and model updates. Monday-night updates include CAE and perceived-utility estimation, empirical-Bayes variance up-dates, RLSVI, and, when applicable, reward redistribution. Prespecified validity checks and fallback rules retain the most recent validated component when an update fails. Deployment will be supported by systematic monitoring of data availability and latency, state construction, weekly model updates, action probabilities, and fallback events, en-abling both real-time detection of operational failures and post-trial assessment of how data quality and algorithm re-liability affected policy behavior (Trella et al. 2026; Ghosh et al. 2025). 

5.2 Limitations and Future Work 

Several limitations remain. First, although the simulator ex-plicitly models missing survey responses, it does not fully reproduce delayed availability of wristband activity tracker, survey, and app data; incorporating realistic data-latency processes would enable a more stringent robustness assess-ment. Furthermore, the system relies on the working causal DAG and associated models, so sensitivity analyses and post-deployment model checking will be important for assessing robustness to structural misspecification.

A.1 ADAPT Factorial Trial Design 

The ADAPT factorial trial will enroll n = 150 cardiac rehabilitation patients, each followed for approximately 36 weeks. The study uses a 2 × 2 factorial design defined by two baseline-randomized intervention factors: ADAPT and Intrinsic Motivation (IM), with participants remaining in their assigned conditions throughout the study. Because RL is used only in the ADAPT-on condition, approximately half of the participants ( n ≈ 75 ) will receive RL-based personalization. The IM factor may affect the intervention context and relevant state variables, but its role is outside the scope of this paper. Before online RL begins, participants pass through two initial phases: a baseline phase and an initial micro-randomization phase. The baseline phase lasts approximately one week but may be extended until sufficient Fitbit data are collected. During this phase, participants complete a baseline check-in containing the 12-item CAE measure and the 2-item perceived-utility measure. Let Y0 denote the mean of the 12 CAE items; Y0 is fully observed for all participants. Physical-activity data are also collected once participants begin wearing the Fitbit. For notational convenience, we refer to the baseline phase as week 0 and the initial micro-randomization phase as week 1, although their calendar durations may be longer. The baseline phase ends after the participant accumulates at least seven days with at least eight hours of Fitbit wear per day. The participant then enters week 1, during which activity-suggestion actions are randomized with probability 0.5.

A.2 ADAPT MRT Design 

The ADAPT micro-randomized trial (MRT) evaluates components of the HeartSteps mobile health intervention for supporting physical activity in cardiac rehabilitation patients. Participants receive the intervention for approximately three months while wearing a Fitbit activity tracker and interacting with the HeartSteps app. Several intervention components are repeatedly randomized during the MRT. Activity suggestions are randomized twice daily with probability 0.5, while morning salience messages and planning prompts are each randomized once daily with probability 

0.5. Other intervention components, including weekly reflection and planning activities, continuously available in-app features, and daily and weekly ecological momentary assessments, are delivered without micro-randomization. The MRT collects longitudinal data on physical activity, intervention exposure, psychosocial measures, survey completion, and app engagement. These data are particularly relevant to the present work because the activity-suggestion decision schedule and many of the measured variables closely correspond to those used by the RL system in the forthcoming trial. We therefore use the MRT data both to estimate the data-generating models underlying the pre-deployment simulation environment and to construct informative priors for participant-specific RLSVI. 

A.3 MRT-to-Factorial Trial Variable Correspondence 

Some variables required by the future factorial trial RL system were measured differently in the MRT. Table 2 summarizes the primary mappings from future trial target variables to their MRT-based surrogates used for simulator construction and RL initialization. 

A.4 Data Preprocessing and Standardization 

As of July 26, 2026, the ADAPT MRT had 43 completed participants, defined as participants with an active-phase span of at least 83 days (the planned 84-day window, allowing a one-day shortfall). Eight of these 43 participants were excluded from the analysis sample using a priori availability rules: four (112, 117, 138, and 219) lacked sufficient wearable data for the primary proximal outcome (fewer than 20 wear-valid decision windows or fewer than 20 non-missing next-four-hour step counts), and five (82, 112, 251, 257, and 259) had no observed weekly surveys, and/or no observed daily end-of-day surveys; one participant (112) met both criteria. An additional seven participants were excluded based on prespecified CAE ceiling criteria: their minimum weekly CAE over the 12-week active phase was at least 6, or their week-1 CAE was 7. After these exclusions, 28 participants remained for fitting the environment models. To improve numerical stability and place continuous predictors on comparable scales, we transformed the merged longitudinal panel prior to fitting the simulation models. Calendar variables, such as study week, were linearly rescaled into centered, unitless covariates ranging approximately within [−1, 1] . The time-of-day indicator is coded as tn = 0 for the morning decision time and tn = 1 for the afternoon decision time. Count-valued variables (step counts) were log-transformed via x⋆ = log( x + 1) to reduce skewness and the influence of extreme values. Page views were also treated as counts but were modeled using hurdle models because of their high proportion of zeros. Continuous variables were subsequently standardized as z = ( x⋆ − μ)/σ , where μ and σ denote the mean and standard deviation computed from the analysis sample for the corresponding variable. The derived centering and scaling constants, along with the empirical support limits, were retained to ensure reproducibility, facilitate clipping of simulated values when necessary, and enable online standardization during the forthcoming trial. Historical MRT data initialize the learner, while subsequent updates use only data accumulated from the trial participant.

MRT data are used to construct informative priors for the particle-filtering working models, the Q-function, and the credit-assignment (Stage-1/Stage-2) models. All priors are Gaussian, ξ ∼ N (μξ, 0, Σξ, 0), and are built by one generic two-stage empirical-Bayes scheme; only the response and design differ across models. 

Generic construction. First, all participants’ MRT rows are stacked and a single ridge regression is fit, yielding the pooled coefficient vector θpool , which becomes the prior mean. Second, the same regression is fit separately to each participant’s rows, yielding per-participant estimates θu and residual variances σ2

> u

. The prior covariance is diagonal, with each element equal to the across-participant variance of the corresponding coordinate of θu — so coefficients that vary strongly between people are weakly constrained, and vice versa — and the noise variance is initialized at the average per-participant residual variance. One exception to diagonality: the joint (α, β ) prior of the bottleneck variant uses the full sample covariance of the per-participant 

(αu, β u), preserving cross-block correlations. For the four particle-filter working models (four-hour step count, anticipated affect, weekly CAE, short CAE) the regressions are the working models themselves on the MRT panel. For the Stage-1 and Stage-1b decompositions, the regressions are (10) and its slot-level analogue on the MRT weeks. For the Stage-2 redistribution model, the response is the shaped weekly target and the design is the week-summed features of (12). Q-function priors cannot be read off a supervised fit; they are constructed by offline fitted-Q iteration (25 iterations per participant) on the MRT trajectories, run separately for each discount setting (¯γ ∈ { 0.5, 0.9}) and for each reward construction, so that every RL variant receives a prior matched to the objective it optimizes: the weekly-CAE target for the base variants, the potential-shaped target for V4, and the redistributed slot rewards for the V5 variant. 

Deployment regime. At evaluation, the policy-side prior means (Q-function and Stage-1 decompositions) are re-centered at zero with covariance inflated tenfold (diagonal floored at 0.1), while the particle-filter and Stage-2 priors retain their MRT-informed centers. 

Held-out evaluation. During pre-deployment evaluation on the testbed, all priors supplied to a simulated participant are refit on the MRT data excluding that participant (28 leave-one-out folds), so no participant’s own historical data informs their priors. For the eventual trial, all eligible MRT data will be used.

C.6 Weekly Update Schedule 

Week 1 is an initial micro-randomization phase in which activity suggestions are assigned independently with probability 0.5.The first online RL update occurs on Monday night of week 2. Thereafter, each week follows the same schedule. The two Monday activity-suggestion decisions occur before the weekly survey response window closes and therefore use the most recently available perceived-utility estimate, CAE belief summaries, and RLSVI ensemble together with the current real-time context. After the Monday-night update, the newly estimated weekly states and RLSVI ensemble are used for the remaining decision times that week. At the Monday-night update, the algorithm: 1. constructs the perceived-utility approximation bew;2. updates the CAE belief state and computes (bbw, ebw);3. updates the required empirical-Bayes variance estimates; 4. updates any redistribution models required by the selected variant; and 5. updates the RLSVI ensemble.

## E ADAPT Simulation Environment 

We construct a closed-loop simulation testbed from the historical ADAPT MRT to evaluate candidate online RL algorithms before deployment in the ADAPT factorial trial. The environment contains three interacting components. Section E.2 models the CAE-related process, including activity-related mediators and weekly CAE. Section E.3 models latent perceived utility together with engagement-related mediators and emissions. Section E.4 models additional time-varying state variables. The fitted components are then combined sequentially to generate trajectories under a specified activity-suggestion policy, as described in Section E.6.

E.8 Simulation-Environment Variants 

The fitted MRT models define the vanilla testbed ( dSTE pop = 0 .479 ). To evaluate the algorithms under weaker and stronger intervention signal, we construct variants by rescaling fitted coefficients along prespecified causal pathways, leaving all other model components unchanged. The low-signal testbed strengthens the burden chain Aw,d,t → M Ew, · → Ew+1 → M Yw+1 ,· → Yw+2 with a single tuning constant κ: the main action effects on the three engagement mediators ( M E,P V occurrence and intensity, M E,F W , M E,P J )are shifted by −κ; the mediator-to-state transition loadings ( a2, a 3, a 4) and the state-to-mediator legs ( Ew → M Y,SC , Ew →M Y,AA ) are shifted by +κ, so that suggestions suppress engagement, suppressed engagement lowers the next week’s state, and the lowered state reaches CAE. The high-signal testbed strengthens the benefit chain Aw,d,t → M Yw, · → Yw+1 : the full action blocks of the step-count and affect models — main effects and all fitted action–state interactions — are multiplied by κ, preserving each participant’s moderation structure, and the step-count-to-CAE loading receives a small compensating shift, c(κ − 1) with c = 0 .05 .Each variant’s κ is calibrated by root finding against a fast Monte-Carlo proxy of the STE and then confirmed with the full reference-policy procedure of Section E.7, giving dSTE pop = 0 .275 (low) and 0.813 (high). Priors supplied to the online algorithms are not refit on the tuned environments, mirroring deployment, where working models are calibrated on historical data from a source environment. 

## F Simulator Validation 

We validate the vanilla simulator in two steps. We first examine fitted-model residuals to identify temporal and cross-variable dependence remaining after model fitting, and then assess whether the connected simulator reproduces the observed MRT trajectories. 

F.1 Component-Model Diagnostics 

The fitted component models are specified in Section E. Here, we assess whether their conditional means and probabilities reproduce the observed MRT data and examine temporal and cross-variable dependence remaining after model fitting. These diagnostics identify residual structure that is carried into the stochastic generator used to construct participant trajectories. 

CAE-related Gaussian model fit. For each CAE-related Gaussian component Z, we examine the participant-conditional residual bϵZi,j = Zi,j − (xZi,j )⊤ bθZi , where (xZi,j )⊤ bθZi is the fitted conditional mean for participant i at observation j. Thus, the residual represents variation remaining after accounting for the participant-specific history, actions, and other predictors included in the fitted model. Because these conditional models form the deterministic backbone of the simulator, structure remaining in their residuals indicates stochastic variation or dependence that must be represented separately during simulation. Figures 3–6 show the residuals for weekly CAE, short CAE, next-four-hour step count, and anticipated affect. Across the four components, residuals generally fluctuate around zero, suggesting little systematic level discrepancy after conditioning on the fitted predictors. Their dispersion and occasional larger deviations nevertheless differ across participants and components. We therefore next examine whether this remaining variation also contains temporal or cross-variable dependence before specifying the stochastic generator. 

Bernoulli component fit. For each Bernoulli component k, we compare the observed outcome zki,j with its participant-specific fitted probability bpki,j and define the residual as beki,j = zki,j − bpki,j . We assess mean calibration by comparing the observed event rate ¯zki with the mean fitted probability ¯pki , and probabilistic accuracy using the Brier score (Glenn et al. 1950), 

BS ki = ( nki )−1 P 

> j

(zki,j − bpki,j )2, where smaller values indicate more accurate probability predictions. Table 5 reports these diagnostics for page-view occurrence, Fitbit wear, daily and weekly check-in completion, active-day status, and suggestion interaction. The observed rates and mean fitted probabilities are closely aligned for the three perceived-utility-related mediators: the largest participant-level absolute differences are 0.011 for page-view occurrence, 0.010 for Fitbit wear, and 0.018 for daily check-in completion. Suggestion interaction is also closely reproduced, with a largest discrepancy of 

0.046 . Larger differences occur for weekly check-in completion and active-day status, primarily for participants with limited variation or few observed outcomes. Overall, the fitted Bernoulli models reproduce participant-specific event rates well for the engagement processes most directly entering the perceived-utility dynamics, while leaving residual variation to be examined for temporal and cross-variable dependence. 

Auxiliary continuous component fit. For prior-two-hour steps, positive page-view intensity, helpfulness, and pleasantness, we assess participant-specific fit using residuals defined as the transformed observed outcome minus its fitted conditional mean. We summarize residual location by the participant-specific mean residual and residual magnitude by the root mean squared error (RMSE). Table 6 reports these diagnostics for prior-two-hour steps and positive page-view intensity. Mean residuals remain close to zero across participants, ranging from −0.067 to 0.038 for prior-two-hour steps and from −0.026 to 0.034 for positive page-view intensity, although residual dispersion varies across participants. The smallest page-view errors occur for some participants with very few positive page-view observations, so they provide limited information about fit across a broader range of intensities. Table 7 shows a similar pattern for helpfulness and pleasantness, with participant-specific mean residuals ranging from −0.019 

to 0.059 and from −0.022 to 0.041 , respectively. Overall, these models show little systematic mean discrepancy while retaining participant-specific residual variation for the subsequent dependence diagnostics. 

Temporal residual dependence. After assessing conditional fit, we examine whether the fitted residuals retain serial depen-dence at the time scale of each process. For weekly components, we calculate participant-specific correlations between residuals at consecutive assessment weeks. For daily components, we examine lags of one and seven calendar days, corresponding to adjacent days and the same weekday in successive weeks. For decision-time components, we examine AM-to-same-day-PM and PM-to-next-day-AM residual correlations. Tables 8, 9, and 10 report these correlations together with the number of available residual pairs. The diagnostics show residual temporal structure beyond the fitted conditional means and probabilities. Most notably, weekly CAE residual correlations are negative for all 21 participants with reportable values, ranging from −0.728 to −0.017 . The other weekly components show more heterogeneous dependence across participants. At the daily scale, residual dependence is also evident for some participants, including a lag-one Fitbit-wear correlation of 0.554 for participant 21 and a lag-seven daily-check-in correlation of 0.370 for participant 11. Decision-time residual dependence is generally more modest for next-four-hour step count, but stronger associations remain for some auxiliary and engagement components. Overall, the direction and magnitude of residual dependence vary across participants and processes, indicating that independent residual draws would not adequately represent the remaining within-participant dynamics and motivating participant- and component-specific serial dependence in the stochastic generator. 

Cross-variable residual dependence. We next examine whether residuals from different component models remain associated after conditioning on their fitted predictors. Weekly residuals are aligned by assessment week, daily residuals by calendar day, and decision-time residuals by AM or PM occasion. We additionally compare AM next-four-hour step-count residuals with same-day PM prior-two-hour step-count residuals because their measurement windows overlap. Tables 11–13 and Tables 14–17 report the participant-specific correlations and available pair counts. The weekly and daily associations vary substantially in sign and magnitude across participants, without a common cross-variable pattern. Two decision-time relationships are more systematic. AM next-four-hour step-count and same-day PM prior-two-hour step-count residuals are positively correlated for 25 of the 27 participants with available pairs. Suggestion-interaction and page-view-occurrence residuals are positive for all 27 participants with reportable AM correlations, while the PM associ-ations are more heterogeneous. These recurring relationships indicate cross-component dependence not captured by the fitted conditional models and motivate the corresponding dependence mechanisms in the stochastic generator. 

Residual dependence in the stochastic generator. The residual diagnostics above inform the stochastic component of the simulator. For continuous streams, participant-specific residual sequences are generated with an AR(1)-style bootstrap that carries first-order serial dependence into simulated trajectories, with partial pooling when participant-specific residual information is limited. The two systematic cross-component relationships identified above are also represented explicitly during generation: AM next-four-hour step count informs same-day PM prior-two-hour step count, and suggestion interaction informs page-view occurrence. Other dependence propagates through the evolving simulated history and the shared CAE and perceived-utility states. Full generation procedures are provided in Section E.6. 

F.2 Simulator-Level Validation 

At the simulator level, we assess whether the fitted components, when linked through the evolving simulated history, reproduce the participant-specific trajectories observed in the MRT. For each of the 28 participant models, we generate 100 trajectories over MRT study weeks 2–12 using the participant’s recorded activity-suggestion sequence and matched initial history. Fitted parameters are held fixed, while generated values recursively update subsequent states and history summaries. We compare the observed MRT measurements with pointwise 95% simulation intervals defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the 100 simulated trajectories. 

Main trajectory reproduction. Figures 7, 9, and 10 compare the observed and simulated trajectories for weekly CAE, next-four-hour step count, and anticipated affect, while Figures 11, 13, and 14 show the three perceived-utility-related mediators. To summarize agreement across participants, we first calculate within each participant the proportion of observed MRT measurements that fall within the pointwise 95% simulation interval and then average these proportions equally across the 28 participants. The resulting participant-averaged coverage is 92.7% for weekly CAE, 89.9% for next-four-hour step count, and 93.8% for anticipated affect. The corresponding coverages for page-view occurrence, Fitbit wear, and daily check-in completion are 97.4%, 97.4%, and 98.4%, respectively, giving an average of 97.7% across the three perceived-utility-related mediators. The trajectory plots also show that the simulator retains substantial participant heterogeneity in outcome and mediator levels. For example, the CAE trajectories preserve the lower level of participant 18, the higher levels of participants 2 and 16, and broad declines for participants 9 and 25. The step-count and anticipated-affect trajectories similarly retain large between-participant differences in their typical levels. Agreement is less uniform for some localized changes and extreme observations, indicating that the simulator reproduces the broader participant-specific trajectory structure more consistently than every individual fluctuation. 

Supporting trajectory reproduction. The remaining trajectory diagnostics provide complementary checks on the observation and engagement processes. Figure 8 shows that short CAE largely follows the participant-level patterns reproduced for full CAE, as expected from its conditional generation model. Figure 12 shows that the simulator retains substantial heterogeneity in positive page-view intensity, although these comparisons are less informative for participants with few positive page-view occasions. Figures 15–17 similarly preserve broad differences across participants in weekly assessment completion, usefulness, and pleasantness. As with the main processes, agreement is stronger for participant-specific levels and broad longitudinal patterns than for every local change over time. Long-horizon stability. Because the candidate RL algorithms are evaluated over the planned 36-week study horizon, we also examine simulator behavior beyond the MRT observation period. We generate 100 trajectories for each of the 28 participant models under never-send, always-send, and Bernoulli (0 .5) suggestion policies, yielding 8,400 trajectories in total. Figures 18–20 show the corresponding 36-week CAE trajectories under the three policies. All 8,400 trajectories complete with finite generated outcomes. Some participant-specific boundary concentration remains, including latent perceived utility for participant 18 and low FourSC values for participant 22. We evaluate the seven RL configurations in Table 4 together with a non-learning reference policies: random send with probability 

0.5. Each policy is evaluated in the vanilla MRT-calibrated testbed and in the alternative simulation environments described in Section E.8. Each simulated participant is followed for 36 weeks. Week 1 is an initial micro-randomization period in which activity suggestions are assigned with probability 0.5. Beginning in week 2, the RL policies are updated weekly according to Algorithm 6. The non-learning reference policies use the same simulated observation process and weekly schedule but do not update from accumulating data. Within each Monte Carlo replication, all candidate policies are evaluated on the same sampled set of participant types. We additionally use common random numbers for exogenous simulator stochasticity whenever possible, so that policy comparisons are paired at the simulated-participant level. We perform 200 Monte Carlo replications for each combination of simulation environment and policy.

G.2 Additional Results 

Figure 2 of the main paper summarizes cumulative CAE but does not reveal how the learned policies differ in their action-selection behavior. We therefore examine the week-specific send probabilities for the morning and afternoon decision slots, denoted πAM and πPM .Figure 21 shows distinct policy behaviors across RL variants. Standard RLSVI with γ = 0 .9 remains close to 0.5 in both decision slots. With γ = 0 .5, the morning probability remains near 0.5, whereas the afternoon probability decreases to approximately 0.42 across all three environments, reducing the overall intervention rate. Because this morning–afternoon asymmetry changes little as the intervention signal strengthens, it does not appear to reflect learning of the amplified proximal treatment pathway. In contrast, the shaping-plus-redistribution variant primarily reallocates intervention probability across decision slots while leaving the overall intervention rate relatively stable. In the vanilla and STE ≈ 0.8 environments, the morning probability increases to approximately 0.53 and the afternoon probability decreases to approximately 0.47 , whereas little separation is observed under STE ≈ 0.2. This pattern is consistent with the cumulative-CAE results in Figure 2 of the main paper: the advantage of redistribution becomes more pronounced as the underlying intervention signal strengthens. Figure 22 illustrates the corresponding participant-specific behavior for a participant whose fitted intervention effect is stronger in the morning than in the afternoon. In this user’s fitted mediator models, a suggestion raises anticipated affect by 0.047 in the morning versus 0.007 in the afternoon; the implied fourSC action effect is likewise positive in the morning and near zero in the afternoon. Under shaping with learned redistribution, the morning send probability increases to approximately 0.71 by the end of the study, while the afternoon probability remains near 0.5. Standard RLSVI with γ = 0 .9 shows little differentiation between slots, whereas γ = 0 .5 again primarily reduces the afternoon probability. Although this single participant is illustrative, the result shows how reward redistribution can translate heterogeneous mediator signals into participant-specific temporal patterns of intervention delivery.
