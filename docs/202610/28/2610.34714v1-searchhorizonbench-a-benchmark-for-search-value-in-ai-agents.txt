## Abstract 

AI agents must decide when another search is worth its cost. Answer-success probabilities capture question difficulty, while this decision requires estimating the additional benefit of search. We introduce SearchHorizonBench, a benchmark for learning and evaluating search value from execution experience. Paired continu-ations and repeated executions separate expected search gain from generation noise and connect prediction quality to decision utility. The resource contains 45,632 executions across 3,168 questions. On 1,632 held-out questions, historical calibration reduces Brier error by 16.2%, with the improvement concentrated in success-level information. Search gains have a standard deviation of 21.4 per-centage points, and tested historical estimators explain less than 2% of this variance in point estimates. Matched-configuration learning curves probe this distinction across training scales. At the reference token price, free selection observations yield a 2.49-point utility opportunity over the best constant action, driven primarily by gain information. Reusable histories, frozen splits, strong baselines and scoring tools provide a common foundation for developing and evaluating value-aware search agents.

## 1 Introduction 

Large-scale agentic search logs document iterative query refor-mulation and lexical links to earlier retrieved context [25]. Each additional search can resolve a missing relation or entity while con-suming time and money. In Test-400, permitting search increases reference-equivalent answer success from 69.38% to 73.56% and mean actor tokens from 1,069 to 6,829. Search-value prediction identifies which questions justify that additional information cost. SearchHorizonBench defines search value as the expected reward difference between continuing with search and answering from the same public history. The actor completes a chosen continua-tion; the forecaster estimates its outcome. Three measurements describe their behavior. Action-success prediction measures cor-rectness under each continuation. Gain prediction measures their difference. Decision utility combines the selected reward with the cost of acquiring its information. Calibration captures question dif-ficulty; paired gain and cost measurements identify the information relevant to choosing further search. The benchmark connects these measurements through native paired executions. Every saved state supports both continuations,

## 2 Related Work 

Retrieval decisions and agent control. Self-RAG, FLARE, DRAGIN and Adaptive-RAG develop retrieval triggers or strategy selection [2, 15, 16, 36]. Search-R1 and R 2-Searcher train search policies with reinforcement learning [17, 47]. Stop-RAG, HALT, TASR and Agen-tic Abstention study stopping through values, verification, stability or confidence [18, 22, 30, 34]. SearchJev scores legal search decisions [4]. SearchHorizonBench supplies aligned executions for measuring future action value and the experience needed to estimate it. 

Execution-derived utility. Calibration Is Not Control studies in-tervention value through matched-prefix execution and evaluates learned controllers [46]. CoBRA learns tool-use boundaries from paired internal and external trajectories [50]. Search-G1 trains intervention-calibrated readouts of retrieval necessity and evidence reliance [5]. Tian et al. predict answer quality and changes across ob-served retrieval iterations [37], and Wu et al. model tool necessity, utility, and affordability [42]. Bridge Evidence measures a docu-ment’s contribution to later agent actions [24]; reader-specific util-ity research examines how evidence preferences and intervention effects transfer [49]. Matched revision studies separate re-solving, scaffolding and draft content [28]; repeat-aware QA audits separate source-induced answer changes from within-source variation [26]. SkillLearnBench evaluates experience-derived agent skills at con-tent, trajectory and task-outcome levels [48]. SearchHorizonBench measures prospective action-value prediction and historical reuse with repeated outcomes and matched historical baselines. 

Budgets, configuration selection, and calibration. Budget-aware control and active-retrieval evaluation account for information costs [11, 23, 32, 43]. Evaluation-budget studies allocate questions, trajectories and repeated answers to improve precision [27]. DRAG selects retriever–generator configurations [1]. Pandora’s Router and stage-aware pruning price value estimation and context use [12, 19]. Search as Computation Allocation formalizes costly in-formation acquisition [40]. THERMOMETER and CALIBFORMER transfer probability calibration across tasks and models [10, 35]. Our statistical tools build on treatment-effect estimation, query-performance prediction, calibration and U-statistics [3, 6, 14, 20].

A state 𝑥 is the complete public interaction history available to the agent, including tool responses and returned reasoning text. A checkpoint is a saved state at a legal action boundary. At each checkpoint, Think reasons with existing evidence and returns an answer. Search may retrieve further evidence before answering. ## 4 Benchmark and Evaluation 4.1 Questions, histories, and transfer settings 

Test-400 contains 120 development and 400 test questions, balanced across HotpotQA bridge and comparison questions and MuSiQue questions requiring two, three, or four reasoning hops [38, 45]. Sup-porting Wikipedia pages are disjoint between development and test. We save the initial state and states reached after the first and second actual search. Test counts at these checkpoints are 400, 377, and 171. Later checkpoints describe the population of agents that reaches them; repeated-question comparisons preserve that dependence. Test-1632 contains 1,632 questions with reference-support screening before execution. It includes 400 questions in each of the first four strata and all 32 eligible four-hop questions. Each stratum receives weight 0.2. The frozen source map uses the original 120 Tavily-search questions; target execution uses DeepSeek search. A separate 40/400 development/test study covers Natural Questions queries [21]. The benchmark also varies completion budgets of 8,192 or 16,384 tokens and search limits of three or six. The component study uses 128 development and 320 test ques-tions at a real pending query. Flash and Pro each execute STOP, DeepSeek search, and Serper search. STOP cancels the pending call and answers; the two search actions execute its query and then answer. Four repetitions produce 10,752 executions. A shared STOP execution supports both backend comparisons. ClueWeb22 and FineWeb supply two additional indexed-source conditions on 32 development and 64 test states [7, 29, 31]. These comparisons pre-serve the query and limit each returned packet to five documents with at most 1,500 characters each. A collection study uses 32 development and 96 test tasks from DeepSearchQA [13]. The actor must return a set of requested items. We compare stopping, executing the pending query on the same source, and switching the query to another source. The reward is set F1, the harmonic mean of precision and recall against curated reference items. Four repetitions of three actions yield 1,536 out-comes. The resource contains 45,632 distinct executions and 8,880 forecasts across 3,168 distinct questions; shared executions count once. Appendix A provides the inventory.

## 5.3 The gain-prediction gap persists with more training 

The same-configuration experiment controls training scale and backend mismatch. It predicts held-out Test-1632 initial states us-ing 20–1,100 training questions from the same Flash actor and DeepSeek search configuration. Raw-score polynomial ridge, public-feature ridge and frozen MiniLM text ridge directly regress the observed gain. Three training-only folds choose regularization; a mean guard also evaluates selecting a constant predictor. Four outer component folds and ten nested samples produce separate fits at every training budget. Public-feature ridge improves from −1.67% gain 𝑅 2 at 120 questions to +0.72% at 1,100, with final interval [− 1.78 , 3.09 ]%. Raw-score ridge reaches +0.27%, and the guarded text model selects the mean at the largest budget. Figure 4 shows the full curves. The tested families remain near the constant-gain refer-ence across these matched-configuration training budgets. The curves establish a common learning-scale reference for evaluating richer decision signals. 

## 5.4 Gain information drives price-sensitive opportunity 

The decision factorial separates the information needed for price-sensitive search. Its four rules use fixed or query-specific gain esti-mates, crossed with fixed or query-specific token estimates. Query-specific values use two selection draws per action; the other two draws evaluate the choice. Fixed values come from forty separate same-configuration development questions. All four rules use the same held-out reward and cost, so their comparison identifies the value of the information supplied for selection. Before charging probes, the selected continuation saves 3,451 tokens relative to always SEARCH. The probes consume 2,210, leaving 1,241 net tokens saved at a 1.87-point success cost. ## 5.6 A common benchmark spans execution settings 

The broader resource tests the same quantities under different agent configurations. Indexed retrieval yields Brier 0.1417 on ClueWeb and 0.1498 on FineWeb, against historical means 0.1982 and 0.1981. Qwen actors and forecasters add a separate model family. Budget interventions distinguish evidence effects from generation capac-ity; increasing the search limit from three to six at 8k tokens raises length failures by 2.5 points, while a 16k budget removes that in-crease. Collection-completion tasks use bounded set F1 and three actions. Their selected mean-gain predictor and constant STOP policy provide reward-specific references. These settings extend the benchmark’s success, gain and decision measurements while retaining each execution and reward definition. 

## 6 Using the Benchmark 

SearchHorizonBench provides public histories and action defini-tions for new forecasters. Researchers fit their models on develop-ment outcomes, then submit action-success probabilities or signed gain predictions for held-out states. Evaluation uses separate out-come records and supporting-page groups. The value scorer reports success error and generation-noise-corrected gain error; the policy scorer charges prediction and continuation tokens. Saved baselines, fixed priors and reusable splits support direct method comparisons. Scoring success, gain and utility on matched executions makes a forecaster’s contribution to search decisions directly testable. 

## 7 Conclusion 

SearchHorizonBench establishes a reusable evaluation of when further Web search is worthwhile. Shared histories, repeated con-tinuations and explicit costs connect success prediction, generation-noise-corrected gain estimation and decision utility. The resource spans 45,632 executions. On 1,632 held-out questions, historical calibration improves Brier error by 16.2%, with task priors account-ing for 88.9% of that change. A search-gain standard deviation of 21.4 percentage points and less than 2% explained variance in tested historical estimators establish a distinct prediction target. Matched-configuration learning curves provide controlled compar-isons across training scales. At the reference token price, factorized decisions identify a 2.49-point free-observation opportunity driven primarily by gain information. The benchmark’s public inputs, frozen splits, historical controls and scoring tools make the contribu-tion of execution experience to search decisions directly testable.
