Generative models support content creation across text, images, videos, and design disciplines, yet their interfaces rely primarily on
natural language. While effective for specifying high-level intent, language can be inefficient and imprecise for fine-grained control,
iterative refinement, and open-ended exploration. We present PolyGandr, a visual interface for exploring relationships between
latent representations and generated outputs, complementing natural-language model steering. PolyGandr formulates generative
control as high-dimensional navigation, enabling users to define starting points, steering directions, and step sizes through a unified
map-and-mixing-palette interface. Combining global exploration with local refinement supports both broad design-space navigation
and fine-grained output manipulation without repeatedly translating visual intent into prompts. We evaluate PolyGandr across three
use cases: StyleGAN2 face generation, Stable Diffusion chair design, and rotational kirigami engineering. A formative study indicates
that PolyGandr complements existing workflows by supporting real-time editing while simultaneously providing an overview and
means to explore the broader design space.

Introduction
Generative models support content creation across text, images, videos, and design disciplines, yet interfaces for steering
or editing their outputs rely primarily on natural language [40, 42]. Exploring these outputs requires users to discover
possibilities and refine promising results. A user may recognize a desirable variation in an example without knowing
how to express it as a prompt or parameter [5].
Prior interfaces support exploring generated examples, discovering and refining semnatic directions, and editing
individual outputs. Building on these capabilities, we investigate how an interface can connect the discovery of a useful
contrast with inspection and revision of the resulting transformation.
We present PolyGandr, a visual interface that connects example-based steering with global exploration and local
refinement. Users specify a starting point, define a direction through contrasting examples, and adjust a step size to
control the strength of the transformation. A global map displays sampled outputs and projects steering trajectories, and
a linked local mixing view supports users to adjust the examples contributing to the starting point or either endpoint of
the direction. Together, these views support an iterative workflow of constructing, inspecting, and revising steering
operations.
We demonstrate PolyGandr with StyleGAN2 face and cat generation, and Stable Diffusion XL chair generation. We
also explore its extension to a parametric engineering design space through an exploratory user study with a domain
expert. The demonstrations illustrate the steering workflow, while the user study identifies opportunities for combining
broad exploration with domain-specific editing and highlights the need for guidance when designs become infeasible.
Specifically, we contribute the following:
• An interaction framework for steering generative models by connecting global exploration with local refinement.
Users construct a steering operation from a base point, contrasting examples, and a step size, then inspect and
revise the examples defining that operation.

Related Work
We review two areas of related work: model steering and exploration. The first covers steering mechanisms, computa-
tional techniques, and their theoretical foundations. The second focuses on interfaces that draw on these methods to
support human-in-the-loop interactive steering and exploration.
2.1
Model Steering and Conditioning Methods
Generative-model steering can operate on latent representations, intermediate features, model parameters, conditioning
signals, or the generated output itself. Early feature or latent space methods manipulate model’s intermediate repre-
sentations directly. Deep Feature Interpolation [50] derives attribute directions from differences between groups of
examples, while GANSpace [24], InterFaceGAN [47], SeFa [48], and StyleSpace [56] identify interpretable directions in
GAN representations. LatentCLR [57] identifies interpretable directions in GAN via contrastive learning. StyleCLIP [38]
further connects such manipulation to natural-language specifications. Other methods provide more spatially explicit
control: EditGAN [28] supports semantic image editing through segmentation-based manipulation, while DragGAN [36]
enables direct point-based manipulation on the generative image manifold. These results motivate our use of simple
vector arithmetic while also highlighting that linear steering should be understood as an approximation whose validity
may vary across the latent space and model architecture.
Diffusion models support an even broader range of control mechanisms. Prompt-to-Prompt [21] manipulates
cross-attention to preserve image structure while modifying textual concepts, while SDEdit [33] uses an input image
as guidance by partially noising and denoising it under a new condition. InstructPix2Pix [7] instead learns image
editing directly from natural-language instructions. ControlNet [59] and T2I-Adapter [34] introduce explicit spatial
conditions such as pose, depth, edges, and segmentation. Other methods provide semantic guidance through attention
or diffusion trajectories [4, 6, 10]. Continuous semantic controls can also be constructed by adapting model parameters.
Concept Sliders [17] learns low-rank parameter directions using LoRA [22], while SliderSpace [18] discovers multiple
interpretable slider directions from a single prompt. AdaptiveSliders [19] further considers how such controls can be
aligned with user intent. These methods provide increasingly powerful mechanisms for defining what can be controlled.
PolyGandr is complementary: rather than introducing another mechanism for learning a semantic direction, we focus
on how users spatially construct, inspect, combine, and refine steering operations through examples.
2.1.1
Geometry of Generative Latent Spaces. Latent space steering often assumes that meaningful changes can be
represented by linear directions in a locally Euclidean space. However, generative models generally can induce non-
uniform and curved geometries. Earlier work formalized this through a pullback of Riemannian metrics induced by
generative mappings, enabling geodesic interpolation and analysis of local distortion [1, 46]. More recent work extends
this geometric view to diffusion and foundation models, showing that latent geometry can vary across timesteps

Together, these results suggest that the validity of linear latent space steering is inherently local and model dependent.
A direction that is meaningful around one point, timestep, or conditioning context may not remain geometrically or
semantically consistent elsewhere. PolyGandr therefore treats linear vector arithmetic as a simple and fast approxima-
tion. With this simplification we encourage users to interactively explore and refine, to expose where this approximation
succeeds or breaks down.
2.2
Interactive Exploration and Steering Interfaces
A parallel line of work studies how users interact with generative spaces once controllable representations are available.
GANSlider [12] exposes multiple semantic GAN directions (via PCA) through sliders and studies the effects of feedfor-
ward previews in film-strip like interface. GANzilla [15] allows users to discover directions iteratively from selected
examples, while GANravel [16] supports refining and disentangling those directions. SwipeGANSpace [35] similarly
supports efficient latent space exploration through pairwise comparison interactions. These systems show the value of
allowing users to discover and refine controls rather than requiring all semantics to be specified in advance.
Other systems emphasize broader exploration and creative navigation. SpaceSheets [31] organizes latent space
exploration through a spreadsheet-like interface, while Browsing the Latent Space [8] treats latent representations as
a space for interactive design exploration. GANCollage [54] uses generative models as an exploratory mood-board
medium for ideation. LatentZoom [32] supports multiscale visual exploration of local behavior in generative latent
spaces. Latent Space Map [61] similarly projects generative representations into a 2D map to support visual exploration
and help users locate regions containing desirable generated outputs. While this work focuses primarily on locating and
selecting promising samples, PolyGandr uses the global map as an interaction surface for constructing, visualizing, and
refining steering operations, generating unseen data with example-based local manipulation. Together, these systems
motivate treating a generative representation not only as a parameter space to optimize, but also as a landscape that
can be browsed and inspected.
For diffusion-based generation, PromptPaint [11] is particularly relevant to our interaction framing: it uses a paint-
mixing metaphor to combine text prompts and supports continuous exploration between semantic specifications.
PromptCharm [55] similarly combines multiple modalities for iterative prompt construction and refinement. These
interfaces move beyond a single text box toward richer spatial and compositional interaction with generative models.
PolyGandr builds on these directions but focuses on a different unit of interaction: navigation through example-
defined vector arithmetic. Users select or interpolate a base point, construct a steering direction from contrasting
examples, vary the step size, and inspect the resulting trajectory in a global latent space overview. Local mixing further
allows each component of this algebra to be refined through neighboring examples. Rather than assuming a predefined
editing objective, PolyGandr emphasizes free exploration: users can browse latent space structure, discover contrasts,
construct directions, and observe how those directions behave across different regions. This complements sliders and
prompt-based controls, which are particularly effective when the desired semantic manipulation is already known and
explicitly parameterized.

Interface Design Goals and Solution Guidelines
This section presents three design goals (DG) that structure PolyGandr around how users reason about and navigate
generative spaces. We first support understanding the model’s global range of outputs, then enable precise refinement
within a selected region, and finally maintain a consistent interaction metaphor across these two scales. For each goal,
we describe the underlying user need and the corresponding design solution guideline (SG) implemented in PolyGandr.
3.1
DG1: Support Orientation within the Sampled Design Space
Users need to understand what a generative model can produce under a given set of conditions before they can effectively
decide where to explore, what to refine, and how to divide work between themselves and the model. This requires
reasoning across many possible outputs rather than inspecting isolated generations one at a time. For example, when
generating images of a particular subject or style, users may want to understand the overall range of forms, materials,
compositions, or other variations that remain available under that conditioning. Such an understanding helps users
identify both the model’s capabilities and its limitations, and informs which aspects are better specified directly by the
user versus left open for model-driven exploration.
SG1: In response, PolyGandr tightly couples this need for understanding output coverage with a global overview of
the generative landscape. The interface presents sampled model outputs in a map-like view, allowing users to recognize
broad patterns, clusters, transitions, and gaps across the space. Rather than limiting exploration to the initially sampled
points, users can place and move lenses over regions of interest, inspect neighborhoods at different spatial scales, and
generate additional samples on demand around a selected region. This supports a progressive workflow in which users
first form a broad mental model of the generative space, then zoom into regions that appear relevant or promising for
more detailed exploration.
3.2
DG2: Support Precise Refinement of Examples and Steering Directions
Once users identify a promising region or output in the global landscape, they need to refine it according to preferences,
constraints, or goals that may not have been fully specified in advance. At this stage, the challenge shifts from broad
exploration to precise local control: users need to understand what variations are available around the current output
and make incremental changes without losing the characteristics that motivated their selection. Because the relevant
directions of variation can depend on the current location in the generative space, these controls should adapt to the
local context rather than rely only on globally predefined editing axes.
SG2: In response, PolyGandr provides a local mixer centered on the current point. The system samples neighboring
latent representations and exposes them through a color-mixing-palette-like interface, where users can continuously
interpolate among nearby examples using weighted combinations. This allows users to inspect locally available
variations, fine-tune the current output, and construct intermediate representations that better match their evolving
intent while remaining anchored to the selected neighborhood.
Local refinement can take different forms across domains. It may use semantic directions in StyleGAN, LoRA-based
diffusion controls, or natural-language and image-space conditioning. In addition to the color-mixing-palette-like
interface, our framework also leaves room for generic local controls grounded in the data and domain context. In
domain-specific settings, refinement can be defined directly by meaningful parameters and constraints; for rotational
kirigami design (Sec. 5), for example, we use a polygonal hub editor together with controls for target height and twist.

3.3
DG3: Promote Consistent Reasoning across Global and Local Navigation
Users need to move fluidly between broad exploration and precise refinement without having to learn fundamentally
different interaction models at each scale. In the global view, they may only know approximately where they want to
go, using visible examples in the overview as landmarks rather than specifying an explicit direction local in a latent
space. In the local view, they need finer control over nearby variations while preserving the same spatial intuition.
Maintaining a consistent interaction metaphor across these scales aims to reduce the cognitive shift between exploration
and refinement and helps users reason about both as navigation through the same generative space.
SG3: In response, PolyGandr uses the same “I want to go there” interaction paradigm in both views. In the global
view, the layout is fixed and users navigate toward existing examples or regions in the UMAP projection. In the local
view, the neighboring examples remain spatial targets, but their layout can be directly rearranged to reshape the
interpolation surface. By repositioning these neighbors, users gain finer control over the relative influence of nearby
examples and can, in principle, access any convex combination within the simplex spanned by the selected neighbors,
given sufficient adjustment of the layout.

Method
We describe the core techniques underlying model output steering in PolyGandr.
4.1
Three Components of Model Output Steering
Many model-steering techniques rely on simple vector arithmetic: starting from one latent vector, we add or subtract
a vector representing a semantic direction. Later in Sec. 5, we examine how these operations enable meaningful
transformations in real-world datasets and generative models.
4.1.1
The Base 𝑧0: Where Do We Start? As with navigating a map, latent space exploration begins from familiar
landmarks rather than raw coordinates. Each sample 𝑧𝑖= (𝑧𝑖1, . . . ,𝑧𝑖𝑀) serves as such a landmark in the high-
dimensional space. A new point can then be defined as a weighted combination of nearby samples. Varying the weights
continuously allows continuous navigation between known examples; smoothness and differentiability depend on the
chosen weighting function.

In general, steering directions can be viewed as a vector field defined over the latent space. In PolyGandr, we
simplify this formulation by defining a direction through weighted averages over neighborhoods of examples, allowing
finer-grained and potentially more disentangled steering via Eq. 1. That is, given two groups representing opposite
concepts (e.g., short versus long hair), we define a global steering direction as the element-wise vector difference
between their mean latent representations. This contrast captures the variation separating the groups and can be
applied to a new base point to steer it toward either concept.
Under this simplifying assumption, the resulting steering vector is applied globally to any base point, effectively
treating the latent space as Euclidean. This simple formulation enables real-time interaction in PolyGandr that allows
users to specify the base point, direction, and step size interactively.

Inverse-distance weighting has global support, so every reference sample contributes at every location and the
mixture changes gradually. The smoothstep and logarithmic functions have compact support controlled by the cutoff
radius 𝑅, producing a sparser and more local mixture. Smoothstep treats nearby points relatively uniformly and fades

Through this interpolation, a simple 2D movement simultaneously changes the contributions of multiple high-
dimensional latent vectors. Unlike a slider that follows a single predefined direction, the interface allows users to
continuously vary the relative influence of several examples—analogous to mixing different proportions of paint.
These choices therefore encode different properties of the navigation surface. The weighting function determines how
influence varies with distance; the cutoff radius determines the locality and sparsity of that influence; and normalization
determines whether interpolation is restricted to convex combinations or may extrapolate beyond them. Rather than
treating these choices as implementation details, they can be viewed as design parameters that determine the geometry
of the 2D navigation surface induced in latent space.

4.4.3
Global-local Consistency. The global and local views operate on the same underlying latent representations,
allowing changes made in one view to propagate to the other. In the global view, the latent codes produced by
interpolation or steering operations are projected back into the overview using the learned parametric dimensionality-
reduction mapping.
Fig. 12 summarizes the data flow through PolyGandr. The system operates across three related spaces: (0) a latent
representation suitable for interpolation and steering; (1) a representation used to construct the dimensionality-reduction
overview; and (2) the generated output space. These spaces need not coincide. In the StyleGAN case, for example, we
perform interpolation and steering in the style representation, while constructing the overview from later feature maps,
which exhibit richer structure under dimensionality reduction than the comparatively uniform style space.
The core idea of PolyGandr is to let users interact with a 2D projection while carrying out interpolation and
steering in the corresponding high-dimensional representation. User interactions in the overview therefore define
operations in space (0), while their results are projected back into space (1) for visualization and decoded into space
(2) for inspection. Mapping consistency is maintained through parametric dimensionality-reduction methods such as
Parametric UMAP [44], which can embed newly generated and interpolated points into the same learned overview
rather than being restricted to the original samples.
Likewise, after a user refines an interpolated point in the local view, the resulting latent representation can be mapped
back into the global overview to show how the refinement changes its position within the broader generative landscape.
A local refinement changes the arrangement of points in the local mixer but should preserve their original positions
in the global map to maintain the user’s mental map. We think of this as navigating with an old map after the
local surroundings have changed: the global view preserves the original reference structure, while the updated local
configuration is revealed when the user revisits that neighborhood. This separation provides a stable global frame of
reference while allowing users to reorganize local neighborhoods for more effective interpolation. Internally, both
views therefore manipulate a shared representation of each point, while maintaining view-specific spatial coordinates.
Each sampled point has a fixed global coordinate derived from the dimensionality-reduction embedding, together with
a local coordinate that may be modified through direct manipulation in the local mixer. This distinction allows users to
reorganize a neighborhood for interpolation without altering the underlying global map.
This separation can otherwise create a consistency problem. For example, if a user drags a neighboring point far away
during local refinement, they may later return expecting to find that point near its original location. Because PolyGandr
retains the original coordinates, we display such points as peripheral “ghost” anchors in the local view rather than
removing them entirely. Users can therefore recover or reposition them while retaining a stable correspondence with
the original global map. In future work, we also envision allowing a single sample to appear at multiple local positions,
supporting multiple locally configured neighborhoods while preserving a shared global identity.

features intended to improve usability during extended exploration.

helps users maintain orientation while zooming and panning across large latent space maps. Users can also drag lenses
directly within the minimap.

simultaneously. For example, moving the positive lens in steer mode can update every sample along the manipulation

5
Evaluation
We evaluate PolyGandr through two use cases involving generative models of varying complexity, together with
a formative study examining PolyGandr’s application to an engineering design task involving rotational kirigami
systems. The three evaluation cases progress in increasing complexity and application realism. StyleGAN serves as a
proof of concept and, because of its fast inference, as a testbed for runtime interaction enhancements such as real-time
neighbor sampling. The diffusion-model case demonstrates the generalizability of the PolyGandr framework to a more
complex generative architecture. Finally, the kirigami case evaluates PolyGandr in a real-world structural engineering
design setting. The polygonal design space in this use case also provides a useful testbed for examining PolyGandr
under conditions where the underlying degrees of freedom, modes and extent of variation, and global topology of the
design space are explicitly known.
5.1
Case 1: StyleGAN2 faces and cats
5.1.1
The generative model and data representation. We sampled 20, 000 points from the input space of StyleGAN2
models trained separately on human faces and cat pictures. Importantly, the representation used to build the global
map need not be the same representation used for semantic interpolation. In the StyleGAN2 case, the overview map is
constructed from sampled activations after the model’s b8 block, yielding 512×8×8-dimensional feature representations
that are projected into 2D or 3D using UMAP. In contrast, the interpolation algebra is performed in StyleGAN2’s
512-dimensional style space, obtained after its mapping network. This separation allows the overview representation to
be chosen for effective visualization while the interpolation representation is chosen for meaningful and controllable
semantic edits.
5.1.2
Use case walkthrough. We first validate the interpolation capacity of the interface by using it to construct
transitions between arbitrary pairs of data points. Using cat images as an example, we trace multiple paths from
different starting points toward regions associated with orange cats in the global overview and inspect the corresponding
generated transitions. As shown in the teaser and Fig. 13, the interface produces smooth and expected changes (turning
cats orange) along these paths, demonstrating that a simple map-based interaction can effectively control interpolation
in the underlying generative space. Importantly, the space used for interpolation and steering does not need to be the
same space used to construct the global map. The overview may be built from a dimensionality-reduced representation
of one model layer, while interpolation is performed in another latent or feature space better suited for generation.
The map therefore serves primarily as a spatial interface for selecting and visualizing trajectories, rather than as the
space in which the interpolation itself is computed. Because the overview is produced by a nonlinear projection such as
UMAP, a straight interpolation path in the underlying latent space may also appear curved or distorted when mapped
back into the overview.

a formative case study of PolyGandr on a problem from their research.
Spin-valence kirigami space frames are deployable structures cut from flat sheets [2, 3, 13]. The cut pattern defines
hub plates connected to the surrounding sheet by slender legs. During deployment, the hubs lift and rotate out of
plane of the sheet until neighboring hubs meet and can be joined, transforming the sheet into a three-dimensional,

enabling predictable deployment without prescribed fold lines. This allows structural materials such as steel sheets to
be fabricated only by cutting, transported flat, and deployed with relatively low force. Once connected, tested structures
sustained loads up to 20 times greater than the force required for deployment [14].

principle to produce structures with different shapes and mechanical properties [3, 13, 14, 41, 58]. Related cut-and-rotate

5.3.4
Findings.
Interface: Global Navigation and Local Refinement. The global exploration map changed how the expert interacted
with the design space. Rather than modifying vertices or deployment parameters one at a time, the expert could move
through the map and rapidly test substantially different polygon geometries. This made broad exploration more natural
and encouraged consideration of configurations that would otherwise require several manual edits. Once an interesting
configuration was identified in the global map, the interaction shifted toward local refinement through direct vertex
editing, thus supporting a two-stage workflow.
The 2D and 3D projections supported different aspects of this exploration. The expert initially found the 2D projection
easier for forming a mental map of the design space and recognizing geometric regions. For example, some boundary
regions contained nearly collapsed quadrilaterals approaching triangular shapes, whereas other regions contained more
evenly proportioned quadrilaterals, including square-like shapes and trapezoids (Fig. 19). The 3D view was initially
harder to interpret, but became more useful once the 2D organization was understood, providing additional spatial
context and finer control over changes such as varying the length of one side. This suggests that global navigation may
benefit from multiple complementary representations: a simpler view for orientation and a higher-dimensional view
for more detailed exploration.
For local refinement, the expert found direct vertex editing more appropriate than interpolation among neighboring
examples. Once an interesting region had been identified, the relevant question shifted from finding nearby polygons
to modifying the candidate while preserving a feasible kirigami construction. Because the polygon editor was coupled
to the geometric solver, moving a vertex changed the cut pattern and deployed geometry and could also make the unit
infeasible. Local refinement therefore became an interaction with the constraints of the physical design.
Interface: Constraint Awareness and Relaxation Guidance. The walkthrough also highlighted the importance to support
constrained exploration. When the solver could not construct a feasible unit, the interface reported infeasibility but
provided little guidance on how to recover. The expert responded by adjusting the hub’s target height and twist angles
and by modifying polygon vertices until feasibility was restored, with limited information about which change would
be most effective. Small vertex edits could often recover a feasible solution, but the recovery process still relied largely
on trial and error.

6
Discussion and Future Work
6.1
Characteristics of interpolation
Fixing the anchor points yields only a fixed 2D parameterization and therefore cannot, in general, provide full navigation
of the latent space. To characterize the maximal coverage of the interpolation scheme, we instead consider a hypothetical
setting in which users are free to place and reposition the anchors, potentially through careful and time-consuming
manual adjustment. Because the interpolation weights are normalized and nonnegative, any interpolated point lies in
the convex hull of the 𝐾anchor points, which is a (𝐾−1)-simplex when the anchors are affinely independent. Without
normalization, nonnegative weighted combinations would instead span the convex cone generated by the anchors. For
any fixed anchor configuration and weighting function, however, sweeping the 2D interface traces only a 2D subset of
this convex hull. We therefore consider the coverage of this induced 2D surface at several levels: its relationship to the
anchor-induced convex hull, to the ambient latent space, and ultimately to the semantic variation of the generated
outputs reachable through the interpolation.
Here, we highlight several desirable properties achieved by our interpolation design. Given a set of latent space
anchor points and their corresponding 2D placements, sweeping the control point across the canvas and applying
the weighted interpolation at each location induces a 2D parameterization of the high-dimensional latent space. The
properties of this parameterization depend jointly on 1) the weighting function, 2) the choice of latent space anchors,
and 3) their 2D layout. We characterize this embedding map and its induced surface into some desirable properties of an
interpolation scheme. In the next subsection of the Discussion (Sec. 6), we further consider inverse-design approaches
that directly optimize anchor layouts and weighting functions with respect to these and additional desirable properties.
Coverage. Movable reference points allow the interface to realize convex combinations within their simplex, preserv-
ing reachability of the corresponding convex region in latent space.
Continuity and Smoothness. Continuous weighting functions induce continuous navigation trajectories, while differ-
entiable weights produce smoother navigation surfaces and reduce abrupt changes in the generated output. Among the
three weighting functions considered in Eq. 4 - 6, all yield continuous interpolation, while the negative-log function
(Eq. 6) with a cutoff is not differentiable at the cutoff radius. As a result, when a neighboring anchor crosses this
Manuscript submitted to ACM


26
Li and Li et al.
boundary, its influence drops to zero with an abrupt change in rate, which can appear as a sudden change in the
“velocity” of the generated output. From the perspective of the 2D-to-latent mapping, sweeping over the interface for a
fixed reference-point layout induces a 2D surface in latent space; non-differentiable weighting functions can introduce
creases into this surface.
Reference-Point Interpolation. Inverse-distance weighting approaches exact interpolation as the control point ap-
proaches a reference point. Smoothstep weighting may not, depending on its support and normalization.
Dimension Sparsity. Finite-support weights restrict mixtures to nearby reference points, reducing local effective
dimensionality. This can support sparse representations and layouts that place correlated or co-activated latent features
near one another.
6.2
Inverse Design of Local Refinement Layout
In this work, we manually designed the interpolation and parameterization scheme underlying the interaction, extending
the intuitive notion of color mixing to high-dimensional space navigation. We demonstrate that this design satisfies
several desirable properties, including smoothness and continuity, as well as geometric constraints such as interpolation
through specified anchor points. However, manually designing an interpolation scheme can introduce implicit trade-offs
or incompatibilities among these properties, and may overlook properties that are important for particular models,
tasks, or interaction contexts. As the intrinsic dimensionality, or degrees of freedom, of the design space increases, a
single 2D or 3D local layout can no longer capture all meaningful variation. How to factorize these variations and map
them into a coherent set of navigable views therefore becomes an important interface-design challenge.
In future work, we would consider formulating this problem in the inverse direction: rather than manually specifying
the mapping, we could derive the low-to-high-dimensional interface-to-latent parameterization from an explicit set
of design desiderata. For example, what neighborhood layout is most appropriate for controlling generative models
through sparse autoencoder representations? How should the interface-to-latent mapping be reparameterized so that
points generated through the interface remain consistent with their locations when projected back into the global
overview? Is there a combination of neighborhood layout, weighting function, and interpolation scheme that produces
a more perceptually uniform landscape in the interface? Such formulations would make the trade-offs among mapping
properties explicit, allow different properties to be weighted according to task requirements, and enable more automated
construction of interaction mappings tailored to different models and use cases.
6.3
Decoupling Latent Space Structure from Navigation
So far, the simplest form of the exploration interface combines a 2D UMAP projection with a 2D mixing palette. However,
our formative study suggests that users benefit from both 2D and 3D representations of the UMAP embedding. This
distinction points to two related but separable tasks: understanding the structure and geometry of the latent space
distribution, and navigating the generative space through interaction. By decoupling these functions, the interface
can be reorganized so that dimensionality-reduction views support structural exploration, while the mixing palette
provides a consistent space for navigation and steering.

6.4
Boundary Conditions and Constraints
Our examples assume unrestricted navigation, but not every location in representation space yields a meaningful, valid,
or desirable output. Safety requirements, physical laws, fabrication limits, and domain-specific constraints may restrict
the regions users should explore.
Navigation could remain near a valid-data manifold or use model density as a soft boundary to discourage movement
far from the training distribution. Interfaces could incorporate these constraints by projecting movements onto valid
regions, adjusting steering directions near boundaries, or indicating when trajectories approach infeasible areas.
Although beyond this article’s scope, constraint-aware navigation is an important step toward practical steering
interfaces.
6.5
Non-Euclidean Curved Spaces
Our vector arithmetic assumes a locally Euclidean latent space, where steering always follows straight-line trajectories.
In practice, valid model outputs may lie on a curved manifold (Sec. 2). Straight-line interpolation can then leave this
manifold, pass through low-density “voids,” and produce poor-quality, meaningless, or unpredictable outputs. Likewise,
a direction that is meaningful locally may not remain semantically consistent elsewhere. In such spaces, navigation
may therefore require locally varying directions or manifold-following paths rather than a single global steering vector.
6.6
Beyond Latent Space
The same geometric intuition extends beyond latent representations. Some steering methods modify the model itself
rather than moving individual samples through a fixed latent space. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), for example,
represents a compact update to model parameters that can be scaled, combined, or contrasted with other updates.
Systems such as SliderSpace expose these learned modifications through continuous controls, making parameter-space
steering conceptually similar to adjusting the step size 𝛼along a latent direction.
This suggests that the framework of base + direction + step size applies more broadly than latent space editing.
The base may be a latent code, activation state, parametric model input, or model parameters; the direction may be a
learned semantic vector, activation difference, or low-rank parameter update; and the step size controls the strength of
the intervention. From an interface perspective, these different mechanisms can be expressed through a common visual
language.
7
Conclusion
We presented PolyGandr, an interaction framework and visual interface for steering generative models through
coordinated global exploration and local refinement. Rather than requiring users to begin with a well-specified editing
goal, PolyGandr allows them to discover useful contrasts from examples, construct steering operations from a base
point, direction, and step size, inspect the resulting trajectories, and refine the examples that define those operations.
Its linked global overview and local mixing view support this process at complementary scales while preserving a
consistent spatial mental model.
Across StyleGAN2, Stable Diffusion XL, and a rotational-kirigami design space, we showed how the framework can
support both generative-model exploration and domain-specific uses. These examples also expose important limitations
and opportunities: steering directions may only be locally valid, diffusion models introduce substantial interaction
latency, and constrained design spaces require stronger feasibility guidance. More broadly, our results suggest that
