# PULSE: Personalized Opportunistic Learning for Wearable Devices

Abstract—Wearable devices support personalized health and well-being applications, but post-deployment data can differ substantially from those used for training, while purely local adaptation may be limited by the data available on a single device during real-world operation. Conventional federated and decentralized learning rely on recurrent communication and stable connectivity, whereas existing opportunistic approaches often exploit available encounters without adequately consid-ering their utility, compatibility, or communication cost. We propose PULSE, an event-driven framework for person-alized opportunistic learning that treats each encounter as an online collaboration decision, selecting either no interaction or a promising nearby peer. Peer gradients are incorporated only when compatible with the local learning direction, while past interactions guide future peer selection. Across human activity recognition, fall detection, and stress recognition under synthetic and real contact traces, PULSE preserves or improves personalized classification performance, while reducing peer-assisted exchanges by 80–94% compared with opportunistic model averaging. On-device experiments further demonstrate its feasibility on wearable hardware: peer-assisted operations require limited energy, while the complete implementation occupies 149.8 KiB of RAM and 561.8 KiB of flash. The obtained results show that selective opportunistic collaboration can provide effective personalization while limiting communi-cation and resource demands on constrained wearable devices. Index Terms—Opportunistic Learning, Personalized Learn-ing, Wearable Devices, Decentralized Learning, On-Device Learning 

I. Introduction Wearable devices support applications for personal well-being and health, such as activity recognition, physi-ological monitoring, and affective-state assessment, by continuously collecting sensor data [1], [2]. They are often designed as sensing and inference endpoints, with machine learning models trained before deployment and subse-quently executed on-device to provide low-latency predic-tions while reducing the need to continuously transmit raw observations [3]. Yet, the data observed after deployment may differ substantially from those used during training because of person-specific behaviours and physiological characteristics, sensing hardware and placement, and changes in daily routines or operating conditions [4], [5]. Although local adaptation can mitigate this issue, a single wearable may not collect sufficient labeled data to learn an effective personalized model in isolation [6]. Collaborative learning offers a complementary source of information: devices can improve their models using knowledge acquired by others without directly sharing raw observations [7], [8]. Human mobility naturally brings nearby devices into contact, creating intermittent opportunities to exchange learning information and to support collaborative adapta-tion. This setting has motivated Opportunistic Learning (OL), in which decentralized model adaptation is driven by transient device-to-device encounters rather than scheduled communication rounds or persistent network connections [9]. Existing OL approaches have explored gradient exchange, contact-aware collaboration policies, and the transfer of model components among encountered devices [10]. While they demonstrate that intermittent encounters can support collaborative learning without centralized orchestration, the heterogeneity of wearable data makes indiscriminate knowledge transfer particularly problematic: a peer update may be irrelevant or harmful when different sensing conditions, activity distributions, or locally adapted representations are merged. In this work we introduce PULSE, a personalized oppor-tunistic learning mechanism that makes peer collaboration explicitly selective at two levels. First, at each encounter, a lightweight utility-guided policy selects one promising peer or chooses no contact when communication is not expected to be useful. Second, rather than averaging models or accepting a peer update unconditionally, PULSE evaluates the agreement between the remote and local gradients and incorporates the remote signal only when it is positively aligned with the local learning direction. Each device fine-tunes its full model on private data, while peer interactions exchange only the current prediction head and its corresponding gradient, keeping the collaboration lightweight and preserving raw-data locality. Related Work Federated Learning enables multiple devices to improve a learning model without directly sharing their raw data, typically by periodically communicating local model updates to a coordinating server [11]. This paradigm can reduce the exposure of personal sensing data and has motivated extensive work on mobile and edge in-telligence [8]. In many contemporary edge deployments, pervasive connectivity and access to a coordinating infras-tructure may indeed be available. However, conventional Federated Learning typically relies on frequent communi-cation rounds and on the transfer of model parameters or gradients, which can impose substantial communication, energy, and computation overhead. Such requirements may be poorly aligned with pervasive sensing scenarios involving resource-constrained mobile and wearable de-vices, for which continuous participation in collaborative learning may be impractical or inefficient. Decentralized Federated Learning removes the central coordinator and enables devices to exchange learning updates directly with peers [12]. This improves robustness to infrastructure failures and avoids reliance on a single aggregation point. However, most decentralized optimiza-tion methods assume frequent peer-to-peer exchanges over persistent or repeatedly available communication graphs, typically through iterative gossip protocols. While these assumptions may be reasonable for smartphones or single-board computers connected through Wi-Fi or local networks, repeated computation and communication can impose substantial energy costs on resource-constrained wearable devices [13]. A line of work closer to our research considers collabora-tion driven by physical encounters among mobile devices. Lee et al. introduced Opportunistic Federated Learning, in which devices personalize locally trained models by incorporating knowledge from encountered peers and use mobility and contact prediction to decide whether collab-oration is worthwhile [9]. This formulation importantly recognizes that encounters differ in their expected utility and duration, rather than treating communication oppor-tunities as uniformly available. At the same time, the approach is centered on exchanging learning updates (i.e., gradients) between devices and relies on estimating future contact properties, which may be inaccurate or unavailable in highly dynamic environments. More recently, OPTIMIST considers opportunistic fed-erated transfer learning under intermittent connectivity and without centralized orchestration during the learning process [10]. It combines a frozen, pre-trained backbone with a trainable classifier head that is partitioned into non-overlapping subnetworks. Each subnetwork is assigned to a node, trained locally, and transferred to an encountered peer, thereby enabling its sequential exposure to multiple local datasets while avoiding parameter conflicts among parallel updates. This design substantially reduces the computational burden relative to end-to-end collaborative training and is evaluated through both simulations and an Android-based prototype. However, the framework presupposes an initialization procedure that creates the partition of the classifier head and assigns a distinct subnetwork to each partic-ipating node. Although this procedure need not entail centralized coordination during subsequent encounters, it implicitly requires either a bootstrap component with global knowledge of the participating nodes or a shared deterministic assignment mechanism; the paper does not further specify how this initialization would be realized in a fully autonomous deployment. Moreover, OPTIMIST formulates learning as the optimization of a classifier head over the virtually merged data of all nodes. Its primary objective is therefore collaborative generalization: subnet-works circulate to incorporate information from multiple datasets, rather than being selected or adapted according to the specific needs, data distribution, or compatibility of an individual user. While nodes may hold different local head instances at a given time because of their distinct encounter histories, this divergence is an effect of asyn-chronous exchanges rather than an explicit personalization mechanism. Finally, its mobile evaluation targets Android smartphones and parameter-level subnetwork transfer, whose resource requirements remain different from those of microcontroller-class wearable devices. A. Real-device evaluation of collaborative edge learning Experimental validation on physical devices is necessary to complement simulation-based analyses, which cannot fully capture the execution time, memory footprint, wire-less latency, and energy consumption associated with collaborative learning. Prior work has developed Federated Learning testbeds on resource-constrained edge platforms, offering valuable insights into the discrepancy between abstract communication rounds and their practical re-alization [14], [15]. These studies enable an end-to-end assessment of system-level behavior beyond predictive performance alone. However, the considered platforms generally rely on single-board computers, smartphones, or other comparatively capable edge devices, typically operating over Wi-Fi or an accessible local network. On the other hand, wearable sensing introduces adistinct operating regime: devices have tighter energy and memory budgets, may collect highly personal and user-dependent data, and encounter peers through short-lived, irregular contacts [16]. The PULSE algorithm PULSE is an event-driven mechanism specifically de-signed for personalized learning on wearable and edge devices experiencing brief and sporadic peer encounters. Before deployment, a shared encoder is pretrained on representative data in a centralized bootstrap phase. Each node i then initializes its local model as fi = hi ◦E i, where 

Ei : X → Rd is initialized from the pretrained encoder and maps an input observation to a d-dimensional latent representation, while a lightweight multilayer-perceptron head hi : Rd → Y maps this representation to the output space. After deployment, each node locally fine-tunes fi on its private, potentially evolving data distribution, allowing the model to adapt to user-specific behaviours and sensing conditions while retaining the task-aware initialization obtained during bootstrap. Rather than averaging com-plete models or exchanging raw observations, PULSE restricts peer-assisted interactions to gradients of hi, thus keeping both the communication payload and peer-side computation lightweight. Since information received from an arbitrary peer may be irrelevant or harmful, PULSE selectively incorporates remote learning signals. During an encounter, node i sends its current hi to a selected peer j, which evaluates a temporary copy using its local Ej and a private minibatch; node i then compares the resulting gradient with its local learning direction and incorporates it only when their cosine agreement is positive. Peer Selection Policy At each peer-discovery event, node i must decide whether communication is worthwhile and, if so, which currently reachable peer should be contacted. This is a sequential decision problem: the usefulness of a peer can only be assessed after interacting with it, while its utility may vary over time as the users’ contexts and data distributions evolve. PULSE addresses this decision through a lightweight policy inspired by the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) principle [20]. Accordingly, each reachable peer is treated as an alternative whose utility is learned online from the gradient agreements observed in previous encounters. Cru-cially, PULSE also includes a no-contact action, denoted by ∅, enabling node i to skip communication when none of the available peers appears sufficiently promising, rather than being forced to contact an arbitrary device (line 9). Selecting ∅ means that node i does not engage in peer collaboration, while still taking advantage of the update opportunity through the local instance of Eq. (7), with mixing coefficient equal to zero. This avoids unnecessary communication without interrupting local adaptation. For every discovered peer j, node i maintains a utility estimate Ui[j] and a selection count nij . The former summarizes whether the peer has historically provided aligned or conflicting gradients, whereas the latter mea-sures the amount of evidence collected about that peer. Simulation Results We firstly evaluate PULSE in a controlled event-driven simulator of decentralized learning among wearable devices under intermittent device-to-device connectivity. Each node represents one user and retains only its associ-ated sensor data, while the simulator replays local-training opportunities and peer contacts over time. We consider heterogeneous wearable applications and synthetic and real-world contact traces, which vary in sensing modality, learning task, user-data heterogeneity, and encounter dynamics. We compare PULSE with rep-resentative opportunistic-learning baselines in terms of classification performance and communication demand, measured by the number of completed peer-assisted mes-sage exchanges. Models Pretraining To provide all nodes with a common yet task-aware initialization, we pretrain a lightweight encoder for each scenario before the decentralized simulations. The archi-tectures are tailored to the available sensing modalities while maintaining a common 64-dimensional embedding space and a limited computational footprint. During pretraining, each encoder is paired with an auxiliary MLP classifier comprising one 64-unit hidden layer. The selected encoder checkpoint then initializes every simulated node, which locally fine-tunes it together with its own person-alized classifier. For HAR, we use a compact one-dimensional CNN with two temporal convolutional blocks over the three-axis accelerometer signal. FD uses a six-channel temporal con-volutional network with residual dilated blocks to process accelerometer and gyroscope windows. For SR, separate convolutional branches encode the accelerometer, blood-volume pulse, electrodermal activity, and temperature sig-nals; their representations are fused before classification. All models are optimized with AdamW and cross-entropy loss using a learning rate of 10 −3. We monitor validation F1-score, retaining the checkpoint with the highest value, and restore it before deployment. Early stopping uses a minimum improvement of 0.001 : HAR and FD stop after 20 and 5 epochs without improvement, respectively, whereas SR is trained for the maximum budget of 100 epochs with a patience of 50. Figure 1 shows the pre-training dynamics, with the horizontal axis normalized by the training budget of each scenario. The training loss decreases consistently in all cases. The selected HAR, FD, and SR encoders achieve validation F1-scores of 0.836, 0.783, and 0.746 at epochs 8, 18, and 53, respectively. Validation F1 is not strictly monotonic, particularly for FD and SR, as expected under stochastic optimization and limited validation data. Selecting the best validation checkpoint therefore provides a robust initialization before local personalization in the opportunistic scenarios. 

Fig. 2: Temporal evolution of contact opportunities. C. Contact Traces We replay time-stamped pairwise contact traces to determine when devices can exchange learning signals. A contact event indicates that the corresponding node pair can communicate at a given time. Repeated observations of the same undirected pair within a snapshot are removed. We consider two controlled synthetic mobility models and the real-world Cambridge/Haggle Bluetooth trace [25]. 1) Synthetic traces: We generate traces using the Community [26] and Edge-Markov [27] models. Each synthetic trace contains ten nodes, spans 24 hours, and is sampled every 20 seconds. For each model, we gen-erate three independent realizations using seeds 7, 42, and 123. For both models, we consider a Dense and a Sparse connectivity regime that differ in the frequency of communication opportunities while preserving the defin-ing property of the respective mobility model. Dense traces provide approximately 4.5–4.9 active contacts per snapshot, corresponding to a mean occupancy of about 10% of all possible node pairs. Sparse traces provide approximately 0.8–1.0 contacts per snapshot, or about 2% occupancy. They therefore reduce the rate of peer encounters by roughly a factor of five, creating a more constrained setting for opportunistic learning. In Community traces, contacts remain predominantly local: approximately 86.6% and 86.3% of events occur within communities in the Dense and Sparse regimes, respectively. For each mobility model, the two regimes retain the same node population and contact-generation mechanism, while varying only the overall frequency of contact opportunities. The Dense–Sparse comparison therefore isolates the effect of peer availability under the same underlying mobility model. 2) Real trace: We additionally use the Cam-bridge/Haggle trace, which records Bluetooth encounters among iMotes carried by members of a research group at the University of Cambridge [25]. It contains 12 nodes, spans approximately 126.6 hours, and is sampled every 120 seconds. Its mean pair occupancy is 4.14%, intermediate between the controlled Dense and Sparse regimes. Unlike the approximately stationary synthetic traces, however, Cambridge exhibits temporally uneven activity, with contacts concentrated in a limited number of intervals. Figure 2 highlights both the controlled separation between Dense and Sparse conditions and the irregular temporal dynamics of the real trace. Together, these traces assess the methods under frequent, scarce, and naturally bursty peer-learning opportunities. D. Baselines We compare PULSE with four baselines. Local trains exclusively on private data, without any communications. OppAVG opportunistically averages the models of all reachable peers. OppFL sends the local model to apeer, receives a gradient computed on the peer’s private minibatch, and aggregates it with the local gradient. OPTIMIST partitions the classifier into N subnetworks, one initially assigned to each of the N nodes; mutually matched nodes exchange their active subnetworks and update only the received one. It performs no update without an accepted exchange. All methods start from the same pretrained model, update both the encoder and prediction head, and never exchange raw data. They use two SGD steps, batch size of 

16 , and learning rate of 0.01 . OppFL uses a zero similar-ity threshold, unit peer-gradient weight, two interaction rounds, and momentum aggregation, thus allowing the method to exploit all non-conflicting peer updates while preserving its original aggregation mechanism. For OPTI-MIST, we set the number of subnetworks as to the number of nodes in each simulation. For PULSE, we set the maximum mixing coefficient to ¯α = 0 .5, the utility-update rate to μ = 0 .2, and the UCB exploration coefficient to 

β = 0 .25 . These values keep peer contributions bounded, let utility estimates react to recent interactions without becoming overly volatile, and provide limited exploration of previously untested peers. E. Classification Results Table II reports the final performance across the three real-world sensing applications and five contact-trace con-figurations. For each simulation user, local data are split into 70% for training and 30% for testing, with Accuracy and Macro-F1 evaluated exclusively on the local test set. Local learning is already a strong baseline in several settings: each node is therefore trained and evaluated on data from the same user, all nodes start from a pretrained encoder, and the simulated users generally retain examples from every target class. Hence, collaboration is beneficial only when encountered peers provide complementary and compatible learning signals. This effect is particularly evident in FD. Local, Op-pAVG, and PULSE reach the same performance with the Cambridge trace (0.92 Accuracy and 0.82 F1), and remain closely aligned in the dense synthetic scenarios (approxi-mately 0.91 Accuracy and 0.81 F1). Sparse contacts lead only to a limited reduction for PULSE, whose F1 reaches 0.80 under Community–Sparse. Since FD is a binary task and its encoder is pretrained on data from other subjects of the same dataset, the strong Local results suggest limited headroom for collaboration. PULSE nevertheless remains competitive, showing that its peer-assisted updates do not introduce substantial negative transfer when local personalization is already effective. A similar pattern emerges in SR. Local obtains the best Cambridge accuracy (0.98), while PULSE remains close at 0.97 accuracy and 0.91 F1. Under all synthetic traces, however, PULSE achieves the highest F1, reaching 0.88 in the dense configurations and 0.87–0.88 under sparse connectivity, while maintaining 0.95 accuracy throughout. Although the margins over Local are moderate, these re-sults show that PULSE can exploit cross-user information without compromising personalized performance. The benefits of collaboration are substantially clearer in HAR, where the encoder is pretrained on UCI-HAR and then adapted to users and devices from a different dataset. In the dense scenarios, PULSE reaches 0.82 accuracy and 0.81–0.82 F1, compared with 0.68 accuracy and 0.64 F1 for Local. OPTIMIST is the strongest baseline in these settings, but remains below PULSE (0.70–0.71 accuracy and 0.69–0.70 F1). PULSE also retains a clear advantage under sparse contacts, obtaining 0.76–0.77 accuracy and 0.75–0.76 F1. In contrast, OPTIMIST and OppFL fall to approximately 0.65/0.63 and 0.54/0.51, respectively, reflecting their stronger dependence on frequent successful encounters. With Cambridge, PULSE matches Local in accuracy (0.87) and slightly improves F1 (0.87 versus 0.86), whereas OppFL and OPTIMIST reach 0.55/0.52 and 0.59/0.57, respectively. This is consistent with the intermittent nature of the real trace, where long periods with few contacts penalize approaches whose optimization is primarily encounter-driven. Overall, PULSE provides the most consistent behavior across the evaluation. It achieves the best accuracy and F1 in all HAR configurations, the highest Macro-F1 in every synthetic SR scenario, and remains comparable to Local on FD. The final-loss values follow the same trend, with PULSE attaining the lowest rounded loss in all HAR settings and in most synthetic FD and SR settings. These results indicate that selective collaboration can preserve strong local personalization when communication is unhelpful, while providing substantial gains when local data and pretrained representations are less well aligned with the target setting. On-Device Evaluation We evaluate PULSE’s feasibility on SensWear, an open-source modular wearable platform based on Zephyr RTOS [28], [29]. Its flexible main board (Fig. 3) in-tegrates a Nordic nRF54L15 wireless SoC with a 128-MHz Arm Cortex-M33 processor, Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) connectivity, 256 KB of RAM, and 1.5 MB of non-volatile memory [30]. A BHI360 six-axis IMU provides inertial sensing, while 4 MiB of external non-volatile memory supports local storage of sensor samples and model parameters. At the firmware level, PULSE learning operations are mapped to event-driven Zephyr work items. During an opportunistic exchange, the device requesting peer assis-tance acts as the initiator (node i), while the selected peer acts as the responder (node j). The initiator sends its personalized head hi to the responder, which evaluates it on a private local minibatch and returns the resulting gradient gj . The initiator then incorporates the received gradient into its local update, completing the peer-assisted interaction without exchanging raw sensor data. A. Experimental Protocol All measurements use the same local model and hy-perparameters adopted in the HAR simulations. Timing and energy are evaluated on two communicating SensWear devices, with five warm-up repetitions followed by 100 measured trials for each execution path, while instrumen-tation is kept outside the measured intervals to avoid affecting the results. Latency is characterized through the mean, median, interquartile range, and p5–p95 intervals to capture variability in wireless communication, while energy is derived from event-level current measurements relative to the corresponding pre-event baseline. Incre-mental flash and static RAM requirements are measured against an otherwise identical sensing-and-BLE firmware image without PULSE, isolating the memory overhead introduced by the framework. B. Power and Energy Power consumption is measured using a Keysight 34465A digital multimeter connected in series with acurrent-limited 3.7-V supply at the SensWear battery input, while the peer device is powered independently. Current is sampled at 50 Hz, with the initiator and responder roles profiled in separate runs. For each event, baseline power is estimated over the 0.4 s preceding its detected onset, and incremental energy is obtained by integrating the power above this baseline over the following 4.1 s, including the return to idle. The resulting value represents source-side energy measured at the board input, while the responder energy is estimated assuming the same 3.7-V supply voltage. As shown in Fig. 4a, the energy required by PULSE remains particularly limited across all profiled operations. Local adaptation is the most demanding operation, with a median incremental energy of 42.88 mJ (IQR 42.18– 43.43 mJ), whereas initiating an opportunistic exchange requires only 26.85 mJ (IQR 26.46–27.28 mJ). The re-sponder incurs a considerably smaller median cost of 6.87 mJ (IQR 2.15–20.65 mJ), indicating that assisting a nearby device introduces only a modest additional energy demand. These results show that peer-assisted learning does not dominate the energy cost of on-device adaptation, despite involving both local computation and wireless communication. This behavior is also reflected in the current traces, where local adaptation sustains elevated consumption for a longer interval, while peer collaboration concentrates its energy demand in shorter computation and communication phases. The measured energy profile therefore supports the feasibility of selec-tive peer assistance on battery-powered wearable devices, particularly when exchanges are performed only when considered beneficial by PULSE. C. Memory Usage Flash and static RAM requirements are obtained from the linker maps of the PULSE firmware and an other-wise identical sensing-and-BLE baseline built from the same firmware revision. The difference between the two configurations is used to isolate the incremental memory footprint introduced by PULSE, with the linker-defined usable regions taken as the available device capacity. As shown in Fig. 4b, the complete PULSE firmware occupies 561.8 KiB of flash (36.9% of the available 1,524 KiB) and 149.8 KiB of static RAM (58.5% of 256 KiB), leaving 962.2 KiB and 106.2 KiB available, respectively. Compared with the considered baseline, PULSE introduces an additional 211.0 KiB of flash and 91.9 KiB of RAM, showing that the complete learning framework fits within the memory resources of the wear-able while retaining substantial capacity for other applica-tion functionality. The classification head contains 4,550 parameters and occupies 18.2 KB when serialized, with the same size required for an exchanged gradient. In contrast, the additional state required for peer selection is negligible, with the complete utility table for the configured eight peers requiring only 208 bytes. The remaining memory capacity confirms that PULSE can be practically deployed alongside the sensing and communication functionality of a resource-constrained wearable. D. Execution Time Execution time is measured using the firmware’s 1-MHz wall counter, excluding subsequent logging operations. We profile both already-connected exchanges, which isolate the PULSE protocol, and discovery-to-update transactions that additionally include BLE link establishment. Each end-to-end path is evaluated over 100 trials, with latency distributions reported in Fig. 4c. Local adaptation completes in 3539.6 ms at the me-dian (3540.5 ms at p95), while an already-connected opportunistic exchange requires 3167.5 ms (3220.2 ms at p95). Including peer discovery and connection increases the median latency to 3428.4 ms, corresponding to an overhead of only 260.9 ms (8.2%) over the connected path. Most of this overhead is attributable to scanning and con-nection establishment, while peer selection and gradient processing introduce comparatively small computational costs. All 100 initiator trials successfully complete in both exchange configurations, showing that the complete PULSE interaction can be reliably executed directly on a wearable device within just a few seconds. VI. Conclusions and Future Work This paper presented PULSE, an event-driven frame-work for Personalized Opportunistic Learning on resource-constrained wearable devices. PULSE treats transient encounters as selective collaboration opportunities, com-bining utility-guided peer selection with gradient-level compatibility assessment.
