Abstract —Third-party community-based bots are central to how social platforms such as Discord work. They moderate content, manage roles, play music, and support AI-assisted con-versations across millions of servers. Yet when a bot is installed in a server, it can access messages, user profiles, and server metadata concerning members who did not participate in the installation decision, with no standardized way to communicate the associated security and privacy implications. In this paper, we analyze 23,102 third-party Discord bots and 183,142 user reviews spanning 49 detected languages and five years of feedback (2021– 2026). We employ LLM-based knowledge distillation and fine-tune a DeBERTa-based classifier to identify candidate security and privacy (S&P)-relevant reviews. After manually validating the complete classifier-positive set, we retain 8,832 reviews iden-tified as S&P-relevant, representing an observed pipeline yield of 4.82%, and map them to a 31-theme S&P taxonomy extending prior work. Our results show that S&P-related reviews focus heavily on Data Sharing (30.5%) and Data Collection (16.2%). Confession and anonymity bots have pipeline-identified SPRRs above 50%, more than ten times the ecosystem-wide pipeline yield of 4.82%. Star ratings may obscure the frequency and valence of S&P-related discussion, while conventional metadata features, such as permission count and administrator status, show only weak associations with user-reported S&P concerns. We then build BotLens, a proof-of-concept auditing framework that combines review-derived S&P signals with bot metadata to provide on-demand decision support for the community-based bot ecosystem. BotLens exposes six tools through a Model Context Protocol server, supporting bot-level checkups, server-wide comparison, and integration with AI agents. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Social platforms like Discord rely on third-party community bots to moderate content, manage roles, play music, support games, and enable AI-mediated interaction across millions of servers. Discord started as a voice-chat platform for gamers and has grown into a large social platform with more than 200 million monthly active users [1]. These bots connect through Discord’s API, appear in servers with an APP tag, and provide functionality for entire communities. Unlike mobile apps or browser extensions selected by individual users, a Discord bot is installed server-wide by an administrator through an OAuth2 authorization flow requiring the Manage Guild 

permission [2]. A single installation decision can therefore expose the data and interactions of potentially thousands of server members to a third-party service over which they have limited control. Bots may continue accessing server data after installation through Discord’s persistent Gateway WebSocket connection, which delivers events such as messages, member joins, reac-tions, and voice-state changes. A permission bitfield assigned during authorization controls the scope of a bot’s access. The Administrator permission grants broad server-level capa-bilities, while Read Message History allows access to prior messages only in channels the bot is otherwise permitted to view. Privileged Gateway Intents, including the Message Con-tent intent, may further allow bots to access message bodies in available channels and process them in real time. Server members affected by these permissions often have no way to determine what a bot can access or how their data are used. The administrator approving the installation may also mis-understand the scope or consequences of the access granted, consistent with prior findings that users often misinterpret permission scopes and runtime-permission risks [3]. Discord differs from platforms like Slack, where bots typically operate within private organizational workspaces [4], and Telegram, which does not expose a comparable structured permission bitfield [5]. Discord therefore combines large community scale with a formal but difficult-to-observe permission model. Mobile-app permissions are generally granted by the person who installs and uses the application, while website consent notices are presented directly to visitors during a browsing session [3], [6]. In contrast, a Discord server admin can install a bot that affects many members who do not participate in the authorization decision. The threat space is broader because overprivileged or compromised bots, rogue admins, and abusive users may exploit bot capabilities within an on-going social environment. App-store oversight, data-protection requirements, and scrutiny of manipulative consent interfaces provide mechanisms for intervention. Discord members may learn about problematic bot behavior only after installation through incidents, moderation outcomes, or user reviews [7].

Mobile app stores have introduced privacy labels, permission prompts, and review-based transparency mechanisms [8], [9], but community-bot marketplaces provide no comparable standardized disclosure framework. In our dataset, only 0.77% of bots reference a privacy policy in their listings, leaving users with limited formal information about how bots handle data. User reviews provide an alternative source of evidence because review writers voluntarily describe experiences they considered important enough to report. These reviews offer complementary, unprompted, in-situ evidence of issues that review writers chose to report publicly and, unlike structured studies, are not elicited through researcher-designed questions. However, S&P concerns in community-bot ecosystems remain underexplored, and no existing tool provides users or administrators with structured, on-demand access to these signals. 

Research Questions. We study the following questions: 1) RQ1: What S&P concerns do Discord bot users raise in reviews, and how are these concerns distributed across bot categories? 2) RQ2: What affective expressions characterize S&P re-views, and how do these patterns vary across S&P themes, bot categories and languages? 3) RQ3: How do requested bot permissions and metadata correlate with user-reported S&P concerns?

II. R ELATED WORK 

Our work draws on three areas: the security and privacy risks of third-party bots, users’ perceptions of bots, and review-based methods for analyzing S&P concerns at scale. 

A. Security and Privacy of Bots on Social Platforms 

Bots and automated agents introduce broader S&P risks on social platforms [10], [11]. Discord bots frequently request permissions beyond those needed for their stated functional-ity [12], [13], [14], [15]. Moderation bots mediate rule enforce-ment and private moderator feedback, while bot adoption de-pends on moderators’ organizational needs and capacities [16], [17]. Bots in group messaging may also access excessive content and enable cross-context tracking, motivating proto-cols that preserve end-to-end encryption while limiting bot access [18]. SnoopGuard provides selective message access and sender anonymity, but it is preventive and does not assess bots already deployed in existing servers [19]. Related studies of voice assistants identify unauthorized data access, dormant intents, risky content, and weaknesses in certification and runtime vetting [20], [21], [22]. Mobile-app research similarly shows that developers struggle with privacy requirements, rely on insecure third-party components, and may retain risky permissions across free and paid ap-plications [23], [24], [25]. For messaging-platform chatbots specifically, prior work found that 55% of chatbots in aleading Discord repository request administrator permission and that only 4.35% of permission-requesting chatbots provide a privacy policy [12]. Other work calls for stronger governance of automated systems through Zero Trust principles, continu-ous monitoring, and improved bot-development practices [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]. Together, these findings motivate our examination of whether requested permissions and other metadata correspond to the S&P reviews. 

B. User Perceptions of Bots 

The most directly relevant study surveyed 374 participants across five messaging platforms and found that only 41.7% correctly understood which messages bots could access. Par-ticipants reported greater S&P concern after permissions were disclosed, and the study identified six expectation-mismatch categories and a five-stage S&P adaptation pathway [32]. Related work examines users’ concerns and attitudes toward conversational AI, synthesizes prior research on chatbot pri-vacy, and studies how trust shapes privacy-preserving disclo-sure behavior [33], [34], [35]. Bot-generated interactions may increase engagement when they are contextually appropriate, but unchecked automation can also distort platform behav-ior [36], [37]. Privacy on social platforms is also collective: one user’s decisions about shared data can affect others [38]. This collective dimension is particularly important on Discord, where an administrator can install a bot that affects every member of a server. Our work complements prior surveys and controlled studies by examining S&P reviews that users voluntarily report. 2C. User Review Analysis 

User reviews are an established source for studying S&P concerns at scale. Prior work has analyzed Android reviews to identify privacy concerns, examine their relationship with app evaluations, and develop systems for navigating privacy feed-back [39], [40], [41], [42], [43]. Akgul et al. [39] developed a 20-theme taxonomy for privacy-related Android reviews and showed that reviews provide structured S&P signals that complement developer disclosures and platform audits. We extend this taxonomy with 11 themes that capture risks specific to community bots. LLM-generated labels can support large-scale text clas-sification while reducing annotation costs [44], [45], [46]. Building on this approach, we use knowledge distillation with an LLM teacher and a fine-tuned student model to identify S&P-relevant reviews. Sentiment, topic, and emotion-analysis methods have also been used to extract structured patterns from user-generated text [47], [48], [49]. We combine S&P classification, thematic analysis, and affective-expression classification to characterize how users describe S&P-related experiences in the community-bot ecosystem. 

D. Our Contribution to Research on Community Bots 

Prior bot research has focused primarily on permission models, protocol-level vulnerabilities, and static or dynamic technical analysis [12], [19], [14]. Less is known about how Discord users experience and publicly report S&P concerns. While prior user studies examine S&P awareness and attitudes under controlled conditions [32], [35], our work examines un-prompted concerns that review writers chose to report publicly in an in-situ platform setting. These reviews provide a distinct source of evidence, although review writers are self-selected and may not represent the broader Discord user population. Existing review-analysis frameworks were developed primarily for mobile applications and do not capture bot-specific risks such as administrator-mediated installation, gateway intents, and server-wide message access [39], [40], [41]. We address this gap with a 31-theme taxonomy adapted to the Discord bot ecosystem and a large-scale analysis of user-reported S&P concerns. BotLens further operationalizes these review-derived signals through on-demand bot-level and server-wide auditing. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

To identify and analyze security and privacy (S&P) signals in the reviews, we built a four-stage automated pipeline. Stage 1 develops a Discord-adapted S&P taxonomy; Stage 2 uses knowledge distillation to train a lightweight classifier from LLM-generated labels; Stage 3 applies that classifier to the full review corpus and enriches each detected review with taxonomy and affective-expression labels; and Stage 4 uses the resulting dataset to answer our research questions and to power BotLens, an S&P auditing tool for community-based bots. Figure 1 shows an overview. 

A. Data Collection and Preprocessing 

We built a crawler using Playwright [50] to collect user reviews and bot metadata from Top.gg, the largest public Discord bot marketplace. For each bot, we collected the full review history alongside bot descriptions, permission flags (en-coded as Discord API bitfields), server counts, tags, category assignments, and GitHub repository URLs where available. In total, we collected 201,733 raw reviews spanning five years (January 2021 through February 2026). We preprocessed the raw data in three steps. First, we ran language detection using langdetect and identified 49 distinct languages. Among the 201,733 raw reviews, English accounted for 49.9% (100,711). Reviews assigned one of the 48 valid non-English language codes accounted for 41.0% (82,786), while 9.0% (18,236) had undetermined or invalid language labels. Reviews assigned a valid non-English language code were translated to English using the open-source Argos Translate [51]. Table IV in Appendix B reports the full language distribution. Second, we applied standard text cleaning: removing reviews shorter than 5 characters, stripping extraneous whitespace, and parsing date fields. Finally, we merged review text with bot metadata and computed derived features, including text length, word count, number of requested permissions, and whether the bot linked a GitHub repository. B. Stage 1: S&P Taxonomy Development 

We developed the taxonomy using a hybrid deductive– inductive thematic analysis [52], [53], in which an existing coding frame supplies the initial categories and further cat-egories are derived inductively from the data. The deductive component was the 20-theme Android app-review taxonomy of Akgul et al. [39], which we applied to our reviews to capture concerns shared with the mobile-app setting. The inductive component addressed concerns that this frame did not cover: two researchers with expertise in security and privacy conducted iterative open coding of 500 S&P-relevant reviews, assigning codes to concerns that no existing theme ac-commodated. Discord’s developer documentation on Gateway Intents, OAuth2 scopes, and bot permissions served as refer-ence material for interpreting platform-specific terminology in the review text, such as references to intents or permission scopes; it was not used as a source of candidate codes [2]. 3Fig. 1: BotLens pipeline: S&P taxonomy development (Stage 1), classifier training through knowledge distillation (Stage 2), S&P review identification and affective-expression classification (Stage 3), and review and metadata analysis with BotLens auditing (Stage 4). C. Stage 2: Knowledge Distillation 

Stage 2 trains a lightweight S&P classifier through a two-model architecture in which an LLM acts as the teacher and a fine-tuned smaller model acts as the student. This design allows us to generate labels for the full 183,142-review corpus without requiring human annotation at scale, while confining LLM inference to an initial labeling pass. The S&P taxonomy from Stage 1 is configured in the teacher system prompt to guide the classifications. 

1) LLM for Labeling: We used Llama 3 8B as the teacher [54], served locally via Ollama on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 (32 GB VRAM). For each review, the LLM pro-duced a JSON output with four fields: a binary S&P label, a confidence score (0.0–1.0), a brief reasoning, and the identified S&P theme. We set temperature to 0.1 to maximize label consistency and capped output at 300 tokens per response. 

a) Labeling-budget Comparison: To examine how the amount of teacher-labeled data affected student-classifier per-formance, we evaluated five labeling budgets corresponding to 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 45% of the 183,142 reviews. At each level, we drew a stratified sample across star ratings (1–5) and text-length bins (short, medium, and long), then trained and evaluated a student classifier using the correspond-ing teacher-labeled subset. As shown in Table II, the 30% condition achieved the highest observed F1 score among the evaluated configurations (F1 = 0.938), with similar precision and recall. Because the labeling conditions used different sampled subsets and evaluation splits, we treat this result as an exploratory comparison rather than evidence that 30% is a universally optimal labeling budget or that performance saturates beyond this point. 3) Teacher Model Labeling Validation: To assess the reli-ability of the teacher-generated binary labels, two researchers with expertise in security and privacy independently annotated a stratified 10% sample of the 54,942-review teacher-labeled subset ( N = 5 ,494 ). Each researcher independently assigned a binary S&P-relevance label without access to the teacher-generated label. Pairwise Cohen’s κ was 0.74 between the two researchers, 0.71 between the first researcher and Llama 3, and 0.84 between the second researcher and Llama 3. Raw agreement exceeded 99.8% across all three comparisons. Af-ter completing the independent annotations, the researchers discussed and resolved the disagreements. We refer to the adjudicated human annotations as reference labels rather than ground truth. 

4) Student Model Training: We fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-large [55] (304M parameters) as our student model for binary S&P classification. Before training, we filtered out LLM labels with confidence below 0.7 and downsampled the majority class (not_sp ) to a 5:1 ratio relative to the sp class to address label imbalance. We then split the data into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% test sets using stratified sampling. We used the following fine-tuning configuration: learning rate 

2 × 10 −5 with 10% linear warmup, AdamW optimizer with weight decay 0.01, training batch size 8, evaluation batch size 16, maximum sequence length 512, and FP16 mixed precision. Training ran for up to 10 epochs with early stopping (pa-tience 3) on validation F1. We set the classification threshold at 0.8 to minimize false positives. For the classifier trained under the selected 30% teacher-labeling condition, the corresponding evaluation split con-tained 98 reviews, including 16 S&P-relevant and 82 non-S&P reviews. At the classification threshold of 0.8, the classifier correctly classified 15 of the 16 S&P-relevant reviews and 81 of the 82 non-S&P reviews, yielding one false positive and one false negative. Precision, recall, and F1 were each 0.938. The 95% Wilson confidence intervals for both precision and recall were approximately 0.72–0.99, reflecting substantial uncertainty due to the small number of positive examples. Be-cause these estimates are based on only 16 positive examples, we interpret them as performance under this evaluation setup rather than as precise estimates of generalization performance. Each labeling-budget condition used its own stratified training, validation, and test split, so the differences across Table II should be interpreted as exploratory and not as a controlled comparison on a shared test set. 

D. Stage 3: Classification 

With the student model fine-tuned, Stage 3 applies it to the full corpus and enriches each identified S&P review with S&P taxonomy assignment and affective-expression classification, producing the structured dataset that feeds Stage 4 analysis. 

1) S&P Review Identification: We applied the trained stu-dent classifier to all 183,142 reviews using batch inference (batch size 64, FP16). Reviews with an S&P relevance score of at least 0.8 were identified as candidates, yielding 8,832 reviews. Both researchers who developed the codebook ex-amined the complete candidate set independently, using the finalized definition of S&P relevance, and disagreements were resolved through discussion. A review was retained when it discussed a bot-related security or privacy concern, prac-tice, perceived incident, data-handling behavior, or privacy-protective feature. Reviews concerning only functionality, us-ability, entertainment value, or general dissatisfaction without an S&P component were outside this definition. The vali-dation assessed whether each review was S&P-relevant; it did not determine whether the reviewer’s underlying claim was factually correct. All 8,832 candidates satisfied the fi-nalized relevance criteria and were retained as the manu-ally validated S&P-relevant dataset used in the subsequent thematic, sentiment, and affective-expression analyses. The manual validation supports the S&P-relevance classification of the retained classifier-positive reviews but does not rule out relevant reviews among the classifier-negative set. The teacher-positive rate of 163 /54 ,942 = 0 .30% and the final pipeline yield of 8,832 /183 ,142 = 4 .82% arose from different sampling and review procedures using the same taxonomy. Section VI-C2 further discusses the resulting limitations. 

2) Taxonomy Assignment: Each manually validated S&P-relevant review was assigned one primary theme from our 31-theme taxonomy using Llama 3 8B. The prompt supplied the complete taxonomy and its theme definitions and instructed the model to return the most applicable primary theme, a brief description of the issue, and an S&P sentiment label. We used a temperature of 0.1 to promote consistent outputs. Returned theme labels were programmatically checked against the finalized taxonomy, and outputs that could not be matched were labeled “unassigned.” Although secondary themes were retained as descriptive notes during processing, all quantitative analyses use exactly one primary theme per review. 

3) Affective-Expression Classification: We analyzed affec-tive expressions in S&P-relevant review text using the GoEmo-tions model [56]. The model assigns 28 fine-grained emotion 5labels, which we aggregated into eight categories: Anger, Dis-gust, Fear, Sadness, Confusion, Neutral, Joy, and Surprise. We used a multi-label threshold of 0.3. These labels characterize affective language expressed in the reviews and should not be interpreted as direct measurements of users’ underlying emotions or longer-term moods. We examine how these textual patterns vary across S&P themes and bot categories.

A. Aggregate Security and Privacy Trend Analysis 

We first give an overview of S&P discussion across the Discord bot ecosystem. Out of the 183,142 reviews, our pipeline identified 8,832 as S&P-relevant, yielding an observed Security and Privacy Review Rate (SPRR) of 4.82%. Figure 2 tracks SPRR in 30-day intervals from 2021 through early 2026. The rate climbed from roughly 4% in early 2021 to a peak around 8% in mid-2023, then fell to about 3% by early 2026. A linear regression trend line ( R2 = 0 .35 ) indicates a modest downward trend over the full observation window. The rise through 2023 may reflect growing user awareness of S&P issues during a period of rapid ecosystem expansion. The decline after that might reflect improved bot practices, lower review activity, or shifts in who uses the platform. The increased volatility in 2025 and 2026 lines up with lower review volumes in those periods, which amplifies monthly swings. This pattern differs from the Android app ecosystem, where prior work observed consistent upward growth in SPRR over a decade (9% biannual relative growth) [39]. The Discord ecosystem’s five-year window shows a rise-then-decline arc, hinting that S&P discourse in bot reviews may be more responsive to ecosystem-level changes.

Figure 3 compares the star-rating distributions of S&P and non-S&P reviews. S&P reviews are more polarized: 50.3% carry a 5-star rating and 24.4% carry a 1-star rating, while 4.6%, 7.7%, and 13.0% carry 2-, 3-, and 4-star ratings, respectively. In contrast, non-S&P reviews are concentrated at 5 stars, which account for 81.3% of the distribution, followed by 7.5% at 4 stars, 6.3% at 1 star, 3.2% at 3 stars, and 1.7% at 2 stars. The distributions indicate that S&P-relevant reviews are more frequently associated with highly positive or highly negative ratings than with mid-range ratings. Highly rated reviews often describe perceived privacy-protective practices, while low-rated reviews report concerns about bot behavior or data practices. V. A UDITING FRAMEWORK FOR COMMUNITY BOTS 

To show that S&P signals from user reviews along with bot metadata can be translated into a practical tool, we built 

BotLens , a proof-of-concept (PoC) auditing framework for community bots on social platforms. 

A. Motivation 

Many users do not read reviews [58]. Even for users who read reviews, there are still risks. A low SPRR across our dataset means that a user needs to scroll through many reviews before hitting one that reveals an S&P concern, assuming uniform distribution. In practice, platforms sort reviews by recency or helpfulness instead of topic, making it easy to miss S&P-relevant reviews. This motivates the core design principle behind BotLens: rather than expecting users to find 10 Fig. 14: Spearman correlation matrix across bot metadata features and SPRR. Server count shows the strongest negative correlation with SPRR ( ρ = −0.34 ). and interpret S&P signals on their own, BotLens performs automated classification, aggregation, and summarization of review-derived signals. 

B. Framework Design 1) Offline classification pipeline: The pipeline from Sec-tion III processes all 183,142 reviews: scraping from Top.gg, text cleaning, labeling via Ollama Llama 3 8B, DeBERTa classification, taxonomy mapping, affective-expression classi-fication with GoEmotions, permission decoding, and statistical aggregation. The output is a pre-classified dataset where every review carries a binary S&P label and a set of taxonomy tags, and every bot has a precomputed SPRR, permission profile, and sentiment composition among its S&P-relevant reviews. 

2) MCP query server: BotLens exposes six MCP tools over the pre-classified dataset: 1) get_bot_signals : SPRR, star rating, requested per-missions, reported S&P themes, and sentiment compo-sition for a named bot. 2) get_taxonomy_breakdown : frequency distribution of taxonomy themes in a bot’s S&P reviews. 3) get_review_samples : representative S&P-relevant review text. 4) compare_bots : side-by-side comparison of two or more bots. 5) list_high_salience_bots : top-k bots ranked by SPRR. 6) scan_server_bots : accepts a bot manifest and re-turns a server-wide comparative report. Because every classification decision is precomputed, tool calls reduce to indexed lookups. No model inference is per-formed at query time. A user asks a question in natural language and receives a structured summary of review-derived S&P signals in milliseconds. The full algorithmic pipeline is detailed in Appendix A. 

C. Use Cases in Practice 

We present two representative BotLens use cases. 

1) Use Case 1 - Checking a specific bot: Figure 15 depicts an example of checking a bot named “Wick.” BotLens returns its SPRR, average rating, requested permissions, reported S&P themes, and sentiment composition without requiring a platform login and in under 2 ms. As shown, Wick has a 4.3-star rating and 28.9% SPRR, requests ADMINISTRATOR -level access, and is most frequently associated with Data Security, Data Sharing, and Data Exposure. Among its 72 S&P reviews, 52.8% are positive, 40.3% negative, and 6.9% neutral. These signals provide S&P information not captured by the aggregate rating alone. 

Fig. 15: Use Case 1: Bot-level checkup. BotLens presents Wick’s SPRR, requested permissions, reported S&P themes, and sentiment composition. 

2) Use Case 2 - Auditing an entire server: BotLens provides a server administrator a way to quickly check all the bots installed on a server. Particularly, BotLens searches for APP -badged members, paginates through the results, and exports a bot manifest. The manifest is then processed by 

scan_server_bots , which returns a comparative report containing SPRR, reported S&P themes, requested permis-sions, and sentiment composition. Figure 16 shows an example of comparing six bots in a server. Three bots have an SPRR above 10%, indicating that S&P topics appear comparatively frequently in their reviews. The report presents sentiment composition alongside SPRR so that an administrator can dis-tinguish bots whose S&P discussion is predominantly critical from those where it is predominantly positive.

VI. D ISCUSSION 

In this section, we discuss the implications for platform de-signers, bot developers, and end users, then address limitations and future directions. 

A. Key Findings and Implications 1) S&P discussion is meaningful but unevenly distributed: 

The observed pipeline yield of 4.82% indicates that nearly 1 in 20 bot reviews was identified and retained as S&P-relevant. This is a substantive review-derived signal, especially because Top.gg review writers are not specifically asked about S&P. The signal is not uniform across the ecosystem. Confession and anonymity bots exceed 50% SPRR, meaning that our pipeline identified S&P topics in more than half of their re-views. This variation suggests that platform disclosures should account for bot function and the sensitivity of the data handled. 

2) Bot function shapes the disposition of S&P concerns: 

SPRR variation across bot categories suggests that S&P dis-cussion is associated with bot functionality and data sensitivity, though these relationships are associative rather than causal. Moderation and governance bots may raise concerns about surveillance, permissions, exclusion, and misuse because they monitor activity and affect participation. Such bots should follow least privilege, disclose monitored activities, explain automated decisions, and provide appeal mechanisms. Con-fession and anonymity bots raise different concerns because they process sensitive disclosures and identity information. They should disclose whether submitter identities can be recovered, how submissions and metadata are stored, and when they are deleted. Platforms should also provide function-specific notices to affected server members, rather than only to installing administrators. The weak association between permission metadata and SPRR may reflect a privacy-paradox pattern in which users tolerate S&P concerns because of func-tionality, community dependence, or limited alternatives. Be-cause our review data do not observe installation or continued-use behavior, this remains a hypothesis for future user studies. 

3) Star ratings do not capture S&P discussion salience: 

Star ratings summarize multiple dimensions of user experi-ence, including functionality, reliability, and ease of use, and therefore may obscure S&P-related feedback. For example, bots such as Wick (SPRR = 28.9%, stars = 4.3) and Auttaja (SPRR = 23.5%, stars = 3.3) have competitive average ratings while also receiving substantial S&P-related discussion. These findings indicate that aggregate ratings should not be inter-preted as endorsements of a bot’s S&P practices. Platforms could complement general star ratings with a separate indicator that summarizes the pipeline-identified rate and composition of S&P-related reviews. 

4) Permission metadata is a weak standalone predictor: 

RQ3 shows weak associations between user-reported S&P concerns and permission count, administrator status, or in-dividual high-risk permissions. Admin and non-admin bots have nearly identical SPRR (5.69% vs. 5.64%), suggesting that broader permission scope does not necessarily correspond to more S&P discussion. RQ1 instead indicates that bot function and data sensitivity matter more. For example, confession bots may request few permissions while processing highly sensitive disclosures. Permission auditing alone therefore provides an incomplete picture. 

5) User reviews capture what policies miss: Prior work found that only 4.35% of Discord chatbots that request per-missions provide a privacy policy [12]; our dataset shows an even lower rate: just 0.77% of bots reference a privacy policy in their Top.gg listings. Reviews can report perceived or experienced bot behaviors that may not appear in formal policies. Review mining can therefore complement policy analysis on platforms that lack consistent disclosure require-ments, although review claims remain self-reported and are not independently verified. 

6) Affective-expression patterns complement star ratings: 

RQ2 shows that affective-expression patterns broadly align with the polarized star-rating distribution. Neutral and Joy are common across several S&P themes, whereas Anger and Confusion appear more frequently in reviews concerning Data Exposure and DM Privacy. These findings describe affective language in review text and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of users’ underlying emotions or as proof of binary S&P judgments. 

B. Recommendations 1) For Social Platforms with Community-based Bots: 

Platforms should consider presenting a dedicated S&P re-view indicator alongside star ratings, analogous to mobile-app privacy disclosures [59], [9]. Such an indicator could report SPRR as the pipeline-identified rate of S&P-related discussion and distinguish reviews that express concerns from those 12 that provide positive feedback. Platforms could also provide category-specific disclosures for bots that handle sensitive content, such as confession, anonymity, and anti-nuke bots, without treating elevated SPRR as verified evidence of risk. 

2) For Bot Developers: Developers should request only necessary permissions and clearly explain their bots’ data access. The Positive Privacy theme (2.2% of S&P reviews) suggests that users reward transparency and privacy-protective features. 

3) For Administrators and Users: Server administrators should examine both requested permissions and review-derived S&P signals when evaluating bots. BotLens supports server-wide comparison by surfacing SPRR, reported S&P themes, sentiment composition, and requested permissions for installed bots. These outputs can help identify bots that warrant closer examination. The comparatively high SPRR among bots deployed in 100–1K servers also motivates closer examination of this range. 

C. Limitations and Future Work 1) Data Source: Our data are limited to bots on Top.gg, and user reviews are self-reported. Users who write reviews may not represent the broader population, and privacy-concerned users may be overrepresented, which could increase the ob-served pipeline yield. We did not verify whether the claims in individual reviews are factually correct. We could not verify claims retrospectively because historical bot versions, server configurations, logs, and incident context were unavailable. Our findings therefore characterize review writers’ reported experiences and perceptions rather than confirmed bot behav-ior or verified S&P violations. Future work can examine their factual basis through longitudinal, dynamic analysis. 

2) Manual Validation and SPRR Interpretation: Both the teacher model validation (Section III-C3) and S&P candidate validation (Section III-D1) used the same S&P relevance criteria through our 31-theme taxonomy. The teacher model validation involved open-ended labeling of a stratified sample without access to the teacher labels. The candidate validation focused on classifier-selected reviews enriched for predicted S&P relevance. The focused context here may have made subtle S&P content easier to recognize. On the other hand, the open-ended labeling in the teacher model validation was not sensitive to such subtle cases, although the annotators agreed strongly. This provides a plausible explanation for the positive-rate difference between the two validation procedures. Cohen’s 

κ and raw agreement establish labeling consistency within the sample, but not labeling sensitivity, dataset completeness, corpus prevalence, or ground-truth status. The 0.30% teacher-positive rate describes the teacher labels within the 54,942-review subset and is not a corpus-prevalence estimate. The 4.82% SPRR represents the observed pipeline yield, not the true prevalence of S&P relevance. 

3) Generalizability Beyond Discord: The methodologi-cal components of this work are platform-independent. The SPRR/TPR metrics and the BotLens auditing workflow are applicable to other bot marketplaces, messaging-platform app directories, and AI-agent ecosystems. Several findings rest on conditions that are not specific to Discord. In particular, star ratings summarize many dimensions of user experience on any review platform, so their tendency to obscure S&P-related feedback is not Discord-specific. The same applies to the weak association between requested permissions and where pipeline-identified S&P discussion concentrates. Permission models describe capability rather than the sensitivity of the content a bot handles, which is common across app stores and bot platforms. Some results are specific to our setting. The pipeline-identified SPRR values, including the 4.82% ecosystem-wide pipeline yield and the rates above 50% for confession and anonymity bots, reflect Top.gg’s reviewer population and bot composition and would likely differ elsewhere. The nine Discord-specific themes are tied to platform mechanisms such as Gateway Intents, webhooks, and administrator-mediated in-stallation. Platforms without these mechanisms would require different extensions, although the four theme groups and the 20 themes inherited from prior work [39] remain applicable. Discord’s combination of community scale with server-wide installation by an administrator also differs from platforms where bots operate in private workspaces [4]. 

4) LLM for Labeling and Taxonomy Assignment: Residual labeling errors may propagate into downstream analyses. The classifier evaluation reported in Section III-C4 rests on a small number of positive examples, and the human annotations in Section III-C3 show substantial but imperfect agreement. Language identification and translation were automated. Short, informal, or code-switched reviews may be assigned an incor-rect language, and translation errors may propagate into S&P classification, taxonomy assignment, and affective-expression analysis. The taxonomy assignments and affective-expression labels were not independently evaluated against a dedicated ground truth. We therefore interpret the resulting distributions as model-assisted descriptive findings and acknowledge that errors may remain. Future work can explore stronger models and multi-pass/multi-label taxonomy assignment. 

5) Real-Time and In-Situ Auditing: BotLens is a PoC to demonstrate feasibility, not completeness. Its outputs summa-rize SPRR, sentiment composition, reported S&P themes, and requested permissions, but they do not verify the claims in the reviews or determine if a bot is safe. We did not evaluate how BotLens improves decision-making or how users respond to S&P signals at installation. Future work can evaluate BotLens during bot-installation decisions. VII. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we present a large-scale study of user-reported S&P concerns in the Discord bot ecosystem, which offers a baseline for S&P auditing of community-based bots on social platforms. We contribute a 31-theme S&P taxonomy and BotLens, an auditing framework that translates review-derived signals into on-demand decision support. Our findings show that S&P discussion is especially prevalent among confession and anonymity bots. We also find that metadata features such 13 as permission count and administrator status have only weak associations with user-reported S&P concerns. These results indicate that bot function and the sensitivity of the content a bot handles are more informative than permission scope alone. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS 

This section discusses the ethics of our work. 

A. Stakeholder Identification and Impact Analysis 

The stakeholders of this work include (1) the research team, (2) Discord bot users, (3) Discord server administrators, (4) third-party bot developers, (5) Discord and bot-listing plat-forms, and (6) the security and privacy research community. We discuss the potential impact on each group below. 

Research Team: We did not recruit participants, interact with users, or collect data from private Discord servers. We did not access direct messages, server logs, or user accounts. Our analyses used publicly available bot metadata and user reviews from Top.gg. The analyzed materials consisted of publicly visible review text, bot descriptions, permissions, tags, ratings, and other listing-level information. Because public review text may still contain usernames, personal references, or other potentially identifiable information, we excluded such details from the paper and reported findings primarily in aggregate. Our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) formally determined that IRB review was not required for this study under DHHS and FDA regulations. 

Discord Bot Users: Bot users are the people this work is most directly intended to help. When a bot is added to a server, the average member has little control over what it can access or do. Our study analyzes reviews that users have already posted publicly to surface the security and privacy concerns they raise without researcher prompting. The aim is to make those concerns easier to identify and act upon. We report findings in aggregate and avoid drawing attention to individual review writers. 

Discord Server Administrators: Server administrators in-stall bots, and that decision can affect everyone else in the server. This work supports administrators by providing clearer review-derived S&P signals when star ratings or deployment counts are uninformative about S&P practices. BotLens can summarize each installed bot’s SPRR, reported S&P themes, sentiment composition, and requested permissions, helping administrators identify bots that warrant closer examination without treating those signals as verified evidence of risk or misconduct. 

Third-Party Bot Developers: Developers may face reputa-tional harm because individual bots are named in the paper. A reported pattern could be interpreted as an accusation even though our analysis does not establish misconduct. To reduce this risk, we distinguish user-reported concerns and review patterns from verified bot behavior. Where we name a bot, we do so to illustrate a pattern, not to single out a developer. We frame the results as signals that may warrant further examination, not as verdicts. 

Discord and Bot Marketplaces: Our findings identify gaps in how bot platforms communicate security and privacy infor-mation to users. Platforms could use these findings to improve permission disclosures, surface privacy policies more clearly, organize reviews more effectively, or provide stronger decision support. We did not bypass platform protections or collect information from authenticated or private pages. Our crawler accessed public pages and used rate limits to reduce load on Top.gg. 

Security and Privacy Research Community: For the re-search community, this work contributes a method, taxonomy, and dataset for studying user-reported security and privacy concerns in community bot ecosystems at scale. The approach may also be applicable to other bot marketplaces, messaging platforms, or AI-agent ecosystems where users provide public feedback. 

B. Potential Harm and Mitigation 

First, public reviews may still contain sensitive or identi-fiable information. We do not publish personally identifiable information from reviews, and we report findings in aggregate. Second, the work may create reputational risk for bot developers. A bot with a high Security and Privacy Review Rate (SPRR) is not necessarily unsafe or malicious. SPRR measures the proportion of reviews identified and retained as S&P-relevant by our pipeline. It does not measure the factual correctness of the reported claims or the underlying technical behavior of a bot. We therefore frame the findings as observations about user-reported experiences and perceptions rather than as accusations. Third, there is a risk of overgeneralization. A high SPRR means that our pipeline identifies S&P topics more frequently in reviews of that bot or category, while a low SPRR does not guarantee a bot is safe. We treat BotLens as a decision-support tool, not a verdict. Automated classification may also introduce errors. We report the classifier’s measured performance and the uncertainty around it (Section III-C4), manually validated the full set of classifier-positive reviews (Section III-D1), and acknowledge that residual error may remain. 

C. Decision and Research Justification 

The motivation for this work is the gap between what bots can do inside a server and what the people in that server can observe. A bot installed by one administrator can affect other members who may not know what permissions were granted or how their data may be handled. Our study shows that public reviews contain security and privacy concerns that are often not captured by the signals users commonly rely on, such as star ratings, deployment counts, or permission lists. We weighed the contribution of this research against the potential costs to users, platforms, and developers, and we believe the balance supports conducting this work. Users, server administrators, developers, and platforms may benefit from a clearer understanding of where pipeline-identified security and privacy discussion is concentrated, while BotLens demonstrates how these review-derived signals can be made 14 practically accessible. We minimized potential harm by using only public data, excluding identifying details, aggregating re-sults, distinguishing reported concerns from verified behavior, and presenting the results with appropriate caution.
