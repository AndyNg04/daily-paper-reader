## Abstract 

Centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE) has become the dominant paradigm in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), motivating extensive study of what privileged information should be made available to the critic during training. While prior work has examined the role of global state in fully cooperative settings, the design of centralized critics for N -agent ad hoc teamwork—in which dynamically varying set of learning agents must coordinate with an arbitrary set of uncontrolled teammates drawn from an unknown distribution—remains poorly understood. We study this question by comparing two natural classes of advantage estimators: controlled history estimators, which condition only on the observation and action histories of the learner’s own agents, and joint history estimators, which additionally condition on the histories of the uncontrolled teammates. We prove that both estimators are unbiased and characterize the conditions under which the joint history estimate attains strictly lower variance than its controlled counterpart. Empirically, joint history critics match or outperform controlled history critics across a range of NAHT tasks, yielding improved sample efficiency and final returns. Together, these results provide both a theoretical justification and an empirical recommendation for incorporating uncontrolled agent histories into centralized critics for N -agent ad hoc teamwork. 

## 1 Introduction 

Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (CMARL) is a paradigm for learning coordinated team policies on fully cooperative tasks, assuming full control over all agents [Gronauer and Diepold, 2022]. In contrast, ad hoc teamwork (AHT) considers the challenge of training a single agent to collaborate with previously unknown teammates on fully cooperative tasks [Stone et al., 2010]. The recently introduced N-agent ad hoc teamwork (NAHT) problem generalizes both paradigms by considering the scenario where a set of controlled agents must collaborate with dynamically varying numbers and types of uncontrolled teammates without the ability to pre-coordinate. The primary challenges of the NAHT problem are (1) dealing with a changing number of (un)controlled agents, and (2) generalizing to unseen uncontrolled teammate behavior [Wang et al., 2024]. For the NAHT problem, we assume that controlled agents only have access to controlled agent information, as the number of uncontrolled agents changes per episode, and the controlled agents must generalize to differing types of uncontrolled agents without being given direct information about their partners at inference time. Previous successful algorithms for NAHT have chosen to also include this restriction for critics, with algorithms [Wang et al., 2025b, 2024, 2025a] only including controlled agent information in critics. The CTDE paradigm then poses the question of if uncontrolled agent information can be helpful in training while still restricting this information during inference. Since the purpose of critics is to estimate the advantage function, it follows that better advantage estimation should lead to better sample efficiency when training policies. We study this question by comparing two natural classes of advantage estimators: controlled history estimators, which

## 2 Related Work 

Previous algorithms for NAHT rely only on controlled agent information in critics. There is no existing study on including privileged information in centralized critics for NAHT, although this kind of analysis exists for MARL. The most relevant previous work focuses on the variance of the Q function and policy gradients for different types of MARL critics. 

Previous Algorithms for NAHT. POAM [Wang et al., 2024] was the first proposed algorithm for NAHT. It uses decentralized, RNN-based critics that attempt to model the value function using only information from an individual controlled agent. Similarly, the Shapley Machine algorithm [Wang et al., 2025b] uses the POAM critic, but models a different value function per-agent. The MAT-NAHT algorithm [Wang et al., 2025a] is the first to use a single attention-based centralized critic with information from all controlled agents. The same paper introduced Cent Obs POAM, which uses a single centralized RNN-based critic with information from all controlled agents. Since our work is focused on centralized critics, we use MAT-NAHT and Cent Obs Poam for experiments, and abbreviate both methods to “MAT” and “POAM.” 

Centralized Critics in MARL. A separate line of work has analyzed the role of privileged information in centralized critics for cooperative MARL. Lyu et al. [2023] provide an extensive theoretical and empirical study of history-based, state-based, and history-state-based critics for Dec-POMDPs. Among their results, they show that centralized history-based critics yield the same expected policy gradient as decentralized history-based critics but with strictly higher variance, that state-based critics may introduce unbounded bias when used to train history-based policies, and that history-state critics are unbiased but again carry higher variance than purely history-based critics. While their analysis covers what kind of global information should be exposed to the critic in MARL, the NAHT setting introduces a different design choice: whether the critic should condition on the trajectories of agents that are not under the learner’s control . Our work extends the bias-variance style of analysis pioneered by Lyu et al. [2023] to this setting, deriving an unbiasedness result and a variance comparison between controlled-history and joint-history advantage estimators in the NAHT problem. Since we use PPO for optimization instead of the vanilla policy gradient, we focus on analyzing the bias and variance of advantage function. 

## 3 Background 

The N-agent ad hoc teamwork (NAHT) problem is formalized in the framework of Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (Dec-POMDPs) [Bernstein et al., 2002]. A Dec-POMDP is described by the tuple ⟨M, S, {Oi}Mi=1 , A, T, r, γ ⟩. Here, M denotes the number of agents, S the global state space, Oi the local observation space for agent i, and A = QMi=1 Ai the joint action space. Let the notation ∆( •) denote a probability distribution over the space •. The transition function T : S ×A → ∆( S) governs the dynamics, and the reward function r : S ×A → R

provides a shared reward signal. In this work, we examine a control scheme where each agent selects actions according to the stochastic policy πi : Hi × A1 × · · · Ai−1 → ∆( Ai), where Hi is the fixed length history of observations and actions available to agent i. The decision-making problem occurs under the horizon T .The NAHT problem considers a controlled set of agents C, and an uncontrolled set U . The learning algorithm generates policies for agents in C, while uncontrolled agents in U follow fixed, poten-tially unknown policies. Each episode begins with a team sampling process X(U, C ), consisting of two steps: first, the number of controlled agents N < M is sampled, and second, a set of 2N agents is drawn from the controlled set C, while M − N agents are drawn from the uncon-trolled set U . ## 6 Empirical Results 

This section addresses the primary research question of if and when performance differs between joint and controlled critics in the NAHT problem. We compare performance across a variety of games and previously proposed algorithms for NAHT. Across every (game, algorithm) pair, a joint critic is as effective as a controlled critic, and for many scenarios, a joint critic exhibits significantly better performance. 

6.1 Algorithms 

As a first solution method for NAHT, Wang et al. [2024] proposed the Policy Optimization with Agent Modeling (POAM) algorithm. POAM deals with a potentially varying number of controlled agents by employing an independent policy gradient approach, where each agent learns an independent policy while performing teammate modeling of all other agents in the team. Each agent’s policy conditions on the learned teammate representations, thus enabling the policy to approximate the optimal behavior for collaborating with each type of uncontrolled team. Since we study centralized critics in this paper, we adapt POAM to include centralized observations in the same fashion as Wang et al. [2025a], creating the method Cent Obs POAM. We simply refer to the method as "POAM" throughout the paper. Wang et al. [2025a] proposed MAT-NAHT, a transformer-based method adapted from the Multi-Agent Transformer [Wen et al., 2022], in which an encoder-decoder transformer jointly serves as the centralized policy and value network for the controlled subteam. The architecture handles the variable number of controlled agents through attention masking, and the centralized critic is conditioned on the history of observations and actions of all controlled agents. We abbreviate the method to MAT for this paper. 

6.2 Connection to Theory 

All experiments were conducted in an environment that satisfies all assumptions from Section 4. Specifically, controlled critic training runs use GAE estimation and boostrap through the controlled critic while joint critic training runs use GAE estimation bootstrapped through a joint critic. This satisfies Assumption 4.2, Assumption 4.6, and also the stronger condition Assumption 4.8. We first present results for the coordination game described previously. Recall that the optimal policy is to always output {0, 0} for every history, so the optimal policy has 0 entropy. 6.4 Stag Hunt 

In Stag Hunt [Böhmer et al., 2020], N predator agents must capture stags and hares in a grid world. Agents and prey can move along the four cardinal directions or stay still, and agents additionally have a catch action. Stags yield a high reward but require coordinated capture: at least two adjacent agents must simultaneously execute the catch action to capture a stag, while a single adjacent agent suffices to capture a hare. If an agent attempts to capture a stag without sufficient teammates adjacent to the same prey, the team is penalized by the miscapture punishment. Upon a successful capture, the participating agents are frozen for the remainder of the episode and removed from the grid, and the captured prey is removed. Agents observe a (2 k + 1) × (2 k + 1) patch centered on their position, while prey movement is governed by a fixed stochastic policy: prey move to a random unoccupied adjacent cell. ## 7 Conclusion 

We studied the design of centralized critics for N -agent ad hoc teamwork, focusing on whether conditioning the critic on the histories of uncontrolled teammates is beneficial. We compared two natural advantage estimators: a controlled history estimator that conditions only on the learner’s own observation-action histories, and a joint history estimator that additionally conditions on the uncontrolled teammates’ histories. On the theoretical side, we proved that both estimators are unbiased for the controlled advantage, and we derived a variance decomposition that isolates the primary force governing whether the joint history estimator reduces variance: the benefit of subtracting a critic correlated to the action-value function as a baseline. We characterized the conditions under which the baseline-cancellation effect 12 dominates—namely, when the joint Q and V functions are highly correlated across uncontrolled histories and have comparable magnitudes of variation—and illustrated both regimes through a simple coordination game in which the joint baseline can either reduce or increase variance depending on the policy under analysis. We made an argument for the joint history estimator having lower variance on average, providing some intuition for our experimental results. Empirically, joint history critics match or outperform controlled history critics across both the coordination game and the Stag Hunt environment, for both the POAM and MAT base algorithms. In the coordination game, joint critics reach near-optimal policies markedly faster. In Stag Hunt, joint critics yield improved sample efficiency during training and higher returns against uncontrolled agents at evaluation, with statistically significant gains across most uncontrolled agent types tested. Taken together, our theoretical analysis and experimental results provide both a justification and a practical recommendation: when training a centralized critic in the NAHT setting, conditioning on uncontrolled teammate histories is generally beneficial, and the variance decomposition we derive offers a principled lens for understanding when and why this benefit arises. 

Limitations and future work. Our variance analysis assumes access to unbiased value functions; extending these results to learned critics, where approximation error interacts with the variance trade-off, is a natural next step. Furthermore, our Visitation Weighted Advantage Variance argument is also currently a conjecture, supported by experimental evidence but not formally established; proving conditions under which the visitation-weighted variance is provably lower under the joint critic would strengthen the theoretical picture. We also restrict our analysis to the variance of the advantage function, but do not connect the ideas that a lower variance results in greater sampling efficiency. One way to conduct this analysis might be by looking at the variance of the policy gradient.
