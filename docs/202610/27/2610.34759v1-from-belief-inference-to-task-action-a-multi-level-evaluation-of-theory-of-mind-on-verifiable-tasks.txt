Abstract 

Effective collaboration requires language mod-els to understand what others know and be-lieve, and to act accordingly. Models that assume others share their information may omit necessary communication and under-mine coordination. Many theory-of-mind (ToM) benchmarks assess mental-state infer-ence through story questions, but correct an-swers do not establish that models can use these judgments in subsequent tasks. We in-troduce a multi-level evaluation built on exist-ing, verifiable tasks. We extend household col-laboration and calendar scheduling with asym-metric information while retaining their orig-inal constraints and validators. Independent interfaces assess world understanding, infor-mation access, beliefs, decisions, and execu-tion. Controls supply personal evidence or cor-rect beliefs to distinguish state recovery from state use. Across six model configurations, these abilities diverge. On matched schedul-ing cases, Claude recovers all agent beliefs cor-rectly but produces subjectively optimal plans in only 67.9% of cases. In household collabo-ration, supplying beliefs raises Qwen-27B suc-cess from 55.0% to 75.0%, yet Qwen-4B an-swers every fixed supplied-belief source ques-tion correctly while completing only 20.0% of the corresponding continuation condition. These results show why ToM evaluation must connect perspective inference to independently verified decisions and task consequences.

We extend C-WAH household collaboration (Zhang et al., 2024) and CalBench scheduling (Zou et al., 2026), preserving their goals, constraints, and validators while construct-ing personal observation and communication his-tories. Figure 1 illustrates the distinction: an un-informed person’s plan can be correct under their evidence yet fail in the world. Conversely, a suc-cessful plan can happen to satisfy several perspec-tives without recovering all of them. A single outcome cannot explain these cases. Failure may arise from misunderstanding events, misattributing evidence, losing an earlier belief, or planning incorrectly after the state is known. We connect five evaluation targets—world, access, be-lief, decision, and execution—and supply personal evidence or reference beliefs in diagnostic con-trols. Requests are independent, preventing errors in one answer from being mechanically propagated to the next. Our contributions are a task-grounded ToM construction with native reference evidence and verifiable plans, a cross-domain protocol sepa-rating state recovery from state use, and a six-configuration analysis of their behavioral differ-ences. The results expose both strong belief in-ference without optimal planning and near-perfect fixed decisions without successful long-horizon coordination. 

2 Related Work 

Mental-state inference. Cognitive AI connects beliefs and intentions to action understanding (Zhu et al., 2020). ToMi controls story regularities in belief questions (Le et al., 2019). FANToM tests beliefs, information accessibility, and answerabil-ity in multi-party conversations (Kim et al., 2023). BigToM links perceptions, beliefs, desires, and actions through causal templates (Gandhi et al., 2023). Hi-ToM examines higher-order reasoning (He et al., 2023), while ToMBench and OpenToM broaden mental states and contextual diversity (Chen et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). ExploreToM searches programmatically tracked stories (Sclar et al., 2025), and ToMATO constructs questions from role-playing conversations (Shinoda et al., 2025). Percept-ToMi and Percept-FANToM iso-late perception attribution (Jung et al., 2024). We additionally evaluate complete plans under per-sonal constraints and their execution. 

Functional and interactive ToM. Riemer et al. (2025) distinguish predicting opponents from us-ing predictions in games. Collaborative bomb disposal combines beliefs with team outcomes 2(Li et al., 2023). LLM-Coordination diagnoses environment understanding, ToM, and planning (Agashe et al., 2025); instruction inference links intentions to plan feasibility and optimality (Saad et al., 2026). SOTOPIA evaluates interactions with private social goals (Zhou et al., 2024). MuMA-ToM studies observers’ social reasoning about em-bodied agents (Shi et al., 2025). Watch-And-Help and CoELA investigate household collab-oration (Puig et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024), and EnactToM measures partner beliefs and task completion under controlled information (Juneja et al., 2026). We combine dynamic interaction with solver-verified scheduling, comparing inde-pendent interfaces and supplied-state conditions on frozen tasks. Subjective correctness remains separate from actual success. 

Verifiable task evaluation. PlanBench checks action sequences and state changes (Valmeekam et al., 2023); ALFWorld connects textual decisions to embodied execution (Shridhar et al., 2021); AgentBench spans interactive tasks (Liu et al., 2024). AgentAD uses LLM agents to generate satellite-scheduling algorithms (Chen et al., 2026). CalBench adds distributed scheduling, private cal-endars, and displacement-cost optimization (Zou et al., 2026). We retain native constraints and val-idators to assess perspective-dependent plans, dis-tinguishing personal evidence from world knowl-edge. 

3 Problem Formulation 

We focus on first-order belief attribution and use under asymmetric information. Two agents can hold different beliefs about the same world be-cause they obtain different evidence. A model reading their complete story must distinguish what it knows from what each agent knows, retain old states without new evidence, and use the resulting perspectives for decisions. C-WAH and CalBench provide complementary tests. Household actions change the world and generate observations, allowing evaluation of be-lief maintenance during sequential coordination. Scheduling has explicit constraints and optimal costs, allowing complete plans to be checked un-der each person’s information. Both provide task-based standards independent of the model’s verbal explanation. Our extensions retain the original environments, goals, and validators. C-WAH builds personal his-tories from actual routes, physical actions, and delivered messages. CalBench constructs initial calendars and subsequent public or private up-dates whose final calendars equal the frozen source task. Information conditions change which con-straints each person knows, not the original task objective. The evaluation distinguishes recovering personal states, producing decisions correct under those states, and succeeding in the actual world. This distinction separates predicting another person’s informed choice from coordinating a suc-cessful joint action. A plan consistent with an out-dated belief can reveal accurate perspective taking even when it fails physically. A coordinator can instead communicate missing information before assigning work. Silently replacing the person’s be-lief with world truth tests neither operation. In our protocol, subjective planning checks whether the model respects the target perspective, while native collaboration tests whether it can use or change available information to advance a shared goal. Evaluating both prevents correct belief attribution from being confused with endorsement of an inef-fective action. 

4 Evaluation Framework 

4.1 Why Five Targets? 

The decomposition follows information dependen-cies. A model must understand what happened (world ), determine who obtained which evidence (access ), and integrate it over time ( belief ). Ev-idence and belief differ because an older message can be superseded by observation, while lack of ev-idence requires retention. It must then use the re-sulting state to select an action or solve constraints (decision ). Actual execution can succeed, fail, or create new evidence ( execution ). These targets lo-cate behavioral errors, not five internal modules or a difficulty ordering.

Reference states follow task rules rather than model-generated an-swers. 3C-WAH records native physical graphs, per-sonal observations, action feedback, and message delivery. Its symbolic room-level observations re-spect container and possession relations. Direct observation can override a conflicting message; a completed unsuccessful search can yield UN-KNOWN. Without new evidence, a belief persists. Main histories contain physical events and public rules, not observer labels or reference beliefs. Rule replay verifies that the text supports the native ref-erences. Incidental encounters and observation-driven corrections remain valid events. In CalBench, five agents broadcast their initial calendars. Two owners then learn that a mov-able commitment must remain in its slot. PUB-LIC informs the group; PRIVATE informs a shared intermediary. Owners and actual recipients up-date, while uninformed agents retain previous cal-endars. Construction is deterministic from source tasks (Appendix I). Access and belief therefore have different refer-ence semantics. In C-WAH, the access target re-tains positive location assertions a person has ob-tained, including assertions later superseded by ob-servation. The belief target instead identifies the current state after those events. Remembering an old assertion is correct in the former and can be incorrect in the latter. Separate targets distinguish selecting evidence from resolving its temporal con-sequences. 

4.3 Independent Interfaces and Controls 

Seven shared interfaces request world/access, nar-rative beliefs, structured-history beliefs, direct de-cisions, decisions with textual beliefs, decisions with symbolic beliefs, and beliefs/decisions with supplied access. C-WAH adds spatial facts and public-map routes (Table 5). Each request has an independent context and reference target. Supplied access explicitly identifies personal ev-idence while retaining temporal integration. In C-WAH it provides deduplicated positive location as-sertions, not a complete ordered observation his-tory; unsuccessful searches must still be inter-preted from the narrative. In CalBench it provides ordered event identifiers. Supplied beliefs remove state-recovery demands but retain perspective-specific decisions. Narrative/structured histories and textual/symbolic beliefs test representation changes. Comparisons on the same stories distin-guish attribution, maintenance, and constraint use without feeding predicted answers between inter-faces. These controls answer progressively narrower questions. If the relevant evidence is explicitly identified, can the model recover the target belief? If that belief is supplied correctly, can it choose an action appropriate to it? If physical movement suc-ceeds, can it recover the perspectives produced by that movement? Independent requests make dis-agreements observable without an earlier incor-rect answer determining every later input. Native continuation complements these controlled snap-shots by allowing decisions to change the subse-quent history. It measures whether the model can sustain coordination when information acquisition and action selection become its own responsibility. 

4.4 Task Decisions and Execution 

C-WAH fixed decisions specify an actor, ob-ject, and goal and ask for the source in that ac-tor’s belief. Native continuation permits assign-ments, messages, and exploration within the orig-inal task budget. Route interventions compare model-selected and supplied public-graph paths from matched native checkpoints, followed by a belief query scored against each resulting trajec-tory. CalBench requires each proposer to schedule five meetings among five agents over sixteen slots. Entries are free, movable at a cost, or immovable. Every pair of meetings shares a participant, re-quiring distinct slots. A subjective solver mini-mizes displacement cost under the proposer’s cal-endars. Native replay validates relocations and meetings. Independent optimization and replay must agree on reference costs; tied optima are ac-cepted. The same proposal is checked in the ac-tual world. A subjectively correct plan need not be world-feasible. 

5 Experimental Setup 

Configurations. We evaluate Qwen3.5-4B, 9B, and 27B with thinking disabled, plus Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, and GPT-6.1 Sol. Frozen inputs, references, and semantic scoring are shared. Qwen uses temperature 0, top-p 1, and limits of 4096 output tokens for C-WAH and 12288 for CalBench. Closed services request medium reasoning and 32768 tokens with unspecified sam-pling controls. Resources and parameters appear in Appendix C. 4Tasks and common support. C-WAH has 43 configurations across seven houses, fixed seeds, 218 event-selected nodes, and nine interfaces: 1962 fixed requests per model. Direct and supplied-belief continuations each schedule 43 episodes; two route arms each schedule 36 con-figurations. Native technical coverage is 158/158 except 9B’s 154/158. Six-model paired compar-isons use 40 common continuation configurations and 35 route configurations. Episodes have a 250-step budget including preparation and at most 20 continuation decisions. Three deterministic scripts provide 129 shared reference episodes. CalBench has 45 uniform-cost and 45 tiered-cost tasks, each with PUBLIC/PRIVATE stories and seven interfaces: 1260 requests per model. Complete coverage is 1136, 1015, and 1251 for Qwen-4B/9B/27B, and 1260 for each closed con-figuration. The main comparison uses 916 com-mon complete request keys. Cross-interface anal-yses further match stories; public/private compar-isons require both variants. All-valid results and per-interface coverage are separately reported. Matching follows the question being compared. Within a calendar interface, every model answers the same complete request keys. A belief–planning comparison also requires both interfaces for the same story, and a PUBLIC/PRIVATE compari-son requires both variants of the same source task. Consequently, these analyses have different de-nominators even though they begin with the same 90 tasks. Native comparisons similarly require both conditions to be technically valid in every configuration. This prevents differences in ob-served task composition from being mistaken for differences between models or input conditions. 

Scoring. C-WAH uses English histories and scores person–object entries and full decisions. CalBench uses Chinese histories, requires all 80 entries for a correct agent belief, and all five meet-ings for a feasible, minimum-cost proposal. Time-outs/truncations receive bounded retries, adopt-ing the first complete output without selecting by correctness. JSON extraction accepts fences and harmless surrounding text without changing content. Missing targets and semantic errors in complete answers remain incorrect. Incom-plete outputs affect coverage, not capability scores. Nodes cluster within 43 configurations and calen-dar agents/stories within 90 source tasks. We re-port paired counts and configuration means, not significance from repeated entries.

6.2 Using Beliefs in Decisions and Action 

Fixed source selection is nearly solved with refer-ence beliefs: five configurations achieve 218/218 and 9B achieves 216/218. Correct direct choices need not imply complete perspectives: all 145 cor-rect 4B choices coexist with some incorrect belief entries, as do 157 of 158 for 9B (Figure 4). A deci-sion may depend on only one correct entry. Con-versely, Gemini has 26 nodes with a correct entire belief table but an incorrect independent source an-swer. State recovery and state use must both be tested. The partition changes how success should be in-terpreted. For Gemini, 177 correct choices coin-cide with a fully correct belief table and only 11 with partial errors. For GPT, the corresponding counts are 113 and 70. The same successful deci-sion can therefore accompany substantially differ-ent state recovery. These are paired observations at the same nodes, not an assumption that a correct choice requires every unrelated entry to be correct. Native continuation makes the distinction con-sequential (Figure 5). Reference beliefs raise 27B success from 22/40 to 30/40 but 4B only from 5/40 to 8/40. For 9B, success remains 2/40 and 1/40 de-spite near-perfect fixed supplied-belief decisions. In each 9B continuation condition, 35/40 episodes issue no assignments. The model instead attempts extensive exploration (1658 and 1712 commands). Answering a specified source question does not es-tablish the ability to select goals, turn information into assignments, or make progress within a de-cision budget. 6.3 Tracking Perspectives After Action 

All supplied-route transports succeed on the 35 common configurations. Nevertheless, no 4B, 9B, or 27B post-action belief table is entirely correct in that condition. Their pooled-entry accuracies are 53.0%, 61.6%, and 61.0%, respectively. Suc-cessful movement therefore does not demonstrate tracking of what each person learned. Claude’s configuration-mean belief accuracy is 93.9% after model-selected routes but 69.0% af-ter supplied routes (Appendix Figure 10); Gem-ini is perfect in both. A route specifies movement, not automatically its informational consequences. Paths can induce different observations and his-tories. Native references expose this dependence while separating transport success from perspec-tive recovery. The 4B model-selected arm also has three unsuccessful transports, which remain valid task failures.

This distinction matters for interpreting ToM: violating a person’s known constraints, find-ing an unnecessarily costly feasible plan, and using information they never received are different be-havioral failures. A task validator makes the first two measurable rather than reducing every unsuc-cessful proposal to a wrong belief answer. Public/private comparisons use 29 complete source-task pairs. Among them, 9B and GPT lose all five optimal proposals on seven and five tasks, whereas Gemini remains perfect (Appendix Figure 11). The manipulation changes both who knows the constraints and how many subjective problems must be solved. Copying world calen-dars illustrates the need for targeted metrics: on common PRIVATE belief cases it scores 98.5% of entries but only 20.0% of complete agent states and zero stale entries. High ordinary entry accuracy can conceal failure to preserve another’s perspec-tive. Planning scores also require a perspective-sensitive interpretation. Reusing the solver’s world-optimal plan for every agent is subjectively optimal in 466/485 common direct-planning cases (96.1%). Only 19 cases from six source tasks have strictly lower subjective than world-optimal cost. On these cases, Qwen-4B/9B/27B, Claude, Gem-ini, and GPT score 0, 0, 13, 10, 19, and 10 out of 19, respectively (Appendix N.2). This privileged solver reference measures overlap between scoring targets, not model behavior or an equal-compute baseline. Aggregate planning success alone there-fore does not establish use of a personal perspec-tive. 

7 Discussion 

Task-grounded evaluation reveals two gaps. Un-derstanding events does not ensure attributing ev-idence and maintaining personal beliefs. A cor-rect state or fixed decision does not ensure effec-tive planning and coordination. The 4B/9B results make the latter visible: fixed supplied-belief de-cisions are nearly perfect, but actual assignments and task completion remain weak. Closed config-urations also separate calendar-state recovery from optimization and physical success from post-action beliefs. These patterns imply different support needs. Explicit personal evidence can assist attribution but cannot replace temporal integration. Reference beliefs remove state uncertainty, but models must still preserve subjective constraints, choose goals, and execute productive commands. A single ToM or success score cannot identify which support is needed. Independent state questions, perspective-sensitive plans, and native consequences provide complementary evidence. The two domains also clarify when information gathering belongs to the task. In scheduling, each proposer is evaluated under a fixed personal cal-endar: adding an unreceived update changes the problem being solved. In household continuation, communication and exploration are available ac-tions that can legitimately change what an execu-tor knows. The coordinator must decide when cur-rent information supports an assignment and when obtaining or transmitting evidence is useful. The common requirement is to respect the information available at the time of the decision, not to keep an agent uninformed indefinitely. This explains why passive belief questions and active coordination should be complementary tests rather than compet-ing definitions of ToM. For evaluation design, the unit of success should match the claim. Entry accuracy describes lo-cal state recovery, whole-agent accuracy tests a complete perspective, subjective feasibility checks known constraints, and optimality checks efficient 8use of those constraints. Native outcomes then measure whether the resulting policy reaches the shared goal. Reporting these targets together ex-poses both overinterpretations: a successful action does not establish complete mental-state under-standing, and a correct mental-state answer does not establish competent collaboration. 

8 Conclusion 

We extend verifiable household and schedul-ing tasks with asymmetric information, retain-ing native constraints and independent references. Across six configurations, belief inference, use in decisions, and task success diverge in both di-rections. Evaluating collaborative ToM requires checking not only what a model says others be-lieve, but also the plans it constructs from those be-liefs and the consequences of executing them. Our controls make the distinction testable on the same source tasks: supplying evidence probes state re-covery, supplying beliefs probes state use, and na-tive execution checks whether either translates into task progress. 

Limitations 

The evaluation covers six service configurations, 43 household configurations across seven houses, and 90 calendar tasks. Each uses one fixed seed without independent repeated runs. Reasoning budgets, sampling controls, and serving hardware differ. Results establish neither pure parameter-scaling effects nor statistical significance across in-dependent runs. 

Ethical Considerations 

We use simulated households and controlled calen-dars, without new human-subject experiments or personal calendar collection. Synthetic messages do not confer real privacy authorization. Task scores cannot establish reliability in real collab-oration or replace consent and verification. Re-source provenance and licensing are described in Appendix H.

I.2 CalBench Story Construction 

Frozen source files contain 45 uniform-cost and 45 tiered-cost tasks, with 30 each at easy, medium, and hard difficulty. Each retains five agents, six-teen slots, five meetings, native commitment costs, meeting participants, and its seed. Within each cost group, tasks are indexed by source-file order from i = 0 . Change owners are i mod 5 and (i +1) mod 5, and the intermediary is (i + 2) mod 5.Roles are fixed before model calls. For each owner, immovable commitments in the final calendar are listed in slot order. We select in-dex ⌊i/5 ⌋ mod k, where k is the owner’s number of immovable commitments. Initially, the story makes these two commitments movable, preserv-ing their original costs and all other entries. Each owner later learns that the commitment must re-main in its original slot and sends an update. The final world calendar equals the source task. Events E01–E05 broadcast initial calendars. E06 and E08 are the two private changes, and E07 and E09 their messages. PUBLIC delivers both messages to all agents. PRIVATE delivers each only to its sender and the common intermediary. The variants share physical changes and differ in recipients. Each task has two stories, with indepen-dent requests for seven interfaces. In PRIVATE, the two owners and intermediary know different updates, while the other two agents retain their ini-tial calendars. 16 J Native Evidence and Reference Belief Rules 

J.1 C-WAH Observations, Messages, and Search 

Personal observations are sampled only at recorded joint-step boundaries. A person occupies the room recorded at that boundary. Their native symbolic local view contains rooms and entities connected to it through INSIDE relations, except entities whose immediate INSIDE parent is not a room and lacks the OPEN state. Objects held by that person are included. The rule checks the immediate parent rather than recursively simulating visual occlusion. A location relation supplies evidence only when both endpoints are visible. Possession takes prece-dence over storage, and storage uses the immedi-ate parent rather than its room. Successful manip-ulation reveals the resulting object location to its actor. Co-presence in a room does not automati-cally reveal closed-container contents. ON and IN-SIDE are distinct. Physical feedback to the coor-dinator is not automatically delivered to executors, and imagined intermediate positions generate no observations. Beliefs update through personal actions, actual observations, and delivered messages. A received assertion can induce a false belief. In the same ex-change, direct observation overrides a conflicting message. A later assertion can update an older be-lief. A completed search that finds the object ab-sent rules out the believed source. Without another learned source, belief becomes UNKNOWN. UN-KNOWN means the source is undetermined, not that the object does not exist. Access scoring retains each person’s positive lo-cation assertions for each object, including super-seded assertions, with duplicates removed. Neg-ative search outcomes are excluded from this set. Belief is the current reference state after temporal updating. Access asks which location evidence the person has obtained, while belief asks where they now think the object is. 

J.2 CalBench Reference States and Subjective Planning 

The initial five broadcasts give everyone the five initial calendars. A personal change updates only its owner. Group messages update the correspond-ing entries for all recipients, while private mes-sages update only sender and recipient. Messages are true when sent. Agents remember accurately and retain earlier values without new evidence. References follow chronological event replay. CalBench access targets are ordered lists of orig-inal event identifiers. Sending, receiving, and per-sonal changes count. A later relay does not addi-tionally grant access to an original event the agent did not experience. These lists differ from C-WAH’s deduplicated sets of location assertions, so access scores are not pooled across domains. Supplied-belief conditions show actual and cor-rect subjective calendars with their roles dis-tinguished.
