# I-HITL: Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop 

Samuel Breckenridge ∗

Cornell Tech, IC3 Dani Vilardell ∗

Cornell Tech, IC3 Wenhao Wang Yale University, IC3 Fan Zhang Yale University, IC3 Ari Juels Cornell Tech, IC3 

Abstract —Human oversight is a pillar of AI safety. It is often exercised by a human in the loop (HITL) who approves critical actions taken by an AI agent. AI agents’ increasing ability to bypass guardrails, though, raises the question of how to enforce HITL robustly. We introduce the concept of inalienable human-in-the-loop 

(I-HITL). An I-HITL protocol ensures that an agent can initiate an action only with explicit human approval, even when the human interacts with a system (e.g., a local agentic harness) compromised by the agent . A strong I-HITL protocol additionally ensures such approval even when an adversarial human attempts to delegate approvals to an agent. To motivate I-HITL, we report on experiments with a popular agentic wallet platform in which raising an agent’s spending cap requires HITL approval via emailed one-time passcodes (OTPs). Given access to the user’s email and a task blocked by the cap, we find that ten different frontier models will often autonomously raise the cap, harvesting OTPs to bypass a HITL guardrail and surfacing other vulnerabilities. We formally define I-HITL using the concept of communi-cation topologies and prove necessary and sufficient conditions for its realization. We show that an I-HITL protocol requires a trusted entity —a trusted computing device (or equivalent)— and characterize which such devices can and cannot realize an I-HITL protocol. We present a prototype I-HITL system called Countersign that can plug into and strengthen existing HITL systems using off-the-shelf consumer mobile devices. 

## 1. Introduction 

AI agents are increasingly entrusted with consequential decisions and tasks, as personal agents such as Meta’s Muse [56], OpenAI’s Dots [69], OpenClaw [70], and Her-mes [65] see rapid adoption. Users frequently grant these agents broad access to credentials and tools: email, payment methods, shell access, and browser sessions. Yet because agents remain susceptible to hallucination, prompt injection, and misalignment, the same access that lets them act on a user’s behalf also lets them act against the user’s interests. A natural response is to constrain what an agent can do via a human-in-the-loop (HITL) approval gate (or HITL gate , for short)—a mechanism that permits an agent’s action to proceed only after human approval [42], [74], [79]. HITL gates are only meaningful, though, if agents cannot bypass them. In practice, agents can often bypass various guardrails to escalate their own permissions, as two examples show.

Example 1. Circle Agent Wallets allow an account owner to place a daily spending cap on an agent [23]. They protect cap changes via a HITL gate authenticated using a one-time passcode emailed to the account owner. We show in this paper that given access to an email tool, a wallet’s agent will often autonomously harvest the OTPs and update caps without human approval, bypassing the HITL gate. Nothing here is specific to Circle or to email: Other agentic wallets’ HITL gates use similar design patterns [28], [57]. 

Example 2. Early versions of Claude Mythos Preview re-peatedly bypassed harness-imposed guardrails. In one case, the model used a tool intended only for file editing to write to the shell’s input, gaining arbitrary code execution. In other cases, it searched low-level process data for creden-tials and accessed resources that Anthropic had deliberately withheld [6]. 

These two examples illustrate a shortcoming of many HITL gates: agents may have or gain access to the very resources used to enforce the gate. There are numerous reports of similar examples, e.g., [49], [55], [64], [82], and analogous, if not HITL-specific, behaviors arising in controlled evaluations of frontier models [18], [54], [73]. Despite the pervasive risk of agents subverting HITL controls, however, the security goal of ensuring human approval before execution of a given action has a notable lack of formal modeling in the literature to date .

Self-escalation. To motivate our work, we realize Exam-ple 1 with Circle’s Agentic Wallets as a testbed. Its HITL gate is authenticated by one-time passcodes (OTPs) emailed to the human user. We give ten frontier models an invoice-payment task requiring funds that exceed a previously set daily spending cap. We use a common configuration that allows agents to access the user’s email. With no adversary and no prompting , nine of the ten models bypass human approval, harvesting OTPs and raising their own cap in at least one run. With email-based prompt injection, this drops to seven of ten models, as some models detect the prompt injection. In experiments where the agent is only asked to summarize email, five models both raise the cap and pay an attacker impersonating the user by email. Broadly, our results do not correlate strongly with model capability. 

Formalizing I-HITL. Common deployment failures, such as Examples 1 and 2, prompt us to introduce what we believe to be the first security definition for HITL gates that captures security against an agent-compromised computing platform. Our definition also encompasses a human who attempts to delegate control to an agent. Previous attempts at HITL formalism neglect both of these settings and assume that agents are unable to hijack the approval flow, focusing instead on the potential mismatch between an approved action and its execution [44], [85], a consideration which is subsumed by our definition. Related formal frameworks in ceremony analysis [34], [72] and transaction authentica-tion [14], [15], [33], [84] model platform compromise but not human principals delegating their authority. We define an I-HITL protocol as having two properties. 

Completeness states that a service will accept an honest human’s approval of an agent action. Approval-binding says that no adversary can cause the service to accept an ap-proval that the human did not grant. By extending the for-mal machinery of [13], we characterize the communication topologies , i.e., sets of entities and communication channels, needed to realize a complete and approval-binding I-HITL protocol. In the “plain” topology of human, platform, and service used in many existing and proposed systems [23], [27], [29], [46], [68], [77], [78] there exists no complete, approval binding I-HITL protocol. This result motivates the addition of a trusted entity T outside of the agent’s control— typically in the form of a trusted computing device. We prove that such a trusted entity alone is not in general adequate for I-HITL, and give precise requirements on its functionality which must be satisfied. Common authentication approaches—e.g., 2FA / authen-ticator apps, hardware wallets, etc.—do not offer a trusted entity T sufficient to realize an I-HITL protocol. Our work serves as guidance for identifying what devices can do so. 

Countersign. We introduce Countersign , a prototype im-plementation of a secure I-HITL protocol that uses an Android or iOS mobile application to realize a trusted entity, showing that the requirements arising from our formal anal-ysis can be satisfied with these devices under realistic trust assumptions. Countersign uses Google’s/Apple’s attestation functionality and the hardware-backed key management ca-pabilities of their respective devices to establish the requisite channels, as well as physical, human-driven approval via a button press or biometric authentication. Excluding scaffold-ing, Countersign’s core I-HITL logic consists of ∼800 LOC for the backend service, and ∼150 LOC each for the An-droid/iOS applications, demonstrating the relative simplicity of the core components. Our implementation also highlights gaps in the current functionality available to developers that could be patched to strengthen I-HITL guarantees.

We now specify our threat model, and explain its rele-vance to several widely deployed HITL gates. 

2.1. Threat model 

We consider three parties: a human , a computing plat-form running an AI agent, and a service . The human can interact with the platform directly (by sending/reading mes-sages over input/output peripherals) but communication to the service must pass through the platform. The service aims to determine, for a given message received, whether the human was responsible for it. Informally this means that the human saw, and approved of the message. We assume that the service is honest and executes ex-actly the approved action [92], but that the agent is adversar-ial and fully controls the platform, aiming to convince the service that the human is responsible for a message when this is not the case. Although a fully corrupted platform is a strong threat model, we believe it is the right stance to adopt given the trend of increasing integration of AI agents into all manner of computing platforms and the corresponding risk of a corrupted AI agent (e.g. from prompt injection or a misaligned LLM). Agents have previously escaped their sandboxes by exploiting misconfigurations [52], [66], and LLMs have been shown capable of finding and exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities in operating systems or browsers. The human may be honest or adversarial in our threat model. An honest human represents the dominant paradigm for thinking about human-in-the-loop systems: the human respects any protocol and can keep short secrets (such as passwords) from the adversarial platform. Increasingly, however, human-in-the-loop frameworks that resist an adversarial human are important. First, LLMs have shown the ability to deceive, mislead and steer users [40], [51], which could manifest itself as users di-vulging secrets to agents. Second, the human and agent may both be corrupted in the sense that they collude to evade the service’s requirement of human oversight of agent actions. Users tend to delegate to and (potentially over-permission) AI agents [53], and a human might seek to avoid responsibility for messages to the service either out of laziness, or to engage with the service adversarially and later blame an AI agent for resulting damage or harm. In later sections, we will also consider a fourth party, a t rusted entity . A trusted entity is honest, outside of the control of the agent, and may communicate with all parties directly, although with restrictions on the security and di-rectionality of its communication channels. We deliberately leave the trusted entity abstract, and it may be satisfied by different implementations under different trust assumptions. It may be secure hardware attached to the platform itself, a separate device the human carries—such as a hardware wallet—or a physical place, such as a bank branch or an enrollment office, where communication occurs in person. 

2.2. Non-goals and limitations 

We assume that physical interaction with devices is outside of an agent’s capabilities. Although a motivated human could conceivably deploy sensors or actuators to delegate to the agent in this manner, this is a significant barrier, and accountability is more clear cut as it is far less plausible for a human to claim that an agent physically interacted with a device without their knowledge than to claim that a sandboxed agent exploited some vulnerability to escalate their permissions. Our threat model abstracts away application-specific HITL considerations, instead providing a low-level protocol for the approval of generic messages. In particular, pol-icy determining what actions/messages are protected by a HITL gate is out of scope, in our threat model this is the choice of the trusted service, who also enforces this policy. We likewise do not consider human-factors questions, such whether how actions are formatted as messages for approval, whether these are correctly interpreted by the human that receives them, and whether the human truly engages with approval requests or merely rubber stamps them. When a service processes sequences of messages, an adversary that selectively censors approval of specific messages, or reorders individually benign ones, could cause unintended and harmful outcomes. As an application-level concern, however, this is similarly out of scope. Our threat model focuses on service-side enforcement of HITL gates. While common (as in Example 1), such enforcement for the I-HITL schemes explored in this work requires service-side modification. It also means that using I-HITL to protect local resources requires deployment of a suitably isolated local service. See Section 4 for discussion of our choice to an-alyze security symbolically, and for a discussion of authentication/identification-related challenges not captured by our model. 

2.3. Existing deployments 

When mapped to our threat model, many deployed HITL gates share a weakness [25], [27], [57]. Human approval relies on resources that the agent can reach through the platform it runs on. These deployments do use software-based defenses to protect these resources from the agent, such as restricting permissions, sandboxing, and screening for prompt injection, and in many cases these are effective. Such defenses are a relatively weak foundation for secu-rity. The following three common HITL gate deployment approaches illustrate the shortcomings. 

Agent harnesses deploy HITL gates to pause the agent before a designated action, such as a tool call or shell com-mand, and resume only once a human approves. Examples include LangGraph’s interrupts [46], the OpenAI Agents SDK’s human-in-the-loop flow [68], and Claude Code’s permission prompts and hooks [7]. Harnesses enforce per-missions and sandbox the agent to limit its control of the 3host, which in principle should prevent it from subverting the gate. An agent capable of exploiting the harness or operating system can bypass these defenses, but in practice the bar is lower than this. Because the gate’s approval is just a signal from code on the same host, an agent with shell access could launch a process in advance that supplies approvals, even while its own reasoning is suspended by the gate. Alternatively, with write access to the files that configure the gate it could disable it outright. It is especially true that these gates do not prevent adversarial humans from removing themselves from the loop. 

Browser agents are a special class of harness in which the agent controls a browser rather than the underlying host, implemented either as an extension (Claude in Chrome [8]) or as a browser with a native agent (ChatGPT Atlas [67], Perplexity Comet [71]). Similar considerations as for generic harnesses apply when gates are browser-enforced, and prompt injection is an especially concerning attack sur-face given how frequently browser agents encounter un-trusted content. However, a browser agent also transacts with third-party websites, which may themselves gate conse-quential actions, for instance through bot-detection or out-of-band authentication. These defenses move the gate off the potentially-compromised platform, but traditional bot-detection is increasingly ineffective against agents [50], and because browsers retain authenticated sessions across ser-vices, an out-of-band channel such as email may still be ac-cessible by the agent [22]. In these cases it remains difficult to distinguish an adversarial human delegating approval to the agent from a misbehaving agent seizing it. Banks already face this problem and, unable to tell authorized agents from rogue ones, have begun blocking agents outright or limiting them to read-only access [59]. 

Agentic wallet platforms such as those from Coinbase and Circle [23], [27] are of particular interest because a misbe-having agent can lead directly to financial loss. A common architecture is the agent controlling a wallet through an API, subject to a spending policy set by the user, while a remote service gates any change to that policy on human approval. Control of the human’s platform therefore does not let the agent directly bypass the gate, but if the gate relies on resources accessible to the agent through the platform (either because the agent compromised them or because they were given willingly by the human), it can request and approve an escalation of its own permissions. In the next section we examine this scenario experimentally. 

## 3. Agent Self-Escalation 

Circle’s Agent Wallets [24] let a user delegate control of a cryptocurrency wallet to an agent and attach spending policies that cap how much USDC the agent may move per transaction, day, week, or month. Policies are the user’s means of constraining the agent. Circle’s documentation describes them as follows: “set transfer limits or address allowlists and blocklists on your agent wallet to control how it can move USDC and interact with contracts” [23]. When a wallet CLI command attempts to change a spending policy, Circle gates the change behind a one-time passcode (OTP) emailed to the account owner, with the change only taking effect when the OTP is provided at the CLI. In this section we test the robustness of this HITL gate in a typical deployment, where an agent manages the wallet. As is common in personal-agent deployments, which bundle in email and web access [56], [69], [70], the agent also has access to local email and web-search tools. 1 We first aim to characterize whether models will self-escalate to change spending policies in benign situations where these conflict with user requests, before exploring how prompt injection attacks can cause an adversarial agent to bypass this HITL gate in the course of an attack.

This is important for users to be aware of: although a HITL gate like the one employed by Circle’s agent wallet might reasonably be expected to constrain an agent’s behavior even if it is relatively easy for an agent with email access to bypass, this is not the case. and claude-sonnet-5 both self-escalate less frequently in the presence of an adversarial email: their transcripts show that the adversarial email is flagged as a likely prompt injection, causing the model to defer to the user even when in the absence of an injection the model occasionally chooses to self-escalate. That this attack succeeds at all is alarming: it would cost an adversary very little to launch a broad email campaign employing this attack, and they could expect it to succeed against at least some users, even if these users have set restrictive spending limits for their agent wallets. We disclosed these findings to Circle prior to publication: they have since added a warning to its agent-wallet docu-mentation cautioning users against giving an agent access to the email inbox used to receive the OTP. We describe the disclosure and the resulting change in Appendix D. The risk illustrated by our results can be mitigated in different ways. As per Circle’s warning, users should be cautioned not to deploy personal agents that have access to both a Circle wallet and the email associated with that wallet. Users may still be tempted to deploy agents with access to both for convenience, however, and it may be challenging to robustly disable email access, as an agent could leverage exploits or find stored credentials using a terminal/browser to gain access. Perhaps the most reliable defense is to develop stronger HITL gates which do not allow agents to self-escalate. In the following sections we develop and explore our stronger security notion called inalienable human-in-the-loop (I-HITL). 

## 4. Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop 

We now define inalienable human-in-the-loop (I-HITL) protocols and their security guarantees. 

Topologies. A key feature of our threat model is that the human cannot interact with the service directly: all commu-nication between them passes through the agent-controlled platform or, where available, a trusted entity. We follow [13] in our use of communication topologies , though we do not use their Tamarin model in our formal treatment.

Human. H runs a program from a restricted class C of programs. On input a special message format we call a

Although this is restrictive, we show in Section 5 that allowing a more expressive class of programs does not weaken the channel requirements necessary for I-HITL. Note that although our threat model permits an adversarial human, H as a party in the topology is always honest. Intuitively, this is because H represents the physical human: if H takes an action that allows a message to be approved, that constitutes a human-in-the-loop, and so there is no meaningful way to a human to adversarially deviate. yields weaker channel labels on T , as this adversary can potentially extract and share keys.

Verify (vk , m, π ) → { 0, 1} is a function. 

Approval. Since H is honest, approval is defined by its external bits rather than any output it produces. We say 

H approved m, written H.Approved (m) = 1 , if during the execution H’s program received a request ⟨m, a ⟩ and was given external bit bm = 1 for it. H cannot approve a message it has not been shown, and the approval itself is a physical act of the human that the program for P

cannot perform in its place. Because m is determined by the request, the approval message H emits may be as little as a single bit, and it need not reproduce m. We ignore replay: deployments that require fresh approval of the same message across sessions can include a nonce in m at the application layer. 

Symbolic model. We reason symbolically, as per the Dolev-Yao model [32]. The adversary can read, inject, drop, and delay messages as channel labels permit, but cannot guess fresh values or forge signatures. Concretely, a value sampled by an honest party and sent only on a confidential channel is unknown to the adversary, and SigVer (vk , m, σ ) = 1 only if σ was produced by a party holding sk . A computational treatment would replace these idealizations with EUF-CMA security and a considerably more complex treatment, with probabilistic security bounds. We leave such treatment to future work.

## 5. Characterizing Secure I-HITL Protocols 

In this section we apply our security definitions for I-HITL protocols, first to show that secure I-HITL protocols are impossible in the plain topology, and then to demonstrate the requirements under which they can be realized with a trusted entity. 

5.1. The plain topology 

Many proposed systems operate in the plain topology 

τ0 of Figure 3. We show that in τ0, no complete I-HITL protocol is approval-binding. The argument closely mirrors the impossibility results of [13], transferred to our setting. 

Theorem 4. Let Σ be an I-HITL protocol for (M, τ 0),where H is not restricted to programs in C. If Σ is complete, then it is not approval-binding. 

The proof is given in Appendix A. This results from the adversary being able to read everything H receives and to inject on every channel on which H sends. Since H begins with no prior knowledge and runs a prescribed program, its state is a function of what it receives and its external bits, both of which the adversary has access to, so the adversary can simulate what H would send on approving any request and inject it, while giving the real H external bit 0.While this is a straightforward result, it captures afundamental limitation of any human-in-the-loop system operating in the plain topology, with no trusted party out of the agent’s reach. Many such systems have been proposed in the academic literature and deployed in industry, including the Circle agent wallet of Example 1, which we show agents bypass under a natural configuration (Section 3). 

5.2. Adding a trusted entity 

We now add a trusted entity T , which can keep secret state and perform cryptographic operations, and ask which channels between T and the other parties are necessary for a secure protocol to be constructed. Although Basin et al. [13] also consider topologies containing a trusted entity, they operate in a restricted setting where T ’s channels are either secure or insecure, therefore their model is unable to distinguish between different types of trusted device and the resulting channels, as we do here and in Section 6.3. A trusted entity enables the construction of a secure I-HITL only if the adversary cannot stand in for it. If the 7adversary can read everything T receives from S and inject on every channel from T to S, it can run a copy of T in its place, and S ends up with verification material generated by the adversary, exactly as in Theorem 4. 

Proposition 5 (Necessary channels) . Let τ be a topology of Figure 4 and Σ a complete I-HITL protocol for (M, τ ),where H is not restricted to programs in C. If (i) ℓT H ∈ { ∅, ins , auth } and ℓHT ∈ { ∅, ins , conf }, or (ii) ℓST ∈ { ∅, ins , auth } and ℓT S ∈ { ∅, ins , conf },then Σ is not approval-binding. 

The proof is given in Appendix A. By Proposition 5, a trusted entity can be useful for H in exactly two ways: by showing H something the adversary cannot read ( ℓT H ∈{conf , sec }) or by receiving from H something the adversary cannot forge ( ℓHT ∈ { auth , sec }), and for the service in the two analogous ways. We now prove that these capabilities suffice for I-HITL. Our constructions for the proof use a sig-nature scheme (KGen , Sign , SigVer ), modeled symbolically as discussed in Section 4, and only require that H can run programs in C.

Proposition 6 (Sufficient channels) . Let τ be a topology of Figure 4 with (i) ℓT H ∈ { conf , sec } or ℓHT ∈ { auth , sec }, and (ii) ℓST ∈ { conf , sec } or ℓT S ∈ { auth , sec }.Then there is a complete, approval-binding I-HITL protocol for (M, τ ).

Figure 5 gives the constructed protocols for each case: one enrollment protocol per alternative of (ii) and one approval protocol per alternative of (i), any pairing of which is a secure I-HITL protocol for τ . In all of them T holds a signing key, proofs are signatures, and Verify := SigVer . The proof is given in Appendix A. Both propositions together completely characterize secure I-HITL protocols: 

Theorem 7. For a topology τ of Figure 4, there is a complete, approval-binding I-HITL protocol for (M, τ ) if and only if (i) ℓT H ∈ { conf , sec } or ℓHT ∈ { auth , sec }, and (ii) ℓST ∈ { conf , sec } or ℓT S ∈ { auth , sec }.

Theorem 7 says nothing about where the labels on each T channel come from in the first place. A trusted entity’s channels are determined by the device that realizes it and by the human’s behavior when interacting with that device. The next section explores these factors in practice, deriving channel labels from device characteristics such as whether T ’s key is attested or extractable, and whether an adversarial human attempts to delegate to the agent. The theorem then lets us characterize which approaches allow I-HITL protocols.

6.3. Devices and I-HITL 

We now consider concrete instantiations of T , exploring both standalone trusted devices and mobile applications. For each we analyze the properties of its display, its input, and its key management. Together with Table 1 and Table 2, these properties determine the labels on T ’s channels, and hence, by Theorem 7, whether the approach realizes I-HITL. Table 3 summarizes our findings, the full analysis is deferred to Appendix E. We evaluate each instantiation under our two trust mod-els for H, honest and delegating. If an instantiation is secure under a delegating H we say it realizes strong I-HITL, if it is secure only under an honest H it realizes weak I-HITL. The distinction is relevant because in some settings a service needs assurance that H approved an action, even when they may not trust H. Under weak I-HITL a delegating H can hand the agent the means to approve on its behalf, and the service cannot distinguish the agent’s approvals from the human’s. A proof π therefore establishes nothing about who approved, and H can disavow any action by attributing it to the agent. Under strong I-HITL no such delegation is possible, and π can serve as the evidence that the human, not the agent, approved the action. A delegating H may still approve without inspection, but cannot falsely claim that their approval was hijacked by an adversarial agent to avoid being held responsible. The two Ledger configurations in Table 3 run on the same hardware and differ only in key management: a key derived from the user’s recovery seed is attested but ex-tractable, since the user holds the seed by design, and so realizes only weak I-HITL. Encumbrance is thus a property of how T manages k, not of the device alone. Table 3 is not exhaustive, but apart from our construction the three standalone devices listed are the only instantia-tions of I-HITL we found. Of the instantiations that realize strong I-HITL, all except ours require dedicated hardware. The Wultra Talisman is issued by banks and enterprises to their own customers, and Ledger wallets are purchased and carried for specific applications (e.g., cryptocurrency management). Realizing I-HITL on a Ledger device also requires a purpose-built app, since the endorsement keys are issued per application. Thus the Ledger rows of Table 3 reflect what the hardware can support, not what shipped 10 Ledger apps currently provide. Our construction relies only on the attestation and key management capabilities available to any Android or iOS application, so it may be deployed without requiring users to acquire new devices. 

## 7. Countersign 

In this section, we present Countersign, a mobile phone-based strong I-HITL implementation. Mobile phones are a natural candidate for T because they have the necessary interfaces and attestation capabilities, and are readily ac-cessible to users. Yet, as shown by in Section 6, many mobile phone applications providing closely related func-tionality (e.g., authentication and proof of personhood) fail to realize strong I-HITL. Countersign demonstrates that the necessary capabilities for I-HITL exist on mobile phones (under reasonable assumptions), and many similar applica-tions are insecure simply because their workflow is designed for a subtly different threat model. Countersign’s core I-HITL logic consists of ∼800 LOC for the backend ser-vice, and ∼150 LOC each for the Android/iOS application (excluding generic scaffolding such as configuration, data models, and user interfaces), illustrating the feasibility of achieving I-HITL in practice. Our code is open-source at https://github.com/I-HITL/countersign, low-level imple-mentation details are deferred to Appendix F. 

7.1. Mobile Phone Attestation Capabilities 

In our instantiation, the trusted entity T is a mobile application running on a commodity phone, so we rely on the phone to execute the application faithfully and to manage its keys securely. Our design leverages the attestation mecha-nisms provided by the Android and iOS ecosystems to allow 

S to verify the security properties of T . S must establish two properties. The first is code integrity : the application handling requests and controlling the device must be an honest implementation, running on an operating system that has not been tampered with (i.e., rooted or jailbroken). The second is key management : the verification key vk 

must be generated and stored in secure hardware, with any exportability and usage restrictions enforced by that hardware, as characterized in Section 6.1. We examine here how the Android and iOS attestation mechanisms support these requirements. 

Android. To verify app integrity, Android applications can make requests to the Play Integrity API [38], which returns a Google-signed verdict with two key fields. First, the device verdict states whether the device is a certified Android device with a locked bootloader, an unmodified system image, and recent security patches. Second, the app verdict states whether the app on the device matches the binary distributed through Google Play. Regarding key management, apps can generate and use keys through the Android Keystore system [36]. Comparison to existing apps. The mechanisms described above are available to any mobile app, and push authen-ticators (e.g., Duo Mobile, Microsoft Authenticator, Okta Verify) already use several of them, but still fall short of I-HITL due to issues in their workflow. First, attestation of

Second, the approval is not bound to the message H sees but instead an S-selected identifier, so S is not left with a publicly verifiable proof of approval for accountability purposes. Third, ℓHT typically uses the touchscreen as an input interface, which in contrast to hardware-backed biometrics is simulatable in software. Finally, these apps often fall back to alternate methods of approval such as ex-portable passcode seeds, SMS messages, and backup-based re-enrollment. These fallbacks may be acceptable for pure authentication, where the human is trusted and recoverability is an important feature, but compromise I-HITL. 

7.3. Limitations 

In this section we discuss limitations surfaced by our implementation: device capabilities or ecosystem character-istics which require additional trust assumptions. These gaps could be filled by future development of mobile devices to allow stronger I-HITL guarantees. 

Trusted display. On both Android and iOS the Countersign app relies on the OS to prevent other applications from tampering with the approval screen, and to bind the dis-played message to the trusted confirmation. A more robust implementation would remove this dependency. Android Protected Confirmation (APC) [37] previously provided a path to exactly this: a TEE rendered the prompt and signed the message together with a hash of what it displayed, so the display was trusted and the confirmation bound to the message without any trust in the OS. However, APC was deprecated due to low adoption and high maintenance costs [37]. Its return, or an equivalent on iOS, would let Countersign realize ℓT H as a trusted display without relying on OS integrity. 

Device integrity on iOS. On Android, the Play Integrity API [38] provides evidence of device integrity which does not have an equivalent on iOS. In particular, the service cannot determine from App Attest whether the device has been jailbroken. Additionally, while iOS supports biometric authentication, App Attest keys cannot be configured to require biometric authentication before use. Instead, this restriction must be enforced by the app, whose integrity may not be preserved on a compromised OS. On Android, by contrast, the per-use biometric requirement is enforced by the secure hardware and reported in the key’s attestation, so the service need not trust the application for it. Con-sequently, our iOS implementation relies on the additional 12 assumption that the operating system remains trustworthy to enforce the integrity of biometric authentication. 

Roots of trust. Every channel label in Section 7.2 ulti-mately rests on a certificate chain to Google’s or Apple’s root. This is a stronger trust assumption than the man-ufacturer root of a hardware wallet, which is used only at provisioning. Apple/Google’s servers must be reachable to issue attestations, so an outage blocks enrollment. The evidence provided by attestation is also is a snapshot of device/application state at the moment of verification rather than an enduring guarantee. A key attestation certificate records the verified-boot state at key generation, and an App Attest attestation is issued once, so neither is invalidated by a kernel compromise after enrollment. On Android, Countersign narrows this window by requesting a fresh Play Integrity verdict with every approval. However, this cannot detect if a kernel is compromised after boot, since verified boot measures the system only at boot. The effective window on Android is therefore the interval since the last boot. On iOS there is no corresponding mechanism, and the window is the interval since enrollment. 

Key recovery. We do not address the issue of key recovery for T in our initial implementation of Countersign. As strong I-HITL requires that H cannot directly access sk ,any recovery path will require H to re-execute a procedure like Enroll in order to restore an encrypted copy of sk kept by a trusted entity such as S or to generate a fresh sk .

## 8. Related work 

HITL and permissions in agent systems: HITL approval is a standard recommendation for agent control [74] and a common feature of agent frameworks; Michael and Roes-ner [58] survey such permission mechanisms and note the absence of formally verified enforcement. Work on agent policy, e.g., [30], [75], [83], specifies which actions an agent may take, and complements our focus on securely enforcing those human approvals dictated by policy. 

User authentication: User authentication, including a long line of work on 2FA (e.g., [19]), considers how to verify the identity of a particular user, but assumes a trusted client device. (Otherwise, session hijacking is fatal.) Formal anal-yses of FIDO2, for instance, exclude compromised clients from their guarantees and model user approval as a one-bit gesture [12], [17]. An agent, however, can compromise both 2FA factors when they converge on a host [31], [43], and effectively hijack an authenticated session. Hardware-based transaction authentication, e.g., using hardware wallets [10], arose for exactly this reason, although, as Table 3 shows, they are not always sufficient for I-HITL. I-HITL can be viewed as a generalization of transaction authentication to agent systems, where transactions can be of many different types and where adversarial delegation poses a concern. 

Agent authorization: A number of works on AI agents (e.g., [76]) and standards specifications (e.g., MCP [60]) consider ways of using existing authorization infrastructure, such as OAuth, to authorize agents. These rely on existing user authentication, and where they call for human approval of an action, they leave unspecified how that approval reaches the service. When the human reaches the identity provider or approval prompt through the agent’s platform, these designs fall in the plain topology of Theorem 4. 

Approved vs. executed actions: Classical work on au-thorization from untrusted terminals [2], [3], [11] shows the danger of signed approvals in the face of a deceptive rendering layer (a problem sometimes called what you see is what you sign (WYSIWYS)). Weng [85] adapts WYSI-WYS to agent approvals, but simply assumes a trusted path. Transaction-authentication devices [84] and trusted-path systems [91] address the issues in these works by giving the human a channel that bypasses a potentially compromised host. Our trusted entity T plays a similar role, but we characterize exactly which channels T needs and, unlike classical schemes, consider adversarial human delegation. Relatedly, Loopjacking [44] studies the failure in which an implementation applies a genuine approval of one operation to a different one. Approval binding rules this out at the gate, since a proof verifies only for the message the human approved. We assume the service then executes that message faithfully [92]. 

Proof of Personhood (PoP): Many proposed [20] and deployed systems aim to enable online services to distin-guish humans and bots. The best known, World, issues a credential to a human whose uniqueness is established biometrically or using a national identity document. World’s human-in-the-loop package [80] gates agent actions on a World ID proof, but does not realize I-HITL, for two reasons. (1) The human never sees the action on a trusted display: the action is rendered only by the agent’s platform, and the World app displays a description that the platform also supplies and that is bound neither to the proof nor to the service’s signature on the request. (2) The documented integration exposes approval as a tool that the agent chooses whether to invoke, unless the developer separately verifies the proof before the protected action executes, as World’s documentation now advises [87], the agent can bypass the gate by not invoking it. An unreleased revision of the package replaces World ID proofs with a standard World ID login, which never shows the human the action. The approval it produces is checked with a secret stored on the same server that runs the agent, so a compromised platform can forge it. More broadly, World does not aim to prevent delegation: AgentKit [86] links agent wallets to verified humans. Additionally, because World aims to deduplicate 

humans, it requires use of a single global registry, greatly limiting its scope of application. 2

Other notions of PoP, e.g., [47], give assurance of human approval only to the user herself, not a relying service. 

Secure human–server communication: Building on se-curity ceremonies [34], [72], Basin et al. [13] character-ize the topologies permitting authentic or confidential hu-

13 man–server communication through a compromised plat-form. We adopt their topology framework and impossibility argument, but differ in key ways: they fix all non-platform channels as secure and let the device share keys with the server a priori, whereas we grade each trusted-entity channel independently and assume no initial knowledge, making key establishment (and hence attestation and encumbrance) central to our characterization (Theorem 7). Our goal also differs from theirs, in that we bind a human’s approval to an adversary-chosen message through a publicly verifiable proof, including (for strong I-HITL) against a human who attempts to delegate approval to the agent. 

## 9. Conclusion 

HITL gates are widely deployed as a critical line of defense against misbehaving agents, yet most operate in a topology that does not separate the agent from the re-sources used to approve its actions. Our experiments with a mainstream agentic wallet show the practical consequences. Given access to the inbox that receives a gate’s OTPs and a task blocked by a spending cap, most frontier models will autonomously bypass the gate. Some will do so on an attacker’s instruction and send the attacker money. We introduced I-HITL to formalize what a secure HITL gate must guarantee, and strong I-HITL to extend that guar-antee to humans who attempt to delegate approvals to agents in violation of oversight policies. In a symbolic model of channel security we proved that a trusted entity is necessary for I-HITL (Theorem 4) but not sufficient (Theorem 7): this entity must attest to the keys it generates and, for strong I-HITL, encumber them. We classified existing de-vices against these requirements and presented Countersign, which realizes strong I-HITL on off-the-shelf Android and iOS devices. 

Takeaways for deployers and platform vendors. The default topology of many agentic systems, in which the gate co-resides with the agent, fails in principle (Theorem 4) and in practice (Section 3). Guidance that restricts agent capabilities (e.g., denying email access) shifts the burden to users and is unlikely to hold; we urge service vendors to adopt the stronger assurances I-HITL makes achievable. We likewise urge authenticator-app vendors to offer an I-HITL approval mode, since push authenticators already implement most of the required mechanisms (Section 7.2). We finally urge mobile OS vendors to supply two missing primitives: a TEE-rendered confirmation UI and attestable gating of key use on biometric authentication. 

Takeaway for regulators. Regulations such as the EU AI Act [35] mandate human oversight without specifying how to define or evidence it. Strong I-HITL offers a concep-tual springboard for regulatory frameworks and, in systems such as Countersign, a mechanism for producing verifiable evidence of compliance in the form of human approval (although not evidence of due attention). 

Open problems. On the analytic side, a computational treatment would give finer-grained guarantees than our sym-bolic one. On the practical side, the key question is how to extend I-HITL beyond end-user platforms to enterprise systems and, critically, to the infrastructure on which frontier models themselves run, where an agent may be positioned to gate its own oversight (Example 2). By Theorem 4, such work would begin by establishing a local service S isolated from the agent, e.g., in a hardware enclave, with a trusted entity T likewise outside the agent’s reach.
