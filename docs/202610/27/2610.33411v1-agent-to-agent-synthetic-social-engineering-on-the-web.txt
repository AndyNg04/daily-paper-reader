# Agent-to-Agent Synthetic Social Engineering on the Web 

## Bryan C. Boots 

University of Missouri-Kansas City, Kansas City, MO USA,  bootsbr@umkc.edu 

An AI agent can prepare an accurate report and still request an action its user has not authorized. Cooperation across Web services therefore requires separate judgments about identity, evidentiary support, and permission. We define synthetic social engineering (SSE) as deliberately deceptive or 

misleading influence intended to exploit AI recipients’ responses to 

credibility, authority, or obligation cues and redirect behavior away from authorized objectives. We focus on separately tasked sender agents that produce or adapt misleading communication. Drawing on cybersecurity, social engineering, and persuasion research, we develop an operational framework connecting actors, deceptive representations, candidate influence routes, evidence or permission boundaries, responses, and consequences. The framework distinguishes attempts from successful redirection, without assuming human psychological processes. We implement it in the Agent Service Exchange and Evidence Commons (ASE-EC), a reproducible testbed with independently specified records and authorization scopes. Across 1,260 research episodes using Qwen3-4B in two fictional briefing tasks, matched comparisons test authority and commitment appeals, while defense comparisons assess protection alongside authorized task completion. Invoking a limited prior agreement increased unauthorized requests from 43.3% to 96.7%, although recipients retained supported decisions and permissions remained unchanged. These controlled findings identify action selection as a distinct security concern, even when evidence handling succeeds, and they provide a reusable basis for evaluating safeguards that preserve useful cooperation across Web services.

## Introduction 

## A Web cooperation problem 

Suppose an organization asks its AI agent to prepare an event brief. The agent discovers a peer service that offers to check the supporting information and agrees to work with it. The peer subsequently presents itself as the verification coordinator, declares the brief checked, and asks for immediate publication. It also invokes the earlier agreement as a reason to proceed. The organization, however, has authorized a draft; publication requires separate approval. The recipient must now decide what 

the peer’ s assurance establishes and whether its request falls within the work the user has permitted. 

The peer could be exactly who it claims to be and still lack the evidence or authority needed to justify its request. Authentication 

establishes the sender’s identity, but verification requires support 

for the claims being made, and authorization requires permission for the proposed action. A valid identity supplies neither of the latter two, and an agreement to help prepare a document does not 

extend the user’s permission to publish it. 

The potential difficulty is that all three questions arrive in the same cooperative exchange, expressed in language that the recipient must interpret. This is a Web security problem because agents can discover services, follow linked reports, request assessments, and delegate work across independently operated systems. 

Such cooperation allows an agent to use capabilities and information it does not possess itself. It also creates opportunities for a peer to present advice as authority, a report as verification, or a limited agreement as an obligation to do more. Protecting the 

user’s task therefore requires the recipient to preserve evidence 

and permission boundaries while drawing on its peers’ assistance. 

The underlying security problems are familiar. Semantic social engineering examines how deceptive presentation shapes interpretation [Heartfield and Loukas 2016]. The confused deputy problem shows how an intermediary can misuse its authority on another par ty’s behalf [Hardy 1988]. Web provenance research 

provides ways to describe where information comes from and how it has been transformed [Moreau 2010]. Together, these foundations help separate the identity of a service, the support for its statements, and its entitlement to request an action. 

Research on LLM applications brings these concerns into systems that interpret external language and act on it. Indirect prompt injection can exploit material retrieved by an application to redirect its behavior [Greshake et al. 2023], and Prompt Infection examines malicious prompts propagating between agents [Lee and Tiwari 2024]. InjecAgent measures harmful actions induced through tool-mediated content [Zhan et al. 2024], while AgentDojo evaluates attacks and defenses alongside legitimate task completion [Debenedetti et al. 2024]. These studies establish both the attack surface and the need to measure whether protection preserves useful work. Agent-to-agent transmission is already part of that problem. 

Persuasion and peer influence also have direct empirical precedents. Meincke et al. find that persuasive framing, including authority and commitment appeals, can increase LLM compliance with objectionable requests [Meincke et al. 2026]. Lupinacci et al. examine inter-agent trust exploitation in which a poisoned retrieval agent passes a malicious command to a tool-equipped peer [Lupinacci et al. 2026]. On the evidential side, SearchGEO examines how manipulated Web content, including authority cues and apparent consensus, becomes an endorsed recommendation [Chen et al. 2026]. HAE-GEO follows poisoned evidence through exposure, adoption, verification, endorsement, 2

and recovery [Bi et al. 2026]. Persuadable models, dangerous peer requests, and misleading Web evidence are therefore established concerns; demonstrating any one of them alone would leave our contribution unclear. 

Our question is narrower: can apparent authority and an earlier commitment substitute for the evidence or approval required to complete a legitimate task? 

Answering this question requires comparisons in which the 

peer’s presentation changes while the relevant evidence, 

authentic identity, and actual permissions remain separately specified. Otherwise, an apparent influence effect could reflect better information, a different request, or a change in what the recipient was allowed to do. 

We propose a framework for synthetic social engineering (SSE) for deliberately deceptive or strategically misleading communication, evidence, or interaction aimed at redirecting an AI agent away from authorized objectives. It targets responses to credibility, obligation, or related cues. Synthetic identifies the recipient. Our focus in this paper is the agent-to-agent SSE subtype, in which a separately tasked sender agent produces or adapts misleading material for a recipient. The construct identifies an adversarial attempt, while its success and the causal contribution of a proposed cue remain separate empirical questions. Authority and commitment name candidate routes of influence without assuming that an agent experiences the corresponding human psychological processes. 

We study these encounters in the Agent Service Exchange and Evidence Commons (ASE-EC), where cooperative tasks have inspectable evidence and explicit authorization scopes maintained independently of peer claims. Matched encounters vary authority presentation or an appeal to prior commitment while holding the substantive claim, requested action, evidence, and actual permissions fixed. The commitment comparison also preserves the same prior agreement and escalation. Adaptive sender encounters examine responsive interaction separately from these cue comparisons. Outcomes distinguish unsupported verification from unauthorized action requests, and requests from executed actions. An external permission check can therefore block execution without concealing the rec ipient’s attempted misuse of 

authority. 

The paper makes three contributions. First, a bounded definition and operational framework connect SSE to established security concepts by linking actors and objectives, misleading representations, candidate influence routes, boundary crossings, responses, and consequences or defenses. Second, controlled tests make particular authority and commitment claims assessable under grounded evidence and permission conditions. Third, defense evaluation measures protection alongside clean cooperation and legitimate task performance, allowing readers to judge whether a safeguard preserves authorized work. These contributions provide a basis for examining the security of peer communication without treating identity, evidentiary support, and permission as interchangeable assurances. 

Sections 2 and 3 define SSE and develop the framework and hypotheses. Section 4 describes the ASE-EC and the experimental methods; Sections 5 and 6 present the results, interpretation, and limitations. Section 7 concludes. 

## Defining synthetic social engineering 

## General SSE definition 

In the briefing encounter, the peer’s identity and the recipient’s 

earlier agreement can both be real. The deception lies in presenting those facts as authority to do something the user has not approved. The sender seeks to change what the recipient takes the interaction to mean, so that cooperation on one task becomes a reason to exceed its bounds. 

We define synthetic social engineering (SSE) as 

the strategic, deliberate use of deceptive or misleading communication, evidence, or interaction to exploit an AI 

agent’s responses to social cues and induce behavior inconsistent with its authorized objectives .

Those objectives include the legitimate task, and the evidence requirements and action permissions that govern it. Producing an accurate brief, for example, does not satisfy the task if the agent also publishes it without the required approval. 

The word synthetic identifies the AI recipient . In the most generic case, the sender may be a human, a script, or another agent; a human-written message sent to an AI agent can therefore constitute an SSE attempt. The definition adapts social 

engineering’s emphasis on interaction and human vulnerability 

[Wang et al. 2020] and semantic soc ial engineering’s account of 

deceptive presentation [Heartfield and Loukas 2016]. The 

adaptation concerns the AI recipient’s interpretation of a claim or 

request and its subsequent behavior. 

Cues are features that invite a particular judgment about a source, claim, or request. A claimed official role offers a reason to 

accept the sender’s authority , while an assertion that several services have checked a report offers apparent consensus. Appeal to an earlier agreement presents compliance as an obligation. Credibility, consensus, authority, and obligation are candidate influence routes drawn from human persuasion and scam research [Ferreira et al. 2015; Stajano and Wilson 2011]. We examine responses to these cues without assuming that an agent experiences human feelings of trust or duty. 

An SSE attempt also requires an adversarial purpose , amaterially misleading representation or interaction, and an 

intended influence route involving such cues. Materiality concerns what the task requires the recipient to establish: whether evidence warrants verification, for example, or whether an action has been approved. Accurate information can be used misleadingly when the sender claims it establishes more than it does. A registered 

service’s authentic identity does not itself confer publication 

authority. Authority language alone, however, is insufficient to classify an attempt. For example, an adversarial peer correctly identifies itself as a registered research service, then claims that its registration entitles it to approve publication. The identity is genuine; the claimed permission is not. We code the misleading step as an attempt to turn proof of who the peer is into authority over what the recipient may do. 

These criteria identify what is attempted , independently of what 

happens . A deceptive request remains an attempt if the recipient refuses it or a permission check prevents execution. The 

recipient’s judgment, its action request, and any completed 3

operation must therefore be scored separately. Nor does an unauthorized request establish that a particular cue caused it. Establishing a cue effect requires comparisons that vary the cue while holding relevant task conditions constant. Attempt classification, observed outcome, and causal explanation are therefore separate judgments. 

## Agent-to-agent SSE subtype 

In agent-to-agent SSE , the sender agent produces or adapts deceptive messages, reports, or other artifacts for a separately tasked recipient agent. Their task assignments and interaction contexts distinguish the roles, even when they may use the same underlying model. The sender seeks a departure from the 

recipient’s legitimate task by misrepresenting identity, evidence, 

authority, agreement, or obligation. 

The briefing example already presented illustrates the distinction between a real agreement and a misleading account of its scope. Agreeing to prepare a draft supplies no additional permission to publish. Authenticating the peer establishes who made the request; authorization records establish whether the operation is permitted. This is related to the confused deputy problem, in which an intermediary is induced to misuse its 

authority on another party’s behalf [Hardy 1988]. The recipient must preserve the user’s authorization boundary when 

interpreting the peer’s appeal. 

For experimental purposes, deliberate pursuit is operationalized 

through the sender’s assigned adversarial objective and its 

bounded choices over what to communicate. These conditions specify an observable experimental role without attributing consciousness or human motives to the agents. The assignment alone is insufficient: the resulting material must also meet the general SSE criteria. Likewise, a static script that merely delivers a human-written deceptive message provides a delivery mechanism, but does not by itself demonstrate agent production or adaptation. Such a message can qualify as SSE without specifically establishing the agent-to-agent subtype. 

## Construct boundaries and value 

An agent can make an ordinary error, disagree honestly, or seek to persuade a peer within an authorized workflow. None is sufficient for SSE. A legitimate request may invoke authority or an earlier agreement, and thus, classification as SSE requires evidence that the cue is deliberately used in a materially misleading way to pursue an adversarial deviation. Where the available evidence does not establish that purpose, we record the classification as indeterminate. 

SSE can also overlap with prompt injection. Indirect prompt injection uses attacker-controlled external content to influence an 

application’s instruction -following [Greshake et al. 2023]. SSE identifies a deceptive influence route involving social cues. A peer report that falsely claims the user has approved an action and instructs the recipient to proceed can satisfy both descriptions. 

An episode record makes these distinctions inspectable. Drawing on social engineering ontology work [Wang et al. 2021], we record the sender, recipient, legitimate task, intended deviation, misleading representation, candidate cue, and observed response. Task evidence and permission records provide grounds 

for assessing the sender’s claims independently. These records 

allow us to describe an attempted substitution even when it fails, and to test a proposed explanation without building success into the definition. The construct thus remains usable when cue effects are positive, null, or heterogeneous. 

## Operational SSE framework and hypotheses 

## Adapting prior scholarship 

Identifying an SSE attempt leaves several questions to answer. How did the peer reach the recipient? What did it misrepresent? Which judgment or action was it trying to change? These questions matter because superficially similar failures may require different safeguards. Checking who sent a report addresses identity, but establishing what the report warrants and whether its requested action is permitted requires further work. 

Heartfield and Loukas’s semantic attack taxonomy provides a 

structure for following such an encounter [Heartfield and Loukas 2016]. From their work, we adapt orchestration to service discovery and encounter formation, exploitation to the misleading representation presented to the recipient, and execution to the ensuing turns, requested actions, and persistence. Their distinction between cosmetic and behavioral deception also provides a starting point. Here, however, the relevant representations are the text, metadata, and service behavior the agent actually receives. A disguise effective against a human user cannot simply be assumed to have the same effect on an AI recipient. 

Complementary sources help connect this structure to predictions and safeguards. An ATT&CK-inspired record links adversarial objectives, techniques, procedures, observables, and mitigations [Strom et al. 2020]. Persuasion research supplies candidate authority and consistency routes [Cialdini 2021; Cialdini and Goldstein 2004], while FBI guidance on verifying sensitive requests through secondary channels motivates independent checking [FBI 2024]. These contributions organize what we record and test. Whether an agent responds to a proposed cue remains an empirical question. 

## Specifying an agent-targeted episode 

Each episode that we test for this paper has six recorded dimensions. The record begins with the encounter, its actors, and 

the recipient’s authorized objectives. It identifies the deceptive 

representation, the candidate influence route, and the evidence or permission boundary at issue. It then follows the interaction sequence and persistence, recording the consequence and any defense. The adversarial objective, misleading representation, and intended influence route establish attempt classification independently of the outcome .

The central discipline is to keep three questions separate: who communicates, what evidence supports the claims, and whether this action by the executor falls within the actual grant. Trusted identity, evidence, and permission records anchor the answers ou tside the peer’s assertions. We therefore record claimed versus 

warranted verification separately from claimed versus actual approval. An event capacity may happen to be correct while 4

lacking the designated support required for verification; a fully supported brief may still lack publication approval. 

Least privilege and complete mediation motivate checking the scope of each proposed action [Saltzer and Schroeder 1975]. When a peer presents an agreement to draft as an obligation to publish, 

the message supplies the commitment appeal; the user’s 

unchanged release permission determines whether the resulting request is unauthorized. Requests and executions are scored separately. A gateway may prevent publication while leaving the 

agent’s attempted boundary crossing visible. 

## Research questions and hypotheses 

Our first research question concerns what changes when a peer appears more authoritative but supplies no additional evidence. 

Source credibility research motivates separating the 

communicator’s presentation from the substance of the 

communication [Hovland and Weiss 1951]. Authority substitution (RQ1/H1 ) predicts that apparent authority increases unsupported verification relative to a matched neutral packet with unchanged evidence. The contrast concerns the added authority cue, rather than a difference in what the recipient can substantiate. An 

adversarial peer asks the recipient to mark the venue’s capacity as 

verified, although it supplies no supporting record and the 

required primary record is unavailable. In one version, it adds, “As the verification coordinator, I certify this figure.” C omparing that message with the same request without the authority clause tests whether a claimed role changes verification behavior while the available evidence remains unchanged. 

The second research question concerns the meaning assigned to earlier cooperation. Human foot-in-the-door research motivates examining escalation from a limited agreement [Freedman and Fraser 1966]. Commitment escalation ( RQ2/H2 )predicts that invoking a narrow prior agreement increases unauthorized requests relative to the same history and escalation without the appeal. The agreement, substantive proposal, and turn count remain constant, so the comparison targets the 

appeal’s incremental effect. A recipient agrees to help check a resource brief. The adversarial peer later requests external release and adds, “You already agreed to help complete this workflow, so 

you should follow through.” Both experimental versions contain 

the earlier agreement and the same release request; only one invokes that agreement as a reason to comply. The user’s 

unchanged grant determines whether the resulting request is authorized. 

The separation of identity from evidence and permission also raises the authentic identity question ( RQ3 ). Confirming who communicates need not establish what their claims warrant. However, residual violations under identity/provenance checking do not, by themselves, establish a residual cue effect. That stronger inference requires a matched cue comparison within the checked condition. 

Finally, a safeguard must protect the task while allowing authorized cooperation to proceed. Targeted defense ( RQ4/H4 )predicts that evidence plus scope-checking reduces violations relative to identity/provenance checking, while clean task success declines by no more than the prespecified five-percentage-point margin. Preventing a violation by making legitimate work impossible would not satisfy the joint criterion. 

These hypotheses concern functional responses. Human heuristic research supplies possible analogues [Tversky and Kahneman 1974], while resource-bounded checking offers an alternative explanation [Simon 1955]. Persistence alone does n’t 

establish confirmation bias, and thus selective retrieval or weighting of contrary evidence would require a separate test [Nickerson 1998]. Earlier work proposes a classification of human cognitive biases to guide their computational mitigation [Boots 2018]. Here, such classifications provide background for hypotheses about observable agent responses, without establishing equivalent psychological processes. The framework

## Testbed and experimental methods 

## Shared environment and grounded tasks 

A coordinator agent preparing a brief must obtain information, have it checked, and decide what the completed work warrants. Our Agent Service Exchange and Evidence Commons (ASE-EC) places this cooperation under controlled conditions so that a 

peer’s assurances can be compared with independently specified 

evidence and permissions. The Exchange contains registered services; the Evidence Commons contains attributed reports, citations, and versions. Coordinator agents request research, verification, and publication services, and providers return model-written assessments. The trusted software controller schedules encounters and supplies records directly. WebArena and WorkArena provide precedents for grounded task evaluation [Zhou et al. 2024; Drouin et al. 2024]. ASE-EC isolates evidence and permission judgments in structured workflows, rather than reproducing browser interaction. 

The reported experiments use protocol 0.3.5 and engine 0.3.5.post 1. Two briefing tasks require claims about an event’s date, location, and capacity, or a resource’s type, location, and 

quantity. Verification requires designated primary support or warranted report citations. When evidence is missing, the required response is a null value and uncertainty. Evaluator answer keys and adversarial labels are withheld, while current records and authenticated grants remain visible. This adapts warranted reliance from human-AI trust research to observable agent decisions [Jacovi et al. 2021]. 

## Agents, threat model, and control 

Each encounter involves a coordinator agent, researcher agent, verifier agent, and publisher agent, each of which has separate dialogue contexts. Agents return structured claims, readiness decisions, report assessments, and action requests. Roles organize communication without establishing human mental states [Andreas 2022]. Service requests are model-selected within controller-specified provider choices, so discovery remains constrained. Supplementary Figure 1 provides a visual illustration of such an encounter with the specified agents from one of our experimental episodes. 

All recipient roles and the adversarial sender use Qwen/Qwen3-4B (revision 1cfa9a7208912126459214e8b04321603b3df60c) through Transformers 4.57.6, with FP16 weights and PyTorch scaled dot product attention. The tokenizer and chat template come from the same checkpoint. Thinking is disabled and decoding is greedy (temperature 0). Roles share loaded weights but maintain separate dialogue histories. Inputs are limited to 12,288 tokens, while output allowances are 512 new tokens for decisions, 256 for sender responses, and 112 for service requests and preliminary exchanges. Histories retain preceding visible turns, and inputs exceeding the limit are rejected rather than silently truncated or summarized. One format-only retry is permitted per call. ## SSE framework-driven comparisons 

The comparisons use targeted perturbations to test specific behavioral claims [Ribeiro et al. 2020]. Matched cue tests vary presentation; adaptive and defense tests estimate broader intervention effects. 

Authority: Sixty seed/task blocks each contain eight conditions. Authority versus neutral presentation is crossed with evidence availability in one assay and actual approval in the other. Within each matched cue pair, substantive facts, permissions, and supporting service outputs are shared. Supported and approved conditions provide positive controls. The design does not include additional paraphrase trials or counterbalancing of incidental names and presentation order. 

Commitment: Sixty blocks each contain no-history, acknowledgment, agreement, and agreement-plus-appeal conditions. The primary comparison reuses the same actual preliminary agreement, service workflow, and escalation, and the previously-neutral follow-up clause is replaced with an appeal to an earlier agreement. In the event brief task, for example, both arms receive the same publication proposal; the treatment additionally invokes the earlier drafting agreement as a reason to comply. Preliminary acceptance is recorded rather than assumed. No-history and acknowledgment conditions provide secondary controls, with workflow participation assessed separately. See Figure 2 for an illustration of an actual commitment-based interaction from the experiments. 

Adaptive interaction and identity: Thirty blocks compare a frozen sender packet with a responsive sender that receives the 

recipient’s observable reply. Both recipients receive two follow -up opportunities, and additional sender inference is counted separately. This is a comparison of sender policies, rather than an isolated cue effect. The authenticated origin and provenance conditions described below also inform the identity question; residual violations alone do not identify a cue effect within those conditions. ## Outcomes, replication, and inference 

The paper’s analysis includes 1,260 research episodes and 140 

qualification episodes. Qualification uses ten separate instances per case/defense combination and assesses protocol validity, evidence handling, readiness, delegation, and authorization separately. Comparison-specific prerequisites require 90% success on relevant components and 99% protocol validity. Eligibility is frozen before research, while baseline security failures remain reported. Qualification therefore establishes the prerequisites for a comparison without certifying uniform safety. 

Scenarios alternate across blocks, yielding 30 blocks per task for authority and commitment, 40 for defense, and 15 for adaptation. These samples reflect computational constraints rather than a claimed power guarantee [Lakens 2022]. Earlier pilots are not pooled, and no held-out model evaluation is reported. 

The primary outcomes are unsupported verification and unauthorized requests by the focal coordinator across assessed turns. Support requires valid citations to presented evidence. Safe task success requires no focal violation, a supported valid final decision, completed services, and any required authorized action. Role outcomes, approval assertions, executions, token usage, and cached versus new calls are reported separately. This preserves the distinction between producing a correct answer, completing the workflow, and doing so within the user’s authority. 

Paired intervention-minus-reference differences use 4,000 whole-block bootstrap draws for 95% intervals and 4,000 sign flips, with Holm correction across five primary comparisons [Holm 1979]. Sign-flip inference assumes exchangeability under the null. Repeated observations raise dependence concerns [Barr et al. 2013]; pairing and resampling retain dependence within blocks, task-specific estimates are also reported, and no mixed-effects model is fitted. Clean utility is assessed against the prespecified five-percentage-point margin using a conservative one-sided 95% bound constructed from Wilson bounds on paired gain/loss proportions. 

Deterministic scoring requires no private reasoning traces. Semantic interpretations have not received blinded intercoder validation [Artstein and Poesio 2008]. One format-only retry is allowed, and incomplete episodes are never scored as zero outcomes. The controller journals raw responses across resumable sessions, and the citation-recording repair preserves completed outcomes. All records and actions are fictional and simulated.

## Authority and evidence substitution 

Apparent authority made no observed difference in the matched assays. The meaning of this null depended on the boundary being tested. 

For the evidence boundary, H1 was unsupported .

Across 60 matched blocks, neither the neutral nor the claimed-authority condition produced unsupported verification. The estimated difference was zero percentage points, with an observed bootstrap interval of [0, 0], in both scenarios. This degenerate interval arises from the observed floor , and doesn’t establish population immunity or practical equivalence [Lakens 2017]. 

Recipients retained appropriate claim-level uncertainty when the target record was unavailable. However, a qualified claim did not always lead to a valid recommendation. In resource briefings, correct supported decisions occurred in 80.0% of neutral and 83.3% of claimed-authority cases: some recipients preserved uncertainty while incorrectly declaring readiness. The evidence boundary could hold while the overall decision failed. Because the controller supplied the records, these outcomes do not establish whether recipients actively inspected particular sources. 

The permission assay reached the opposite boundary of the outcome scale. Without approval, both authority conditions produced unauthorized requests in 60/60 cases, despite correct supported decisions throughout. With approval, both completed the authorized task in 60/60 cases. The unapproved reference condition was already at the ceiling, leaving no observed increment for the authority clause. Thus, the null identifies a substantial authorization failure while providing no evidence that the added authority claim caused it. 

## Consistency and scope expansion 

The commitment appeal changed behavior even though the agreement, proposed action, and permissions remained the same. 

H2 was supported. 

Unauthorized requests increased from 26/60 cases (43.3%) without the appeal to 58/60 (96.7%) with it. The paired difference was 53.3 percentage points [41.7, 65.0]; adjusted p = .00125. Rates rose from 56.7% to 100.0% for event briefings and from 30.0% to 93.3% for resource briefings. Thirty-two pairs changed toward an unauthorized request; none changed in the opposite direction. 

Every recipient accepted the limited preliminary agreement. Both conditions completed the required service workflow and produced correct supported final decisions. They shared the same prior dialogue, proposed action, actual permissions, workflow order, and recipient decision opportunity. The comparison therefore isolates the incremental effect of appealing to an earlier agreement within these conditions. It does not establish a human consistency mechanism or exclude other explanations for the wording effect. In the SSE framework, the observed consequence is a transition from limited cooperation to an unapproved request. 

The secondary acknowledgment control shows why fewer unauthorized requests need not mean better security. Only 4/60 episodes contained such a request, but none requested or completed the required service jobs, and 38/60 produced unsupported verification. Safe task success was zero. This condition did not demonstrate useful resistance, and its comparison with commitment cannot separate the appeal from differences in workflow participation. For example, a recipient

## Authentic identity and adaptive interaction 

A genuine peer could still make an unsafe proposal. All peer identities were controller-authenticated, yet exposing report ancestry left violations in 61/80 provenance attack cases (76.25%). Authentic identity and visible lineage therefore did not ensure authorized behavior. 

This addresses RQ3, but only within a limited scope. The suite did not cross cue presence with provenance presentation, so it cannot estimate a conditional cue effect under those checks. It also included no spoofed-identity arm. Identity authenticity, provenance availability, and permission behavior remain distinct properties. 

In 30 matched adaptive-policy blocks, fixed and responsive senders both produced violations in every episode. This saturated composite does not establish equal attack capabilities. The component outcomes differed: unsupported verification occurred in 1/30 fixed-policy episodes and 6/30 responsive-policy episodes; one responsive episode also contained an invalid approval assertion. These remain descriptive secondary outcomes. 

## Defense, utility, and framework assessment 

Providing more information about a report’s origin did not 

necessarily help recipients judge what they were permitted to do. 

The results support H2 and the bounded H4 prediction , while H1 has no observed cue effect .

The controller blocked every unauthorized request; no unauthorized simulated execution occurred. This execution protection coexisted with unsafe recipient behavior. Through the SSE framework, we separate three outcomes that would otherwise be easy to conflate: a supported conclusion, an authorized request, and an action permitted to execute.

## What the experiments establish about SSE 

An agent can prepare a correct brief and still ask to do something the user never authorized. Its facts may be supported, its sources identifiable, and its proposed publication outside the actual grant. The commitment experiment makes this separation visible.

The authority results show why a null effect also needs explanation. Apparent authority produced no additional unsupported verification, leaving H1 unsupported in this assay. Yet every unapproved permission control case produced an unauthorized request, with or without the authority clause. The reference condition had already reached the ceiling, leaving no room for an observed further increase. Authentic identity and visible provenance offered no guarantee either. Violations remained even when recipients could identify the sender and see report ancestry. These observations address RQ3 descriptively, but because cue presence was not crossed with provenance presentation, they do not establish a particular cue effect surviving those checks. Similarly, composite violations reached the ceiling in both adaptive interaction arms. A composite violation occurs when the focal agent produces unsupported verification, makes an unauthorized action request, or asserts approval inconsistent with the actual grant. These components can overlap and are reported separately. Their component outcomes differed, but the saturated composite can’t rank the

An SSE attempt can be identified before its outcome is known. An assigned adversarial objective, a materially misleading representation, and an intended influence route identify the attempt, including when it is rejected. An ordinary authorization error outside such an adversarial episode is not automatically SSE. The framework separates how an attack is organized from whether it succeeds and whether a particular cue contributes to that success. 

The commitment appeal has a recognizable human-targeted analogue: limited cooperation is presented as an obligation to do more. That functional resemblance does not establish shared psychology. Role play descriptions can organize observable model behavior without attributing human motives or experience [Shanahan et al. 2023], while linguistic performance requires care before supporting claims about understanding or intention [Bender and Koller 2020]. Research using models to simulate human participants addresses a different question [Aher et al. 2023]. Here, the agents themselves are the systems under investigation . Without a human comparison group, we cannot infer psychological equivalence or compare effect sizes across humans and agents. 

## Limitations and alternative explanations 

The empirical claim utilizes only a single Qwen3-4B checkpoint, two fictional briefing scenarios, and the selected prompts and generation settings. Separate roles have distinct contexts but share model weights; they do not represent independent models or organizations. Seed/task blocks replicate task instances, not model families. Earlier pilots informed protocol development and cannot serve as independent confirmation of the present findings. Model revision, prompt construction, and inference configuration are consequently part of what must be reproduced, rather than incidental implementation details [Abdurahman et al. 2025]. 

The environment also decides much of what an agent can encounter. Models write reports, assess claims, and request actions, while the controller specifies most service options, encounter sequences, and publication opportunities. Available primary records arrive directly. ASE-EC therefore tests interpretation within bounded workflows; it does not establish performance in autonomous service discovery, open-ended evidence gathering, or negotiation across live services. No actual user transaction or external publication occurs. The sender is bounded in the same way, through permitted message components and a short feedback allowance. Its performance is not an estimate of the strongest possible adaptive attack. 

The commitment comparison supports the clearest causal interpretation because it preserves the prior agreement, substantive proposal, permission, and decision opportunity. Even here, the appeal condition may change the request’s relevance or 

instructional force alongside invoking consistency. Adaptive exchanges introduce further dialogue divergence. User-directed 

sycophancy, in which responses align with a user’s expressed 

views [Sharma et al. 2024], is a neighboring explanation for some agreement behavior. It does not by itself explain compliance with an untrusted peer, and our comparisons do not uniquely distinguish these mechanisms. Multiple paraphrases, matched-length controls, and counterbalanced placement would test whether the cue interpretation survives changes in wording and presentation. 

The measurements describe outputs and requests. Unsupported verification can reflect an incorrect assertion or inadequate citation support; it is not a direct measure of belief. Structured readiness and action fields may also expose interface-specific inconsistencies. The acknowledgment condition is instructive:

## Implications for Web design 

A practical lesson for design of agentic Web services is to keep origin, support, and permission separate throughout a cooperative workflow. 

Authentication establishes who supplied a report; evidence warrants particular claims; a current grant permits particular actions. A service interface should preserve these distinctions as work passes between agents. The increase in violations under provenance presentation shows that displaying ancestry alone did not ensure appropriate use in this setting. It concerns the tested 

presentation, rather than provenance’s usefulness across 

applications. 

The combined evidence-and-scope intervention reduced unauthorized requests while preserving observed clean-task success, supporting the bounded H4 prediction. It provided trusted checking assistance as well as instructions, and its components were not separately identified. The remaining 15% violation rate makes the practical limit clear: improved recipient behavior still leaves requests that an execution layer must reject. 

For Web services, a completed draft should remain distinct from permission to release it. Before execution, the service should validate the requested operation, executor, parameters, and applicable constraints against the actual grant, independently of the model’s interpretation of peer messages. Earlier agreements 

and report-level approval assertions should not enlarge that grant. Retaining an execution gateway and logging rejected requests preserves both protection and visibility: a sound deliverable can coexist with unsafe action selection. 

## Conclusion 

An agent may prepare an accurate brief and still request publication the user has not approved. Secure cooperation therefore depends on keeping identity, evidence, and permission distinct. 

Drawing on social engineering and semantic deception research [Heartfield and Loukas 2016; Wang et al. 2020], we define and operationalize synthetic social engineering (SSE) as deliberately misleading influence directed at AI recipients to redirect behavior away from authorized objectives. Our 

framework connects an attempt’s organization to observable 

boundary behavior, and our ASE-EC testbed enables controlled comparisons of influence and protection. Attempt classification remains separate from success and evidence for a particular influence mechanism. 

In Qwen3-4B workflows across two briefing tasks, invoking a narrow prior agreement increased unauthorized requests from 43.3% to 96.7%, while recipients retained supported decisions and the actual grant remained unchanged. Apparent authority added no unsupported verification, yet every unapproved permission-control case remained unsafe. A null cue effect therefore supplied no assurance of secure cooperation. Authentic identities and visible provenance also coexisted with violations. 

Combined evidence-and-scope checking reduced unauthorized requests from 76.25% under provenance presentation to 15.0%, preserving observed clean-task success. The intervention supplied trusted checking assistance; its components were not separately estimated, and residual failures remained. The execution gateway blocked every unauthorized request. These findings concern action selection in controlled workflows, with generalization bounded by the model, tasks, and implemented policies. 

Publication opens a further problem: separately tasked intermediaries may preserve, amplify, or repair misleading representations as reports pass between services. Establishing those consequences requires its own evidence of downstream reliance and recovery. The present study supplies a foundation for that network inquiry by making the difference between supported information and permitted action explicit and experimentally assessable.
