Abstract 

Language-model agents must adapt as users revise their requirements and priorities during complex tasks. We introduce IntentDelta, a human-constructed multi-turn benchmark with 638 trajectories and 3,766 turns across Web-Shop and TravelPlanner. Each turn pairs a user utterance with a cumulative intention state and human reference actions. The trajectories are cross-verified and cover feasibility- and preference-driven changes in constraints and priorities. We evaluate eleven agents on in-tention recovery and action quality. Average Intention Recovery is 33.1% in TravelPlanner and 44.7% in WebShop, while average Ac-tion Score is 31.6% and 41.0%, respectively. Agents struggle to preserve earlier require-ments and distinguish soft preferences from must-have constraints. Correct intention re-covery also does not guarantee successful ex-ecution, highlighting challenges in translating evolving user intentions into actions. 

1 Introduction 

Language-model agents are increasingly expected to assist users with complex, multi-step tasks such as product search and travel planning (Zhou et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024b; Ma et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024a; Yao et al., 2022). Success in these settings depends not only on executing tools or producing a plausible final answer, but also on maintaining an accurate understanding of what the user currently wants. In practice, user intentions are often not fully specified at the beginning of an interaction. Users may revise their requirements after observing concrete results: a set of products may reveal that the original combination of price and attributes is infeasible, while a proposed itinerary may cause the user to reconsider the trade-off between cost and travel time. Such feedback may add, relax, replace, or re-prioritize a constraint, requiring the agent to preserve requirements that remain valid while discarding those that have been superseded. Existing benchmarks study requirement revi-sion and task switching (El Asri et al., 2017; Tack et al., 2026), as well as trade-offs between hard constraints and soft preferences (Oh et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025). As summarized in Table 1, existing benchmarks have three main limitations. 

First , most rely on synthesized intention updates or simulated user interactions. Synthesis alone provides limited evidence that these changes re-flect plausible human decisions. Second , existing benchmarks offer limited coverage of users’ deci-sion making, including how they weigh compet-ing requirements and under what conditions they revise their intentions. Third , human reference actions for each turn’s current intention are not con-sistently provided, limiting comparisons between agent outcomes and human execution as require-ments evolve. To address these gaps, we introduce IntentDelta ,a multi-turn benchmark for evaluating whether agents can maintain and act on evolving user in-tentions. Human annotators construct and cross-verify episodes in WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) and TravelPlanner (Xie et al., 2024a), pairing natural-language updates with cumulative annotations of active constraints and priorities, as well as human reference actions at each turn. The benchmark cov-ers feasibility- and preference-driven changes, in-cluding additions, relaxations, overrides, and repri-oritizations. We evaluate intention recovery and action quality separately at each turn, supporting joint analysis of errors in the agent’s reported in-tention state and failures in its resulting actions. We evaluate eleven LLM agents in WebShop and TravelPlanner using a shared ReAct framework. Our experiments reveal substantial weaknesses: av-erage Intention Recovery is 33.1% in TravelPlan-ner and 44.7% in WebShop, while average Action Score is 31.6% and 41.0%, respectively. 2 Related Work 

Existing agent benchmarks typically evaluate task completion under fixed user requirements (Yao et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2024). Conversational evaluations further examine multi-turn instruction following (Li et al., 2025), sim-ulated user interactions (Yao et al., 2025; Mehri et al., 2026), or clarification of underspecified tasks (Kumar and Black, 2020; Li et al., 2026). How-ever, multi-turn interaction alone does not estab-lish whether agents can maintain user requirements as they are added, removed, overridden, or repri-oritized in response to task results. IntentDelta preserves grounded task execution while making the user specification a controlled, time-varying component of the episode. Task-oriented dialogue and conversational rec-ommendation have long studied evolving user re-quirements. Dialogue state tracking represents needs through structured slots and values, including corrections to earlier values (Jacqmin et al., 2022; 2Mcginty and Smyth, 2006). The Frames corpus models multiple goal frames and references among competing alternatives, while conversational rec-ommender systems support iterative preference re-finement through user critiques (El Asri et al., 2017; Schulz et al., 2017; Mcginty and Smyth, 2006; Jan-nach et al., 2021). These settings are closely related, but primarily evaluate state recovery, response gen-eration, or recommendation efficiency. IntentDelta instead grounds each change in task results and sep-arately evaluates both the maintained intention state and the downstream behavior of a task-executing agent. 

3 IntentDelta Benchmark 

IntentDelta evaluates whether agents can maintain and act on user intentions as constraints and prior-ities evolve. We formulate the agent’s and user’s decision processes, and model evolving intentions and trade-offs. We then define metrics for intention recovery and action execution.

User process. We characterize the user-side inter-action as a Markov decision process (SU , AU , T U ).A user state sUt = ( It, C t) consists of the current intention It and the interaction context Ct. For ex-ample, in a travel-planning task, It may specify a $1,800 budget and a preference for an earlier return, while Ct contains the relevant flight and itinerary information together with the agent’s latest result. The user’s action is an utterance ut ∈ A U ; after see-ing that an earlier flight conflicts with the original plan, the user may say to the agent, “I can stretch the budget a bit if that gets me home earlier.” This idealized description captures how user in-tentions may evolve in response to task outcomes. During dataset construction, we ground intention updates in human exploration of the task environ-ment. During evaluation, we replay the resulting fixed user utterance sequence across agents. Subse-quent utterances do not depend on the tested agent’s actions, while each agent independently interacts with the environment. This protocol enables con-trolled comparisons under the same evolving re-quirements. 

3.2 Modeling Evolving User Intention Representing intention as constraints. We rep-resent user intention as a set of task-relevant con-straints that make an abstract goal concrete and as-sessable, such as a budget below $500, a required hotel rating, or a preferred return time. This ab-straction is grounded in prior dialogue-state repre-sentations and empirical studies showing that users progressively articulate and revise their require-ments through interaction (Zhu et al., 2020; Cai and Chen, 2019; Vaithilingam et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). At turn t, the intention It consists of the currently active constraints together with their values, scopes, and priorities, allowing us to charac-terize how user requirements and trade-offs evolve over time. 

Conditions for intention change. Dur-ing dataset construction, intention changes are grounded in the interaction context Ct, including environment observations and human reference out-comes. These results provide information that can motivate a revision to the current intention. We consider two common conditions for such changes. First, real-world feasibility may reveal that pre-viously stated requirements cannot be jointly sat-isfied. For example, a user searching for a laptop may initially request high-end performance while maintaining a budget that is incompatible with the available products, leading the user to relax the bud-get or compromise on another requirement. Second, 

user preference revision may occur after the user observes concrete alternatives. A user who initially prioritizes performance, for instance, may decide after comparing several laptops that portability or battery life is more important for their actual use case. In both cases, reference exploration provides information that can motivate a transition from It

to It+1 .3Priority and trade-offs. A defining feature of IntentDelta is that user decision making often re-quires resolving trade-offs among competing con-straints. Such trade-offs are central to realistic de-cision making but are often underrepresented in existing agent benchmarks, where constraints are typically treated as equally important or indepen-dently satisfiable. To capture these trade-offs, we associate each constraint with one of three prior-ity levels: must-have , preferred , and optional .Must-have constraints represent requirements that should be preserved whenever feasible, preferred constraints are desirable but may be sacrificed when necessary, and optional constraints represent lower-priority preferences. When no available solu-tion satisfies all active constraints, these priorities specify which requirements the user is more will-ing to compromise on. Importantly, priority is itself part of the evolving intention state and may change as the user observes new outcomes. More gener-ally, an intention update may introduce or remove a requirement, revise its value, or change its priority.

Accordingly, we evaluate agents at two complemen-tary levels: intention recovery and action execution .The former measures agents’ intention recognition, while the latter evaluates agents’ task execution.

Action-Level Evaluation. We evaluate whether the agent’s actions satisfy the gold intention under environmental constraints. Following our priority model, must-have constraints define the minimum conditions for an acceptable outcome, while pre-ferred and optional constraints distinguish the qual-ity of acceptable outcomes. We therefore report two metrics: Hard Success and Action Score .

Hard Success evaluates satisfaction of must-have constraints. Human annotators execute each task to produce a gold trajectory and assess task feasibility. A task is considered feasible if all must-have con-straints can be jointly satisfied, and infeasible oth-erwise. For feasible tasks, the agent must satisfy all must-have constraints; for infeasible tasks, it must satisfy at least as many as the human execution. 4 Benchmark Construction 

4.1 Domain 

We build IntentDelta upon two existing bench-marks, WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) and TravelPlan-ner (Xie et al., 2024a), reusing their task environ-ments and original user requests as initial tasks. WebShop provides crowdsourced shopping instruc-tions grounded in real-world product data, where agents search, inspect, and select products based on 4attributes, prices, and specification options. Trav-elPlanner provides synthesized travel requests with human-annotated reference plans, supported by structured information and search tools for trans-portation, accommodations, restaurants, and attrac-tions. These two domains offer complementary settings for evolving intentions: WebShop focuses on product-level requirements, while TravelPlanner involves interdependent constraints across multi-ple itinerary components, where changes to one requirement may affect the feasibility of others. 

4.2 Human Construction Human Trajectory Creation. Members of the au-thor team manually construct 638 trajectories based on the initial tasks from WebShop and TravelPlan-ner, with each trajectory taking approximately 5–10 minutes. At each turn, authors construct three com-ponents: (1) a structured intention containing con-straints and priorities, where authors consider po-tential trade-offs based on previous task outcomes and determine which requirements are must-have, preferred, or optional; (2) a natural-language user utterance that communicates the updated intention to the agent; and (3) a human reference execution that reflects the current intention. Authors have full access to the task environment, allowing them to independently explore available options and con-struct reference executions. Further details of the annotation procedure are provided in Appendix A, with the WebShop and TravelPlanner interfaces shown in Figures 6 and 7, respectively. 

Two-Stage Annotation Validation and Quality Assurance. Since user intention reflects a subjec-tive mental model, the same utterance or trade-off may admit multiple interpretations. To mini-mize ambiguity and ensure consistent interpreta-tion, we conduct a two-stage cross-verification pro-cess. First, each trajectory is cross-verified by an author who did not construct it. Reviewers exam-ine the consistency of the user utterances, cumu-lative intentions, priorities, and reference actions. Ambiguous or unreasonable cases are discussed and revised until consensus is reached. Second, we sample 20% of the dataset (128 trajectories) for independent annotation by external annotators. External reviewers assess the consistency and clar-ity of the three annotated components (intentions, utterances, and executions) to identify potential in-consistencies or ambiguities, achieving 97% agree-ment with the authors’ annotations.

Agent and tools. All models use a common ReAct framework (Yao et al., 2023), interleaving reason-ing, actions, and environment observations. We reuse the search tools and data resources provided by WebShop and TravelPlanner. Alongside its ac-tions, each agent predicts the complete active inten-tion as constraints with fields, values, and priorities. Execution details and prompts are provided in Ap-pendix B. 

Inference settings. All agents use each model’s de-fault decoding configuration. We do not explicitly set temperature, sampling parameters, or reasoning budgets; reasoning models therefore use their de-fault reasoning effort. TravelPlanner agents may take up to 8 steps per turn, with up to 12 search calls per step and a 12k-token output limit, increased to 48k if the response is truncated. WebShop agents may take up to 20 environment actions per turn, with an approximately 8k-token output limit. 

Evaluation. We evaluate all models on 304 Trav-elPlanner trajectories (1,946 turns) and 334 Web-Shop trajectories (1,820 turns), replaying the same annotated user utterance sequence for every model. These updates are grounded in annotators’ inde-pendent exploration and reference executions in the shared environment, enabling controlled com-parisons under the same evolving requirements. Agents receive the user history and public environ-ment observations; gold annotations and human ref-erence outcomes are withheld. All LLM judgments are produced by GPT-5.6 Sol, with one judgment per item, default decoding, and a 16k-token out-put limit. GPT-5.6 Sol is also an evaluated model. The judge assesses constraint matching and satis-faction against fixed, human-derived criteria and environment evidence, using human reference exe-cutions for outcome comparison. Model identities are withheld from judge prompts, and all metrics are computed deterministically in code following Section 3.3. We validate LLM judgments against human evaluations on 100 sampled agent outputs, achieving 95% agreement. Evaluation details and judging prompts are provided in Appendix C.

6 Discussion 

Successful task execution requires both valid ac-tions and an accurate understanding of what the user intends to achieve (Bansal et al., 2024). Our benchmark examines this connection by evaluat-ing whether agents maintain and act on user in-tentions as constraints and priorities evolve. The results expose difficulties in (1) revising intentions while preserving earlier requirements, (2) under-standing trade-offs, and (3) executing the resulting constraints. These challenges motivate studying how agents interpret revisions within their con-text. This connects to prior work on preference trade-offs (Chen, 2009) and memory updates (Wu et al., 2024). Future approaches could jointly con-sider user feedback, dialogue history, and environ-ment state to determine which requirements have changed and which remain active. When context leaves the user’s intention uncer-tain, agents could ask targeted clarification ques-tions. Prior work aims to maximize information gain to determine when and what to ask for clarifi-cation (Wang et al., 2025; Kobalczyk et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). Our benchmark further specifies what this information consists of, including con-straint content, scope, priority, and changes across turns, providing finer-grained targets for deciding what to ask as user intentions evolve. 

7 Conclusion 

We introduced IntentDelta, a multi-turn bench-mark for evaluating agents under evolving user con-straints and priorities in WebShop and TravelPlan-ner. Human-constructed trajectories pair natural-language updates with cumulative intention anno-tations and reference actions, enabling separate evaluation of intention recovery and action quality. Across eleven models, the results reveal incomplete recovery of active requirements, frequent priority errors, and execution failures despite higher inten-tion recovery. These findings motivate improved methods for maintaining current requirements, up-dating their priorities, and verifying actions against environment evidence. 

8 Limitations 

IntentDelta uses human-authored trajectories in two task environments, which may not fully repre-sent natural user interactions or generalize to other domains. For infeasible tasks, Hard Success uses the human reference as a comparison threshold (Ma ≥ Mg). The reference captures one plausi-ble compromise among competing requirements, while other reasonable solutions may satisfy differ-ent subsets of constraints. Consequently, this met-ric depends on the selected reference and does not fully capture the quality of alternative trade-offs. Three discrete priority tiers also simplify prefer-ences that may be contextual or continuous.

A Human Annotation 

Our annotation interface supports the construction and review of multi-turn trajectories in WebShop and TravelPlanner. Each turn contains a natural-language user utterance, the current cumulative intention state, and a human reference action. An-notators inspect environment evidence alongside these components to check consistency between what the user communicates, what the user intends, and what the reference execution achieves. Fig-ures 6(a) and 7(a) show the utterance, constraint, and priority editors. Figure 6(b) shows product evi-dence used to review a reference selection, while Figure 7(b) shows the itinerary editor and cost breakdown. 

Ethics approval. The data collection and anno-tation protocol was approved by our institutional review board. 

Recruitment. For the external validation stage described in Section 4.2, we recruited university students through word of mouth and recruitment posters. These participants validated sampled tra-jectories constructed by the author team. Partici-pants provided informed consent through a consent form before beginning annotation and received $15 for every 30 cases annotated. 

Utterances and cumulative intentions. Annota-tors can edit the user utterance and add, revise, or remove structured constraints. The intention state records all currently active requirements, including those carried over from previous turns. Constraints are assigned to three priority tiers: Must-have , Pre-ferred , and Optional . The interface displays these tiers as separate columns and supports drag-and-drop reassignment. Constraint edits propagate to subsequent turns where applicable, while preserv-ing explicit later overrides. Annotators can navi-gate between turns to review the resulting trajectory and save all changes at the trajectory level. 

Environment evidence and reference actions. 

For WebShop, annotators inspect candidate prod-ucts, prices, descriptions, attributes, and available options. A reference action specifies a selected product and its exact option values. For TravelPlanner, annotators inspect transporta-tion, accommodation, restaurant, and attraction records and edit a day-by-day reference itinerary. Each day specifies its location, transportation, meals, attractions, and accommodation. The in-terface also provides a cost breakdown based on the available records, including party size, accom-modation occupancy, and minimum-night billing. The displayed breakdown describes billing assump-tions; minimum-stay validity is assessed sepa-rately using the nights explicitly represented in the itinerary. Components not costed by the benchmark are explicitly marked. 

Feasibility and trade-offs. Annotators record whether the active must-have requirements are jointly feasible in the reviewed environment and may document unavoidable trade-offs. Reference actions represent concrete human choices under these conditions and need not satisfy every active requirement.

12 B Agent Execution and Prompts 

B.1 Execution Protocol Fixed-user interaction protocol. During dataset construction, annotators formulate intention up-dates in response to outcomes obtained along a human reference trajectory. During evaluation, we replay the resulting user utterances in their original order, using the same utterance sequence for every evaluated agent. At each turn, the agent infers the cumulative intention from the available user history and interacts with the task environment. The gold intention, human priorities, feasibility annotation, and reference action are withheld from the execu-tion agent. Result-grounded intention evolution describes dataset construction. Evaluation replays a shared sequence of annotated user updates rather than generating new user responses to each tested agent’s actions. 

Intention prediction. The agent reports the com-plete active intention, including requirements car-ried over from earlier turns, using the schema in Appendix B.2. Each requirement includes its value, priority, and any necessary traveler, location, or time context. 

Domain-specific execution. In WebShop, the agent observes the current page and can search, inspect products, select options, navigate between result pages, and finalize a product selection. Its prompt includes the user utterance history and a concise trace of earlier internal steps within the current turn. In TravelPlanner, the agent can invoke CitySearch , FlightSearch ,

AccommodationSearch , RestaurantSearch ,

AttractionSearch , and 

GoogleDistanceMatrix . Retrieved informa-tion is recorded through NotebookWrite , and the agent submits a complete itinerary through 

Planner . The underlying reference database is exposed through tool observations rather than directly supplied as gold information. Execution proceeds within a configured internal-step budget and records the intention prediction, actions, obser-vations, and termination reason. C Evaluation Details and Prompts 

C.1 Evaluation Procedure 

The offline evaluation pipeline separates baseline preparation, model-output judging, and determin-istic score aggregation. Both domains use the same high-level prepare–run–summarize work-flow. Each model is evaluated once per trajec-tory. We report mean scores over scored turns, and the Average row in Table 3 is the arithmetic mean across the eleven models. 

Baseline preparation. For each turn, prepara-tion constructs a shared baseline from the human annotations. The baseline freezes the active gold constraints, their priorities, atomic requirements for intention matching, per-constraint satisfaction criteria, human feasibility, and reference-action judgments. The same baseline is reused across evaluated models. Source and configuration finger-prints are recorded to prevent reuse of incompatible cached judgments. 

Intention judging. The intention judge decom-poses predicted requirements into atoms and matches them one-to-one with the frozen gold atoms. Content correctness requires both the value and applicability scope to match. Joint correctness additionally requires the predicted priority to match the human annotation. Priority is taken from the agent’s original prediction and cannot be inferred or replaced by the judge. The judge also identi-fies changes relative to the previous prediction to support change-specific evaluation. 

Action judging. The action judge evaluates the submitted outcome against the frozen criteria inde-pendently of the agent’s intention prediction. For each in-scope constraint, it returns a satisfaction verdict and supporting evidence. WebShop judg-ments use the selected product’s catalog informa-tion and actual option selections. TravelPlanner judgments use the submitted itinerary and support-ing environment records, supplemented by deter-ministic cost and accommodation-validity checks. A claimed attribute or a stated intention alone is insufficient evidence that an action satisfies a re-quirement. 

Deterministic action scoring. Action evaluation uses each in-scope gold constraint field as one scor-ing unit. Atomization is used for intention match-ing and does not split a field into additional action-scoring units. For a field containing a conjunction of requirements, every component must be sup-ported for that field to receive satisfaction credit. Scores are computed using the metrics defined in Section 3.3. Only a satisfied verdict receives satisfaction credit; violated and unknown verdicts receive none. 

WebShop selection checks. A selection fails these checks when there is no final product selec-16 tion, when selected options have an invalid repre-sentation, or when a selected option name or value is not supported by the product record. An avail-able option is not evidence that the agent selected it; requirements involving size, color, or other variants are judged using the recorded selection. A selected ASIN that is absent from the catalog snapshot is treated as a data error by the evaluator. 

TravelPlanner accommodation checks. The ac-commodation checks assess minimum stays and capacity using matched accommodation records. Each itinerary day with a non-empty accommoda-tion slot counts as one booked night. Every con-secutive stay at a matched listing must satisfy that listing’s minimum-night requirement. For each night with matched listings, their combined max-imum occupancy must accommodate the travel party. A detected violation of either condition fails the accommodation-validity check. Minimum-stay validity is based on the booked nights, rather than on an assumed payment for additional, unbooked nights. These checks assess accommodation feasi-bility; individual user requirements are evaluated separately through per-constraint judgments. 

Missing information and aggregation. An un-known constraint-satisfaction verdict receives no satisfaction credit. If the human feasibility label is absent, the scorer uses the feasible branch of Gt.If a human reference outcome is absent, the scorer assumes that the reference satisfies every in-scope constraint when computing the reference counts. Missing gold intention is an evaluation error. For each metric, turn-level scores are averaged over turns for which that metric is defined. Inten-tion and action evaluation maintain separate cover-age counts and denominators. Evaluator, API, and data errors must be distinguished from unsuccess-ful agent outcomes when interpreting this coverage. F Prediction Precision and Obsolete Retention 

Intention Recovery does not penalize additional predictions. We therefore provide two supplemen-tary diagnostics. Precision measures the fraction of predicted constraint atoms matched to active gold constraints. Obsolete Retention measures how often a previously correctly recorded requirement remains immediately after the user overrides, re-laxes, or removes it, without matching any active constraint. Retention is identified through exact matches to the agent’s previous prediction and may miss paraphrased cases. Precision should be interpreted cautiously be-cause unmatched predictions may include valid fixed task parameters omitted from the gold inten-tion or differences in constraint granularity.
