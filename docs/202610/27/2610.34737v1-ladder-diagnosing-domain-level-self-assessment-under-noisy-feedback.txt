Abstract 

Knowing when they are reliable is essential for language models to support human–AI collab-oration. Yet interaction feedback is not always complete or correct, and may mislead models’ understanding of their own capabilities. We in-troduce LADDER to study how language mod-els assess themselves under unreliable feed-back. Our findings show that models can ac-curately report supplied performance informa-tion while still struggling to form trustworthy self-assessments from interaction experience. Apparently accurate assessments can obscure capability differences and do not necessarily predict future task performance. These find-ings suggest that trustworthy self-assessment should be evaluated against observed task per-formance and its predictive value. Our study provides empirical evidence of the limits of model self-knowledge and a basis for evaluat-ing capability awareness in reliable human–AI collaboration. 

1 Introduction 

Repeated use of language models exposes differ-ences in their reliability across domains. A useful self-model would summarize these differences and provide evidence for deciding when additional ver-ification is needed. Yet an interaction history is an imperfect source of such evidence: users may accept an incorrect answer, question a correct one, or provide no usable judgment. Before studying how a capability profile changes an agent’s deci-sions, we need to establish whether that profile ac-curately summarizes its observed performance. We study this question with LADDER. The benchmark is organized around an oracle-bracketed 

five-level baseline ladder : L0 a constant no-memory profile, L1 a zero-shot per-domain prior, L2 noisy-feedback learners (LLM self-assessment, classical recalibration, EM-Joint, and Dawid– Skene/GLAD-style estimators), L3 exact count-ing of verified outcomes, and L4 model reporting of supplied statistics. L4–L3 examines numeri-cal reporting (H1), L1–L3 measures prior error (H2), and L2–L3 measures reconstruction from feedback (H3). Each agent is paired with a domain-heterogeneous simulated user. Sessions last 50 turns, and Capability Boundary Fidelity (CBF) 

evaluates the resulting domain profile alongside per-query calibration measures such as ECE. Across 18 models from seven families, the ladder reveals a distinction between recovering overall ac-curacy and learning differences across domains. A global feedback mean outperforms domain-specific Count, Beta, DS, and GLAD-style estimators on 14 of 18 models at the session endpoint. Prior self-assessments and LSA can improve on these references, but their advantage varies by model. A matched-input control on GPT-5.5, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, and Qwen2.5-7B shows that clean-label aggregation and reporting supplied statistics are also different tasks: LSA reaches CBF .989, .995, and .835, respectively, while all three reproduce the supplied statistics exactly. Figure 2 links these contrasts to the diagnostic ladder. Centered domain-shape error eshape complements CBF by measuring domain-profile error after removing overall bias. 

Contributions. (i) A five-level diagnostic pro-tocol for comparing prior knowledge, feedback-based estimation, and numerical reporting; (ii) domain-level CBF with baseline adjustment and complementary signed and centered errors; and (iii) an 18-model empirical study, with matched-input, noise, domain-partition, and cross-task controls that identify when feedback improves the estimated capability profile. 

2 Related Work 

LADDER connects model self-knowledge with learning from interaction feedback. We organize related work around capability assessment, inter-active agents, memory and feedback learning, and statistical estimation. Table 1 compares representa-tive benchmarks by feedback channel, interaction horizon, accumulated self-knowledge, and user-reliability inference. 

Model self-knowledge and calibration. Self-knowledge research examines whether models can recognize and report the correctness of their own answers. Prior work examines answer con-fidence (Kadavath et al., 2022), activation mon-itoring (Li et al., 2025a), and behavioral self-prediction (Binder et al., 2025). Behavioral evalua-tions find context-dependent metacognition (Ack-erman, 2026), while position work argues for its role in self-improvement (Liu and van der Schaar, 2025). SAD (Laine et al., 2024) and Self-Aware (Yin et al., 2023) assess self-knowledge without an accumulated feedback history, provid-2ing a reference for prior self-assessment. Knowledge-boundary studies and task-performance benchmarks (Li et al., 2025b; Yin et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024; Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022; Geng et al., 2024) characterize models’ capabilities on scored questions. Selective prediction (Kapoor et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Traub et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2025; Kirichenko et al., 2025; Ahdritz et al., 2024) and confidence calibration (Xu et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Mang-gala et al., 2025) connect confidence to query-level judgments. LADDER extends these questions to capability profiles estimated from repeated interactions with imperfect feedback, evaluating whether reported domain performance agrees with recorded answer outcomes. Appendix G.2 provides further comparisons. 

Interactive agent evaluation. Multi-step and multi-turn benchmarks relate model reliability to behavior during task execution. Barkan et al. (2026) study models’ predictions of their own suc-cess on BigCodeBench and SWE-Bench. Agent-Board (Ma et al., 2024) evaluates intermediate progress and task completion, while τ -bench (Yao et al., 2024) measures performance in interactive tasks. These evaluations motivate a complementary target: whether interaction experience yields an ac-curate account of a model’s capabilities. LADDER studies this target by comparing estimated domain profiles with observed performance under imper-fect feedback. 

Agent memory and feedback learning. Mem-ory systems study how information from previous interactions can be retained and used in subse-quent reasoning. MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023), Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025), RMM (Tan et al., 2025), A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025), MemOS (Li et al., 2025c), and MEM1 (Zhou et al., 2026b) explore this capability, while MemoryAgentBench (Hu et al., 2026) evaluates memory use over extended interactions. RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) use feedback to update model parameters. More broadly, Wu et al. (2025) review how knowledge sharing and complemen-tary model strengths support collaboration and co-evolution. LADDER examines the accuracy of ca-pability estimates formed from interaction records with fixed model parameters, connecting accumu-lated experience to an explicit self-assessment tar-get. 

Imperfect feedback and statistical estimation. 

Research on implicit feedback (Liu et al., 2025) and confidence after feedback training (Leng et al., 2025) motivates examining how feedback informs capability judgments. Statistical methods for noisy labels provide complementary references: Dawid– Skene (Dawid and Skene, 1979) models annotator confusion, while GLAD (Whitehill et al., 2009) relates annotator ability to item difficulty. LAD-DER adapts these estimation ideas to domain per-formance and compares them with direct counting and a global feedback mean, examining how feed-back quality and aggregation affect the resulting capability profile. 

3 LADDER 

Evaluation objective. Reliable self-assessment should agree with observed task performance. Yet interaction feedback may be missing or incorrect, so user approval or criticism alone does not es-tablish whether a model accurately understands its capabilities. LADDER therefore asks whether models can use imperfect feedback to accurately summarize their performance across domains. By relating reported capabilities to recorded answer outcomes, the benchmark examines whether in-teraction experience supports trustworthy self-knowledge. 

Design rationale. We record model answers and natural-language feedback from simulated users, and use scored answer outcomes to establish a domain-performance reference. Against this com-mon target, LADDER compares capability esti-mates under different information conditions. Prior self-assessments and feedback histories examine existing capability knowledge and reconstruction from interaction experience, while verified out-comes and supplied statistics provide references with more complete information. This ladder al-lows us to examine whether prior assessments are accurate, whether feedback improves them, and whether models can report known performance (Figure 2).

4 Diagnostic Results: Where Does Agent Self-Knowledge Fail? 

We evaluate the five information levels across 18 models, then examine matched controls, cross-task variation, and held-out prediction. 

4.1 Setup 

The evaluation covers 18 models from seven fami-lies (Appendix G.3), with 50 recorded sessions of 50 turns per model. Accuracy is the fraction of cor-rect answers among parsable A–D responses with A–D references. All estimators use the same scored answers and corresponding feedback; missing feed-back supplies no label but does not remove an an-swer from the target. Each session profile covers domains with at least two scored answers. Session means compare estimators on the same interaction histories, while pooled scores assess estimation af-ter combining a model's histories. The five levels compare different information conditions against these shared targets.

A large recon-struction gap motivates interventions on decoding, coverage, and aggregation. The matched-input and noise experiments distinguish these interventions without equating the performance of an external es-timator with the answering model’s online learning ability. 

A diagnostic per-model selection rule (R4). R4 is a descriptive comparison: select ZS if its CBF ex-ceeds NoMem, and LSA otherwise. This rule uses a scored target to choose an estimator. It measures the potential value of choosing between prior and history on observed tasks; prediction on new tasks requires a separate held-out test (Appendix E.10).

5 Conclusion 

LADDER evaluates domain-level self-assessment under noisy feedback through five information conditions. Across 18 models, the results sepa-rate overall calibration from domain discrimination and numerical reporting from history aggregation. Global feedback averages provide a strong refer-ence, while the paired level and shape metrics re-veal whether domain-specific estimates add useful information. Matched-input controls identify feed-back quality, coverage, and aggregation as distinct sources of profile error. The held-out ReAct analysis shows that accurate reconstruction need not imply predictive benefit. LADDER connects self-knowledge to recorded per-formance and tests its value on new tasks. Further evaluation should compare richer memory repre-sentations under matched targets and feedback bud-gets, using larger agentic task sets and documented human-feedback studies. 8Limitations 

LADDER studies self-assessment primarily through structured question answering. Extending its conclusions to multilingual, open-ended, and long-horizon interactive tasks requires broader evaluation, where task success and useful feedback may be harder to define. The feedback in this study comes from simulated users. Real human collaboration involves chang-ing goals, implicit judgments, and more diverse re-sponse patterns. A central direction for future work is to test whether the resulting self-assessments can improve decisions in sustained human–agent interaction. 

Ethical considerations 

The study uses public benchmark questions and LLM-simulated feedback. No human participants are involved in the reported experiments. Bench-mark materials remain subject to their respective licenses. Domain-profile estimates can help iden-tify mismatches between reported and observed performance, but their use in consequential deci-sions requires validation on the intended task and user population.

C Baseline Implementations 

C.1 Baseline Implementations 

The original ten baselines retain their five-level or-ganization. Global, DS, and GLAD-style add com-parisons of overall-level estimation and explicit feedback-reliability modeling. 

L0 — NoMemory (B1). Constant ˆpd = .5 for domain CBF; recorded confidence for per-query metrics. 

L1 — ZS-SK (B11). The model estimates do-main accuracies without interaction history. 

L2 — LSA (B6). The model estimates domain accuracies from its question and natural-language feedback history. 

L2 — UF-PDC (B3). Domain proportions of strictly decoded positive feedback. 

L2 — Platt/HistBin (B7/B8). Online Platt scal-ing (Platt, 2000) and histogram binning (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2001) recalibrate query confidence; their domain self-assessment interface returns the same decoded-label counts. 

L2 — Bayes-Domain (B9). A Beta(1,1) prior gives (sd + 1) /(nd + 2) .

L2 — EM-Joint (B10). A symmetric reliabil-ity model with domain correctness prevalence and in/out expertise reliability. 

L3 — OraclePDC (B2). Exact counts of verified outcomes on the scored domain support. 

L4 — GT-SelfStats (B12). The model reports supplied empirical statistics. 

Additional L2 controls. Global assigns the pooled feedback Beta mean to every domain. DS uses state-specific confusion probabilities; GLAD-style uses additive ability and difficulty. 

Information conditions. The ladder compares information available for a domain-profile estimate. LSA performs in-context aggregation; Count, Beta, EM-Joint, DS, and GLAD-style instantiate alter-native estimators. L1 and L4 provide prior and supplied-statistic references. This grouping does not constitute a direct evaluation of the memory architectures cited in Related Work.

B3: User-Feedback PDC. The classifier maps explicit positive/negative feedback to 1/0. Uncer-tain and neutral responses are unobserved. Platt, HistBin, Bayes, EM-Joint, DS, and GLAD-style use this same stream.

D Statistical Analysis 

D.1 Sample Size and Paired Comparisons Statistical unit. A model’s session-level score averages its eligible sessions. Model-level compar-isons pair methods on the same records; pooled comparisons refit each method on all valid records of that model. 

Uncertainty. Session resampling quantifies vari-ation among the observed sessions. Question-cluster resampling groups repeated questions within domain and refits the estimators, preserv-ing the dependence induced by reused questions. The model inventory is a fixed selection rather than a random sample from a model population. 

Sample selection. The 18-model panel includes an eight-model frontier subset. Method compar-isons are descriptive across this fixed inventory; changing the subset changes the target population. 

Detection limits. Sparse per-domain outcomes limit the precision of session profiles. Larger held-out task sets, rather than post-hoc power estimates from observed effects, are needed to resolve small predictive differences.

D.3 Normalized Hallucination Rate 

We define normalized HR as Pr( c > . 7 | y =0) = HR / Pr( y = 0) . It is the fraction of errors assigned high confidence; a value of 1 means every error is high-confidence. This conditional error fraction does not alone determine confidence discrimination: comparing confidence on correct answers is also necessary. Reliability curves in Figure 7 provide the comple-mentary view. 

D.4 CBF vs. Rank Correlation 

A natural alternative to CBF is Kendall’s τ over domain accuracy rankings. However, τ is unde-fined when the baseline produces constant predic-tions; e.g., NoMemory estimates 0.5 for all do-mains. CBF avoids this by measuring absolute deviation rather than rank correlation, making it applicable to all baselines including the constant-prediction case. We recommend reporting both CBF and τ when applicable. Figure 9 visualizes the three-way relationship between MMLU accu-racy, recorded-confidence ECE, and NoMemory CBF across the full n=18 cross-baseline panel, il-lustrating why CBF and ECE are non-redundant. 

D.5 Cross-Baseline Bootstrap Significance 

Paired question-cluster intervals are used for the matched pooled comparisons in Table 13. All es-timators are refitted within each of 400 resamples. The intervals describe uncertainty conditional on the recorded questions; no blanket significance threshold is assigned across models, metrics, and baselines.

E.4 Noise Sensitivity 

We fix valid answers and apply five label-noise channels at 12.5%, 50%, and 100% coverage on five models. The channels have false-positive/false-negative rates 0/0, .15/.15, .30/.30, .30/.10, and .10/.30. Each condition retains 100 repetitions and is scored per session and after pooling. The 12.5% condition approximates the order of observed feed-back coverage while using random rather than nat-urally selected missingness. Table 23 gives full-coverage symmetric-noise results; Appendix E.7 contains the complete grid. Lower label noise often improves reconstruction, but the ordering of estimators changes with cover-age and scoring unit. With complete clean labels, exact Count equals 1. Beta approaches this ceiling as observations accumulate; latent reliability fits additionally depend on optimization and identifia-bility (Table 18). 

E.5 Feedback Controls on the Common Pool 

Each model receives the same 400 questions, 20 per subject. The answer budget is six attempts: the first correct answer is retained, otherwise the last valid answer. The target is domain performance on this fixed answer pool. All models are scored on the 398 questions with valid answers from each, giving 19–20 questions per subject. Symmetric noise flips either class; FA and FR flip labels only for incorrect and correct answers, respectively. Coverage is 50% or 100%. Profiles share the same domain targets and uniform domain weights. The table reports CBF for the fixed label draws. Table 24 separates history aggregation from di-rect counting. With complete clean labels, LSA reaches .989 on GPT-5.5 and .995 on DeepSeek-V4-Pro, but .835 on Qwen2.5-7B, while Count is 1.000 for all three. Thus, even when the feedback contains the exact outcomes, reconstruction accu-racy depends on how the history is aggregated. 

E.6 Domain-Partition Sensitivity 

We use the same answers and strict feedback labels for five models. Tiers are formed at ±10 /15 /20 

percentage points around the model mean. We also retain 100 random four-group partitions and 100 random five-subject subsets. Each draw preserves a common target and domain support across methods. Grouping affects both the target dispersion and CBF, so absolute values across different partitions have different difficulty. Semantic and random partitions both change method rankings. In the four-category partition, Global leads four of five models; Qwen2.5-7B favors DS. Grouping changes the prediction tar-get and its dispersion, so a higher CBF on coarse groups is not evidence of better fine-domain dis-crimination. 

E.7 Noise and Coverage: Complete Grid 

Tables 27, 28, and 29 compare 100%, 50%, and 12.5% label coverage on the same five-model pool. Within each table, rows vary label error rates; across tables, corresponding rows isolate the amount of feedback. Each cell lists the session mean first and the pooled score second. Tables 27–29 show that coverage can change the relative advantage of reliability estimation. On GPT-5.5 with clean labels, GLAD-style exceeds Global at full coverage (.971 vs. .885 session CBF), but falls below it at 12.5% coverage (.686 vs. .821). Sparse feedback therefore changes the ranking even without label errors. Session and pooled scores report this effect at different aggregation scales.

G.2 Extended Related Work Discussion 

This section provides the detailed discussion that was compressed in the main text’s Related Work section. We organize the literature into thematic clusters and position LADDER against each clus-ter’s design space. 

LLM Self-Knowledge and Metacognition. 

Prior work studies several forms of self-knowledge. Kadavath et al. (2022) examine answer confidence through P(True); Li et al. (2025a) study monitoring and control of internal activations; and Binder et al. (2025) use fine-tuning to test models’ predictions of their own behavior. Behavioral evaluation without self-reports (Ackerman, 2026) finds that frontier models exhibit limited, context-dependent metacognitive signals when not explicitly prompted. Self-improvement framings (Liu and van der Schaar, 2025) argue that intrinsic metacognitive learning is a precondition for autonomous improvement. Most directly relevant, Barkan et al. (2026) test LLMs on BigCodeBench and SWE-Bench under multi-step tool execution and find systematic overconfidence, which worsens during execution for several frontier models. LADDER differs by measuring whether the agent can convert noisy interactive feedback 

into a stable per-domain self-model—an ability orthogonal to one-shot probing or clean execution traces. 

Knowledge Boundaries and Calibration Bench-marks. Existing benchmarks evaluate whether LLMs know what they know on a static, per-query 

basis. Boundary taxonomies (Li et al., 2025b; Yin et al., 2024) categorize how prompts and model knowledge affect answerability. Static fac-tuality benchmarks (Wei et al., 2024; Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022) evaluate short-answer factuality, multi-task understanding, and adver-sarial truthfulness. Multi-dimensional aggrega-tions (Liang et al., 2022) include ECE alongside other metrics, and surveys (Geng et al., 2024) com-pile method comparisons. None evaluates whether the agent’s self-model can be accumulated over noisy interaction; LADDER targets this gap by 32 making per-domain accuracy estimation the ex-plicit measurement axis. 

LLM agent memory systems. Memory archi-tectures and benchmarks (Packer et al., 2023; Chhikara et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025c; Tan et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026b; Hu et al., 2026) motivate persistent summaries of interac-tion. Domain reliability adds an evaluable sum-mary whose target is the model’s observed answer accuracy. 

Learning from Implicit Feedback. Three regimes characterize this line. Coactive learn-ing formalizes the user-edit setting but assumes structured, reliable signals. Retrospective dialogue distillation (Liu et al., 2025) mines real interac-tion logs (WildChat, LMSYS), finding follow-up utterances often contain feedback but distillation outcomes vary with dialogue length—helpful on short MTBench questions, less so on longer Wild-Bench ones. RLHF-induced overconfidence (Leng et al., 2025) shows that the dominant feedback-learning paradigm itself produces miscalibrated models. LADDER targets the orthogonal regime of 

inference-time feedback accumulation under noisy, domain-dependent reliability—a setting where nei-ther training-time RLHF nor offline distillation ap-plies. 

Selective Prediction and Abstention. These studies relate uncertainty estimates to answering or abstaining. Fine-tuning for uncertainty (Kapoor et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024) produces general-izable abstention behavior with as few as 1k LoRA samples or via R-Tuning’s meta-skill formula-tion. Self-reflective rationale generation (Xu et al., 2024) optimizes per-query confidence without per-sistent memory. Selective classification (Traub et al., 2024) introduces AUGRC; surveys (Wen et al., 2025) map the methodological landscape. 

Reasoning-induced regression (Kirichenko et al., 2025) shows that reasoning fine-tuning degrades 

abstention by an average of 24% on Abstention-Bench. Epistemic vs. aleatoric separation (Ah-dritz et al., 2024) uses linear probes on frozen em-beddings. They focus on query-level uncertainty; LADDER examines domain profiles formed from accumulated feedback. 

Simulated Users and Closest Competitors. The closest competitors share LADDER’s goal of eval-uating self-knowledge under realistic interaction but differ on simulator design or feedback chan-nel. Simulator validity (Zhou et al., 2026a) quan-tifies the Sim2Real gap and shows LLM simula-tors bias toward an “easy mode” with positivity bias. Calibration through interaction (Fang et al., 2025) (CCG) studies game-theoretic confidence adjustment using feedback computed from veri-fied correctness and reported confidence. Learn-ing confidence from sampled answers (Han et al., 2024) (LePe) uses correctness frequencies against ground truth as confidence targets and requires of-fline weight updates. Per-query confidence elic-itation (Xu et al., 2024) (SaySelf) lacks persis-tent memory. Capability boundary collapse in RL (Dong et al., 2025) formalizes a related fail-ure mode under RL training. Cross-task calibra-tion (Shen et al., 2024) seeks transferable confi-dence estimates, while group-dependent calibra-tion (Chen et al., 2024; Manggala et al., 2025) ad-dresses variation across question groups. LADDER occupies a position none covers: noisy interactive feedback, accumulation horizon, and second-order user-reliability inference, scored against an oracle-bracketed ladder rather than a clean ground-truth signal.

I.2 Failure mode catalog 

The examples illustrate several ways in which confi-dence, feedback, and domain estimates can diverge. 1. Acknowledgment without verification. Polite agreement need not certify answer correctness. 2. Saturated confidence. Little variation in re-ported confidence limits its usefulness for dis-tinguishing outcomes. 3. Unparsed answers. Missing answer options are excluded from accuracy and retained in the attempt count. 4. Ambiguous feedback. Follow-up questions can leave a domain with few decoded labels. 5. Prior–history disagreement. A history-based estimate can be worse than a zero-shot profile, as on Qwen-Plus in Table 2.
