# Multimodal Socially Aware Framework for Human-Robot Interaction in Virtual Reality 

Richard Xu 1, Dakarai Crowder 1, Alexis E. Block 2, and Wenzhen Yuan 1

Abstract — Social robots must coordinate speech and move-ment while interpreting multimodal human input. We present a multimodal framework that integrates speech, vision, and touch to generate contextually appropriate robot speech and movement. We evaluated the framework in virtual reality with 18 participants across five social interaction scenarios, com-paring its performance against a human-teleoperated baseline. Although participants rated the autonomous system above the neutral midpoint for both naturalness and social appropri-ateness, the teleoperated baseline received significantly higher ratings on both measures. Behavioral coding and qualitative feedback revealed that conversational timing, clarification of ambiguous requests, and spatial coordination were important factors contributing to this performance gap. These findings highlight key challenges in developing autonomous robots capable of natural, multimodal social interaction. 

Index Terms — Human-Robot Interaction, Touch in HRI 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

As robots become more integrated into daily life (e.g., in homes, schools, hospitals, public spaces), how can they behave in a socially appropriate and natural manner? En-dowing robots with this ability is no simple task, as they must continuously coordinate speech, body movement, and physical contact while adapting to interruptions, misunder-standings, and shifting social contexts. Humans manage this coordination effortlessly, yet this task remains extremely challenging for robots. Robots struggle to integrate multi-modal data streams, adapt to unpredictable human behavior, and respond through coordinated modalities, which are necessary for an interac-tion to feel natural. Previous researchers have attempted to solve this problem by fusing vision, eye gaze, and speech [1]–[5]. While humans use social touch as an important modality to communicate with each other, using touch as an input in robotics remains underexplored. Furthermore, most of these systems are validated through single-turn interactions or through videos of a robot interaction that participants watch, rather than through first-person interac-tion with the robot [1], [3], [6]. The absence of multi-turn interactions with an embodied robotic agent makes it difficult to understand the extent to which these systems are capable of social intelligence and to identify the system limitations contributing to poor perceptions of social intelligence.

To enable robots to interact naturally and appropriately across multiple modalities, we present a multimodal socially aware interaction framework that integrates speech, vision, and social touch to generate coordinated speech and robot movement. We represent all modalities as text or image and keep a running interaction history to enable multi-turn inter-actions. We deploy this framework on a mobile humanoid robot in a simulated environment where participants interact with it through a virtual reality (VR) headset. We evaluate the framework in VR rather than on a physical robot for five reasons: (1) VR provides a consistent and controlled environment across scenarios and participants; (2) VR allows instant switching between scenario contexts without physically resetting the environment; (3) simulation provides dense contact signals over the robot’s entire surface, which are difficult to obtain reliably from onboard tactile sensors; (4) the immersive headset and haptic gloves preserve the visual and tactile experience as closely as possible; and (5) a virtual robot removes the risk of injury during close-proximity contact. We evaluate the system across five social interaction scenarios using a within-subject comparison between our autonomous system and a human-teleoperated baseline. For each condition, the participants rated the perceived natural-ness and social appropriateness of the interaction and shared open-ended feedback regarding their expectations of robot behavior. Additionally, interaction failures in each condition from video recordings were coded. arXiv:2610.34534v1 [cs.RO] 25 Oct 2026 Our results show that participants generally perceived the autonomous system as socially appropriate, although the teleoperated baseline received significantly higher ratings for both appropriateness and naturalness. Behavioral anal-ysis revealed more missed acknowledgments, repeated user attempts, interruptions, and physical touch in autonomous interactions, consistent with participants attempting to repair communication breakdowns through multiple modalities. In this work, we contribute a multimodal VLM-driven framework that integrates speech, vision, and social touch to generate socially aware robot behavior. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Social-Physical Human-Robot Interaction 

Prior work has explored social touch across different robotic embodiments. Zoomorphic robots usually support free-form touch interaction, but respond from a predefined repertoire of pet-like behaviors [7]–[9]. Humanoid robots supporting social touch also respond through a predefined set of reactions [10]–[12]. One study advances this approach by modeling a continuous emotional state that evolves with the interaction history, but the meaning assigned to each touch and the robot’s expressive repertoire remain predefined [13]. However, a humanoid form may raise expectations of higher social intelligence [14]. Other works focus on touch within structured activities, such as hugging [15]–[17] or hand-clapping games [18]. Still, a review revealed that most works that combine speech and touch used Wizard-of-Oz control or scripted routines [19]. Thus, a robot should have the ability to interpret touch in the context of the interaction and respond autonomously beyond a predefined set of behaviors. 

B. Physically Embodied Agents for Social Interaction 

Generating socially appropriate robot behavior has tradi-tionally relied on rule-based systems grounded in human behavioral research [20], [21] or on models learned from hu-man interaction data [22], but large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used as high-level reasoning engines for social robots due to their ability to reason over natural language and environmental context. These models can coordinate multimodal behaviors like speech, gestures, and head movement [6], [23], as well as physical assistance [23]. Beyond action selection, LLMs have been used to generate expressive verbal and nonverbal behavior and to model social dynamics such as turn-taking, posture, and gaze [1]–[3]. Recent work shows these capabil-ities can be achieved using smaller, locally deployed models and refined through self-feedback mechanisms [24], [25]. This body of work demonstrates that LLMs can generate socially aware robot behaviors; however, these systems rely almost entirely on visual and audio modalities, with social touch largely unexamined. Evaluations of subjective ratings or isolated behaviors offer little insight into where interac-tions break down or how users respond when they do. 

C. Virtual Reality for Human-Robot Interaction 

Developing robotic systems that interact with humans can be time-consuming and costly and may introduce safety concerns during early-stage testing. Virtual reality provides an alternative platform for evaluating robot behaviors in a controlled environment and has been adopted in HRI research [26]–[28]. Prior work has shown that perceptions of robots are often preserved across live, VR, and screen-based presentations [29], and that trust and engagement measures may not significantly differ between real-world, VR, and screen-based interactions [30]. Although some differences have been observed, such as increased interpersonal distances in VR [31], these findings suggest that VR is a useful and experimentally controlled platform for studying human-robot interaction. Fig. 2 presents the proposed system, which enables multi-modal social interaction between a human and a robot. The system integrates speech, tactile, and visual data as inputs and uses a pre-trained VLM to output a sequence of action-speech pairs. Speech and tactile inputs act as triggers that prompt a new VLM response, whereas visual input is in-cluded in the prompt only as contextual information and does not by itself initiate a response. For example, asking the robot a question will trigger a VLM response, but waving to the robot will not. The system maintains a chronological textual history of up to N = 200 recent interaction entries, which is included in each prompt and cleared at the start of each scenario. This summary is included in the prompt to provide history to the VLM and preserve the context necessary for multi-turn interaction. The system was deployed on a Meta Quest 3 headset with Meta XR SDK hand tracking, running in a Unity simulation environment with Pollen Robotics’ Reachy 2021 as the virtual robot embodiment. Speech was processed using Deepgram Nova-3 for transcription and a voice-cloning Qwen3-TTS model for speech generation, with Gemini-3-flash serving as the pre-trained VLM.

For speech input, audio is collected through the VR headset microphone and transcribed to text in real time using the Deepgram API. To obtain social touch input in the form of natural language so it can be understood by the VLM, we first record collision events in the simulated VR environment. Then, we use a classifier to determine the gesture performed, contact location, and number of repetitions. In the simulated environment, each of the user’s hands is represented using 24 spherical colliders: three spheres of 1.4 cm in diameter along each finger and a 3×3 grid of 2.0 cm spheres on the palm, at locations shown in Fig. 3. Each collider is attached to a skeletal point on the hand mesh through a spring-damper system. Collisions between each on-hand collider and the robot collision mesh are detected through Unity’s built-in collision detection [32] and used to generate a spatiotemporal point cloud. When a participant makes contact with the robot, the system collects the point cloud data in each frame until it detects no contact for 0.5 seconds. The system then parses the data as a single touch event and forwards it to the gesture classification module. To classify the touch event, selected features are extracted from the point cloud, including duration, force, number of contacts, centroid movement, and contact location. We collected a dataset of 1756 samples across 8 social touch gestures (listed in Fig. 4) and 7 pilot participants before the experiment. We classify the performed gesture by averaging class probabilities from three classifiers: Gradient Boosting, an RBF-kernel SVM, and Extra Trees. Each sample in the dataset is summarized by a 60-dimensional feature vector composed of pre-defined statistical features, of which the most discriminative are the total path length of the contact centroid across frames, the event duration, the per-frame contact count, and the mean and peak force — i.e., whether the contact slides across the body, how long and how hard it presses, and over how large an area. Our leave one subject out accuracy was 78.2 ± 8.2 %. 

B. Social Reasoning & Behavior Generation 

To generate socially aware robot motion and speech, we used a VLM with chain-of-thought reasoning (Gemini-3-flash). Whenever the speech or tactile module triggers prompt generation, a new prompt is formulated. Each text prompt consists of three parts: a fixed pre-prompt, the robot’s current execution state, and interaction history. In addition to the text prompt, the VLM also receives an image captured by the robot’s eye camera in the virtual environment at the time of prompt generation. 1) The pre-prompt gives relevant contextual information about the interaction and a list of motion primitives as possible output actions. If set, the running motion is canceled, the robot resets to asafe pose, the motion queue is cleared, and the new action–speech pairs are executed; if not set while the robot is busy, the current behavior continues, and the input is retained only as context. 3) Speech interruption: Robot speech is cut off imme-diately if the user starts speaking, or skipped entirely if the user is already speaking. IV. E VALUATION 

To evaluate the system and assess the perceived natu-ralness and social appropriateness of the interactions, we conducted a VR user study using a simulated Pollen Robotics Reachy (2021). Participants completed ten counterbalanced scenarios in total: five with our proposed autonomous system and five with the teleoperated upper baseline. We selected a VR setup to enable collision detection, safe physical interaction, and controlled environmental conditions during human–robot contact, while also facilitating standardization across experimental conditions. 

A. Setup 

The study included a custom haptic glove system and a monitor displaying a mirrored view of the participant’s VR environment for the experimenter. A camera, facing the participant at an approximate height of five feet, was placed in the room to record the participant. The custom haptic gloves were constructed using six linear resonant actuators per glove, positioned at the fingers and palm and controlled by an Arduino Nano microcontroller communicating via Bluetooth [26]. The virtual robot was represented as an articulated body to allow compliant in-teraction; participants could move the robot’s arms and head through direct contact, though the base remained rigid unless a base movement command was being executed. Participants could also interact with the robot via speech. To avoid the need to remove the headset between scenarios, a question-naire interface was embedded within the VR environment, allowing participants to complete all ratings in-headset. 

Human-Teleoperated Baseline: To evaluate our system, we created a human-teleoperated baseline, which serves as a goal our system strives to reach. For this system, the operator was situated in a separate room with access to the robot’s camera feed and the participant’s audio, allow-ing real-time perception of the interaction. The operator responded using their own voice and selected movements from the same set of motion primitives available to the autonomous system through a web-based control interface. A single trained operator conducted all teleoperated sessions to ensure consistency across participants. Our autonomous system used text-to-speech (TTS) output in place of the operator’s voice. To minimize bias from using different voices across conditions, we used a voice-cloning TTS model that imitates the teleoperator’s voice using pre-recorded voice clips. LLM generation accounted for most of the response latency (M = 2.3 s, SD = 0.5 s; 62% of the 3.7 s mean), followed by TTS synthesis (M = 0.85 s, 23%). 

B. Interaction Scenarios 

We created five different social scenarios that were chosen to elicit different types of social interactions. We designed scenarios based on tasks people want home robots to perform [33], spanning functional and emotional interactions (Fig. 5). Scenarios S2 and S4 encouraged physical interaction without explicitly requiring touch: in S2, the robot blocked a narrow passage while dusting, while in S4, vacuum noise discour-aged verbal communication.

This experiment was approved by our university’s Institu-tional Review Board. Upon arrival, participants (age 18+) were told the general purpose of the study and provided informed consent, including consent to video recording. Participants were fitted with the VR headset and haptic gloves, and then the camera was turned on. To explore behavioral differences that might explain par-ticipants’ differing ratings of the two systems, behavioral coding of video recordings was performed. One author de-veloped a codebook and coded all recordings. Codebook de-velopment occurred in two phases. First, the author watched all recordings from both conditions once for familiarization, then again to inductively identify interaction breakdowns and indicators of breakdowns. Throughout this work, we operationalized interaction breakdowns as observable failures in communication or task execution, or behaviors suggesting such a failure, such as a participant repeating themselves. In the second phase, the author reviewed all open-ended participant responses to identify additional behaviors that participants perceived positively or negatively. Candidate codes from this phase were retained if they could be un-ambiguously observed from the videos, which the author confirmed by reviewing the recordings again. The full set of behavioral coding categories is reported in Figure 6(C). The author then used the finalized codebook to code all recordings. Interaction breakdowns were analyzed using a negative binomial regression to model the total number of interaction breakdowns with condition, scenario, and their interaction as predictors, and standard errors clustered by participant to account for repeated measures. Slider ratings for naturalness and appropriateness were analyzed using linear mixed-effects (LME) models with condition, scenario, and their interaction as fixed effects, participant as a random intercept, and prior robot attitude as a covariate. C. Touch Incidents 

Participants touched the robot in 47.8% of the autonomous interactions and 34.4% of teleoperated interactions. In 12 cases, participants touched the robot only in the autonomous condition; the reverse never occurred (Fig. 6(D)). Touch served different functions across scenarios. Participants used taps, pokes, and presses to get the robot’s attention in the vacuum and make-way scenarios and strokes and rubs to convey affection when comforting the robot. Incidental contact occurred when participants took the drink from the robot’s gripper. VI. Q UALITATIVE ANALYSIS 

Beyond the observable behaviors and subjective measures, we wanted to identify recurring themes in participants’ qual-itative feedback that contextualized the quantitative differ-ences between conditions. Thus, we conducted a qualitative analysis of the open-ended responses the users gave after each interaction. 

A. Verbal Interaction 

Participants described verbal interaction through two con-versational behaviors: timing (10 out of 18 participants) and clarification (8 out of 18 participants). Timing was discussed most often, encompassing both response latency and conversational pacing. P12 noted that, compared with the teleoperated robot, the autonomous system’s responses “were a little bit delayed [...] it would [feel] more natural [...] if the robot responded [faster].” Pacing mattered too: during the joke scenario, participants said the teleoperated robot’s pause between question and punchline felt natural, while the autonomous system’s immediate delivery broke the flow, since “the pause [was] essential to the joke” (P5). Clarification was another aspect of verbal interaction: 8 out of 18 participants described seeking clarification as the preferable way to handle ambiguous requests. During the drink scenario, several remarked that they would have preferred the autonomous system to ask which drink they wanted rather than choosing automatically. For instance, when P15 asked the robot to pass them a drink, they appreciated that the teleoperated robot followed up: “I liked how this time he asked me which drink I would like.” This suggests participants expected robots to resolve ambiguity collaboratively rather than independently. 

B. Embodied Coordination 

Fourteen out of 18 participants evaluated the robot not only by what it said but also by how it coordinated its movement and orientation within the shared environment. Expectations included moving in contextually appropriate directions, ori-enting toward the participant while speaking, and responding appropriately to physical touch. As one participant noted, “it agreed to move out of the way but it continued facing the wall while talking which was a little weird as people normally turn around to face each other.” Participants frequently described failures in movement, orientation, or touch responsiveness as disrupting the flow of interaction, indicating that appropriate embodiment formed an important component of perceived naturalness. 

C. Emotional Expectations 

The Comforting Robot scenario revealed differing expec-tations regarding the emotional role of robots. Many partici-pants (6 out of 18 participants) found the robot’s expression of sadness surprising, awkward, or unsettling, while others responded positively when the interaction felt meaningful and reciprocal. As one participant explained, “in [their] mind, a robot shouldn’t have to have human emotions, especially sad human emotions. I feel like if anything it should be happy-go-lucky all the time.” Three out of 18 participants also described feeling uncomfortable comforting the robot, and one participant attributed the interaction to the uncanny valley. In contrast, a smaller group (3 out of 18 participants) preferred interactions in which the robot expressed emotion with sufficient context and responded reciprocally, suggest-ing that emotional interactions were generally accepted when they aligned with participants’ expectations of the robot’s social role. VII. D ISCUSSION 

Participants generally perceived the autonomous system as natural and appropriate, with the mean ratings on both measures above the scale midpoint (Table III). Thus, our system produced reasonable behavior across the scenarios. However, the teleoperated baseline was rated significantly higher on both measures, particularly naturalness (Table II). We found no evidence that the condition difference varied across scenarios, as the condition × scenario interactions were not statistically significant. The behavioral coding and participants’ open-ended re-sponses suggest why this gap exists between the systems. The most common autonomous breakdowns (user repetitions, unacknowledged input, and user interruptions) reflect slow or poorly timed responses (Fig. 6(C)), and participants even described the autonomous responses as delayed or poorly paced (Section VI-A). The system’s mean response latency was 3.7 s, longer than the typical 250 ms gaps between turns in human conversation [35], suggesting that the delay was experienced as a communication breakdown. In contrast, interactions with the teleoperator involved few user repetitions, and the teleoperator asked for clarification when requests were ambiguous. Participants stated that they valued this behavior. Navigation errors also occurred with our autonomous system but were absent from the baseline. However, the teleoperator’s familiarity with the environment may explain this gap. These findings suggest that one of the main reasons why the system has a lower rating is the pacing of the interaction. Taken together, these findings suggest that improving pacing, ensuring the system seeks clarification in ambiguous situations, and improving spatial awareness could help close this gap. Participants touched the robot more often in the au-tonomous condition and never touched the robot only in the teleoperated condition (Fig. 6(D)). The greater frequency of touch in the autonomous condition, alongside more unac-knowledged input, suggests that participants may have used touch as an alternative communication channel when speech was unsuccessful. This observation motivates incorporating touch into multimodal interaction systems, although further evaluation is needed to determine whether touch improves recovery from communication breakdowns. Interestingly, emotional expression did not converge on a single preferred behavior. Whether the robot’s sadness seemed natural or unsettling seems to depend on whether the participants viewed the robot as a tool or a social agent. In this case, we cannot simply match the teleoperated baseline; we must align the behavior with each user’s expectations of the robot’s role. A. Limitations 

There were several limitations of this work. The teleop-erator’s familiarity with the virtual environment may have provided an advantage in spatial reasoning and localization. In addition, the voice-cloned TTS system had less expressive prosody than the teleoperator’s natural speech, which may have influenced perceptions of naturalness. Finally, the study was conducted in virtual reality, and the findings should be validated through future studies involving physical robots in real-world environments. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a multimodal framework integrating speech, vision, and social touch to generate socially aware robot behavior. In a VR study, participants rated the system as generally appropriate and natural and used touch alongside speech to communicate with it. The observed breakdowns and participant feedback suggest that conversational timing, clarification, and spatial coordination contribute to the gap between the autonomous system and the teleoperated base-line.
