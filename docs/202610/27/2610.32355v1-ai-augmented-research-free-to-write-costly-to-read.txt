# AI-Augmented Research: Free to Write, Costly to Read 1

Wolfgang Kuhle Corvinus University of Budapest, Hungary, E-mail wkuhle@gmx.de MEA, Max Planck Institute for Social Law and Social Policy, Munich, Germany 

Abstract: We study a model in which artificial intelligence lowers the cost of writing faster than the cost of reading. Authors differ in reputation, and time-constrained readers, such as referees, choose between ignoring, verifying, and trusting papers. As writing costs fall, the band of verified papers shrinks: outsiders are no longer read, and established authors are no longer checked. In the “costless writing limit,” the share of verified papers vanishes, and a paper’s impact depends on its author’s reputation alone. Readers can escape the flood by asking AI to summarize the literature. These summaries aggregate papers reliably only if their errors are sufficiently independent. Once the share of papers written with the same AI exceeds a threshold, summaries inherit the AI’s errors, and AI review filters out the original research that could correct them. 

Keywords: Attention, Verification, Reputation, Artificial Intelligence JEL: D83, D82, O33, I23 

# 1 Introduction 

Writing a page of research used to be expensive. Modern large language models have made it nearly free. At the same time, understanding and verifying a proof, a data set, or a calibration still takes time. This paper asks what happens to the market for research when the cost of writing falls faster than the cost of verification. Our model is populated by authors and readers. Authors differ in reputation, the probability that their research is correct, and readers allocate limited attention. For each paper, readers can ignore it, verify it at a cost, or trust it without checking. Authors benefit from producing papers and from having their work used. As writing becomes

Building on this model, Section 3 shows that, in equilibrium, readers verify papers by authors of intermediate reputation, ignore those with low reputation, and trust those with high reputation. As writing becomes cheaper, more papers compete for the same attention, making verification increasingly costly. The range of authors whose papers are verified shrinks: outsiders are no longer read, and established authors are no longer checked. In the costless writing limit, only a vanishing share of papers is verified, and a paper is used if and only if its author’s reputation exceeds a fixed threshold. Section 4 studies whether AI summaries can overcome the scarcity of attention. Asummary reporting the majority view of a literature works like the jury of Condorcet (1785): it recovers the correct answer when sufficiently many papers reach independent, better-than-random judgments. Papers written with the same AI, however, share its errors. Summaries thus overcome the attention bottleneck but create a new problem of correlated errors. In an alternative interpretation, our model studies journals’ editorial policies: ignoring corresponds to a desk rejection, verification to refereeing, and trusting without verification to acceptance on reputation. Taking this view, Section 4.1 examines what happens when the AI also acts as a referee. An AI that favors papers agreeing with its own answer filters out dissenting research. When the AI is wrong, these are precisely the papers that could correct its error. AI review, therefore, lowers the threshold at which a literature’s consensus is captured. If the AI is sufficiently resistant to disagreement, its answers dominate the literature even when no paper is written with it. Section 5 shows that the simplest remedy is an unrefereed preprint server from which summaries draw. It restores a correct consensus as long as AI-written papers stay below a certain threshold. 

Related literature. Simon (1971) observed that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. Related, Dasgupta and David (1994) argue that reputation drives the attention that different research results get. The growth in the number of papers that motivates our model is documented by Hanson et al. (2024). Card and DellaVigna (2013) document the rising burden of refereeing in economics, and Bergstrom and Gross 2(2026) show that review noise that rises with submission volume can push peer review into collapse. Liang et al. (2025) and Kobak et al. (2025) measure the rising share of scientific text written with large language models. Russo et al. (2025) find that AI-assisted reviews raise paper scores and acceptance rates. Our result on filtered originality relates to the evidence of Wang et al. (2017) that novel research is penalized. Our results on AI summaries and AI review relate to the evidence of Doshi and Hauser (2024) that generative AI reduces the collective diversity of novel content, and of Qian et al. (2026) that grant proposals written with heavy use of large language models are less distinct from previously funded work. Ladha (1992) shows that correlated votes undermine the jury theorem, and Zollman (2007) shows that scientific communities can be more reliable when their members share less. Relative to this literature, we derive how cheap writing leads to two failures. When writing becomes cheaper than verification, scarce attention is rationed by reputation rather than spread evenly as in Bergstrom and Gross (2026), and the ratio of the two costs decides whether AI helps unknown/low reputation authors to be read. When read-ers and journals turn to AI, errors that are correlated through a common model capture the consensus above an explicit share of AI-written papers, and AI review that screens on agreement lowers this share further because it removes the evidence that would correct the AI.

# 4 AI Summaries 

Scarce attention makes summaries and AI review attractive, and the cost of writing deter-mines the composition of the literature. Suppose that readers, instead of reading papers, can ask an AI to summarize the literature consensus on a question. A summary requires 7the same attention however many papers it covers, and we normalize this attention to zero, so that the flood of papers no longer exhausts readers’ attention. Consider a question with a true answer θ ∈ { 0, 1} and a large number of papers on it. A fraction 1 − s of these papers is written carefully: each reports the correct answer with probability q ∈ (1 /2, 1), independently of the others. The remaining fraction s is written with the same AI. These papers share the AI’s errors: they all report the same answer, which is correct with probability p ∈ (1 /2, 1). 8 We call these papers AI-written. The term refers to papers that adopt the AI’s answer without independent work. To make production consistent with Section 2, let a careful paper cost cw + cI , where cI > 0 is the cost of independent work, which AI does not lower, while an AI-written paper costs only 

cw. With this cost function and a common use probability u, a mass m ∈ (0 , 1) of authors who copy the AI writes ( b0 + bu )/c w papers each, and the others write ( b0 + bu )/(cw + cI )each, by (1). The share of AI-written papers is then s(cw) = m(cw +cI )/(cw +mc I ). Hence 

s(cw) is decreasing, with s → 1 as cw → 0. We assume that the errors of careful papers are independent of each other and of the AI’s error. The summary reports the majority answer of the literature, and a tie counts as an error. Majority rule is a benchmark in which the summary is faithful to the literature it reads. A summary that leans toward the AI’s own answer is captured at a lower share than the threshold below, so the threshold is an upper bound for such summaries. 

Proposition 4 (Captured Consensus) . Let ¯s := 1 − 1/(2 q) ∈ (0 , 1/2) . In the limit of a large literature, the summary is correct with probability one if s < ¯s, and with probability 

p if s > ¯s. Hence, if s(cw) < ¯s at high costs of writing, there is a cost ¯cw such that, for 

cw < ¯cw, the error rate of the summary jumps from 0 to 1 − p. Its errors are then the same for all readers who use it. 

Below the threshold, careful papers outvote the AI’s errors, and the summary is more reliable than any single paper. In a finite literature, the probability that they do so converges to one exponentially in the number of papers. Suppose that each AI-written paper adopts the AI’s answer only with probability γ ∈ (0 , 1], and otherwise reports an independent answer that is correct with probability q. When the AI is wrong, the share of correct papers is then q(1 − γs ), so that the summary is correct with probability one if and only if γs < ¯s.

## 4.1 AI Peer Review 

Suppose that journals, whose referees verify an ever smaller band of papers under the reading rule of Proposition 1, eventually delegate review to the AI, and that every paper must pass AI review to enter the summary. An AI referee judges a paper against what it has learned: it accepts papers that agree with its own answer, and accepts papers that disagree with it only with probability r ∈ [0 , 1], the probability that the evidence in a dissenting paper persuades it. Since r does not depend on whether a dissenting paper is correct, the AI screens on agreement. Dissenting papers are the original research of the model: when the AI is wrong, they are the papers that are right. 

Proposition 5 (Filtered Originality) . Let ¯s(r) := 1 − 1/(q(1 + r)) . With AI review, the summary is correct with probability one if s < ¯s(r), and with probability p if s > ¯s(r).The threshold ¯s(r) is increasing in r, equals ¯s for r = 1 , and is negative for r < (1 − q)/q .In the latter case, the summary repeats the AI even if no paper is written with AI. 

# 5 Policy Implications: Preprint Servers 

The simplest remedy for the filter of AI review is an unrefereed preprint server from which the summary draws. No acceptance rule can do better, since ¯ s(r) is increasing in r, as Figure 3 illustrates. When the AI is right, incorrect dissent on the server does not change the majority and thus does not harm the summary. 10 6 Discussion 

The model identifies two consequences of cheaper research writing. First, as writing becomes cheaper, the number of papers rises while readers’ attention remains fixed. Ver-ification is rationed to an increasingly narrow band of intermediate-reputation authors: outsiders are ignored, and established authors are trusted without being checked. In the costless writing limit, the share of verified papers vanishes, and whether a paper is used depends on its author’s reputation alone. Second, AI summaries allow readers to bypass the attention bottleneck, but their re-liability depends on the independence of the underlying research. In a large literature, a majority summary recovers the truth when sufficiently many papers reach indepen-dent, better-than-random judgments. Once enough papers adopt the same AI’s answer, their common errors capture the majority, and the summary repeats the AI’s mistakes. AI review that favors agreement compounds this problem: when the AI is wrong, the dissenting papers it filters out are precisely those that could correct the consensus. Asummary that draws on unrefereed preprints removes this filter but cannot always restore a reliable consensus. The share of authors who adopt the AI’s views is exogenous in our model. If it were endogenous, an AI that aggregated research perfectly would remove the incentive to produce research, as in Grossman and Stiglitz (1980). Independent work would thus need a reward that adopting the AI’s views does not earn. Finally, the mechanism we describe can run in reverse: If AI lowers the cost of verifying papers faster than the cost of writing, the verified band widens, such that outsiders are read and established authors are checked again. Likewise, a summary that draws on unrefereed preprints and identifies AI-written papers remains reliable as long as enough original papers remain to outvote the AI’s errors.
