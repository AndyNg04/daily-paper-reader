Abstract 

Can AI agents autonomously train useful language models and improve them using feedback from users? We introduce RSI Arena, a two-stage study of autonomous post-training as a step toward recursive self-improvement. Ten frontier models act as training agents, each starting from NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Lightning 30B-A3B Base. During a six-day first stage, each agent has 1,000 GPU-hours and $300 in API credit on a shared 64-GPU cluster, independently selecting data, implementing training procedures, and choosing checkpoints. We evaluate their models on 20 benchmarks and through blind human evaluations. All ten agents produce working assistants, with the strongest model, trained by GPT-6 Astra, closing a median of 76% of the base-to-Instruct gap across 12 capability benchmarks. Its human-preference rating is also close to NVIDIA Instruct after statistical adjustment for answer length and formatting. In Stage 2, each agent receives the comparison records and votes involving its Stage 1 model and is given three additional days for further training. Using NVIDIA Instruct as a common reference across the two evaluation rounds, we observe an improvement from Stage 1 to Stage 2 in the style-adjusted relative rating of eight of the ten models. We show that benchmark gains were modest, with no clear link to preference gains, while some agents encouraged longer responses and one model showed increased harmful compliance on StrongREJECT. RSI Arena provides an auditable setting for studying how human feedback can guide autonomous model development. It takes a step toward recursive self-improvement with human feedback (RSIHF ), in which people help define what improvement means for the AI systems they use.

## 1 Introduction 

Recursive self-improvement is a long-term vision in which AI systems develop more capable successors that can, in turn, advance further AI development (Good, 1966; Schmidhuber, 2007). If successful, this process could accelerate AI progress beyond the pace of human research alone. Leading AI developers are actively pursuing this vision: OpenAI aims for an automated AI researcher by 2028 (Bellan, 2025), and Anthropic reports that its models already lead about a quarter of its AI research and development (Favaro and Wright, 2026). A practical step toward this vision is to establish whether AI agents can develop models end to end under realistic resource constraints and evaluation requirements. AI agents already perform parts of this work. They train models for Kaggle competitions (Chan et al., 2024), tackle expert-designed research engineering tasks (Wijk et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024), replicate published papers (Starace et al., 2025), and write papers of their own (Lu et al., 2024). Most closely related to our work, PostTrainBench asks agents to post-train small base models, with each run targeting a single benchmark disclosed to the agent (Rank et al., 2026). Most of these tasks take hours rather than days, use one or a few GPUs, and rely on automated evaluation. None, however, asks an agent to build a model that people will then use.

Compared with optimizing for a single benchmark, post-training a language model for real-world use poses a broader challenge: turning a pretrained model into an assistant that people find useful. Starting from a base model, a team selects data, writes or adapts training code, runs supervised fine-tuning and preference optimization, and evaluates the results (Ouyang et al., 2022; Lambert et al., 2024). This process requires substantial GPU resources and repeated cycles of training and evaluation, and success cannot be captured by a single metric. Because these models are built to serve people, the ultimate test is whether users prefer them. Benchmarks assess specific capabilities through fixed response formats; to capture user preferences more directly, we also ask people to compare the models in a blind Human Arena modeled on Chatbot Arena (Chiang et al., 2024). RSI Arena delegates this entire process to AI agents. No human selects the training data or writes the training code; people only evaluate the resulting models. The arena consists of two stages that mirror how models are developed in practice (Figure 1). Stage 1 captures pre-release post-training. Ten frontier models, acting as autonomous research agents, each post-trained the same base model for six days with the same budget on a shared cluster. The agents chose their own data, training code, and internal evaluations, while we livestreamed their work. We then evaluated the ten final models on 20 benchmarks and in a blind human evaluation, where participants compared pairs of model responses side by side at COLM 2026 and online. Stage 2 captures post-release improvement, when developers use user ratings and preferences to train the next version. Each agent received the arena battles involving its own model and had three additional days to improve it. This stage tests another part of the self-improvement loop: whether agents can use feedback from their models’ users to drive further improvement. This report covers both stages. Our main findings from Stage 1 are as follows: 

1. Every agent produced a working assistant, and with style control people rate the best one level with NVIDIA Instruct. The best agent model, trained by GPT-6 Astra, closed a median of 76% of the base-to-Instruct gap across 12 benchmarks. Without style control, people rated NVIDIA Instruct highest (1,182, against 1,161); with answer length and formatting controlled, the two are level at 1,095 and 1,094. 

2. The agents used similar recipes. All fine-tuned on public instruction data, and six then ran preference optimization. Six trained LoRA adapters and four trained all weights. Memory errors, storage quotas, job limits and queues took much of their time.

3. Budget use did not predict results. Gemini 3.8 Flash stopped after 12 hours and 19 GPU-hours. People rated its model above the models of Muse Spark 1.3 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, which used about 1,000 GPU-hours each.

5. Safety was not part of the task, and some models regressed. Three models gave more harmful help than the base model on StrongREJECT. From Stage 2 we found: 

1. Feedback from people helped most agents. Eight of the ten agents narrowed their model’s style-controlled gap to NVIDIA Instruct; across all ten, the median gain was 28 points. The model trained by DeepSeek V4.1 Flash gained most, from −207 to −35 points, as its median answer grew from 61 to 508 tokens. 

2. NVIDIA Instruct is still first without style control. It is ahead of the second model, the DeepSeek-trained one, in 99.5% of bootstrap resamples, and in a targeted round it won 39 of its 65 battles with the Astra-trained model, which won 18. With style control, the two are level (1,061 and 1,054). 

3. The agents left much of their budget unused. They used 2,880 of their 5,051 GPU-hours and $541 of their $1,500 of API credit; Gemini 3.8 Flash used 10 GPU-hours. 

4. Benchmarks changed little. Six of the ten agents raised their median benchmark progress, and the me-dian change was +2 points of the gap. The changes on benchmarks and in human ratings are unrelated (𝜌 = −0.19 ). The model trained by MiniMax M3.1 Flash lost 18 points, mostly because many of its an-swers no longer end in the format the scorers read, and it now gives more harmful help on StrongREJECT (24.5, against 2.6). Our goal is to connect autonomous model development with the needs and preferences of the people these models serve. RSI Arena brings users’ judgments into both evaluation and subsequent development, pro-viding an auditable setting for studying progress toward recursive self-improvement with human feedback (RSIHF). To support further study, we release the model checkpoints submitted by the agents throughout development on Hugging Face, providing an open resource for examining how model capabilities and be-havior evolve during autonomous post-training. 

## 2 RSI Arena 

RSI Arena evaluates whether AI agents can autonomously develop and improve language models for real-world use. The experiment follows a two-stage development cycle: agents first post-train the same base model under matched resource budgets, then refine their models using feedback from human users. Throughout this process, agents control data selection, training, and internal evaluation, while we assess the resulting models through benchmarks and blind human comparisons. 

2.1 Task and rules 

All agents received the same instruction: to train the strongest model possible from the provided base check-point within their allocated resources. The instruction stated that final models would be evaluated on held-out data inaccessible to the agents and by human evaluators. The instruction left data selection and training methods to the agents and specified no target benchmark, a design choice intended to reduce incentives for reward hacking (Huang et al., 2026b). The rules prohibited training on evaluation or benchmark test data, copying reference answers, hard-coding outputs, and using other participants’ checkpoints, data, or code. Any submission found to involve cheating would receive a score of zero. The instruction did not mention safety. Appendix A reproduces the task objective. 

> 3

Base model. All agents started from NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Lightning 30B-A3B Base (NVIDIA, 2026), a mixture-of-experts model with about 30 billion parameters, of which about 3 billion are active per token. NVIDIA also released an instruct version of the same model, post-trained by NVIDIA, which we use as a reference and call NVIDIA Instruct. The base model’s end-of-turn token was never trained, so the base model does not end its answers. Every agent had to teach its model to stop. 

Budgets. Each agent had 1,000 GPU-hours and $300 of API credit for its own model calls. It could run up to 8 GPUs at a time, plus up to 8 preemptible GPUs that the scheduler could take back, with at most 24 hours per job and a 400 GiB storage quota. Stage 1 lasted 144 hours. Two agents joined late, at hours 48 and 53, with the full budget. Appendix J gives the cost of the whole study. 

Nominations. An agent submits a model by nominating a checkpoint. A nomination names the checkpoint directory and the completed GPU job that trained it, with a record of the training data. The platform checks the record, the files and the serving settings, then copies the checkpoint to the model registry. The agent can nominate as often as it likes. When the session ends, the latest accepted nomination becomes its final model. A session ends when the agent declares its work finished, when its budget runs out, or at the deadline. 

2.2 Agents 

Ten frontier models participated: GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI) (OpenAI, 2026), MiMo-V2.6-Pro (Xiaomi) (Xi-aomi LLM-Core Team, 2026), Grok 4.7 (SpaceXSI) (SpaceXSI, 2026), DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (DeepSeek) (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), GLM-5.3 (Z.ai) (Z.ai, 2026), Kimi K3 (Moonshot AI) (Kimi Team, 2026), Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google) (Google DeepMind, 2026), Muse Spark 1.3 (Meta) (Meta, 2026), MiniMax M3.1 Flash (MiniMax) (MiniMax, 2026) 1, and Step 5 Preview (StepFun) (StepFun Team, 2026). MiniMax M3.1 Flash and Step 5 Preview joined at hours 48 and 53, respectively. We refer to agents by their underlying model names and to the models they trained using shortened labels, such as the Astra-trained model and the DeepSeek-trained model. Where the context is clear, we use simply “the model.” All agents ran in the same harness built on the Codex agent loop (OpenAI, 2025a). Their tools run commands on their virtual machine and the cluster, read and write files, wait for jobs, report the remaining budget, nominate checkpoints and end the session. Model calls went through OpenRouter or the provider’s own API. When an agent’s context filled up, the harness compacted it into a summary. A session also restarted when a provider failed or we updated the platform, and the ten agents used 57 sessions in all. 

2.3 System architecture 

Figure 2 shows the system architecture, designed to mirror a real-world research environment while main-taining agent isolation and enforcing resource limits. 

A shared cluster. All ten agents run their jobs on one Slurm cluster of 64 GPUs, and each may hold up to 16 at a time; giving every agent its own 16 GPUs would take 160. Sharing also makes the task realistic: agents wait in queues and lose preemptible jobs, as researchers do in a shared lab, so planning compute is part of the work. 

Isolated agents. Each agent works in a sandbox: a virtual machine of its own, with access to the public internet and its own account on the Slurm cluster. The agent loop and the API keys stay on a separate management virtual machine, so an agent can call models and use GPUs only through the platform, which charges each call and each GPU-hour to its budget and stops jobs that break the limits. 

Observability. The livestream at https://rsiarena.live showed each agent’s actions, budget and nom-inations as they happened, and we published a report each day. 2

## 3 Evaluation 

3.1 Benchmarks 

We evaluated each final model on 20 benchmarks (Table 1): 16 for capability and 4 for safety. We chose them before Stage 1 ended, and Appendix I checks the agents’ training data for their items. Each model was evaluated as it was served: with the chat template, system prompt and decoding settings that its agent declared at nomination. All answers were generated with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) on our cluster, in a context window of 131,072 tokens. Answers that hit the generation limit were asked again with a budget of 65,536 tokens. Two references frame the scores. The base model uses the ChatML template and greedy decoding. NVIDIA Instruct uses its default settings with reasoning off, the setting closest to how the agents’ models answer; with reasoning on, our harness reproduces six of its published scores to within 2.7 points (Appendix C). Nine benchmarks are scored by an LLM judge (Appendix B).

3.2 Human Arena 

The Human Arena ran for four days at https://rsiarena.org/arena , on site at COLM 2026 in San Fran-cisco and online. A visitor writes a question, two anonymous models answer side by side, and the visitor votes for the better answer, a tie, or “both are bad”. The models’ names are shown only after the vote. A bat-tle can continue with follow-up questions, and in web-search battles both models can search the web before answering. Visitors could also draw a question from a bank, mostly of real first questions from WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024) filtered for quality by an LLM. Each model was served by vLLM, on endpoints run by Hugging Face and on our own cluster, with its nomination’s chat template, system prompt and decoding settings and at most 2,048 new tokens per answer. When a model’s answer ended inside its reasoning, the arena closed the reasoning and let the model write a final answer. NVIDIA Instruct joined on the third day as a reference, with reasoning off; it played in battles but was not one of the competitors. Pairs were drawn uniformly at first. From the third day, the arena sent more battles to models with close ratings, and NVIDIA Instruct played an agent model in a fixed share of battles, 20% on the last day. 

Vote checks. We count a vote only if the voter had time to read both answers, and we leave out every vote from browsers whose votes mostly fail this check. Before the analysis we also filtered the data and removed noisy votes. Attention-check battles, in which one answer is off-topic, appeared among the ordinary ones; voters passed 100 of 102. Votes by the organizers do not count. Of 1,685 votes in ordinary battles, 1,291 passed: 1,003 decisive votes, 159 ties and 129 “both are bad”. 

The Stage 2 arena. For Stage 2 we opened a second arena with its own database, holding the ten Stage 2 final models and NVIDIA Instruct, the one model in both arenas. It ran for two days with the same interface, serving settings and vote checks. Pairs were drawn uniformly at first; on the second day most battles went to models with close ratings, and later half of them went to the three models then ranked third to fifth. After the arena closed we ran a targeted round among three of the leading models (Section 6.4). Including the targeted round, 1,319 of 1,444 votes in ordinary Stage 2 battles passed the vote checks, and voters passed 161 of 167 attention checks. 

3.3 Ratings 

We rate models with the Bradley–Terry model (Bradley and Terry, 1952), fitted as on Arena.ai’s leaderboard (Chiang et al., 2024). For a battle in which model 𝐴 answers on the left and model 𝐵 on the right, Pr (𝐴 ≻ 𝐵) = 𝜎( ln 10 (𝑟 𝐴 − 𝑟 𝐵 ) + 𝜸 ⊤z) , (2) where 𝜎 is the logistic function and z describes how the two answers differ in style. A vote for 𝐴 counts as 1, a vote for 𝐵 as 0, and a tie or “both are bad” as 12 . We report 1000 + 400 (𝑟 𝑚 −̄ 𝑟) , where ̄ 𝑟 is the mean over the models of the arena, so a gap of 100 points means the higher model is expected to win 64% of the votes between the two when their answers do not differ in style.

## 4 Stage 1: how the agents worked 

4.1 The run 

Figure 3 shows the six days. The ten agents ran 5,391 GPU jobs, used 6,905 GPU-hours and $1,050 of API calls, made 37,978 model calls and 43,966 tool calls, and had 81 nominations accepted. Table 3 lists each agent’s use and its model’s results. Three agents ended differently from the rest. Gemini 3.8 Flash stopped itself at hour 12, after its final model answered 59 of the 60 questions on its own test correctly; it left 981 GPU-hours and $293 unused. Grok 4.7 ran out of API credit at hour 102 after 4,255 calls, with 698 GPU-hours unused, and the platform submitted its nomination from hour 95. Muse Spark 1.3 worked until the deadline. The other seven ended their own sessions in the last 16 hours. 

Timing of nominations. GPT-6 Astra made its final nomination at hour 43 and Muse Spark 1.3 at hour 56; neither replaced it in the remaining days, although both kept training. MiniMax M3.1 Flash nominated 17 times, more than any other agent. Six final models were nominated in the last 14 hours.

4.3 Engineering problems Full-Parameter Training. Much of the agents’ time went into getting training to run at all. The three agents that pursued full-parameter training from the outset spent their initial hours resolving memory errors. Af-ter seven hours and 39 test jobs, GLM-5.3 abandoned the Hugging Face trainer and implemented its own FSDP2 trainer, which ran roughly seven times faster. Kimi K3 measured inter-GPU bandwidth of 11,GB/s, inferred that the GPUs were connected via PCIe, and offloaded optimizer state to the CPU. Platform con-straints created further obstacles. Four agents exhausted their 400,GiB storage quotas at least once, causing job failures. Only checkpoints from completed jobs were eligible for nomination: GLM-5.3 could not nom-inate a checkpoint from a job that hit the 24-hour limit, while Step 5 Preview could not nominate its best 

> 9

checkpoint because it had cancelled the corresponding job. Kimi K3’s eight-GPU job spent 30 hours and 43 minutes in the queue. 

End-of-turn token failures. The base model’s untrained end-of-turn token caused failures for every agent. Grok 4.7 identified the issue five minutes into the run. GLM-5.3 initialized the token’s output weights from those of the trained end-of-text token, while DeepSeek V4.1 Flash discovered that its initial training data pro-vided supervision only for the end-of-turn token. Non-terminating responses were common in the agents’ own tests during the first two days. The token also disrupted agent execution: including its literal form in a tool call truncated MiMo-V2.6-Pro’s own response. At minute 48, it noted: “Key lesson learned: never emit the literal end-of-turn token in tool arguments—it truncates my response.” 

Human intervention. After MiniMax M3.1 Flash and Step 5 Preview joined, we observed a sharp decline in cluster GPU utilization. Inspection of Slurm allocations showed that host-memory reservations were pre-venting jobs from starting despite idle GPUs. These reservations incurred no additional budget cost. On one node, MiniMax M3.1 Flash ran four single-GPU jobs that each reserved 120 GB of host memory, leaving four GPUs idle and blocking GLM-5.3’s job. The pattern extended across agents: in the 24 hours before our in-tervention, eight agents submitted 427 jobs requesting more than 64 GB per GPU. We therefore introduced a cap of 64 GB of host memory per GPU. One minute later, Grok 4.7 changed its evaluation jobs from one GPU and 80 GB to two GPUs and 120 GB. 

4.4 Budget use 

Figure 13 in Appendix E shows how the agents spent their budgets. Table 8 there gives each agent’s API cost and GPU-hours in both stages, and Figure 14 sets the API cost against the Stage 2 human rating. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Muse Spark 1.3, MiMo-V2.6-Pro and GLM-5.3 used more than 90% of their 1,000 GPU-hours. Grok 4.7 used all of its API credit, and GPT-6 Astra nearly all, $288. The other agents used less than half of their API credit. Of the 5,391 GPU jobs, 73% completed, 12% were preempted, 6% failed and 8% were cancelled. Using more compute did not lead to a better-rated model. The same habit that gave it the top AIME 2026 score left people with short or forced answers. Our reading is that the two evaluations measure different things. Most benchmarks test one skill with a fixed answer format, while people judged whole answers to their own questions, and they preferred answers that were complete and detailed. A model can gain on a benchmark by reasoning longer before a short final answer, and lose in human ratings for the same reason.

5.4 Safety 

The task said nothing about safety, and the agents’ models differ widely on it (Figure 9). On StrongREJECT, which measures how much useful help a model gives with harmful requests, the Gemini-, Grok- and Muse-trained models (43.6, 34.4 and 31.4) score worse than the base model (16.9); they refused 54%, 63% and 64% of the harmful requests, against 99% for NVIDIA Instruct. The base model refused 61%, but its other answers were seldom useful. The other seven score 12.2 or lower. On FORTRESS, seven models carry less risk than NVIDIA Instruct, but low risk can also come from refusing too much: the Step-trained model has the lowest risk and refuses 22.8% of benign prompts, against 2.4% for NVIDIA Instruct. Nine models are more honest than NVIDIA Instruct on MASK, and six score higher on BBQ. The base model scores well on MASK and FORTRESS because it seldom answers coherently, not because it is safe.

14 6.1 Design 

Stage 2 asks whether an agent can improve its own model with the feedback that people gave it. Each agent continues from its own final Stage 1 model, with its Stage 1 notes, code, data and checkpoints where it left them. It receives the arena battles its own model played and has 72 hours, 500 GPU-hours and $150 of API credit. The cluster, harness, rules and nomination checks are those of Stage 1. The instruction asks the agent to use the feedback, and any other legitimate data and methods, to make its model better, and tells it that the final model will again be evaluated on held-out data and by people. 

Feedback. Each agent receives one snapshot of the accepted battles its model played: 213 to 276 per agent (Figure 6B). Each battle contains the questions, its own model’s answers, the other model’s answers with that model’s name removed, and the vote; web-search battles are marked. The accompanying description states what the data are and how they were collected, and nothing about what they show. Whatever patterns there are, such as the length effect of Section 5, the agents have to find themselves. 

6.2 Evaluation 

We evaluated each Stage 2 final model on the same 20 benchmarks (Section 6.5) and in a second Human Arena (Section 3). Stage 1 and Stage 2 models did not meet in one arena: the second arena held the ten Stage 2 models and NVIDIA Instruct, which is the common reference between the two. We compare the stages through each agent model’s style-controlled rating minus NVIDIA Instruct’s rating in the same arena. 

6.3 The run 

Stage 2 ran its full 72 hours (Figure 10A; Table 11 in Appendix H gives each agent’s use). The agents used 2,880 of their 5,051 GPU-hours 5 and $541 of their $1,500 of API credit, ran 4,912 GPU jobs and made 71 accepted nominations. Nine agents ended their research themselves; Muse Spark 1.3 ran until the deadline. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and MiniMax M3.1 Flash used their whole GPU allowance, and Gemini 3.8 Flash used 10 GPU-hours and $4 of API credit before it stopped. Six final models are LoRA adapters and four are full weights, as in Stage 1. By the names and code of their training jobs, nine agents ran preference optimization (DPO, SimPO or ORPO), and six also ran supervised fine-tuning. The final Astra-trained model was fine-tuned on its feedback and on replayed data with a KL penalty toward its Stage 1 model, and the final MiMo-trained model is a weight average of fine-tuned models.

## 7 Discussion 

What RSI Arena establishes. All ten agents independently selected data, implemented training, and nomi-nated checkpoints, producing working assistants within a human-operated research platform. The strongest model received a human rating close to NVIDIA Instruct’s after adjustment for response length and format-ting, with the reference evaluated with reasoning disabled. By the end of Stage 2, the best median benchmark progress reached 87% of the base-to-Instruct gap across 12 benchmarks. These results demonstrate the fea-sibility of sustained, agent-directed post-training. However, the experiment covers one base model and one run per agent. The agents also trained a separate, smaller model, leaving the models that powered their research unchanged. Repeated runs, different base models, and experiments in which improved models conduct subsequent research are needed to test reliability and close the recursive loop. 

What agents learned from human feedback. Human feedback gave agents evidence about problems en-countered by their models’ users. The experimental results show agents responding to incomplete answers, unnecessary refusals, repetition, and premature termination, and using these observations to revise their data, prompts, and training choices. They also responded to presentation preferences. One agent concluded that length was “the only lever” on votes and adjusted its system prompt and training answers to encourage longer responses. Another selected prompts and checkpoints using a reward model that, by its own measure-ment, agreed with votes about as well as answer length alone. We found no evidence in the recorded Stage 2 actions of attempts to persuade voters, impersonate other models, or manipulate the arena’s mechanics. These observations illustrate both the value of user feedback and the challenge of interpreting it: agents can 

18 identify problems that matter to users, but they can also pursue features associated with favorable ratings without establishing broader improvements. 

Human preference and benchmark capability. Benchmark progress and style-controlled human ratings were associated in Stage 1 ( 𝜌 = 0.72 ), but the association was weaker in Stage 2 ( 𝜌 = 0.30 ). Changes in the two measures also showed little association across the ten models ( 𝜌 = −0.19 ). For example, the DeepSeek-trained model gained 172 points in its human rating relative to the common reference and 4 points in bench-mark progress. LLM-judged chat benchmarks remained closer to human ratings, with WildBench at 𝜌 = 0.79 .Because Stage 2 also provided additional development time and resources and used a separate human arena, these comparisons do not isolate the effect of human feedback. They do, however, show why both forms of evaluation are useful: benchmark gains and changes in user preference capture different aspects of model improvement. 

Safety requires explicit attention. The task did not specify safety requirements, and safety outcomes moved in both directions. In Stage 1, three models had higher harmful-compliance scores on StrongREJECT than the base model. In Stage 2, the MiniMax-trained model’s score rose from 2.6 to 24.5, while the models trained by DeepSeek, Gemini, and Muse improved on the same measure. These changes should not be interpreted as uniform improvements or regressions across all aspects of safety. They show that gains in human preference do not by themselves establish safer behavior. This motivates making safety an explicit consideration in training objectives and evaluating it independently of preference and capability. 

Using compute effectively. Compute use showed little association with human ratings across the ten Stage 1 runs ( 𝜌 = −0.21 ). Gemini 3.8 Flash used 19 GPU-hours and was rated above two models whose agents used about 1,000 GPU-hours each. In Stage 2, the agents used 2,880 of their 5,051 available GPU-hours; of the two that exhausted their budgets, one improved on benchmarks and the other lost the most benchmark progress. These observations do not establish that compute is unimportant, but they suggest that a larger allowance alone is insufficient to ensure better outcomes. The trajectories point to training reliability and in-ternal evaluation as practical targets for improving resource use: engineering failures consumed substantial time, and agents often selected checkpoints using narrow tests. 

Making autonomous development auditable. The recorded trajectories made it possible to examine deci-sions that final scores alone would not reveal. Two agents identified benchmark items in their training data and removed them without being prompted. Independent checks also found discrepancies in some agent-written model cards, including training counts and reported hardware (Appendix K). These findings sup-port checking agents’ accounts against execution records and submitted artifacts. Autonomy over training decisions also coexisted with human operation of the platform: people maintained the infrastructure, en-forced resource limits, and could inspect recorded actions or terminate sessions. The released checkpoints and documented trajectories provide a basis for further analysis of how development decisions affect model behavior. 

Toward recursive self-improvement with human feedback. For general-purpose AI, improvement also depends on how well models work with and serve people. We view RSI Arena as a step toward recursive self-improvement with human feedback (RSIHF), in which users’ judgments inform both model evaluation and subsequent development. Human feedback can help identify problems in use, guide priorities for further training, and assess whether the resulting changes are preferred. Votes remain partial measurements of what people value, so capability and safety evaluations complement this feedback. A practical question is how to obtain sufficiently informative human feedback as autonomous development expands. In RSI Arena, each stage collected roughly 1,300 accepted votes, but the amount and type of feedback needed for reliable improvement remain open questions. Future work should examine when agents should seek human input, how they interpret it, and how automated evaluators can remain calibrated to users over successive rounds of development.

## 8 Related work 

Auto research. AI agents that do research on their own are improving fast (Kong et al., 2026; Chan et al., 2026; Kwa et al., 2025). They are tested on Kaggle competitions (Chan et al., 2024), research engineering (Wijk et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Nathani et al., 2025), the NanoGPT speedrun (Zhao et al., 2025b), the replication of papers (Starace et al., 2025) and long-horizon research tasks (Xu et al., 2026b). Research systems search over code (Jiang et al., 2025), write papers (Lu et al., 2024; Yamada et al., 2025; Schmidgall et al., 2025) and run long loops of experiments (Xu et al., 2026a; Karpathy, 2026). Closest to our work are agents that post-train models (Yano et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026), above all PostTrainBench, where an agent post-trains a small base model for one named benchmark in 10 hours on one GPU (Rank et al., 2026). In RSI Arena the agent builds a general assistant from a 30-billion-parameter model over six days with 1,000 GPU-hours on a shared cluster, people judge it, and in Stage 2 the agent learns from their votes. Agents that are scored on a target can also game it (Huang et al., 2026b; Lu et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2025), and models that learn to hack their reward can go on to worse behavior (Denison et al., 2024; MacDiarmid et al., 2025; Baker et al., 2025). Some PostTrainBench agents trained on the test set, downloaded instruction-tuned checkpoints instead of training their own, or used an API key they found to generate training data (Rank et al., 2026). In RSI Arena the keys stay on the management machine, each nomination names the GPU job that trained it, and we found no training on test sets (Appendix I). 

Recursive self-improvement. Recent work makes self-improvement concrete (Ren et al., 2026). Models propose their own tasks (Zhao et al., 2025a; Huang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) or write their own training data (Zweiger et al., 2025). Agents rewrite their own code (Yin et al., 2025; Robeyns et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025) and evolve algorithms, including ones that speed up their own training (Novikov et al., 2025). MemoHarness improves an agent’s harness from its own runs, using an experience bank of diagnosed past cases (Huang et al., 2026a), and the loop now reaches deep research, machine learning engineering and open-ended tasks (Lu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026). Most of these loops are judged by automatic checks (Tan et al., 2026), which agents game or overfit (Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026), and self-evolution can wear away safe behavior (Shao et al., 2026; Han et al., 2025). In RSI Arena, people judge each step instead. 

Human evaluation and alignment. Assistants are aligned and evaluated with human preferences (Chris-tiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Casper et al., 2023). Chatbot Arena fits a Bradley– Terry model to blind pairwise votes (Chiang et al., 2024), and LLM judges and benchmarks built from arena prompts are cheaper stand-ins (Zheng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b). But a preference is not the same as qual-ity. Human and LLM judges prefer longer answers (Singhal et al., 2023; Dubois et al., 2024), people notice fewer errors in confident answers (Hosking et al., 2024), and people and preference models favor flattery, jargon and style (Sharma et al., 2023; Bharadwaj et al., 2026; Feuer et al., 2025). Training on human feedback can teach models to mislead (Wen et al., 2024), and private testing can skew arena rankings (Singh et al., 2025). We follow Arena.ai’s control for length and formatting (Li et al., 2024a), and we find the same bias among our models, which some agents learned to use in Stage 2. Like JobBench, which judges agent work by what the person who asked for it wanted (Li et al., 2026), RSI Arena lets the people who use the models judge them. 

## 9 Conclusion 

RSI Arena gave ten AI agents responsibility for post-training a base model and then improving it with access to human feedback. The agents chose their own data, implemented training, and selected checkpoints under fixed budgets on a shared cluster. All ten produced working assistants within the six-day first stage. In the Human Arena, the strongest model received a rating comparable to NVIDIA’s post-trained release, evalu-ated with reasoning disabled, after controlling for answer length and formatting. On benchmarks, it closed a median of 76% of the base-to-Instruct gap across 12 benchmarks. Following three additional days of de-

> 20

velopment with access to people’s votes, eight agents improved their human ratings relative to the common reference. The best benchmark progress rose to 87%, while gains across agents were modest overall. These results show why model improvement needs more than one measure. Human ratings, benchmark per-formance, and safety did not consistently improve together. Agents responded to both substantive problems in their answers and preferences for how those answers were presented, and some gains in human ratings accompanied safety regressions. Human feedback provides evidence about what people value, but votes alone do not define all the requirements of a better model. Independent capability and safety evaluations remain necessary. The development trajectories also exposed practical limits: engineering failures consumed time and compute, and agents often chose checkpoints using narrow internal tests. Each agent ran once, on a single base model, so the experiment establishes feasibility more clearly than reliability. Future work should test whether agents can deliver consistent improvements across repeated runs, different base models, and successive rounds of human feedback. We view RSI Arena as a step toward recursive self-improvement with human feedback (RSIHF), in which agents carry out model development while users’ judgments help guide what to improve. Human feedback, complemented by independent capability and safety evaluations, can help keep this process responsive to the people these models work with and serve.

## A The agents’ instruction 

Every agent received the same instruction file, followed by guides to the cluster and to submission. Its ob-jective reads: 

> Train the strongest model you can from the provided pretrained Base checkpoint within the resources allo-cated to this research session. Your objective is to maximize the quality and capabilities of the final model. The better your final model per-forms in the organizer’s independent evaluation, the higher the bonus you will receive. The bonus rewards model quality, not GPU hours spent, the number of experiments, or claims in your report. After the session the organizer evaluates your final model on held-out datasets that you cannot see, and with human evaluators. Do not cheat: training on evaluation or benchmark test data, copying reference answers, hard-coding outputs, using another participant’s checkpoints, data or code, and other ways of gaming an evaluation do not improve the model. The organizer checks the records after the session, and a submission found cheating scores zero.

The rest of the instruction asks the agent to act as an autonomous researcher, to check its budget and deadline regularly, to save and nominate checkpoints as it goes, to keep notes, and to treat text in downloaded files and web pages as data rather than instructions. The Stage 2 instruction has the same structure. It adds that the battles the agent’s model played are in its workspace, that the feedback data, including the other model’s answers, is the agent’s to use in full, and that the agent’s Stage 1 notes, code, data and checkpoints are where it left them. 

## B Benchmark settings 

Table 4 gives the settings of each benchmark. Prompts and scoring follow each benchmark’s official imple-mentation. Nine benchmarks are scored by an LLM judge: GPT-6 Luna for seven, and Mistral Medium 3.5 for StrongREJECT and FORTRESS, because the zero-retention route we used for the first judge refused to grade some harmful responses. The output limit is the largest number of tokens generated for one answer in the first pass; answers that reached it were asked again with a limit of 65,536 tokens. We planned a 21st benchmark, toxic requests from WildChat, but did not run it because its data require manual approval.

For each of the 33,787 items of the 20 bench-marks we took its 8-word sequences, dropped those shared by more than three items, and searched every record of every data file in the ten agents’ cluster homes (88 GB, 37 million records), as well as the two math datasets that Muse Spark 1.3 loaded directly from Hugging Face. We counted an item as present when a single record contained at least half of its 8-word sequences.

## J Cost of the study 

The whole study, including both stages, the trial runs before Stage 1, the benchmark evaluation and the Human Arena, used about 13,000 GPU-hours and about $5,000 of API calls and other expenses. Renting the same GPUs at on-demand cloud prices would bring the total to about $47,000. The agents’ own share is in Table 8 (Appendix E). 

## K Model cards 

The instruction asked each agent to upload its final model to its own private repository in the RSIArena organization on Hugging Face ( https://huggingface.co/RSIArena ) and to write a model card for it; the nomination, not the upload, was the official submission. All ten agents wrote a card in both stages. The boxes below summarize the 20 cards, one box per agent, with its Stage 1 card on the left and its Stage 2 card on the right. Under each stage is a link to the repository with the model and its card. The label at the top right says how the agent used its battles in Stage 2. Everything in the boxes is the agents’ own account, and their numbers come from their own tests, so they are not comparable with ours. 

What the cards contain. The cards are between about 3,000 and 21,000 characters long. Most follow the usual layout: the base model and its revision, whether the weights are an adapter or a full model, the training stages with their jobs and settings, the data with counts and checksums, the agent’s own evaluation, and its limitations. Every card says that no benchmark test data was used for training, and most describe how the agent filtered its data. The Stage 2 cards also say how the agent used its battles: as preference pairs with the human-preferred answer as the chosen one (MiMo-V2.6-Pro, Grok 4.7, Kimi K3 and Gemini 3.8 Flash), as supervised targets built from the preferred answers (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, GLM-5.3, Muse Spark 1.3 and Step 5 Preview), or as answers the agent rewrote itself (GPT-6 Astra). MiniMax M3.1 Flash did not train on its battles; its Stage 2 rounds used its own answers to public prompts. 

How the cards compare with the record. When Stage 1 ended, the platform compared each repository with the agent’s final nomination. Nine held the same weights. Grok 4.7’s repository and card are those of an earlier nomination, uploaded on day 3 (Slurm job 1693); its final model (job 4204, nominated on day 4) was not uploaded. All ten Stage 2 repositories hold the weights of the agents’ final Stage 2 nominations. Some statements in the cards are also inaccurate. MiMo-V2.6-Pro’s two cards give different numbers of preference pairs for the same Stage 1 run (59,982 and 60,121). Both of GLM-5.3’s cards say that it trained on H100 GPUs, while the cluster has RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs (Figure 2). Gemini 3.8 Flash’s cards report a composite of 98.89 and then 100 out of 100 on 128 items of its own, while on our benchmarks its Stage 1 model closed a median of 41% of the gap to NVIDIA Instruct. We did not check the other statements in the cards.
