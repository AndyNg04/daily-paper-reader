JONES, University of California, Irvine, USA As AI coding agents take on increasingly autonomous development work, developers’ roles are expanding from writing code to directing, coordinating, and checking agents. Yet little is known about how these activities unfold in everyday professional software development. We conducted a mixed-methods workplace shadowing study of 11 professional developers using AI coding agents on their own work. Across approximately 15 hours of observation, we manually coded developers’ activities and task transitions, analyzed 255 submitted prompts and their surrounding activities, and conducted 4.6 hours of follow-up interviews with all 11 participants. We find that working with agents involved substantial management work. Developers spent an average of 33.4% of their observed time formulating prompts or checking AI responses, while task-switching rates ranged from 0 to 101.6 switches per hour. Their prompts served not only to produce software artifacts, but also to understand agent behavior, clarify requirements, configure agents, and coordinate work. Connecting developers’ prompting, checking, and task-management practices revealed three recurring patterns: Supervisor ,in which developers delegated work while directly inspecting resulting artifacts; Delegator , in which developers coordinated agents through dialogue and delegated both implementation and checking; and Learner , in which developers used agents primarily to build understanding. Developers relied on agents for technical information accessible from code and documentation, while turning to colleagues for organizational context, authority, and collective alignment. Together, our findings characterize agentic development in the workplaces we studied as a redistribution of developer control: from producing artifacts toward directing delegated work, deciding what to verify, and allocating attention across tasks.

1 Introduction 

Software engineering practice is being rapidly transformed by advances in large language models (LLMs). AI-enabled development tools have progressed from code completion [6, 49] to conver-sational coding assistants [28, 38] and, more recently, coding agents capable of carrying out development tasks with increasing autonomy [14, 39]. As developers delegate more work to these agents, their activities extend beyond writing code to directing agents, coordinating concurrent work, and deciding how to check generated outputs. Yet these activities occur within the broader demands of professional software development, where developers must also manage ongoing tasks,

information needs, tools, and collaboration. Understanding how coding agents fit into this everyday work remains an open question. Prior research has examined AI-assisted development through repository analyses [22, 37], surveys [29], observations of live-streamed programming [14], and controlled experiments [7]. These studies showed a substantial code outputs from developers with AI agents [22], and higher perceived productivity and reduced workload [9]. However, researchers have also reported substan-tial time spent waiting for generated outputs [14], less code quality [22], effort required to assess their correctness [20], and, in some cases, longer task-completion times [7] and review time [15]. However, these perspectives provide a limited account of how professional developers incorporate coding agents into their everyday work. Repository-level outcomes do not reveal the activities through which developers direct and check agents, while controlled tasks and publicly streamed sessions capture different conditions from workplace development [4, 14]. Professional software development already requires developers to divide their attention across multiple tasks, artifacts, and collaborators [1, 30, 31], while continually seeking answers to questions around artifacts, such as code and program behaviors [18, 27, 45]. Developers must also coordinate dependencies and maintain awareness of other people and ongoing work [8, 18], sometimes across globe and teams [3, 11]. Coding agents add another source and recipient of information in this environment. Developers may now wait for agents while advancing other tasks, return later to inspect their progress or outputs, provide additional context, or seek information needed to decide what should happen next. Understanding these interactions therefore requires examining how developers allocate attention and how information flows between developers, agents, artifacts, and collaborators during everyday work. To address this gap, we conducted a mixed-methods observational field study of 11 professional developers working with AI coding agents on their own development tasks in workplace settings during approximately 90-minute sessions. We manually coded observable activities and task transitions, then linked prompts to preceding activities and subsequent checks. Follow-up interviews used visualizations of participants’ sessions to examine their interpretations of the observed patterns, checking strategies, and preferences for seeking information from agents versus colleagues, while allowing us to co-construct interpretations of the observed practices with participants [21]. We investigate two sets of research questions: 

• RQ1: Developers’ Attention – RQ1-1: What activities do developers engage in, and how is their time distributed? 

– RQ1-2: How frequently do developers switch tasks? 

• RQ2: Information Flow Between Developers and Agents – RQ2-1: What information do developers observably seek from and provide to AI agents? 

– RQ2-2: How do developers observably check agents’ outputs? 

– RQ2-3: What management patterns emerge from prompting and checking behaviors? 

– RQ2-4: What information do developers prefer to obtain from agents versus human peers? RQs, pertaining to Developers’ Attention , examines how developers allocate attention across activities and tasks while working with agents, building on prior work on AI-related waiting [7, 14] and context switching in software work [1, 31, 33, 35]. The second set of RQs, pertaining to 

Information Flow , examines how developers interact with agents, and how these practices form recurring management patterns. Together, the RQs shape how developers manage their AI agents in everyday development [27]. Our findings foreground the management work involved in using AI coding agents. Prompt formulation and AI-response checking occupied an average of 33.4% of observed session time. Par-ticipants worked on 1–17 distinct tasks, with task-switching rates ranging from 0 to 101.6 switches per hour. Developers not only requested development outputs but also clarified requirements,

Connecting preceding activities, prompt purposes, and subsequent checks revealed three recurring patterns : (1) Supervisor , combining work requests with direct artifact inspection; (2) Delegator , emphasizing clarification, agent configuration, and coordination through dialogue, often delegating both implementation and checking; and (3) Learner , seeking explanations to build understanding of unfamiliar code, tools, and data. These patterns overlapped within ses-sions, describing ways of working rather than fixed developer types. Our study makes two contributions: (1) an empirical account of how professional developers allocate attention, manage coding agents, and check their outputs in everyday work; and (2) three empirically derived management patterns— Supervisor , Delegator , and Learner —that capture how developers combine artifact inspection, dialogue-based coordination, and explanation seeking. 

2 Background 

This section first reviews prior research on developers’ use of AI-assisted development tools (§ 2.1), highlighting gaps in our understanding of developers’ productivity and work practices when using AI agents. We then review broader observational research on developers’ everyday work practices (§ 2.2) before the release of AI, which informs the design of our study. 

2.1 Understanding Developers Working with AI 

The rapid adoption and advancement of large language models (LLMs) have transformed how developers perform software engineering tasks. Prior research has examined the effects of AI-assisted development through repository mining, controlled experiments, and observations of publicly available programming sessions. Through mining open-source GitHub repositories, Hao et al. found that although AI-enabled development tools substantially increased the amount of code produced, their use could also reduce software quality as measured through static analysis [22]. In a randomized controlled trial, Becker et al. found that experienced open-source developers completed tasks 19% more slowly when permitted to use AI tools; their analysis of screen recordings further showed that developers spent part of their implementation time prompting AI systems, waiting for generation, and reviewing generated outputs [7]. Similarly, through observations of livestreamed programming sessions, Chou et al. found that all observed vibe coders spent more than 20% of their session time waiting for AI-generated outputs [14]. Huang et al. observed 13 experienced developers and found that they maintained control over coding agents through planning, supervision, and output validation rather than fully delegating their work [23]. Khojah et al. collected ChatGPT histories of 24 professional developers over five consecutive workdays and qualitatively analyzed their dialogues, including the project-specific context developers provided and the purposes for which they sought assistance [25]. However, these studies examined AI-assisted development in settings that differ from ongoing workplace activities. Becker et al. focus their study around predefined repository issues and mea-sured developers’ completion time at the individual-issue level [7], whereas Huang et al. observed each participant performing a single self-selected task during a 45-minute think-aloud session [23]. These designs provided limited insight into how developers transition among multiple concurrent workplace activities and coordinate their work across agents, tools and colleagues. Chou et al. 

examined livestreamers, whose behaviors may have been shaped by the need to entertain their audi-ence [4, 14]. Despite the growing body of research on AI-assisted development, close examinations of how developers work with AI coding agents in their actual workplaces remain limited. To address

2.2 Observing Developers at Work 

Because direct observations of developers using AI coding agents in professional workplaces remain rare, we primarily draw methodological inspiration from observational studies conducted before the emergence of agentic software development. These studies have examined developers’ work in situ through think-aloud protocols, direct observation, field notes, interaction traces, and surveys, providing established approaches for understanding software work in its organizational context. Ko et al. conducted a 90-minute observational study with a think-aloud protocol to characterize developers’ information needs in collocated software development [27]. They identified recurring unmet information needs, many concerning development artifacts such as source code and program state. This work motivates our examination of the information exchanged between developers and coding agents. A complementary line of research has examined how developers allocate attention across everyday work. Meyer et al. , through observational and survey studies, documented the fragmented nature of developers’ work and frequent transitions among activities [33–35]. Chong and Siino similarly studied developers in their workplace, but relied on extended direct observation and field notes: over seven months, they accumulated more than 40 hours of observations and analyzed 242 interruptions [12]. More broadly, Mark et al. ’s research on workplace attention has shown increasingly short periods of engagement with individual screens or applications [30–32]. These findings motivate our examination of how developers divide attention across tasks while working with coding agents, particularly when agent work can continue in parallel. Drawing on these traditions, our study combines direct observation, field notes, video recordings, and follow-up interviews. We avoid a concurrent think-aloud protocol to reduce interference with participants’ ongoing development work and the additional burden of participation. Because interpreting screen recordings can nevertheless require some inference about task boundaries and observed practices, we use follow-up interviews to present participants with visualizations of their sessions, check whether the observed patterns reflect their typical work, and refine our interpretations of those patterns. 

3 Method 

We conducted a two-phase mixed-methods observational field study of professional developers using AI agents on their own work at their workplace to strengthen ecological validity. The first phase combined quantitative summaries of manually coded activity traces with qualitative analysis of prompts and their surrounding activities. After completing the observational analysis, we conducted follow-up interviews where participants reflected on visualizations of their own sessions and anonymized patterns from other participants.

3.2 Phase 1: Observational Study & Preliminary Analysis 

3.2.1 Observational Study. We asked each participant to reserve a 90-minute period free of meetings and to work on their own development activities rather than a researcher-assigned task. We intentionally left “development work” open to participants’ interpretation. The resulting sessions included feature development, maintenance, code review, and exploration of unfamiliar codebases, as summarized in Table 1. 

Briefing (5–10 min): Each session began with a briefing during which the researcher briefed the study procedure, captured consent and recording setup, and answered participants’ questions. 

Observation (70–90 min): A researcher remained present throughout each session, either in person or remotely (P7, P8). All sessions were recorded through videoconferencing software: Microsoft Teams for in-person sessions and Google Meet for remote ones. We did not employ a think-aloud protocol because continually verbalizing thoughts could disrupt participants’ workflows. During the sessions, the researcher recorded field notes about activities that could not be inter-preted from the screen alone, such as stepping away, using a phone, or speaking with a colleague. Because recording software recorded only one screen at a time, we asked in-person participants to conduct most of their work on a single screen. They could use another screen for private activities, such as messaging; the researcher noted the activity and its timestamp without recording its content. Remote participants were asked to work on a single screen and notify the researcher when they stepped away or were otherwise inactive. These field notes contextualized the screen recordings during analysis by documenting off-screen activities.

3.2.2 Phase 1 Data Analysis. 

RQ1: Activities, time allocation, and context switching. To understand how developers devoted their time, we manually divided each recording into activity segments. We adapted the activity coding scheme from Chou et al. [14], a recent study of vibe coding practices that similarly analyzed video-recorded development sessions. Because screen recordings do not reveal participants’ attention or cognitive states, we coded only observable actions and artifacts, using labels such as Check AI Response rather than Read AI Response . We adapted the initial codebook from the same study, added or refined codes as new activities emerged, and revisited previously coded recordings whenever definitions changed. Each recording was coded in full by one researcher. A second researcher then watched the entire recording and reviewed all segment boundaries and assigned codes, identifying potential corrections and ambiguous cases. During weekly meetings, researchers discussed proposed corrections and ambiguous cases identified during review, including disagreements about segment boundaries (i.e., Wait or Check AI ) and the observable evidence required to assign a code. They reached consensus on the final coding and refined the codebook when needed. For example, researchers clarified the minimum duration of observable inactivity required to operationally label a segment as Wait .

RQ2: Information between Agents and Developers. We used each sent prompt as an analytical anchor to examine the observable practices of developers, both (RQ2-1) before their prompt to examine the information that informs their prompts, and (RQ2-2) after their prompt to examine the information provided by the agent’s output. For RQ2-1, we conducted a backward analysis . We analyzed each prompt’s content and reviewed the coded activities during the ten minutes before the developer began formulating it. This interval captured preceding activities such as inspecting source code, documentation, tickets, or previous agent responses. When visible evidence suggested that information included in the prompt had been obtained earlier ( e.g., a paste from an earlier copy action), coders extended their review beyond the initial ten-minute window. For each prompt, we coded three aspects: (1) its intent (inquiry, request, or reply); (2) the type of context explicitly copied and pasted into it , such as documentation, source code, or terminal output; and (3) the recorded on-screen activities that provided evidence of where the pasted context originated ,(4) the recorded preceding activities without assuming that every inspected source contributed information to the prompt. For example, P4 spent the minutes before one prompt reading a ticket, the associated GitHub PR page, and an internal wiki; they then pasted the wiki content directly into an inquiry asking the agent to explain what changes were still required for a migration with external dependency. Here we could trace a clear chain from the embedded context ( pasted documentation )back to its observed source (the wiki). Prompts could receive multiple intent labels. Our coding thus rested on two kinds of observable evidence: context the developer explicitly pasted into the prompt, and activities visible on screen shortly before it. Information that a developer may have drawn on from their memory, or from sources consulted outside the recorded session, were not observable and therefore fell outside our analysis. For RQ2-2, we conducted forward analysis by treating each submitted prompt as the unit of analysis and traced the recording forward to identify all subsequent activities, such as Check AI Response or Interaction with Terminal , that were potentially associated with the prompt, stopping at the next prompt within the same task. For each prompt, we recorded the number of distinct types of “checking” it received, and coded the checking practices performed on its resulting output. These practices spanned both inspecting the content of the output ( e.g., checking generated code or the agent’s response) and exercising it ( e.g., running the program, tests, or a command and examining the resulting output). A prompt could therefore receive multiple checking labels when a participant used more than one type of practice. A follow-up prompt could itself serve to verify the prior turn. For instance, asking the agent “are you sure?”, which we coded as a follow-up . We traced checking activities until the next submitted prompt within the same task, or the end of the recording if no subsequent prompt occurred in that task. Checks occurring after that subsequent prompt were not linked back to the earlier output. If no checking activity was observed within this interval, we labeled the prompt No Check Observed . Response checks and verification activities were not necessarily adjacent to the corresponding prompt. Participants could switch to another task while an agent was working and later return to check or verify its output, as illustrated in Figure 3. We therefore traced prompt-related activities across intervening tasks and windows rather than restricting the analysis to activities immediately following prompt submission. 

3.3 Phase 2: Follow-up Interviews 

Only after completing the observational coding and preliminary analysis did we invite all partici-pants to a 20-minute follow-up interview. The final dataset contains interviews with the same 11

participants from the observational study. While initial interviews were scheduled for 20 minutes, sessions were allowed to extend up to 44 minutes when participants offered rich and unsolicited accounts and insights that required further exploration. This flexible approach ensured thorough data collection while prioritizing participant time. Informed by Harvey’s [21] dialogic approach, we used the follow-up interviews for member-checking and to develop interpretations of the observed patterns through discussion with them. The interviews followed a common semi-structured guide supplemented by participant-specific questions derived from each recording. Each topic was accom-panied by a visualization of the relevant observations. The interviewers asked whether the activity segment (Fig. 2) and task-switching pattern (Fig. 3) were representative of participants’ usual work, elicited explanations for patterns and surprising moments to them, and provided perspectives not directly observable in the recordings. For example, when discussing task switching, participants viewed their own task-transition pattern alongside anonymized patterns from other participants. We analyzed the interview responses primarily through deductive coding, using the guide’s topics: time allocation, task switching, documentation, collaboration, and verification practices, as predefined categories. Within these categories, we developed inductive subcodes to capture distinctions in participants’ accounts. For RQ1, these accounts helped explain time allocations and task switching in relation to tasks, responsibilities, and perceived effects of agents on focus. For RQ2, they helped interpret observed information-seeking and agent-management practices by providing participants’ perspectives on information-source preferences and checking strategies. 

3.4 Threats to Validity 

Credibility. Participants may have altered their behavior because a researcher was present and their screens were recorded. We asked them to work on their own development activities rather than assigned tasks, but cannot determine whether they would have prompted agents, checked outputs, or switched tasks in the same way without observation. We used member checking in the follow-up interviews. Their responses helped contextualize the recordings but cannot rule out an observer effect [26]. The recordings capture visible actions rather than participants’ attention or intentions. For example, P6, P7, and P8 frequently switched between agent sessions, but we cannot distinguish whether they read an agent’s output or briefly checked whether it needed input. We therefore coded observable actions without inferring the depth of participants’ engagement. One researcher coded each recording, a second watched and reviewed it in full, and the team discussed ambiguous cases. We also maintained reflexive notes documenting our perspectives and used 

peer debriefing [16] with three software-engineering professors to examine our interpretations. 

Dependability. Our time allocations and task-transition counts depend on how observable ac-tivities were segmented and assigned to tasks. Under our stated definition, even a brief visit to another task counts as a transition. The counts thus describe visible movement between tasks, not necessarily substantial changes in work focus. Readers should interpret comparisons in light of this definition, particularly for participants who made frequent brief visits to other tasks. 

Transferability. We observed 11 professional developers working on their own tasks for ap-proximately 90 minutes each. The participants were recruited through the authors’ access to two workplaces; their projects, organizations, and tools may differ from those of other developers. Sessions were scheduled to be free of meetings and thus do not represent every part of a workday. The 11 sessions cannot capture the full range of ways developers work with AI coding agents. As P6 noted: “[when looking at developers with few tasks], you maybe have interviewed the wrong people or at the wrong time, ...[busy developers]...you may never actually get to interview those people because those people be like, I don’t have time for you” . We therefore present the findings as practices

observed in these work contexts, not as an exhaustive account or estimates of their prevalence among professional developers. 

Limits of recordings and retrospective accounts. Video recording software recorded one screen at a time. P1, P4, and P9 were the only participants using two monitors during recording. They could move private messaging to the unrecorded monitor if they did not want its content captured. When they did so, the researcher noted that messaging occurred and recorded its timing in field notes, without documenting the message content. We therefore could not determine whether an off-screen message informed a later prompt. All 11 participants took part in follow-up interviews between one week and three months after observation. This interval may have limited recall of specific moments. We anchored questions in visualizations of their recorded activities and asked primarily about their general workflows. We treat their explanations as retrospective perspectives rather than evidence of what they were thinking during each recorded action. 

Ethical considerations. The study followed an approved Institutional Review Board protocol, and participants consented to observation and recording. One member was employed at Company A but did not supervise participants, conduct recruitment, or have access to participant identities or recordings. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that results shared beyond the first three authors, who coded the recordings, would be anonymized. The researchers’ workplace access was governed by the applicable study and confidentiality arrangements. Although some messages with colleagues were visible in the recordings, we did not analyze their content to protect participants’ and colleagues’ privacy. We therefore report time spent on communication but do not characterize the information exchanged in those messages. 

4 Results 

We answer the research questions by bringing together observational videos ( 4) and follow-up interviews ( ×). The observational videos ground our account in what developers did during the sessions; the interviews capture how developers interpreted those activities after seeing the observational findings and how they related them to their usual practices. We draw on both sources for RQ1-1 through RQ2-3. RQ2-4 draws primarily on participants’ retrospective reflections. Throughout the results, terms such as “checking” describe actions coded from screen recordings and do not establish participants’ gaze, attention, or thought processes, which we did not capture through eye tracking or think-aloud data. 

4.1 RQ1-1: Time Allocations Among Activities 

Finding 1 ( 4): Agent interaction occupied a substantial share of session time , with consid-erable variation across developers. We segmented the videos by activity and aggregated the time spent on each activity (Fig. 2). Participants spent an average of 33.4% of their sessions on AI-related activities: 11.3% formulating prompts (range: 6.0%–18.6%) and 22.1% checking AI responses (range: 6.6%–59.4%). They spent 8.9% of their sessions inspecting code (range: 0.5%–36.2%), 5.4% checking documents, and 8.7% interacting with the terminal. Individual variation was substantial, consistent with Chou et al. [14]. P1 spent 36.2% of their session inspecting code, whereas P6, P7, P8, and P11 each spent less than 3% doing so (0.5%, 2.0%, 1.3%, and 2.4%, respectively). P6 spent 77.9% of their session formulating prompts or checking AI responses. For ten of the eleven participants, these agent-related activities occupied more time than direct code inspection.

In these cases, the response itself was often the information being sought rather than an intermediate artifact requiring further inspection. Learners worked on an average of 3.3 tasks and made 18.3 task switches per session, showing less task interleaving than Delegators . Their interaction with agents was primarily centered on building understanding rather than directing implementation. The patterns could also overlap within a session. P9, for example, primarily behaved as a 

Learner while exploring an unfamiliar repository, but later shifted toward a Supervisor pattern in another task when requesting changes and inspecting the resulting artifacts. P10 and P11 similarly combined Supervisor and Learner behaviors while working with external libraries and applying them to their own projects. Together, they worked on an average of 2.0 tasks and made 11.0 task switches per session; their prompts included both inquiries (45%) and requests (36%), while 49% were followed solely by AI-response checking. We therefore interpret Supervisor , Delegator , and 

Learner as recurring patterns of agent management that can shift with the task and context, rather than as fixed developer types. 

4.6 RQ2-4: Information from AI vs. Colleagues 

Developers spent an average of 9.9% of their observed session time on messaging and email. Although some communication content was visible in the recordings, we did not analyze it to protect participants’ and their colleagues’ privacy. We therefore report communication time without characterizing the information exchanged. Instead, the follow-up interviews asked what information developers preferred to seek from AI agents versus colleagues. 

Finding 12 ( ×): Developers turned to colleagues when questions required organizational context, human authority, or collective alignment, while using agents for technical infor-mation accessible from code, documentation, or public knowledge. Participants’ accounts highlighted two distinctions. The first concerned organizational context and human authority . De-velopers consulted colleagues about the intent behind feature requests (P4, P8), historical reasoning not apparent in code (P1, P7), permissions requiring human authority (P8), and the meaning of domain-specific concepts in their work (P6). P8 summarized this distinction: “I mostly only talk to

humans now about intent and access.” P1 emphasized colleagues’ knowledge of “historical reasons why certain things had to be done.” In contrast, developers described turning to agents for technical questions answerable from code, documentation, or public knowledge (P1–P4, P7, P8, P9, P11). P4 distinguished requirements from implementation guidance: “requirements come from a col-league...but with AI, it’s more like how can I accomplish this?” P7 described their team’s expectation: 

“if a agent can reasonably answer it, then you should ask an agent.” The second distinction concerned 

collective decision . Developers consulted colleagues about scope, system design, and coordination over who was working on what (P3, P4, P7–P9, P11). P11 explained: “if this question requires more like alignment, then I’ll reach out to my colleague first before I reach out to AI.” 

Developers spent an average of 7.3% of their observed session time reviewing or editing doc-umentation. This contrasts with the pre-AI survey by Stettina & Heijstek [46], in which half of respondents reported spending less than 15 minutes per day maintaining documentation despite recognizing its importance. 

Finding 13 ( ×): Documentation served dual purposes in agent-assisted development: preserving knowledge for people and providing context for agents. During interview, P6 questioned the premise directly: if AI could explain codebase from code alone, “What’s the point of documenting more?” finding it “absolutely absurd that one person writes a lengthy documentation that then the next person feeds into the AI to shorten it into a summary.” P7 offered two reasons that covered most participants: “docs maybe serve two purposes pre-AI, (1) help you understand things at a high level but then also (2) very mechanically like how to implement something ..., that [(2)] just for agents at this point.” Both reasons summarize across participants. The first was preserving knowledge that might otherwise be lost or remain implicit (P1, P3, P5). P1 and P5 adopted structured pre-work documents to maintain shared understanding as AI accelerated code production, while P3 configured an agent to log design and architectural decisions in real time. As P5 described, this involved “distilling yourself and extracting your information into a markdown document so that everyone else can take up on the task.” The second was providing context for agents. P2 moved team documentation into a repository so Claude could access it and check its consistency with the code. AI also lowered the effort of maintaining documentation (P2, P4, P5, P7, P8, P10, P11): participants used agents to generate wiki pages, notes, and drafts, though outputs still required cleanup. As P8 put it, “the agent writes all the docs up front” , but they still had to “deslop the docs all the time” .

5 Discussion 5.1 Developers as Managers of AI Agents 

Our findings show not only how much time developers spend working with underlying artifacts and interacting with agents , but also where they exercise attention and control when working with coding agents (Finding 1–6). The Supervisor , Delegator , and Learner patterns (Finding 11) make this redistribution visible: Supervisors remained close to generated artifacts, Delegators managed work primarily through dialogue and coordination, and Learners used agents to build understanding. Across these patterns, agent management involved three recurring demands: bounding agent autonomy , exercising technical judgment , and allocating attention across concurrent delegated work. Kalliamvakou et al. ’s account of effective engineering management provides a useful lens for bounded autonomy and technical judgment [24], while prior work on coding agents has documented developers’ continued use of planning, supervision, and validation [23]. Our observations extend these accounts by showing that control can occur at different points in the workflow.

First, developers bounded agent autonomy through task scope, tests, careful plans, and checked outputs through response review, artifact inspection, execution, and follow-up prompts (Findings 9– 10). Control therefore occurred not only after generation, but also upstream by harnessing the conditions under which agents operated. Second, developers relied on technical judgment, or “gut feeling (P11)” to decide when closer inspection or additional investigation was warranted (Finding 10). The Learner pattern further shows that agents could help developers build the understanding needed to make those judgments. Third, coding agents introduced additional parallelism into already fragmented software work [1, 30, 35]. Participants used deliberate task switching, reminders, and external notes to manage concurrent agent-mediated work (Findings 5–6). Among Delegators , control was exercised less through artifact inspection and more through deciding when human attention should return. Agent management also remained embedded in workplace collaboration. Prior work has shown that developers’ information needs involve both technical artifacts and coordination with others [8, 27]. Our participants used agents mainly for technical information, while turning to colleagues for organizational history, intent, authority, and collective alignment (Finding 12). Agent capability may therefore shift, rather than eliminate, human information seeking. Taken together, our findings characterize agent management as a redistribution of human control: developers constrain agents, decide when intervention is needed, and direct limited attention across concurrent work. 

5.2 Designing for Supervising, Coordinating, and Learning with AI Agents 

Our findings suggest that development tools should support not only the execution of programming tasks but also the work involved in managing AI agents. The Supervisor , Delegator , and Learner 

patterns reveal different needs: visibility and verification support for generated artifacts [5, 48], coordination across concurrent agent work [8, 19], and support for building understanding [38]. For Supervisors , management depended on inspecting generated artifacts, executing programs, and deciding which changes warranted closer review. As P1 remarked, “Sometimes, 80% of my day is code review” . These practices suggest opportunities for tools that make verification evidence more visible and accessible by linking agent-generated changes to relevant artifacts, highlighting complex or fragile parts of the software that may warrant closer inspection [48], and presenting executed tests and their results [17]. Tools could also help developers locate changes that implement specific requirements or business rules and connect them to the intended behavior and relevant tests, supporting review of whether the generated logic matches domain expectations. Beyond presenting evidence, tools could challenge developers to examine their assumptions [41], identify remaining uncertainties, and formulate questions about the generated work, since effective ques-tioning depends partly on recognizing what is not yet understood [36]. These interfaces could help developers assess an agent’s report of completion and decide where to direct their review effort. For Delegators , the central challenge was less inspecting each artifact than tracking multiple ongoing agent tasks and deciding when human attention was needed. Participants improvised with reminders, notes, and deliberate switching strategies (Findings 5–6), suggesting opportunities for coordination interfaces that indicate which agent tasks are progressing independently, blocked, awaiting approval, or otherwise require human intervention. Such interfaces could build on prior work on workspace awareness, dependencies, and traceability in software development [42, 43, 47]. Furthermore, some developers explicitly directed agents to exchange information or coordinate their work (Finding 7), suggesting opportunities to expose dependencies and information flow across concurrent agent sessions [42]. Future studies should examine whether frequent switching among agent tasks affects cognitive load, task resumption, and developers’ perceived control [9], and evaluate whether coordination interfaces can mitigate the “context switching hell” (P2) [10].

For Learners , agents became part of developers’ process of understanding unfamiliar codebases, tools, and data, extending prior observations of LLM-assisted code comprehension by Nam et al. [38]. Tools could therefore connect agent-generated explanations to underlying artifacts, helping developers inspect and contextualize the information they receive. Furthermore, participants’ accounts of documentation serving both human and agent needs (Finding 13) suggest opportunities to preserve the evidence, reasoning, and actively elicit tacit knowledge behind software artifacts from developers [2, 40]. Building on our previous work [14], future tools could also provide personalized pedagogical support that draws on the developer’s task context and helps establish shared understanding between developers and agents [44]. Rather than assuming a single interaction model for agentic development, these patterns suggest that tools should support different forms of human involvement as developers move between supervising artifacts, coordinating delegated work, and building understanding. Because these patterns can overlap and shift within sessions, such support should adapt to the task and context rather than treating developers as fixed types. 

6 Conclusion 

Through workplace shadowing and follow-up interviews with 11 professional developers, we examined how developers allocate attention, direct coding agents, and check their outputs during everyday work. Developers spent an average of 33.4% of observed time formulating prompts or checking AI responses. Within approximately 90-minute sessions, they worked on 1–17 distinct tasks, with switching rates ranging from 0 to 101.6 switches per hour. We identified three recurring patterns, Supervisor , Delegator , and Learner , that combined prompt purposes, task interleaving, and checking practices in different ways: directly inspecting generated artifacts, coordinating delegated work through chats, and seeking explanations to build understanding. Participants also described using agents for technical information while turning to colleagues for organizational context, authority, and collective alignment. Future work should deepen our understanding of how developers manage attention across concurrent agent tasks, and inform tool designs that support verification, coordination, and learning.
