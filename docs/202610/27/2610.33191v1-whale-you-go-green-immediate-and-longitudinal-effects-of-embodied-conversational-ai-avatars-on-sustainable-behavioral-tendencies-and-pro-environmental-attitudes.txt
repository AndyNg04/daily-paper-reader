## Abstract 

Embodied conversational AI can be persuasive, but can it moti-vate people to develop sustainable behavioral tendencies and in-crease pro-environmental attitudes? We conducted a randomized experiment ( 𝑁 = 576) comparing four embodied avatars—a beluga whale, Greta Thunberg, Emma Watson, and a generic human— with a text chatbot and no-intervention control. All embodied conditions increased immediate sustainable product selection rel-ative to the control ( 𝛽 = .401– .609, all 𝑞 ≤ .0126) and chatbot (𝛽 = .398– .604, all 𝑞 ≤ .007), without differing significantly from one another. Only the Beluga condition increased immediate pro-environmental attitudes relative to the control after correction. Among 287 returners assessed two months later, following an in-termediate assigned-condition session, Beluga also showed signifi-cantly higher pre-booster sustainable product choice compared to the control ( 𝛽 = .657, 𝑞 = .0176). Although it did not significantly outperform other embodied agents, these findings establish the immediate potential of nonhuman embodied avatar interfaces as a promising direction for memorable environmental interventions.

## 1 Introduction 

Climate change is psychologically and temporally difficult to com-municate. Its consequences are often experienced as distant from everyday decisions, while many opportunities for individual action occur through ordinary and seemingly inconsequential choices. Conversational AI may help bridge this gap by translating general environmental information into interactive, personally relevant di-alogue. Unlike static messages, conversational systems can respond to questions, adapt explanations, and invite users to reflect on the relationship between their values and behavior. At the same time, generative AI can deliver persuasive messages at considerable scale, creating both opportunities for public interest communication and risks of manipulation or unwanted influence [49]. Understanding whether such interventions work, how long their effects persist, and which representational choices shape their influence is therefore an increasingly important problem for human-computer interaction. Embodiment may play a central role in this process. A visible and voiced avatar can transform an informational exchange into an encounter with an apparently social partner. Prior research suggests that an agent’s perceived identity and embodiment can influence social presence, interpersonal judgments, and responses to persuasive requests [45, 67]. The modality through which an AI is presented, including text, voice, and visual embodiment, can likewise shape how users experience and respond to a conversation [4]. However, embodiment does not guarantee persuasion, and the effects of a recognizable persona may differ from those of a generic avatar. The identity represented by an agent may affect the credibility, emotional meaning, and memorability of its message independently of the information it communicates. Nonhuman avatars offer a particularly consequential design possibility. Rather than describing environmental harm from the perspective of a distant expert, an AI can represent an animal or ecosystem positioned as an affected stakeholder. A beluga whale de-scribing changes to its habitat, for example, can connect an abstract environmental consequence to a particular living being. Research on anthropomorphized nature suggests that representing nature in humanlike ways can strengthen feelings of connectedness and protective concern [73]. Related work with conversational marine characters found that interactive first-person conversations can en-courage sustainability-related intentions and product preferences while eliciting especially strong emotional engagement with an an-imal character [59]. Yet it remains unclear whether such responses extend beyond the immediate interaction or whether a nonhuman character offers an advantage over embodied human agents, a text chatbot, or no intervention. This temporal question is especially important for AI persuasion. Many experiments evaluate attitudes or intentions immediately after exposure, when the conversation remains salient. Such mea-surements establish an initial response, but not whether a condition difference remains detectable later. Immediate effects may dissipate, or a memorable cue may become relevant at a future decision point. Persistence may also differ across outcomes. An intervention fo-cused on packaging and consumption may affect later performance on the same choice task without producing an equally persistent change in broader environmental beliefs. Climate interventions therefore need to distinguish immediate from later effects and con-crete choices from generalized attitudes. Prior large scale research similarly demonstrates that climate interventions can affect dif-ferent outcomes in different ways, with changes in beliefs, policy support, information sharing, and behavior not necessarily occur-ring together [76]. We conducted a registered, randomized longitudinal online ex-periment with 576 U.S. participants assigned to a nonhuman avatar represented by a beluga whale, an activist avatar represented by Greta Thunberg, a celebrity avatar represented by Emma Watson, a generic human avatar, a text chatbot, or no intervention. The four embodied conditions used real-time spoken dialogue, whereas the chatbot used typed dialogue. Participants completed a four-product choice task and pro-environmental attitude measures before and after Wave 1; 287 returned approximately two months later for a pre-booster assessment and booster. The primary finding is deliberately narrow. All four tested embodied-interface packages increased immediate sustainable selection on the screen-based task relative to both no intervention and the text chatbot, including the direct Generic Avatar–Text Chatbot com-parison; the embodied conditions did not differ significantly from one another. The Beluga condition remains central to the longi-tudinal contribution: it alone showed corrected differences from the no-intervention control in immediate GREEN attitudes and in sustainable product choice before the Wave 2 booster. Direct tests did not establish that Beluga outperformed every other embod-ied condition, and the follow-up reflects the assigned intervention sequence rather than Wave 1 exposure alone. Accordingly, this work contributes (1) randomized evidence that assignment to the tested embodied conversational interface pro-duced greater immediate sustainable product selection than both parallel text-only conversation and no intervention on a repeated product-choice task, relative to text conversation and no interven-tion; and (2) evidence that an affected nonhuman agent merits lon-gitudinal study, because the Beluga condition produced the clearest later condition–control contrast. Analyses of credibility, dialogue, and feedback characterize possible explanations for the Beluga pattern without establishing a causal mechanism. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Persuasive Conversational AI and Consumer Influence 

Persuasive technologies can influence attitudes and behavior as tools, media, and social actors [29]. Conversation adds adaptive questioning, personalization, and source identity. Donation stud-ies show that strategy, recipient characteristics, and bot identity affect persuasive responses [67, 77]; anthropomorphic cues can also increase compliance through social presence [1]. Generative systems extend this capacity: psychologically tailored messages can outperform generic ones [49], and conversational AI has changed conspiracy beliefs and political judgments under some conditions [20, 34, 64]. These effects are neither uniform nor interchangeable across beliefs, intentions, and behavior. The same capabilities can steer consumer decisions without reli-able awareness [63, 79]. Researchers have found low detection of manipulative conversational turns and reduced compliance after

a just-in-time warning [60]. Persuasive efficacy must therefore be evaluated alongside transparency, user interests, and meaningful choice [8]. Computational taxonomies and multi-turn benchmarks further show that persuasion comprises distinguishable strategies whose effects depend on source, recipient, and conversational con-text [12, 13, 84]. We use this literature to characterize agent lan-guage, while treating behavioral effects on people as an empirical question. 

## 2.2 Embodiment, Represented Identity, and Celebrity Likeness 

Embodied conversational agents integrate language with a visible representation and communicative cues such as voice, gaze, facial expression, and gesture [17]. These cues can shape social presence and expectations, but reviews do not support a uniform embodi-ment advantage across tasks [42, 51, 54, 66]. Effects also depend on design metaphor, realism, role, and modality [7, 22, 83]. For exam-ple, embodiment increased presence but not reliably healthy snack choice in one immersive study [45], while text, voice, and avatar versions of Future You produced similar focal effects [4]. These mixed findings motivate direct comparison rather than assuming that visual presence is inherently persuasive. Recognizable figures introduce prior attitudes and parasocial associations. Celebrity-endorsement research links expertise, trust-worthiness, and attractiveness to persuasion, with effects varying across sources, products, and audiences [43, 57]. Environmental endorsement can affect behavior [37]; Greta Thunberg may also function as a moral exemplar [48]. Simulated likenesses can extend parasocial relationships into interaction, while raising disclosure and non-endorsement concerns [40]. Repeated interaction introduces an additional design dimension. Relational-agent research shows that conversational systems can establish continuity across encounters and that users’ responses to an agent may develop through an ongoing human–computer relationship rather than a single exposure [10]. This makes longitu-dinal evaluation particularly important when an agent’s identity is intended to remain memorable over time. Nonhuman agents may instead embody an affected stakeholder. This framing also intersects with Animal–Computer Interaction, which has argued that animals should be understood as stakeholders in technological design rather than only as resources for human-centered objectives [47]. Our study does not involve an animal participant; instead, it examines the persuasive consequences of an AI-generated representation speaking from an affected animal’s perspective. Anthropomorphized nature and virtual animal em-bodiment can increase connectedness with nature [3, 73]; cuteness may also focus attention, although it does not imply environmental persuasion [53]. OceanChat found that conversational marine char-acters increased prospective intentions and sustainable preferences, with especially strong empathy toward a beluga [59]. This moti-vates testing whether an affected nonhuman character produces a distinctive immediate or longitudinal pattern. 

## 2.3 Digital Interventions for Pro-Environmental Behavior 

Sustainable HCI encompasses multiple approaches to understand-ing how interactive systems relate to environmental action, ranging from persuasive interventions and feedback systems to broader ac-counts of everyday practices and infrastructures [23]. Environmental attitudes do not consistently translate into action, and intervention effects vary by strategy and outcome [26, 52, 80]. Sustainable HCI scholarship has also cautioned that persuasive systems can narrow sustainability to individual behavior while obscuring the institutional, material, and political systems in which that behavior occurs [15]. We therefore evaluate a bounded con-sumer decision rather than positioning individual product choice as a sufficient response to climate change. Eco-feedback can make consequences visible, but its value depends on integration with ev-eryday practice [72]; a 63-country intervention tournament likewise found heterogeneous effects across beliefs, policy support, shar-ing, and behavioral effort [76]. Environmental outcomes should therefore be analyzed separately rather than treated as a single pro-environmental response. Embodied and conversational interventions can integrate infor-mation, affect, and action-oriented guidance [2, 9, 32]. OceanChat found stronger prospective intentions and sustainable preferences after conversational character narratives, but more limited effects on several broader outcomes [59]. Prior work rarely combines text-only and no-intervention controls while comparing recognizable hu-man, generic human, and affected nonhuman agents over time. Our six-condition design addresses that gap while distinguishing sus-tainable behavioral tendencies from pro-environmental attitudes. 

## 3 Methodology 

We conducted a randomized between-subjects online experiment with an initial intervention (Wave 1), an intermediate assigned-condition session analyzed exploratorily, and a follow-up assess-ment and booster (Wave 2). Participants were assigned to one of four embodied agents, a text chatbot, or no intervention. The design tested the following questions: 

RQ1. Do embodied conversational agents produce greater pre-to-post increases in pro-environmental attitudes and sustainable behavioral tendencies than non-embodied con-ditions? 

RQ2. Are condition differences in perceived source credibility statistically associated with condition differences in sustain-able behavioral tendencies and pro-environmental attitudes, and how do attractiveness, trustworthiness, and expertise contribute to these associations? 

RQ3. Do condition differences in pro-environmental attitudes and sustainable behavioral tendencies remain detectable at the final follow-up after repeated assigned-condition exposure? Prior research has shown that conversational climate characters can elicit engagement, intentions, and sustainable product prefer-ences [9, 59]. The contribution of the present study is not simply another demonstration that an environmental character can be persuasive. Instead, we use a six-condition randomized design to distinguish effects shared across four embodied avatar interfaces,

This design provides a stronger comparative test of when representational and interface choices matter, while preserving the distinction between immedi-ate task performance, broader pro-environmental attitudes, and longitudinal condition differences. 

## 3.1 Intervention Design 

Figure 2 summarizes the Wave 1 procedure and condition structure. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the six conditions in Table 1. Four conditions used an embodied conversational interface. In this study, embodied conversational interface refers to the imple-mented multimodal configuration comprising a visible avatar, syn-thetic voice, and spoken interaction. Accordingly, the Generic Avatar– Text Chatbot contrast estimates the effect of assignment to this em-bodied interface configuration relative to text-only conversation. (1) The Beluga whale nonhuman avatar spoke as an af-fected animal, using warm first-person descriptions of Arc-tic change and linking consumption to its home and pod [59, 70]. (2) The Greta Thunberg activist avatar prompt emphasized moral exemplarity, value–action consistency, collective norms, and reasoned challenge without shaming [21, 48, 65]. The 

Emma Watson celebrity avatar prompt emphasized warmth, identification, parasocial familiarity, and feasible action [18, 37, 38, 41]. (3) The Generic Avatar condition used a visible and voiced unnamed woman with no public identity or special exper-tise. (4) The Text Chatbot condition used a similarly ordinary, unnamed conversational persona through typed text alone. The two generic conditions were aligned on their neutral identity, sustainability topic, participant-profile context, short-turn format, and environmental-facts block; neither used a named persona’s strategy. The Generic Avatar added visual presence, synthetic voice, and spoken interaction, making this direct contrast the closest test of embodiment while not separating its components. (5) The no-intervention control completed the measurement sequence without a conversation. 

3.1.1 Technical Implementation. We implemented the study in Svel-teKit/Vite on Vercel (Node.js 20), integrating Prolific, Qualtrics, con-dition routing, product-choice tasks, conversation, and data capture. Server-side Anthropic routes assembled condition-specific context; ElevenLabs provided real-time spoken sessions for the embodied agents, while an Anthropic route generated typed chatbot replies under parallel instructions. Google’s Nano Banana was used to generate stylized, semi-realistic images resembling Emma Watson, Greta Thunberg, a beluga whale, and a generic woman. Rather than pursuing photorealistic likenesses, we gave the images a more ani-mated appearance to reduce the risk of discomfort associated with highly humanlike but imperfect artificial characters, commonly described as the uncanny valley [50]. We also incorporated sub-tle cues such as softened features and rounded forms, particularly in the Beluga character, to make the agents appear approachable; prior research suggests that cute stimuli can increase attentional focus and careful behavior [53]. These visual properties were de-sign choices rather than independently manipulated factors, and perceived uncanniness and cuteness were not measured. At session creation, the server inserted the same fixed climatiqStats 

factual block into the system prompt for all five conversational con-ditions. The block contained fixed packaging-footprint estimates for a plastic bottle, refill pouch, glass bottle, and cardboard pack-age derived during implementation from Climatiq emission factors, together with OECD statistics on plastic recycling and lifecycle emissions [19, 55, 56]. The values were embedded rather than re-trieved live. Every conversational condition could introduce the refill comparison once when relevant and otherwise used numerical facts only upon request; personas changed framing, not access to facts. Participants had to spend a minimum of five minutes convers-ing with the conversational AI before transitioning over to the next step in the experiment. Firebase stored condition, session, prompt, choices, timestamps, completion, and conversation records. Prolific identifiers linked sources before replacement with study IDs. 

## 3.2 Procedure and Longitudinal Structure 

At Wave 1, participants first completed the baseline survey and the pre-session product-choice task. Participants in one of the five conversational conditions then interacted with their assigned agent, whereas participants in the no-intervention control did not receive a conversation. All participants next completed the post-session product-choice task and post-session survey. Returning participants kept their original condition assignment. Figure 3 illustrates the screen-based shopping task. Participants selected one option from each pair, but no purchase or product delivery occurred. The longitudinal protocol comprised the Wave 1 initial interven-tion, an intermediate assigned-condition session, and the Wave 2 follow-up booster intervention, with participants retaining their original randomized assignments throughout. The intermediate session was administered from August 3–17, 2026. A client-side caching issue substantially reduced the number of complete, link-able application records in the conversational conditions. We there-fore restricted the intermediate-session sample to participants with completed, matching Qualtrics pre- and post-session records during this period. This yielded 142 participants, with 13–21 participants in each conversational condition and 56 in the no-intervention control; 141 also had complete application-based product-choice records. Because these small and unequal samples provided insufficient sen-sitivity for the planned condition comparisons, the intermediate session was excluded from confirmatory inference and is reported exploratorily in the Supplementary Material. Wave 2 remained the next assessment with sufficiently complete data for the primary longitudinal condition comparisons.

## 3.6 Statistical Analysis and Model Specification 

We estimated condition differences in immediate outcomes and follow-up outcomes using regression models adjusted for baseline measurements and participant characteristics. The measures and their timing followed the preregistration; departures in the imple-mented analyses are specified below. 

3.6.1 Dependent Variables. The preregistered outcomes comprised two categories: sustainable behavioral tendencies and pro-environmental attitudes . The former included sustainable product choice, donation, and willingness to share climate information. The latter included retrospective self-reported habits, prospective pro-environmental intentions, GREEN attitudes, three denial subscales, psychological distance, and policy support. Measures were analyzed separately rather than combined into category-level composites. 

• Sustainable behavioral tendencies – Sustainable product choice ( TotalEco , 0–4): At each pre- and post-session assessment, four price-matched image pairs (fruit, shampoo, snacks, and soap) con-trasted lower-packaging with conventional options. Sustainable selections were summed as TotalEco , and the four choices were also analyzed separately. The task design balanced longitudinal comparability against exact stimulus repetition. We retained the same prod-uct categories, price matching, response format, and lower-packaging contrast to ensure that differences across assessments were not simply caused by testing different kinds of products. Changing categories would have made it unclear whether differences across waves reflected intervention-related change or category-specific preferences [39, 78]. Conversely, repeating identical images could cue recognition of the earlier products or responses. Research indicates that detailed visual information about objects can be retained after a sin-gle exposure [14] and that retrieval is facilitated when later cues overlap with those available during encoding 

> 6Whale You Go Green?

[74]. We therefore retained the underlying product cat-egories and decision structure while varying surface details across waves, including soap color and snack contents. This approach was intended to preserve com-parability while reducing exact image-based retrieval cues. It does not eliminate task learning, remembered decision rules, or measurement reactivity [81]. Controlled simulations can provide meaningful evi-dence about behavior when they preserve the central decision structure of the real-world action. Here, par-ticipants did not merely report whether sustainability was important; they had to choose between mutually exclusive, visually presented products that varied in an actionable environmental attribute. The task re-produced a familiar consumer decision, selecting one packaged product over another, while holding price, category, and response structure constant. This control made it possible to detect whether the intervention changed how participants applied sustainability con-siderations when making a concrete choice. Prior research further indicates that actions practiced in mediated environments can extend beyond those en-vironments. Researchers found that embodied virtual experiences concerning resource consumption affected subsequently measured pro-environmental behavior in the physical world, including effects assessed be-yond the immediate virtual experience [2]. Related work found that observing a virtual self perform a behavior subsequently increased exercise outside the mediated environment [31]. More broadly, experimen-tally elicited choices in simulated decision settings have corresponded closely with consequential real-world choices when the task preserves the relevant choice structure [35]. These findings suggest that me-diated choices can function as behavioral rehearsal: they allow people to practice noticing a relevant cue, applying a decision rule, and selecting an action that may later become accessible in a comparable situation. The present product-choice task therefore provides evidence beyond generalized attitudes or stated inten-tions. It tests whether participants applied the inter-vention’s message while resolving a concrete product tradeoff. Because the same product categories, packag-ing contrast, and decision structure recur in everyday shopping, the observed changes identify a plausible pathway through which the intervention could affect subsequent consumer action.

## 4 Results 

Wave 1 established the shared immediate embodied-interface result; Wave 2 identified a distinct Beluga condition–control pattern before the booster. Secondary and exploratory outcomes are reported after these focal tests.

## 4.5 Exploratory dialogue and feedback analyses suggest candidate explanations for effects observed with the Beluga whale avatar 

Automated language analyses and participant feedback described candidate explanations for the Beluga pattern; they were not tests of causal mechanisms.

## 5 Discussion 5.1 Embodied interfaces can support consumers in making sustainable product choices in the moment 

Immediate sustainable product choice was higher in each embod-ied condition than in the no-intervention control after adjustment: the estimated differences were 0 .491 choices for the Beluga whale condition, 0 .609 for the Greta Thunberg condition, 0 .480 for the Emma Watson condition, and 0 .401 for the Generic Avatar con-dition (all 𝑞 ≤ .0126). The Text Chatbot estimate was effectively zero ( 𝛽 = 0.002, 95% CI [− 0.276 , 0.279 ], 𝑝 = .9918). The direct agent-only analysis strengthens this inference: each embodied con-dition exceeded the Text Chatbot by 0 .398–0 .604 choices after the four-comparison correction (all 𝑞 ≤ .007). Most importantly for isolating the role of a named persona, the Generic Avatar exceeded the Text Chatbot ( 𝛽 = .398, 95% bootstrap CI [.103 , . 674 ], 𝑞 = .007), even though both used an unnamed, ordinary conversational iden-tity, parallel sustainability instructions, and the same factual block. The immediate advantage therefore did not require a recogniz-able persuasive persona in this implementation and is consistent with an advantage of the embodied avatar interface. The four em-bodied conditions did not differ significantly from one another (𝐹 (3, 541 ) = 0.790, 𝑝 = .4997). Because the Generic Avatar–Text Chatbot contrast jointly varied visual presence, synthetic voice, and spoken rather than typed interaction, it does not identify which component produced the difference. The experiment therefore es-tablishes effects of the tested interface packages without isolating a single embodied feature. At the Wave 2 pre-booster assessment, after the intermediate assigned-condition session, only the Beluga whale condition showed a statistically significant product-choice advantage over the no-intervention control ( 𝛽 = 0.657, 95% CI [0.216 , 1.098 ], 𝑞 = .0176). The direct comparison with the mean of the other embodied con-ditions was smaller and did not reach conventional significance (𝛽 = 0.325, 95% CI [− 0.008 , 0.658 ], 𝑝 = .0558), and the omnibus comparison among embodied conditions was also nonsignificant (𝐹 (3, 252 ) = 1.481, 𝑝 = .2201). The design implication is therefore to investigate affected nonhuman characters as a promising approach, rather than to treat the Beluga whale condition as an established optimal persuasive agent. Because participants retained their as-signed conditions during the intermediate session, the follow-up contrast captures the longitudinal intervention sequence rather than the isolated persistence of the Wave 1 effect. The product-choice task was deliberately standardized to mini-mize differences arising from the stimuli themselves. All conditions encountered the same price-matched pairs, product categories, re-sponse format, and lower-packaging contrast. Across waves, the categories and decision structure remained constant, while sur-face details such as soap color and snack contents varied. This design balanced comparability across assessments with reduced repetition of identical visual cues, responding to concerns about stimulus-specific inference and visual recognition [14, 39, 74, 78]. Within this common measurement structure, the Beluga whale condition was the only condition with a significant corrected product-choice difference from the no-intervention control before the Wave 2 booster ( 𝛽 = .657, 𝑞 = .0176). The longitudinal model adjusted for Wave 1 product-choice scores and participant characteristics and weighted for measured differences in follow-up participation. More-over, none of the 29 examined Wave 1 characteristics differed sig-nificantly across assigned conditions within the returning sample after correction (all 𝑞 ≥ .972). When Wave 1 water use was addition-ally included in both the observation-weight and outcome models, the Beluga contrast remained significant ( 𝛽 = .633, 𝑞 = .0217). Shared task familiarity or a general practice effect therefore does not readily explain why this condition–control difference remained detectable. Together with participants’ references to the affected animal and one linked account of remembering refillability dur-ing later purchasing, the pattern is compatible with the Beluga character serving as a memorable cue for the repeated choice. Cue accessibility was not measured, and the direct comparisons did not establish that Beluga outperformed the other embodied con-ditions, so this explanation remains a promising mechanism for future testing. 

## 5.2 An affected non-human may provide a memorable cue to encourage sustainable behavior 

The Beluga whale condition paired Reflective Thinking in 53.3% of agent-text windows with Non-expert Testimonial language in 55.1%, compared with only 2.0–22.6% for the latter technique in the other conversational conditions. Participant feedback also referred to affected animals, ecosystems, or the animal’s perspective in 22 of 97 responses (22.7%), compared with one response in the Greta Thunberg condition and none in the other conditions. Topic profiles emphasized ocean and Arctic content, and feedback connected pack-aging decisions to helping “her ocean.” One longitudinally linked account further associated initial empathy and guilt with remem-bering refillability during later purchasing. Together, these findings suggest that the character may have connected an environmental consequence to an actionable product attribute. Experiments on anthropomorphized nature have linked human-like representation to connectedness and protective behavior [73]. Research on reminders through association also shows that distinc-tive cues can support acting on intentions when an opportunity arises [62]. Applied here by analogy, the Beluga whale character could provide a memorable cue for considering environmental con-sequences at a purchasing decision. Cue accessibility and unaided recall were not measured, so this remains an interpretation to test, rather than an established mechanism. Cuteness and novelty are additional hypotheses. Viewing cute animal images can narrow attention and increase careful task per-formance [53]; that evidence does not establish an effect on pro-environmental attitudes or sustainable behavioral tendencies. Nei-ther perceived cuteness nor novelty was measured. Work on novelty in social robotics also cautions against treating novelty as a single explanation [68].

## 5.3 Conversational positivity is an incomplete indicator of sustainable product-choice effects 

The conditions with the most positive language were not consis-tently those with the clearest behavioral evidence. The Emma Wat-son condition had the highest mean agent sentiment ( 𝑀 = .861, 

𝑆𝐷 = .081) and participant sentiment ( 𝑀 = .472, 𝑆𝐷 = .188), whereas the Greta Thunberg condition showed the largest increase in participant sentiment from the first to final conversation third (Δ = +.182). Mean agent sentiment was nearly identical in the Beluga whale and Text Chatbot conditions ( 𝑀 = .727 and .729, re-spectively), despite different participant-sentiment means ( 𝑀 = .403 and .223) and different sustainable product-choice outcomes. These descriptive comparisons show why positive agent language, posi-tive participant language, and sustainable product choice should be evaluated separately. They do not establish that positivity is irrelevant or that sadness improves persuasion. The contextual classifier likewise indicated lower joy and higher sadness in the Greta Thunberg condition ( 𝑀 joy = .245, 𝑀 sadness =

.187) and Beluga whale condition ( 𝑀 joy = .485, 𝑀 sadness = .166) than in the other conditions. In contrast, the Emma Watson condi-tion had the highest mean joy ( 𝑀 = .749). Together with feedback about empathy, guilt, and discomfort, these profiles suggest a pos-sible role for engagement with environmental harm. Yan et al. [82] found that animal-harm narratives increased empathy in one ex-periment; in another, narrative effects involved sadness and anger, with sadness and empathy associated with anticipated guilt and pro-environmental intentions. These findings provide a relevant account of how concern and discomfort can accompany motivation, while also indicating that the pathways vary across settings. For climate communication, the implication is to make conse-quences and feasible responses clear while assessing both user expe-rience and subsequent outcomes. A message need not be uniformly reassuring to be useful, but these data do not justify maximizing sadness or guilt. Direct measures of experienced emotion and ex-perimentally varied framing are needed to test whether concern for affected nonhumans explains the product-choice results. 

## 5.4 Changes in sustainable product choice do not establish broader changes in climate beliefs 

The outcome pattern was selective rather than uniformly pro-environmental. Wave 1 sustainable product choice differences were detected in all four embodied conditions ( 𝛽 = 0.401–0 .609, all 

𝑞 ≤ .0126), whereas only the Beluga whale condition showed sig-nificantly higher GREEN attitudes than the no-intervention con-trol, and this difference survived correction ( 𝛽 = 0.361, 95% CI 

[0.180 , 0.541 ], 𝑞 = .0008). For lifestyle intentions, the primary cor-rection retained lower intended water use and greater refillable-product intention in the Beluga whale condition and lower intended energy use and greater refillable-product intention in the Greta Thunberg condition; only the Beluga whale refillable-product con-trast survived the stricter 25-test sensitivity correction ( 𝛽 = .668, 

𝑞 = .0216). The Emma Watson condition increased the importance assigned to environmental product attributes under the primary correction ( 𝛽 = .521, 𝑞 = .0249), but not under the stricter sensitivity correction. No differences in denial, psychological distance, policy support, donation, or sharing were statistically significant after correction. A large multinational intervention tournament likewise found that effects differed across climate-related outcomes, with gains in beliefs or sharing not guaranteeing gains in an effortful behavioral task [76]. The Beluga whale condition’s Wave 2 pre-booster behavioral difference ( 𝛽 = .657, 𝑞 = .0176), alongside its Wave 1 GREEN differ-ence, is compatible with a remembered cue for a specific purchasing decision. It does not establish that behavior changed independently of attitudes or that attitudes returned to baseline; indeed, no Wave 2 pre-booster GREEN contrast survived correction (all 𝑞 ≥ .2051). More sustained engagement may be necessary to change broader beliefs, but intervention duration was not experimentally varied, and longer exposure cannot be assumed to produce such changes. Future studies should target specific beliefs, measure their pro-posed mechanisms, and test whether effects generalize beyond the product choices practiced in the intervention. Prior Sustainable HCI work has similarly emphasized that per-suasion strategies encode different assumptions about how and why people change their behavior [44]. These findings offer two bounded implications for designing per-suasive conversational agents. First, representational and interface choices may be most consequential when an intervention is tied to a concrete decision that users can make in the moment; all four embodied avatars had an effect on immediate sustainable product selection, but not consistently for broader climate beliefs. Second, an affected nonhuman character may help connect an abstract envi-ronmental externality to an actionable product attribute, although this mechanism requires direct experimental testing. Designers should therefore evaluate persuasive agents against the specific decisions they are intended to support rather than assuming that engagement, positive sentiment, or generalized attitude change will serve as adequate proxies for behavioral effectiveness. 

## 5.5 Ethical implications and limitations 

The capacity to influence purchasing decisions also creates op-portunities for commercial or malicious persuasion. Earlier re-search demonstrated that virtual-agent mimicry can increase per-suasion without users detecting it [6]. Consistent with persuasive-technology ethics [8], commercial deployments should disclose sponsorship and persuasive objectives, obtain meaningful consent to personalized persuasion, and provide an accessible way to de-cline. Humanlike or celebrity-based avatars should disclose their synthetic identity and avoid implying personal endorsement. The present study examined academic research interactions; it did not evaluate commercial deployment or the effectiveness of these safe-guards. Furthermore, the primary product-choice outcome was an unin-centivized, repeated screen-based task administered shortly after a conversation focused on packaging and sustainable consump-tion. The no-intervention control also increased by 0.568 choices, indicating measurement reactivity, practice, or increased familiar-ity with the decision rule across administrations. The randomized condition estimates account for this shared increase, and all four

embodied avatar conditions exceeded both the no-intervention con-trol and the Text Chatbot, so general task repetition alone does not explain the between-condition pattern. Nevertheless, stronger condition-specific salience, demand, or rehearsal following spo-ken avatar interaction remains plausible. The findings therefore establish greater sustainable selection within this task, not verified changes in purchasing outside the study. Lastly, the product-choice task deliberately prioritized compa-rability by using price-matched alternatives from the same four familiar household and grocery categories across assessments. This allowed changes to be evaluated against a consistent decision struc-ture without introducing differences caused by entirely different product domains. At the same time, this controlled structure leaves open how the findings generalize to products for which preferences are more heterogeneous or identity-linked. 

## 5.6 Computational Resource Use and Environmental Considerations 

Following prior work that treats computational sustainability as a design consideration for AI-based environmental interventions [59], we documented the resources used by our system. The archived logs contained 722 conversational sessions, comprising 481 sessions at Wave 1 and 241 at Wave 2. These sessions lasted 4,176.0 minutes in total ( 𝑀 = 5.78 minutes) and contained 9,386 agent turns and 8,697 participant turns. The visible transcripts contained approximately 704,645 to 736,345 tokens under two commonly used tokenizers. These figures exclude system prompts, repeated conversational con-text, safety instructions, and provider-side processing and therefore should not be interpreted as billing-token totals. At ElevenLabs’ public rate of $0.08 per additional call minute, the recorded conversation time corresponds to a list-price equiva-lent of approximately $334.08, excluding subscription allowances, taxes, burst pricing, and separately billed language-model use [25]. Exact Anthropic costs could not be reconstructed because provider-reported input and output token totals were not retained [5]. The participant-facing system used static visual assets and made no runtime Replicate calls. Provider-specific environmental measurements were unavail-able. For scale contextualization only, treating the 9,386 agent turns and 722 prompt-construction calls as 10,108 text-prompt equiva-lents and applying Google’s published production median yields an illustrative value of approximately 2.43 kWh, 0.303 kgCO 2e, and 2.63 L of water [27]. This is not an estimate of the complete study footprint. It does not measure the Anthropic or ElevenLabs infras-tructure and excludes speech processing, networking, participant devices, storage, asset production, and model training. 

## 6 Future Work 

Results from the Beluga whale condition motivate further study of affected nonhuman agents. It uniquely showed corrected immediate-attitude and later product-choice differences from the control, while participant feedback connected the character to empathy, environ-mental consequences, and refillable purchasing. Future experiments could vary nonhuman identity, affected-stakeholder perspective, novelty, and cuteness, while measuring empathy, guilt, moral stand-ing, and recall. This would clarify whether affected nonhuman characters make environmental consequences more salient and memorable at later decisions. The standardized, price-matched task enabled controlled compar-isons across conditions. Future studies could extend it to products shaped more strongly by aesthetics, brand, price, fit, or gendered marketing [39, 78]. Novel products, incentivized choices, simulated shopping baskets, and actual purchases could test whether the effect generalizes to decisions involving stronger preferences or personal costs. Related work with EcoEcho found that multimodal agents paired with visible action consequences increased sustain-able intentions [85]. Moreover, evidence that simulated participants can overestimate AI-persuasion effects highlights the value of the present large-scale human experiment and continued evaluation using human behavioral outcomes [24]. Finally, the significant Beluga difference approximately two months later demonstrates the value of studying effects beyond the immediate interaction. Future work could include additional follow-up periods, independently vary booster exposure, and mea-sure unaided recall before each new interaction. This would clarify how long affected nonhuman agents remain accessible as decision cues and when booster conversations are most useful. Deployment studies could also examine how disclosure, user control, and refusal affect persuasive outcomes and perceived autonomy. 

## 7 Conclusion 

These findings establish that, in this implementation, conditions us-ing the embodied avatar interface increased sustainable selection on a repeated screen-based task relative to both the text chatbot and no intervention. Within this broader effect, the Beluga condition war-rants further longitudinal study: it was the only condition to show a corrected post-intervention advantage in pro-environmental atti-tudes over the no-intervention control, while also outperforming the chatbot, and the only condition to show a corrected pre-booster sustainable product-choice advantage at Wave 2 over no interven-tion. Together, these findings position affected nonhuman agents as a promising direction for designing memorable environmen-tal interventions and open new questions about how embodied characters can shape decisions over time.
