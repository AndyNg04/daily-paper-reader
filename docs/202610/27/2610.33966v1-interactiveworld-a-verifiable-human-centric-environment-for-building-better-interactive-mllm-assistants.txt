# InteractiveWorld: A Verifiable Human-Centric Environment for Building Better Interactive MLLM Assistants 

Qinsi Wang 1,2,∗, Wei Wen 1, Chong Zhou 1, Saksham Suri 1, Zechun Liu 1, Lemeng Wu 1, Hai Helen Li 2, Yiran Chen 2, Raghuraman Krishnamoorthi 1, Qi Qian 11Meta, 2Duke University

People have long dreamed of an assistant that is always there to guide them and remind what they forget, from new dishes to lost keys. Powered by multimodal large language models (MLLMs), egocentric AI assistants on wearable devices are bringing this dream within reach: they see what the user sees and give instructions for user’s physical work when necessary. However, testing such assistance is challenging and usually requires real people. The expensive and rare real interaction data leaves a key question unanswered: Can today’s MLLMs truly help people in physical world? To answer this question, we propose InteractiveWorld , the first verifiable environment for closed-loop interaction between human and AI assistant. Unlike existing robot-centric simulators, InteractiveWorld lets the assistant guide a human agent who observes the scene, understands instructions, and acts independently. It thus realistically simulates how AI assistant collaborates with human: the assistant instructs, the human acts, and the environment executes actions and updates what they see next. Building on it, we introduce InteractiveBench , the first dynamic benchmark for egocentric AI assistants, with 120 multi-turn tasks across six categories. In each task, the assistant guides the human in real time, and every instruction shapes the outcome, which is scored by verifiable, state-based rewards. Experiments with 12 frontier MLLMs show they are still far from reliable assistants. Even the strongest model, Claude Fable 5.1, achieves at most 20% and 25% on long-horizon Guidance and Planning tasks, respectively. Overall, our work offers a verifiable platform and a rigorous evaluation set, laying a solid foundation for research on interactive MLLM assistants that truly help people in the physical world.

## 1 Introduction 

A long-standing goal of AI is to help people in the physical world. In everyday tasks, people need a companion that sees what they see, remembers what they forget, and tells them what to do next. Egocentric AI assistants aim to fill this role: running on wearable devices, they share the user’s first-person view and guide users in natural language. Unlike robots, they avoid physical control: the human acts, while the assistant focuses on perception, memory, reasoning, and communication. As MLLMs rapidly improve (Anthropic, 2026a; OpenAI, 2026b), it makes egocentric AI assistants a practical, near-term path to helping people in the physical world. However, evaluating whether assistants truly help people remains challenging. Real-world experiments are costly and hard to scale with human. Moreover, existing benchmarks are almost all static : a model watches a pre-recorded first-person video and answers questions about it (Grauman et al., 2022; Cheng et al., 2024a) or predicts when and how to intervene (Wang et al., 2023a; Zhang et al., 2025a). Whatever the model says, the video remains unchanged. On the contrary, real-world assistance is dynamic : the assistant and human interact over many turns with diverse rewards, requiring abilities that fixed videos cannot test. (1) Closed-loop adaptation. The assistant must continually adapt its guidance to the evolving scene and human responses, since its instructions directly change the human’s actions and what it sees next. (2) Compositional capabilities. The assistant must combine skills such as tracking object states, remembering events, and communicating clearly, whereas static benchmarks typically test one skill at a time. To answer this question, we propose InteractiveWorld , to the best of our knowledge the first environment for closed-loop interaction between humans and assistants. Built on Isaac Sim (NVIDIA, 2025), it contains over 8,000 objects and 50 scenes. As shown in Fig. 1 (top), interactive loop in InteractiveWorld consists of three components: (1) an

Each task requires the assistant to guide the human over many turns by combining perception, memory, reasoning, and communication. Task success is verified automatically from the final object states in the simulator. Since the assistant’s guidance directly shapes the human’s actions for the task, InteractiveBench can faithfully exam whether a model helps human. We also provide several demos in the supplementary material. 

Experiments with 12 frontier MLLMs on InteractiveBench show state-of-the-art models still have substantial room for improvement in interactive assistance. Even the strongest model, Claude Fable 5.1, succeeds on only 41.7% of the tasks, and all other models stay at or below 33.3%. In particular, on Guidance and Planning, the best models reach only 20.0% and 25.0%, respectively, indicating models still perform poorly on long-horizon interactive tasks. We further analyze model behaviors in detail and distill five key findings in Sec. 4.1. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We present InteractiveWorld , the first verifiable human-centric interaction environment, in which an assistant’s natural-language guidance drives a human agent’s actions and changes the world, enabling realistic simulation of their everyday collaboration. • We introduce InteractiveBench , the first multi-turn, closed-loop benchmark for evaluating models as egocentric AI assistants in the physical world, where assistant outputs shape task outcomes and success is measured by verifiable, state-based rewards. • Experiments reveal major gaps in current MLLMs’ dynamic interaction. Our analyses identify perception, 2memory, and communication as key bottlenecks. These findings underpin future interactive MLLM research, with InteractiveWorld providing a verifiable platform. 

## 2 InteractiveWorld 

In this section, we introduce InteractiveWorld, the first closed-loop human–assistant interaction environment providing a realistic, verifiable platform for evaluating egocentric AI assistants. 

Challenges . Existing simulators provide robust physics and rich 3D assets for embodied AI. However, they are robot-centric, focusing on low-level motion planning and direct joint control. Transitioning to egocentric AI assistants introduces a paradigm shift: the agent must guide human via natural language rather than directly manipulating hardware. Simulating interaction between human and assistant requires modeling the human as a capable collaborator with three key aspects: • Human-centric egocentric view. The assistant observes the world through the human’s first-person view, which constantly shifts with body postures and hand movements. • Reliable execution of everyday actions. Unlike robots, humans can reliably and naturally execute everyday actions (e.g., GRAB , OPEN ) given high-level intents. • Autonomous translation of guidance into actions. Humans can autonomously translate free-form guidance into executable actions based on what they see in the current scene. Thus, while existing simulators provide a foundation, building a human-centric environment requires a new human representation, reliable semantic execution, and interaction mechanism. 

Overall. To address these challenges, we propose InteractiveWorld, a human-centric interaction environment built on Isaac Sim (Fig. 2). From bottom to top, it consists of three layers: (1) The Physical Layer ensures stable, repeatable human actions that are physically consistent with the environment. (2) The Human Action Layer enables the “Human” to navigate and manipulate objects naturally and autonomously. (3) The Semantic Layer connects natural language guidance to executable human behaviors. Together, these layers make the Human an interactive agent that observes the environment, understands instructions, and executes actions independently , rather than merely a directly controlled animated character. This enables InteractiveWorld to realistically simulate collaboration between human and assistant. We introduce each module in detail below. 

2.1 Physical Layer 

The physical layer serves as a foundational 3D environment, providing realistic humans, assets, and physics engine. It supplies the basic physical states needed for high-level planning. 

Stable Human Representation. We use SMPL+H (Romero et al., 2022) to model the full body and hands, placing a camera on the head for a first-person visual perspective. To prevent physical failures like random falls, the human model is driven kinematically. This ensures that the physical layer can successfully and deterministically execute any valid pose sequence provided by the upper layer. 

Collision-Free Interaction. To prevent severe collisions between the human model and the environment, the system pre-checks navigation paths and arm reachability before execution. It automatically corrects minor collisions by smoothly adjusting the grasp position. For unsafe actions, the system rejects them and returns clear failure reasons to the upper layer. 

Rich and scalable asset support. To support diverse daily tasks, we imported over 8,000 object and more than 50 environment assets from BEHAVIOR-1K, including furniture, containers, appliances, tools, and food. Thanks to Isaac Sim’s asset import features, InteractiveWorld can load scanned models of real-world scenes and objects to simulate specific environments for future development.

Collision-Aware Auto-Navigation. Given a target object, the system first identifies a reachable standing position suitable for manipulation. It then constructs an obstacle map based on environment and plans a safe path using A* search (Hart et al., 1968). Following this path, the human executes continuous walking and turning motions, moving smoothly toward target without collisions. 

Target-Driven Adaptive Manipulation. Upon arrival, the system adapts its posture to stand, lean, or squat and plans a collision-free arm trajectory. Grasp position and hand posture depend on object shape, functional parts, and task. Predefined motion sequences for basic postures and actions let the hand wrap around a cup to lift it or grasp a handle to open a refrigerator. If the initial position offers no feasible trajectory to the object, the system replans its position. 

2.3 Semantic Layer 

The Semantic layer builds upon the Human Action layer to link natural language with behavior, enabling the Assistant to guide the Human via dialogue rather than direct simulator control. 

Unified action interface. We define 36 executable action primitives covering navigation, observation, manipulation, and daily activities. Supported actions range from basic ( [Walk] , [LookAt] , [Grab] , [Put] , [PutInside] , [Open] 

and [TurnOn] ) to complex operations ( [Pour] , [Cook] , [Cut] ). Commands specify only actions and targets (e.g., 

[Grab]<cup> or [PutInside]<pizza> <oven> ) and are translated into goals for the human action layer, which autonomously handles underlying navigation, positioning, and body motions. 

Verifiable multi-turn interaction. During interaction, the Assistant provides natural-language guidance based on the Human’s first-person view and the conversation history. The Human interprets this guidance, then selects and executes the corresponding actions. After each action, the simulator updates the environment state and renders a new first-person view, forming a continuous "observe-guide-execute" loop. By tracking actual object positions and states throughout the interaction, the system can evaluates final object relationships to automatically verify task success. By integrating these three layers, InteractiveWorld builds a comprehensive pipeline from the physical environment and human actions to natural language interaction. ## 3 InteractiveBench 

Building on InteractiveWorld, we further propose InteractiveBench. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first closed-loop interactive benchmark designed for egocentric AI assistants. 

Task Design. InteractiveBench contains 120 tasks across six categories (Tab. 1): Guidance, Planning, Accessibility, Decision, Prevention, and Finding. These cover the main everyday scenarios for egocentric AI assistance. All tasks undergo human review and meet three criteria: First, each addresses a realistic need for assistance, rather than a simple action the Human can complete unaided. Second, it requires the Assistant to use first-person information to observe, remember, reason, or plan, and to help over multiple turns. Third, outcomes must be verifiable from clearly defined environment states. Appendix C details the full task construction and review process. 

Task Evaluation. During evaluation, the target model serves as the Assistant agent and an auxiliary one acts as the Human agent. The Human agent is provided with all valid commands and can freely perform actions. However, it is instructed to follow the Assistant’s guidance rather than acting on its own intentions. Meanwhile, the Assistant agent is prompted to help the human complete the task. Detailed settings are in Appendix C. Overall, each evaluation consists: • Initial Scene. Each task defines a complete starting world. This includes the scene layout, the human’s starting position, and the locations and states of target and distractor objects. • Pre-interaction Setup (Warmup). Before formal interaction begins, the human performs predefined actions and provides necessary opening context (e.g., looking around or reading). This stage establishes the background and gives assistant visual and temporal information. • Closed-loop Interaction. After warmup, the assistant and human begin interaction. The assistant uses the egocentric view and conversation history to choose whether to stay silent, provide guidance, or end the task. The human then issues action commands and feedback based on the assistant’s guidance and their own observations. The action executor performs these commands in the environment. After each action, both sides get a new chance to respond, and keyframes sampled during the action are added to the visual input. • Termination conditions. The task ends and evaluation begins when the assistant outputs $done$. Alternatively, it force-terminates upon reaching a maximum limit of interaction. • Task Scoring. Each task has a predefined set of core states (e.g., "the pizza is in the oven" or "the fridge is closed"). When the task ends, the system checks the actual states of the environment. 4.1 Benchmark Results and Analysis 

We evaluate models on InteractiveBench through five lenses: (i) Performance : measuring the Human’s task success rate and mean completion rate (Tab. 2); (ii) Efficiency : calculating the ratio of actual human actions to those required with an oracle assistant (Fig. 3); (iii) Capability Assessment : evaluating nine capabilities (perception, memory, reasoning, and interaction) based on predefined state conditions (Tab. 3); (iv) Failure Analysis : classifying the initial error in failed tasks into six categories (Fig. 4); and (v) Complexity Impact : analyzing the correlation between success rates and task length (Fig. 6). From these evaluations (details in Appendix D), we distill five key findings. 

Finding 1: Closed-loop assistance is far from solved: even current strongest MLLM cannot yet serve as a reliable egocentric AI Assistant, helping the Human complete fewer than half of the tasks. 

As shown in Tab. 2, Claude Fable 5.1 achieves the highest average success rate at only 41.7%, with all other models at or below 33.3%. Since we verified that an Oracle Assistant achieves a 100% success rate with the same Human agent, this substantial gap indicates that current models still fail to provide reliable guidance, leaving immense room for improvement in collaborative AI. 

Finding 2: The difficulty lies in sustaining the loop: models make good one-shot decisions but break down when they must keep tracking progress and re-planning.

Finding 5: Action efficiency also remains low: current models lead the Human through many unnecessary actions, making efficiency an important dimension for improving assistance alongside task success. 

Compared to the Oracle, almost all models exhibit severe action redundancy (Fig. 3), peaking in Accessibility and Guidance (where models require 2.35 ×–3.66 × the Oracle’s cost). Furthermore, efficiency is highly task-dependent rather than a fixed model trait. For instance, Claude Fable 5.1, despite being the most successful overall, is the least efficient on Accessibility ( 6.75 ×), yet highly efficient on Prevention ( 1.20 ×). Similarly, Kimi K3 matches the Oracle on Finding ( 1.00 ×) but suffers the highest cost on Guidance ( 3.66 ×). Since unnecessary instructions directly translate to wasted human effort, optimizing interaction efficiency is as critical as maximizing success rates. 

Emergent proactive behaviors. Beyond task success and efficiency, we also observe that models exhibit proactive information-seeking behaviors that are not explicitly required by the Assistant prompt. First, when the surrounding objects are unclear, models ask the Human to walk around or look around the space to obtain a clearer view of the scene. Second, they ask the Human to flip packages or other objects to reveal labels and information that is otherwise hidden. Third, they actively ask about spatial information, such as where objects are located relative to the Human, to better ground their guidance. Instead of passively waiting for informative frames, these models actively shape what they observe through the Human. Such behaviors are an essential part of helping people in the physical world, where key information is often occluded or outside the current view. 

4.2 Ablation Study 

Furthermore, we conduct ablation studies to investigate (1) the effectiveness of inference-time memory augmentation, and (2) the impact of varying the Human agent model on task performance. 

Inference-time memory augmentation. To address state-tracking limitations, we append a hidden $Memory$ field to the Assistant’s responses, allowing it to record events for subsequent turns. This augmentation improves success rates by 5–15% on Prevention and Finding across all three models (Fig. 5). However, its effects on Decision, Guidance, and Accessibility vary across models, while Planning success drops by 5% for two models. These mixed results suggest that this simple memory augmentation alone is insufficient for reliable long-horizon assistance, motivating future work on training native memory mechanisms for dynamic interaction. See Appendix D for details. 

Effect of the Human agent. To evaluate the Human’s impact, we test all 16 Assistant–Human pairings among four diverse models, reporting MCR on Guidance, Planning, and Accessibility, long-horizon tasks reliant on instruction following (Fig. 5). An Assistant’s performance varies with its Human partner, although rankings remain relatively stable: Fable 5.1 consistently ranks first, while GPT-5.6 ranks last. Performance also differs across roles: although Gemini 3.1 ranks second or third as an Assistant, all four Assistants achieve their highest observed MCR when it acts as the Human. ## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced InteractiveWorld, a verifiable environment for closed-loop human-assistant interaction, and Interac-tiveBench, a dynamic benchmark of 120 multi-turn tasks. They provide a foundation for developing and evaluating interactive MLLMs.

## A Background & Related Work 

Egocentric AI Assistant Benchmarks. Large-scale first-person datasets such as EPIC-KITCHENS (Damen et al., 2018), Ego4D (Grauman et al., 2022), and Ego-Exo4D (Grauman et al., 2024) have laid the foundation for egocentric perception. Building on them, a growing body of benchmarks examines whether MLLMs can act as egocentric assistants: EgoSchema (Mangalam et al., 2023) and HourVideo (Chandrasegaran et al., 2024) test long-form video understanding; EgoThink (Cheng et al., 2024a) and VidEgoThink (Cheng et al., 2024b) evaluate first-person reasoning; EgoPlan-Bench (Chen et al., 2023) targets next-step planning; EgoLife (Yang et al., 2025a) and TeleEgo (Yan et al., 2025) probe long-term memory over continuously recorded daily life; and EgoBlind (Xiao et al., 2025) focuses on visual assistance for blind users. Closer to task guidance, HoloAssist (Wang et al., 2023a) records real-world instructor–performer collaborations, while ProAssist (Zhang et al., 2025a) and the ESTP task (Zhang et al., 2025b) evaluate when and what a proactive assistant should say along a streaming egocentric video. Despite their diversity, these benchmarks are 

open-loop : model outputs are scored against fixed recordings and never influence what happens next. EgoBench (Liu et al., 2026b) introduces an LLM-simulated user and tool calls, yet its visual context remains pre-recorded. User-in-the-loop studies (Verghese et al., 2025; Dasgupta et al., 2026) do close the loop with real participants, and Verghese et al. (Verghese et al., 2025) further show that offline metrics do not reflect online performance; however, such studies involve only a handful of participants and are costly to scale and hard to reproduce. InteractiveBench closes this loop in simulation: every instruction changes the human’s actions and thus the world, and success is verified automatically from final object states. 

Improving Egocentric AI Assistants. In parallel, many works improve the models and frameworks behind egocentric assistants. Early efforts learn egocentric video–language representations from narrated first-person videos (Lin et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023), and subsequent MLLMs such as MM-Ego (Ye et al., 2025), AlanaVLM (Suglia et al., 2024), and EgoGPT (Yang et al., 2025a) are tuned on large-scale egocentric instruction data. VideoLLM-online (Chen et al., 2024), Dispider (Qian et al., 2025), and proactive assistant models (Zhang et al., 2025a,b) learn to decide when to respond as the video unfolds. For long-horizon memory, LifelongMemory (Wang et al., 2023b) and EgoRAG (Yang et al., 2025a) retrieve from textualized histories, and Ego-R1 (Tian et al., 2025) trains an agent with reinforcement learning to invoke retrieval and perception tools step by step over week-long videos. At the system level, VLaMP (Patel et al., 2023) leverages pretrained language models to plan the next steps for human assistance, while Vinci (Huang et al., 2024) and Egocentric Co-Pilot (Yang et al., 2026) integrate memory, planning, and tool use into assistants deployable on portable devices. However, these methods are trained and evaluated on static data or small-scale user studies, so it remains unclear whether their gains carry over to closed-loop assistance, where the assistant must track how its own guidance changes the scene and the human. InteractiveWorld offers a verifiable testbed to answer this question, and its state-based rewards can further support training interactive assistants. 

Robot-Centric Embodied Simulators. Simulators have long provided scalable and reproducible testbeds for embodied AI. AI2-THOR (Kolve et al., 2017), Habitat (Savva et al., 2019; Szot et al., 2022), iGibson (Li et al., 2021), and BEHAVIOR-1K (Li et al., 2022) offer interactive household scenes for navigation, rearrangement, and everyday activities; ManiSkill3 (Tao et al., 2025) and Isaac Lab (Mittal et al., 2025) provide GPU-parallel physics and rendering for large-scale robot learning; and RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024) and GRUtopia (Wang et al., 2024) scale up scene diversity from kitchens to entire cities. On top of these platforms, benchmarks such as ALFRED (Shridhar et al., 2020) and EmbodiedBench (Yang et al., 2025b) evaluate language-conditioned agents and MLLMs by having them directly command a robot. In these settings, the evaluated model is the actor : it outputs actions that the simulator executes on a robot body, while humans are either absent or merely part of the scene. HumanCLAW (Li et al., 2026) replaces the robot with a physically simulated human body and decouples decision making from motor control, yet the VLM still decides every move of that body. In contrast, the assistant in InteractiveWorld never acts on the world directly; it can influence the outcome only through natural-language guidance that an autonomous human must interpret and carry out, mirroring how assistants help people in practice. 

Interaction-Oriented Simulators. Another line of work brings humans or dialogue into simulation. In dialog-based 16 embodied tasks such as CVDN (Thomason et al., 2019), TEACh (Padmakumar et al., 2022), and DialFRED (Gao et al., 2022), an instruction giver guides a follower through natural language; however, models are typically evaluated as the follower, with guidance replayed from recorded human dialogues or produced by a rule-based oracle. Human–AI collaboration platforms instead simulate human-like agents alongside AI helpers: VirtualHome (Puig et al., 2018) and Watch-And-Help (Puig et al., 2022) model household activities of humanoid avatars; Habitat 3.0 (Puig et al., 2023) and PARTNR (Chang et al., 2025) study human–robot coordination with simulated humanoids and real humans in the loop; and CoELA (Zhang et al., 2023), Virtual Community (Zhou et al., 2025), and HRIBench (Liu et al., 2026a) extend this to LLM-driven cooperation, open-world societies, and role-conditioned interaction. FreeAskWorld (Peng et al., 2026) further employs LLM-driven avatars that give directions to a navigating agent. Across these platforms, the AI remains the physical actor, while humans serve as scripted partners, co-workers, or sources of information. InteractiveWorld reverses these roles: the AI is a disembodied assistant that shares the human’s view, and an autonomous MLLM-driven human decides how to act on its guidance, enabling closed-loop, verifiable evaluation of assistants that help people rather than act for them. 

## B Implementation Details of InteractiveWorld 

As mentioned in the main text, the simulator comprises three layers: a physical layer that maintains a geometrically consistent human, scene, and set of rigid objects (§B.1); a human-action layer that converts semantic goals into locomotion and manipulation trajectories (§B.2); and a semantic layer that exposes these capabilities through a compact command language and a verifiable multi-turn interaction loop (§B.3). 

B.1 Implementation Details of Physical Layer  

> B.1.1 Stable Human Representation

The camera wearer is a fixed-shape SMPL+H model: the male model with zero shape coefficients ( β = 0), a 52-joint kinematic tree, 6,890 vertices, and 13,776 triangles. Each frame is parameterized by a 156-D axis–angle vector 

q = [ ωroot , θbody , θleft , θright ] ∈ R3+63+45+45 , (1) comprising the root orientation, 21 body joints (torso, head, arms, legs), and 15 articulated joints per hand; root translation is stored separately in world coordinates. A fixed 90 ◦ rotation about x maps the y-up model to the z-up world. Standard SMPL+H forward kinematics and linear blend skinning yield joint transforms and deformed vertices. The body mesh is authored once at /World/Body ; each frame updates only vertex positions and normals, so topology, material binding, and camera attachment remain fixed. Locomotion reuses body motion from ACCAD/AMASS SMPL-X clips: shared body rotations are copied, incompatible shape parameters are discarded, and the hand channels either keep a task-specific pose or are overwritten by the manipulation controller. The importer detects the source up axis, canonicalizes the initial heading, and estimates the gait period by autocorrelation. Gait phase advances with distance traveled rather than wall-clock time, so slowing, turning in place, or pausing to replan causes no foot sliding; the lowest foot is continuously aligned with the estimated support floor. Manipulation poses are layered onto the same skeleton, avoiding a model switch between walking, reaching, and object interaction. The human is deliberately kinematic rather than a ragdoll, which makes the camera trajectory reproducible and prevents falling or numerical drift under contact. Collisions are nevertheless enforced: every desired pose is screened against an collision representation before it is committed.  

> B.1.2 Collision-Free Interaction

Proxy geometry. Rendering uses the full SMPL+H surface, whereas collision queries use a compact proxy rig of 17 capsules (pelvis, spine, neck/collars, upper and lower arms, hands, thighs, shins, feet) plus a head sphere. Capsule endpoints are regenerated from the current joints and radii are derived from the corresponding body segments; hand capsules extend from the wrist toward the palm. Contacts between adjacent segments are filtered, while contacts with the scene, manipulated objects, and non-adjacent body parts remain active. 17 Shared collision world. A single collision world serves scene initialization, navigation, stance selection, reaching, and post-action validation. It enumerates collision-enabled scene geometry, follows USD instance proxies, preserves each mesh’s vertical extent, and groups multi-mesh articulated links under one owning object. Proposed poses are checked without one-frame lag by sampling spheres along each capsule at the requested pose rather than the last rendered one. Candidate stances are tested both in the idle configuration and with the relevant arm extended; the intended contact region around the target (nominal radius 0.22 m ) may be excluded from the reach test, but the rest of the target and the scene remain obstacles. On collision, bisection estimates the penetration and the controller tries another stance, posture, or approach. Manipulation trajectories use a stricter acceptance tolerance of ≈2 mm .

Carried objects. The held object’s collision geometry is propagated to every sampled wrist pose and tested against the scene and all non-held objects; only the intended hand–object grasp contact and the commanded target contact are allow-listed. An arm-safe trajectory is therefore still rejected if the carried object would strike a shelf, container rim, or neighboring object. Placement candidates are checked against non-target collision owners before release, and a post-settle overlap check catches contacts introduced by rigid-body dynamics. 

Object physics. Static architectural and furniture assets use their authored collision meshes when available. Movable BEHAVIOR objects use the asset’s authored convex pieces or, failing that, a convex decomposition of the concave visual mesh rather than a single concave dynamic collider. Initial packing enforces a nominal 2 cm separation; objects are then settled under gravity ( 9.81 m s −2, 60 physics steps/s) until their speed falls below 10 −3 m s −1. A grasped object becomes kinematic and follows the wrist through a fixed relative transform. On release it becomes dynamic, is settled and, if necessary, minimally depenetrated, and its resulting transform is written back to the semantic state. Contents of a carried container follow via their stored relative transforms. 

Scope of the guarantee. “Collision-free” denotes an operational guarantee: every planned route and manipulation is screened against the same geometry and rejected or repaired if it exceeds the configured contact tolerance. It does not claim exact separation between continuous triangle surfaces. In the egocentric evaluation configuration, small body-only contacts may be retained as diagnostic telemetry provided the camera/eye volume stays clear, whereas an object penetration above 5 cm fails the final placement gate. This prevents harmless skin-level numerical contacts from destabilizing behavior while still rejecting visually or physically consequential interpenetration.  

> B.1.3 Rich and Scalable Asset Support

We use BEHAVIOR-1K v3.9.0, which contains 1,829 object categories, 8,635 model directories, and 51 scene layouts. The layouts place 13,516 object instances covering 3,707 distinct category/model pairs. The generic importer omits purely decorative or non-interactive classes (e.g., ceilings, pictures, mirrors, windows, curtains, lighting fixtures), leaving 11,428 instances and 3,189 distinct models, all present in the deployed cache. At the time of this audit, the cache holds 6,831 plain-USD models spanning all 1,829 categories. Of the 51 layouts, 44 pass generic task admission (successful bake, usable floors and walls, and at least one work surface or container). BEHAVIOR’s 996 material/particle systems are catalogued separately and are not counted as manipulable rigid objects in our action interface. The layouts span domestic, office, retail, educational, hospitality, and leisure environments, e.g., Beechwood_0_int ,

Ihlen_0_int , house_single_floor , office_cubicles_right , restaurant_diner , school_chemistry , grocery_-store_cafe , and hotel_gym_spa . Manipulable objects include apples, soda cans, bananas, bars of soap, tennis balls, bell peppers, mugs, bowls, cabinet doors, and refrigerator contents. Asset preparation is fully automated. An offline baker decrypts the distributed USD when necessary, re-roots PBR texture paths, removes dataset-specific physics scenes and helper rigid bodies, and extracts articulated-joint metadata. It emits a plain USD and a compact specification (category/model identifiers, bounding box, base offset, canonical orientation, mass or density, openable links, semantic tags, and joints). At load time, the scene importer references this USD, applies the scene-JSON translation, scale, and quaternion (converted from xyzw to wxyz ), aligns the asset base with the floor, registers its semantic roles, and installs its collision geometry. > B.2.1 Collision-Aware Autonomous Navigation

Stance selection. Navigation targets an interaction-ready stance rather than the object’s centroid. Target grounding estimates a task-relevant contact point and samples floor-supported standing poses around it, each oriented toward that point and evaluated for (i) connectivity to the current pose, (ii) camera and full-body clearance, (iii) arm reachability, and (iv) collisions in both the idle and the anticipated arrival pose. The closest reachable valid stance is preferred. A blocked nominal stance can be snapped to a valid point within 0.60 m ; the last 4–6 frames then execute a short (≈0.10 m ) approach to establish the final interaction distance. 

Occupancy grid. Routes are planned on a 0.10 m ground grid rasterized from the same collision geometry used by manipulation, with separate layers for walls, furniture, door leaves, and floor support. Obstacles are included only where they intersect the human’s traversed height band, i.e., 0.20 –1.90 m above the local floor for walls and 0.05 –1.90 m 

for furniture; cells without floor support are blocked. A Euclidean distance transform supplies the clearance D(n) of each free cell. To avoid wrongly declaring narrow rooms disconnected, the grid is inflated by the largest radius in 

{0.20 , 0.15 , 0.10 } m that still preserves a connected, reachable solution. 

Search. We run eight-connected A* with diagonal corner cutting disabled and transition cost 

c(n, n ′) = r ℓ8(n, n ′) + λ max  0, d safe − D(n′) + cdoor (n′) , (2) where r = 0 .10 m is the grid resolution, ℓ8 ∈ { 1, √2} the step length, λ = 8 , and dsafe = 0 .35 m . The clearance term biases paths toward open space without turning narrow passages into hard obstacles. For a closed door, the planner identifies the blocking leaf, routes to a collision-validated handle stance, executes the door-opening action, updates occupancy, and replans; if no such sequence exists, the action returns PATH_BLOCKED rather than teleporting the human. String-pulling simplifies the polyline only when a shortcut is collision-free and does not increase the weighted cost. 

Execution and 3-D validation. The route is converted to an ACCAD gait rendered at 12 fps with a nominal speed of 1.1 m s −1. A heading controller uses a 1.2 m look-ahead, limits yaw change to 10 ◦ per frame, and blends toward the final object-facing orientation over the last 1.5 m . Translation may pause during large turns, keeping the offset between body heading and travel direction below ≈35 ◦. Finally, the articulated body, arm, and egocentric eye volumes are checked along the generated 3-D trajectory. If a cell that appeared free in 2-D fails this check, a temporary 0.20 m 

avoidance disk is inserted and the route is replanned; a bounded repair budget prevents infinite retries and yields a structured failure when no safe route exists.  

> B.2.2 Target-Driven Adaptive Manipulation

Manipulation is synthesized from a small set of reusable pose prototypes, target-relative end-effector curves, and online inverse kinematics (IK), rather than from a stored motion clip per object instance. 

Contact and grasp selection. The target’s oriented bounding box determines the contact region: broad, flat objects are approached near a reachable perimeter edge, and volumetric objects at a body- or face-centered target. Geometry also selects the grasp family: PINCH for small or flat objects, CYLINDRICAL when height exceeds ≈1.15 × the cross-sectional diameter, and ROUND otherwise. This estimate is considered jointly with stance selection, so navigation never commits to a stance from which the chosen contact is unreachable. 

Body posture and arm IK. ## C Implementation Details of InteractiveBench 

InteractiveBench evaluates an egocentric assistant in closed loop rather than as a static video-question-answering model. In each episode, the evaluated Assistant receives chronologically ordered first-person-view observations and natural-language speech; and a separate Human executor converts the Assistant’s utterances into environment actions. The benchmark comprises 120 frozen episodes in six equally weighted reported families of 20 tasks each. The unit of evaluation is a versioned TaskPack , which fixes the scene, entities, opening event history, public utterances, executable action programs, score contract, and provenance hashes. 

C.1 Benchmark Construction 

Construction. We construct each InteractiveBench task from a task family and a target capability. We first propose a causal scenario with a concrete goal or error to avoid, a role for the assistant, and actions the Human can perform. Two independent reviews check whether the proposal fits the family, requires assistance, can be enacted in a physical scene, and has a measurable outcome without help. Proposals that fail these checks are revised within a fixed budget or discarded. Before choosing a scene, we define an assessment contract. It records the question being tested, the evidence the assistant must use, when help is needed, the information gap, the success condition, and the expected outcome without help. This contract remains fixed throughout construction. We then bind the scenario to an available scene, objects, and actions, checking that the chosen assets exist and preserve the intended test. Next, we author the initial world state, the events before the Human’s request, the request itself, an oracle action sequence, a no-help sequence, and state-based goal checks. These elements form an executable task specification. We run the event history and both action sequences through deterministic checks. They test action syntax, object affordances, hand occupancy, geometry, goal states, and whether the task is already complete or its answer is exposed too early. For repairable errors, we revise the scene binding, world state, and task script together, then rerun the checks. If a valid repair would change the assessment contract, we discard the candidate. A final, non-editing review checks that the task still requires the intended evidence, timing, and assistance. Accepted specifications are compiled into TaskPacks and replayed in Isaac Sim. We execute the oracle, score the resulting physical state, and inspect the rendered views for visual errors. We also test the interaction with scripted and model-based Humans, together with no-help, incorrect-help, and early-help controls. Failures caused by task content lead to a new task revision and another validation pass; simulator or infrastructure failures are fixed and retried without changing the task. Finally, we select 120 validated tasks, with 20 in each of the six families. We freeze the TaskPacks, code, prompts, scoring rules, and per-task revision records for reproducibility.  

> C.1.1 Design objective and candidate generation

Design objective. Construction targets interactive episodes rather than scripted renders. In a valid episode, (i) task-relevant evidence is genuinely visible from the wearer camera; (ii) this evidence can later leave the field of view or be occluded by intervening activity; (iii) the wearer asks for help, or proceeds toward a mistake, at a causally appropriate time; (iv) the Assistant can change the outcome through natural-language intervention; and (v) correct and incorrect assistance leave different verifiable world states. Requirement (v) excludes tasks whose success exists only in prose. 

Candidate generation. Stories were generated per family. Up to six family workers ran in parallel, but generation within a family was serial, so each new proposal saw digests of previously accepted and rejected same-family proposals. This “avoid-from” context, containing both prior stories and rejection reasons, discouraged paraphrased duplicates and repeated design failures. The frozen authoring campaign used gemini3.1pro as author and gpt5.5 as judge, with random seed 11, deterministic decoding (temperature 0), two independent review passes, and up to eight author revisions after rejection. > C.1.2 Interaction assembly and release freeze

Interaction TaskPack. Each physical TaskPack is converted into a derived interaction TaskPack without changing its parent physical state. The derived layer adds a natural prelude_say , deterministic opening actions, a handoff utterance, frame selection, public and Human-only task contexts, spoken-cue contracts, WATCH intervention windows, and completion requirements. Pre-sweep controls. Before entering a model sweep, each task’s closed-loop assembly was challenged in a fixed order: (1) an oracle-verbal Assistant with a scripted Human checked wiring and action anchors; (2) the same oracle with the model Human measured the executor ceiling; (3) silent, deliberately wrong, and deliberately early responders checked that the Human cannot solve the task independently and that failures are observable; and (4) a strong-model canary exercised free dialogue. The resulting audit separates eight layers—infrastructure, available evidence, Assistant decision, Human grounding, process/transition, world state, completion, and visual quality—so that a physically successful oracle video is evidence of feasibility, never a model score. 

C.2 Evaluation Protocol  

> C.2.1 Two-agent closed loop

Each evaluation cell pairs one evaluated Assistant with a fixed Human executor and one simulator instance. In the documented configuration the Human model is Gemini-3.1-pro , held constant across compared Assistant models. An episode proceeds as follows. 1. Load. Load the exact TaskPack and verify its digest, family-specific interaction specification, scene assets, model route, frame policy, and fresh-process configuration. 2. Opening. Execute the deterministic opening. Each successful setup command yields a chronological FPV interval; setup failure invalidates the cell. Opening actions are unscored. The score baseline is captured after the opening, so a temporarily satisfied predicate cannot leak free credit. 3. Assistant turn. At interaction tick t, send the Assistant the newly selected FPV images, any new wearer speech, and the literal marker “Latest segment.” The first turn also carries the common system prompt and family 25 addendum. No private symbolic state is included. 4. Parse. Parse exactly one Assistant control response. Speech is passed to the Human. The response $silent$ 

yields no speech, and $done$ freezes the world for scoring; the freeze is deferred only while an already accepted Human action sequence is draining. 5. Human turn. Build a stateless Human turn from the public task context, current motor-grounding legend, held object, last action result, new Assistant speech, and any still-active instruction. WATCH additionally supplies the Human’s private next no-advice action, which is never visible to the Assistant. The Human returns tagged reasoning, zero or more requested commands, and optional speech. The harness executes at most one command per simulator tick and polls the Assistant again after the resulting visual segment. 6. Log. Record the proposed and executed command, success/refusal receipt, frames and their provenance, structured visibility, held state, semantic state transitions, both agents’ raw and parsed outputs, and latency. A rejected action is observable and must be recovered in a later turn; it is never silently converted into success. 7. Terminate. Continue until the Assistant declares done or the authored action/tick budget is exhausted. Goal satisfaction never auto-terminates the episode; otherwise a model could enumerate alternatives and be scored at the first lucky state. The final state and complete trace are then evaluated offline. 

Frame selection. The FPV buffer preserves causal temporal structure. Each simulator command forms one interval, uniformly sampled to a configured number of frames while always retaining the true result frame; a LookAt interval additionally pins a look_hold frame so that the readable cue is not replaced by the release frame. The authored opening history is pinned or budgeted as specified by the TaskPack; later turns normally contribute only the latest interval. Stateless backends receive a bounded temporal selection that keeps the two most recent visual turns intact and samples older history uniformly, capped at 32 images in the documented configuration. All families supply chronological FPV evidence to the Assistant; NARRATE supplies no images to the Human.  

> C.2.2 Information boundaries and Human restrictions

Information is partitioned by role. The Assistant sees FPV images, wearer speech, its own dialogue history (or an explicitly declared memory treatment), and the public task request. It never sees simulator identifiers, the motor legend, the answer key, verification points, score, hidden completion state, oracle actions, or the Human’s private WATCH plan. The Human sees only the information needed to ground an Assistant instruction to an available action handle, and is explicitly forbidden to infer the answer, select a destination from the task semantics, or repair the Assistant’s plan. The following family-specific restrictions are central to causal attribution. • ASK: the Human cannot answer, count, read, calculate, or choose; only new Assistant speech may authorize one exact action. An unseen movable object requires an explicitly named search source; otherwise the Human asks for a visually grounded target and action. • FIND: the Human cannot infer a remembered location or choose a search order. A movable target handle is exposed only while visible or held, so valid retrieval requires Assistant-directed search and visible-before-Grab grounding; this prevents semantic-ID auto-navigation from turning memory failure into physical success. • GUIDE: the Human has no independent procedure. It executes the first currently actionable semantic step for one object, continues only its local motor consequences, and asks for the next step when idle. • WATCH: without a motor instruction, the Human advances exactly one command along the private no-advice plan. A new explicit Assistant instruction preempts it; acknowledgements do not. Authored wearer speech is emitted at its fixed action cursor and never authorizes a motor act. • NARRATE: the Human receives no images and cannot invent visual, audio, tactile, or other evidence. Private handles encode only an object or destination already identified by the Assistant, and at most one immediate action is returned. • ORCHESTRATE: the Human knows the requested outcome but may not create, optimize, reorder, or repair the plan. > C.2.5 Validity and causal attribution

Validity is audited before task scoring. A valid cell requires a successful scene/setup, the intended TaskPack hash and model route, a complete interaction trace, usable frames, and functioning Assistant, Human, transport, and simulator components. > C.3.3 Efficiency and reproducibility

Efficiency. Efficiency is computed only on strict-success episodes, against a task-matched shortest legal oracle. Each Human macro action (Walk, Look, Open, Grab, Put, etc.) counts as one step, including refused actions, which consume an interaction; speech, frames, the deterministic opening, and post-completion cleanup do not count. The stable-completion index is the earliest action after which all strict core conditions hold and remain true. Ratios are paired per task before aggregation; dividing a model’s mean on one success set by an oracle mean on another is invalid. Efficiency is reported alongside, never folded into, MCR or Pass Rate. 

Reproducibility. For each model–task cell, the released manifest preserves the fields source_family and assigned_ family , the expected and observed TaskPack hashes, the hash origin, the rubric/version, the completion protocol, the evaluation status, and the evidence path. These fields ensure that a reclassification cannot silently change the scorer and that a run-level observed hash is not conflated with a frozen-rubric fallback hash. All aggregate tables are recomputable from the per-task and per-atom ledgers.

## D Evaluation Setting Details and More Experiment Results 

D.1 Experiment setting detail of error type analysis. 

We developed the error taxonomy from the model results rather than defining it in advance. Each task family had its own success criteria. These criteria checked the final environment state and, when needed, the required action order or intervention time. For every valid failed episode, we reviewed the full conversation, visual frames, action results, and final state. We then found the earliest mistake that meaningfully caused the failure. Runs caused mainly by missing outputs, backend errors, or simulator problems were not treated as model errors. We first wrote short descriptions of the repeated failure patterns and then merged similar patterns. This produced six main categories. R1 means that the model misreads a visible object or location. R2 means that it loses track of what happened or ignores an action result. R3 means that it applies the wrong rule or misses an important consequence. R4 means that it creates or updates the wrong multi-step plan. R5 means that it gives an instruction that cannot be executed in the current situation. R6 means that it speaks at the wrong time or fails to speak when help is needed. Each failed episode received one primary error label so that it was counted only once in the distribution plots. Other problems could be recorded as secondary notes, but they did not change the primary count. Ambiguous cases were checked again using the original screenshots, model outputs, action feedback, and evaluator results. Therefore, the pie charts show the percentage of model-attributable failures assigned to each error type, rather than the percentage of all benchmark episodes. 

D.2 Experiment setting detail of Memory Augmentation. 

We added a simple long-term memory to the assistant. At every step, the assistant produced both its next action and a new memory entry. The new entry was added to all earlier entries, rather than replacing them. The full memory was then included in the input for the next step. The prompt asked the assistant to make each memory as detailed as possible. It was told to record useful facts such as object names, appearance, locations, states, spatial relations, visible text, and OCR results. It could also record earlier actions and their outcomes. This helped the assistant keep information that might be needed later. We kept the visual input exactly the same as in the original setting. The assistant received at most 32 images at each step. When more than 32 frames are available, we retain the 8 most recent frames and uniformly sample 24 from the older history. Therefore, memory was the only change between the original and memory.

When a pending item is resolved or an object state changes, add an explicit resolution or correction. The newest evidence wins. Keep the location of an event separate from the object’s current location. One action proves only its own result. Walking proves a location change, and grabbing proves possession. Neither proves placement, closing, turning something off, or task completion. A stated plan or destination also does not prove that the action happened. Prefer evidence in this order: a successful action result or direct post-action view; a current explicit statement from the person; older visual evidence or memory; and inference. Mark conflicts and uncertainty instead of treating weak evidence as certain. Before returning $done$, check every PENDING item against later evidence. Every required physical change must have a successful action result or direct post-action evidence. Confirm that no task object remains held unless the request requires it. Restore any door, lid, or other object moved for access unless the request requires a different final state. If any required placement, closing, turn-off, release, or restoration is not confirmed, keep it PENDING and do not return $done$. Do not put control tokens, hidden reasoning, simulator ids, answer keys, or instructions to the person inside memory. The complete accumulated memory ledger is provided again on the next turn and is never truncated by the system. 

D.3 Additional Experiments about Comparative of Dynamic vs. Static 

We made a static version of the same 120 tasks used in the interactive benchmark. The final benchmark has six task families: Guidance, Planning, Accessibility, Decision, Prevention, and Finding. Each family has 20 tasks. We used the same scenes and recorded first-person episodes as the interactive benchmark, so we did not create easier scenes for the 35 static test. We stopped each episode just before the assistant had to answer or make a decision. For a Prevention task, we stopped after the warning cue appeared but before the assistant could intervene. The static model saw a fixed sequence of images instead of a live environment. Each image sequence was paired with one short question. The question kept the main goal of the original dynamic task. A Finding item asked where an object was. A Guidance or Accessibility item asked for the next useful instruction. A Decision item asked for the answer or choice needed at that point. A Prevention item asked whether the user should be stopped and what was wrong. A Planning item asked for the full plan. We wrote the reference answer from the original task definition and the correct reference run, but only used facts available before the cut. The model did not see the reference answer, scoring rule, future frames, action results, or later user feedback. It also had no chance to act, check the result, or recover from an error. We evaluated eight models once on every static item. A frozen semantic judge compared each response with the reference answer. Exact wording was not needed; the answer passed when it gave the same key fact, location, instruction, warning, or plan without a harmful contradiction.

D.4 Additional Experiment of Task Horizon and Model Performance 

In this experiment, we asked a simple question: do models succeed less often when a task requires a longer sequence of actions? We used 120 tasks from six task families. We evaluated eight models on each task. We measured task length using a reference solution, rather than the number of actions taken by the model. This avoids a major bias: a failed model may repeat actions or run until the step limit, while a successful model may stop early. Let Bt be the best available reference action count for task t. For visualization, we fitted a separate Firth logistic regression for every model and task family. The colored curves in Figure 9 show the fitted success probability of each model. The red curve is the pooled fit across models, and the gray band is its pointwise 95% task-bootstrap interval. The curves were not forced to decrease. For the main statistical result, we first averaged the eight binary model outcomes for each task and then fitted a task-level linear probability model. The overall model included task-family fixed effects. During bootstrap and permutation tests, all eight outcomes from the same task were kept together. The family-adjusted slope was −0.431 , corresponding to a 43 .1-percentage-point decrease from the shortest to the longest normalized horizon ( 95% bootstrap CI: −58 .1 to −28 .9; one-sided permutation p < 0.0001 ). All six family-level point estimates were negative, and 45 of the 48 model–family curves also decreased. However, not every family-level test was individually significant. These results show an association between longer tasks and lower complete-task success, but they do not prove that task length directly causes failure. Longer tasks may also require more search, more object interactions, more state changes, or stricter safety constraints. Some labels were reconstructed from saved traces, so we treat this result as provisional. 

D.5 Additional Human-Agent Validation with Real Human Participants 

Purpose and setup. We examine whether replacing a model-based Human agent with a human participant changes the evaluation of an Assistant. We compare four model-based executors—Fable 5.1, Muse 1.1, GPT-5.6, and Gemini 3.1—with three human participants in InteractiveWorld. The comparison covers the same 36 tasks, with six tasks from each of the six benchmark families. Each executor is paired with each of four Assistants. The human participants take the executor role in the simulator; this is not a physical-world deployment study. Implications and scope. Together, these results provide preliminary support for prompted Human agents as a practical proxy for human instruction following in InteractiveWorld. Both the broad Assistant comparisons and the detailed Fable 5.1 outcomes show correspondence with evaluations involving real participants. This addresses whether the benchmark results depend entirely on a particular simulated executor. 

## E Future Work 

During this study, we identified several interesting directions to explore next. (1) Since InteractiveWorld provides verifiable, state-based rewards, we will build a reinforcement learning platform on it to train interactive abilities efficiently. (2) We will make the Human agent behave more like real people, through training or other means, and simulate users with different personalities and skill levels. (3) As InteractiveWorld supports scanned real-world scenes, we will bring in more real environments and situations where people need an assistant, making the tasks more realistic. 39
