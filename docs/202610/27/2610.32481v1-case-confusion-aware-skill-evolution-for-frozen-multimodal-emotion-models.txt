Abstract 

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have substan-tially advanced Multimodal Emotion Recognition (MER) by enabling fine-grained emotion understanding through reason-ing over multimodal evidence. However, they still exhibit sys-tematic confusion between semantically adjacent emotions with highly overlapping multimodal cues. Existing approaches typically address these errors through global parameter adap-tation, making confusion correction costly and inflexible. We instead observe that emotion confusion is highly localized and therefore better addressed through reusable pair-specific expertise. Building on this insight, we propose Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution (CASE), the first agent-skill framework for MER. CASE maintains a sparse, self-evolving library of pair-specific EmotionSkills that externalize affective exper-tise from model parameters, enabling the learned expertise to be continuously refined and readily transferred across di-verse frozen MLLM backbones. During inference, CASE dy-namically retrieves the appropriate EmotionSkill to reinforce boundary-aware reasoning while keeping the MLLM back-bone frozen. Extensive experiments on CA-MER, DFEW, and EMER demonstrate the effectiveness and transferability of CASE across both general-purpose and emotion-specialized MLLMs. Notably, CASE improves the average accuracy of Qwen3-VL-8B on the CA-MER benchmark from 46.80% to 59.13%, while achieving consistent gains across MLLMs. 

# Introduction 

Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown impressive performance on video-based Multimodal Emotion Recognition (MER). Beyond predicting emotion labels directly, MLLMs can integrate visual, temporal, and contextual evidence to provide in-terpretable explanations for their decisions, making them a promising foundation for grounded affective comput-ing (Lian et al. 2023; Cheng et al. 2024). Despite this progress, fine-grained emotion recognition remains a fundamental challenge. Although current MLLMs often identify the relevant affective evidence, they still strug-gle to distinguish semantically similar emotions, such as 

worry versus sadness or fear versus surprise (Lian et al. 2025a; Hu et al. 2025). This limitation suggests that the bot-

More importantly, these misclassifications are not random prediction failures but exhibit a systematic structure. In psy-chological theories, emotions are characterized as a contin-uous affective space rather than isolated categories, where neighboring emotions naturally share semantic and behav-ioral characteristics (Barrett et al. 2025; Plutchik 2001). The psychological structure is also reflected in MER, where pre-diction errors consistently concentrate on neighboring emo-tion pairs rather than being uniformly distributed across the label space. The observed structured confusion pattern sug-gests that improving fine-grained MER requires more than stronger general reasoning; it calls for mechanisms that ex-plicitly reinforce discriminative boundaries between system-arXiv:2610.32481v1 [cs.AI] 23 Oct 2026 atically confused emotion pairs. Existing approaches, how-ever, primarily rely on global parameter adaptation or task-specific fine-tuning, implicitly treating all emotion categories equally. As a result, they overlook the localized nature of emotion confusion and lack an effective mechanism for ac-cumulating and reusing pair-specific affective expertise. Motivated by the systematic nature of emotion confusion, we propose Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution (CASE) , a general agent-skill framework that models fine-grained emo-tion recognition as a collection of pair-specific discrimina-tion problems rather than a globally shared reasoning task. As shown in Figure 1, CASE maintains a sparse, self-evolving li-brary of EmotionSkills. Each EmotionSkill encapsulates the discriminative knowledge required to resolve one systemati-cally confused adjacent emotion pair. Specifically, CASE lo-calizes ambiguous predictions to candidate confusion pairs, evolves confusion-specific skills based on recoverable errors, and selectively expands the skill library only when recur-rent confusion patterns emerge. During inference, the most relevant EmotionSkill is dynamically retrieved to reinforce boundary-aware reasoning while keeping the MLLM back-bone frozen. In this way, CASE externalizes affective exper-tise into modular knowledge that evolves independently of model parameters and can be readily reused across diverse frozen MLLM backbones. More broadly, CASE shifts affective recognition from fixed label classification toward reusable knowledge-based reasoning, enabling frozen MLLMs to flexibly support evolv-ing emotion taxonomies without continual model adaptation. Our contributions can be summarized as: 

• We introduce CASE, which reframes MER from global multiclass classification toward selective discrimination on ambiguous fine-grained emotion boundaries. 

• We develop a recoverability-aware skill evolution mech-anism that learns from boundary-specific affective con-fusion. By contrasting recoverable failures with valid an-chors and verifying candidate skill updates, CASE en-ables reliable, sparse skill evolution atop a frozen multi-modal backbone. 

• We evaluate CASE across closed-set in-the-wild recog-nition (DFEW), audio-visual conflict-aware recognition (CA-MER), and open-vocabulary emotion reasoning (EMER). Skills evolved only on DFEW directly improve CA-MER and EMER without target-dataset evolution or backbone updates, demonstrating transfer across tasks, label spaces, and MLLMs. 

# Related Work 

Multimodal Emotion Recognition with MLLMs 

Recent MLLM-based emotion research has expanded affec-tive label spaces and multimodal evidence modeling (Guo et al. 2025; Hu et al. 2025). Emotion-specific architec-tures and instruction-tuning strategies further improve gen-eral emotion understanding (Cheng et al. 2024; Lian et al. 2025a; Yang et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025), open-vocabulary for-mulations relax predefined emotion taxonomies (Lian et al. 2025b), and retrieval-augmented methods introduce exter-nal context for compound-emotion reasoning (Wen et al. 2025). Conflict-aware methods additionally mitigate incon-gruent evidence through adaptive fusion or modality selec-tion (Wang et al. 2023; Yu et al. 2026). Despite these ad-vances, existing methods primarily optimize one-shot pre-diction and rarely address recurrent errors between confus-able emotion categories (Han et al. 2025; Hu et al. 2025). In this paper, CASE instead treats such errors as local decision-boundary failures. It uses pair-specific external knowledge to refine these boundaries while keeping the underlying MLLM frozen. 

Non-Parametric Learning through Memory and Skills 

Non-parametric learning methods differ in how they repre-sent and reuse experience. Memory-based approaches re-trieve previous examples or feedback (Madaan et al. 2022), whereas reflection-based agents retain verbal summaries of past interactions (Shinn et al. 2023; Park et al. 2023). It-erative self-feedback can refine individual outputs without additional training (Madaan et al. 2023), while prompt evo-lution adapts reusable task instructions through performance-guided search (Fernando et al. 2024). More recent methods distill experience into reusable instructions, workflows, tool-boxes, or executable skills (Wang et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2024; Wang, Neubig, and Fried 2024; Wang et al. 2025), with persistent skill libraries supporting con-tinual refinement and verifier-guided evolution (Wang et al. 2026; Zhou et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2026; Suzgun et al. 2026). CASE adapts this paradigm to multimodal emotion recognition. Each EmotionSkill captures discriminative cues for one confusable emotion pair, is selectively activated by the model’s top-2 predictions, and is retained only when it improves the corresponding local decision boundary.

Given an emotion label space Y = {y1, . . . , y K }, the task is to predict the ground-truth emotion y∗ ∈ Y for each sample. We treat the recognizer as a frozen prompt-conditioned MLLM interface 

fθ : X × P → O , where P is the prompt space and O is the structured output space whose schema is determined by the prompt. Phase 3: Behaviorally Validated Update and Expansion 

Validated skill update. A candidate EmotionSkill revision is not activated immediately, since a textually plausible rule may still overcorrect the targeted failures or bias the pairwise prediction toward one emotion. CASE therefore evaluates the current and candidate EmotionSkills on the same fixed pair-specific validation subset. The candidate is committed only when it strictly improves the pair-specific predictions without collapsing toward either label; otherwise, the existing EmotionSkills are retained. This behavioral gate ensures that only revisions with measurable corrective effects enter the active library. 

Sparse skill expansion. The missing-pair path in Figure 2 handles routed boundaries that are not yet covered by the active library. As summarized by BootstrapExpand in Al-gorithm 1, an uncovered pair contributes expansion evidence only when the ground-truth emotion belongs to that pair. Once sufficient recurrent evidence has accumulated, the ini-tializer proposes a new pair-specific EmotionSkill. Because no active parent EmotionSkill exists for direct comparison, the candidate is evaluated by a standalone bootstrap gate and is activated only when it has sufficient validation support and does not produce label collapse. Rejected candidates re-main inactive. Consequently, the library expands only toward recurrent and behaviorally supported confusion boundaries rather than exhaustively enumerating all possible emotion pairs. Detailed validation-set construction, behavioral metrics, acceptance criteria, and implementation thresholds are pro-vided in the supplementary Sec. A. 

# Experiments 

We evaluate CASE through four research questions: 

(RQ1) Does the complete CASE pipeline remain effective across different backbones and tasks? 

(RQ2) Where does selective routing concentrate its interven-tions? 

(RQ3) Does trajectory-driven evolution improve a static pair-wise EmotionSkill library? 

(RQ4) Does behaviorally validated deployment filter unsup-ported revisions and expand skill coverage without exhaus-tive library growth? Experimental Setup 

Datasets and metrics. We evaluate CASE across three complementary settings: cross-modal emotion conflicts on CA-MER (Han et al. 2025), in-the-wild dynamic facial ex-pression recognition on DFEW (Jiang et al. 2020), and fine-grained multimodal emotion reasoning on EMER (Lian et al. 2023). CA-MER contains the Audio-Conflict, Visual-Conflict, and Consistent subsets, for which we report accu-racy (Acc.). For DFEW, we report unweighted average recall (UAR) and weighted average recall (WAR), while accuracy is used for EMER. 

Backbones and implementation. We evaluate CASE with three frozen backbones: Qwen3-VL-8B (Bai et al. 2025a), Emotion-Qwen (Huang et al. 2025), and Gemini-3.5-Flash (Google DeepMind 2026). GPT-4o (OpenAI 2024) or GPT-5.6-sol (OpenAI 2026) serves only as the external skill editor. CASE-Full injects the complete routed skill, whereas CASE-Retrieval retrieves only relevant skill fragments. Evo-lution, validation, and test sets are strictly disjoint. Further implementation details are provided in Supplementary Sec. B. 

Overall Effectiveness and Generality (RQ1) 

We first examine the in-distribution effectiveness of CASE on DFEW, where the skill library is constructed. We then eval-uate CASE on CA-MER, a benchmark specifically designed to assess emotion recognition under conflicting multimodal cues. Finally, we evaluate CASE on the open-set EMER benchmark, which requires fine-grained emotion reasoning beyond a fixed closed-set label space.

Emotion-Qwen with CASE-Retrieval achieves 51.58%, surpassing prior methods and demonstrating effec-tive cross-backbone transfer. Moreover, substantial gains over the backbones are observed for all three inference models, demonstrating that CASE generalizes effectively across back-bones and open-set emotion reasoning settings.

# Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced CASE, a confusion-aware frame-work for improving frozen MLLMs in multimodal emo-tion boundary discrimination. Rather than globally updating model parameters, CASE localizes ambiguous predictions to recurrent emotion-pair boundaries and selectively invokes pair-specific EmotionSkills for focused discrimination. Ex-periments across three emotion tasks and multiple backbones demonstrate consistent effectiveness and generality. These results highlight external skill evolution as a promising alter-native to parameter-based adaptation for multimodal emotion understanding.
