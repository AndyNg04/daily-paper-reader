# Can LLMs Trust Appropriately? Evaluating Calibrated Reliance on Imperfect Partners 

Mengdi Chu 1 Jintao Huang 1

https://huggingface.co/datasets/90879c/llm-collaboration-trustbench 

Large language models increasingly collaborate with humans and peer AI agents whose contributions may be incomplete, inconsistent, or incorrect. Effective collaboration requires calibrated trust, whereby decisions to rely on, verify, or assist a partner adapt rationally as empirical evidence accumulates. We investigate whether models translate partner evidence into appropriate reliance policies. We introduce a benchmark of 324 scenarios across 23 collaboration categories. Each scenario involves three sequential decisions with explicit transition dynamics, feedback emissions, and cost schedules, permitting exact analytical evaluation of choices against their expected consequences over the remaining interaction. Evaluating seven models alongside 36 controlled-history probes, we dissociate evidence sensitivity from normatively appropriate action. While the strongest frontier model approaches near-optimal decisions, other models exhibit recurring pathologies: procuring excessive safeguards, purchasing verification tests with zero expected value of information, prioritizing immediate savings over cooperative learning, and misallocating assistance to suboptimal channels. Supplying explicit posterior distributions resolves certain errors while introducing new failure modes. Calibrated trust in autonomous agents critically demands coupling empirical evidence with forward-looking decision utility. 

## 1. Introduction 

LLM agents increasingly collaborate with human users and peer agents by exchanging infor-mation, partitioning tasks, and reviewing mutual outputs [20]. Effective cooperation requires strategic decisions regarding whose advice to follow, which tasks to delegate, and when to verify contributions. Accepting unreliable work risks propagating errors; conversely, exhaustive verification incurs prohibitive overhead and defeats delegation. Automation research has long formalized the dual hazards of excessive reliance and disuse [15]. LLM collaborators face this foundational challenge of calibrating reliance on imperfect partners. Research on trust distinguishes perceived trustworthiness from the willingness to depend on a partner [13]. Automation research connects trust to calibrated reliance within specific contexts [11]. In human–AI decision making, this entails accepting valid guidance while retaining independent judgment when advice is flawed [16]. We build on these foundations to investigate the behavioral expression of trust calibration in LLMs. Our central question is whether models translate partner evidence into normatively calibrated reliance, and under what conditions this translation fails. Calibrated reliance depends on both accumulated evidence and the downstream task horizon. For example, success under guided assistance provides limited evidence regarding a partner’s ability to operate autonomously. A reliable contribution may still warrant checking when error costs are severe, whereas inspecting an unreliable one yields negligible benefit if the result cannot alter the decision. By coupling diverse cooperative workflows with analytical refer-ence policies, our benchmark provides an objective testbed for evaluating whether empirical evidence translates into calibrated cooperative decisions. 

## 2. Background and Related Work 

Research in psychology and human–computer interaction (HCI) distinguishes perceived ability, benevolence, and integrity from the decision to depend on a partner [13], while automation research frames appropriate reliance in terms of the context and goals of use [11]. Studies in advice taking examine how people use others’ judgments [22], when they accept valid advice or reject errors [16], and how verification effort modulates reliance choices [18]. Crucially, trust evolves dynamically with experience and circumstances [7]. Trust repair depends on the type of violation and how the violating party responds [10], while intrusive oversight can undermine collaborator effort [5]. These findings support a dynamic conception of trust grounded in adaptation and coordination [3], coupling reliance to accumulated evidence and the downstream effects of one’s actions. In LLM research, studies examine both model trustworthiness [8] and human reliance on AI [23]. A complementary line investigates LLMs as trusting agents. Xie et al. [21] use trust games to compare LLM and human behavior, identifying partner-related biases. Lerman and Dover [12] examine how competence, benevolence, integrity, and demographics shape LLM trust in humans. Chen [1] operationalizes trust via costly verification against a memoryless baseline, analyzing formation, collapse, and repair. Recent work explores how models exploit source metadata. Wu et al. [19] benchmark learning from motivated communication against rational models, finding sensitivity to motives improves when made salient. Sun et al. [17] show that both human and LLM judges assign higher trust to identical content labeled as human-authored than as AI-generated. Cheng et al. [2] examine how source credibility and framing affect vigilance against harmful beliefs, while Pan et al. [14] integrate credibility into retrieval pipelines. We extend source evaluation to sequential cooperation, where relying, checking, and assisting incur tangible costs and shape downstream interactions. 

## 3. Benchmark Design 

Our benchmark evaluates whether LLMs translate empirical evidence about collaborators into normatively calibrated reliance. We operationalize calibrated reliance as choosing actions (e.g., delegation, verification, or assistance) that minimize cumulative expected cost under the scenario’s fully specified rules. This decision-theoretic criterion connects an agent’s evidentiary state with the normative validity of its actions. 

3.1. Interaction and reference decisions 

Each scenario formalizes three sequential decision stages within a simulated environment. The initial prompt describes the setting, partner performance rules, candidate actions, and associated costs. At each stage, the model chooses an action (such as delegation, verification, assistance, or takeover) and receives the feedback that action reveals. Many environments involve two contribution channels, denoted P and Q, representing disparate partners, advisory sources, or modular sub-components with distinct performance rules. 3.2. Task construction and coverage 

The benchmark contains 324 scenarios spanning 23 categories of collaborative tasks across 74 blueprints. A category groups functionally related collaborative contexts; a blueprint defines the contribution mechanics and candidate action space. Each blueprint instantiates four or five scenarios by varying environmental parameters, cost schedules, and interaction processes (Appendix A). We ground these coordination dilemmas in empirical literature on advice taking, trust repair, and behavioral responses to oversight [5, 10, 22], as well as demonstration learning and col-laborative grounding [4, 6]. These empirical paradigms inform the operational challenges formalized in our benchmark: adjudicating conflicting advice, assessing uncertain evidence transfer across domains, diagnosing communication breakdowns, and tracking non-stationary partner capabilities. Each scenario specifies latent partner state distributions, observation emis-sion functions, and action-cost schedules, yielding an analytically tractable reference policy (Appendix A.1).

All blueprints, task specifications, and environment rules were formulated and curated by the authors across structured domain rubrics. We exhaustively enumerate every decision tree to analytically verify optimal actions, transition probabilities, and continuation values along suboptimal branches (Appendix A). In addition to the 324 primary scenarios, 36 pairs of controlled histories span all 23 categories. Each pair holds preceding action trajectories invariant while systematically varying the evi-dentiary history or the underlying data-generating process. Models execute a single decision per condition in isolated contexts. Optimal action sets are disjoint in 31 pairs and identical in five. These probes isolate evidence sensitivity from policy calibration, verifying whether mod-els adapt reliance when warranted while maintaining stability when optimal policies remain invariant. 

## 4. Experimental Setup 

4.1. Models and interaction protocol 

We evaluate seven models: GPT-6 Luna, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Haiku 4.5, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and Qwen3.8 27B. Prompts and the evaluation protocol are detailed in Appendix B.2. All models receive identical task instructions and candidate actions, without external tools or auxiliary retrieval. Each scenario initializes in an isolated context window; subsequent turns append the cumulative history of public instructions, actions, and emitted feedback. Models return actions formatted as JSON. We evaluate one trajectory per primary scenario and one response per controlled-history input. Environmental feedback draws are pre-sampled and fixed at each node of the decision tree, ensuring strict comparability across models while allowing different policies to trace divergent paths. On the 24 specialized scenarios, we also supply the exact Bayesian state distribution implied by the public rules and observed evidence. This condition isolates belief inference from policy selection, testing whether explicit reliability information aids models in selecting subsequent actions (Appendix B.4).

5.2. Deciding when to verify 

In local replacement, models can audit either of two collaborator channels before execution; one channel is subsequently subject to potential replacement. For all 74 scenarios, models receive identical initial inputs. Foregoing verification is optimal in 32 scenarios spanning 19 activity categories, whereas testing is optimal in the remaining 42. Six models persistently acquire superfluous tests (Figure 2c), with Gemini doing so in 26/32 cases (81.3%) and Sonnet in 29/32 (90.6%). Strikingly, of the 108 unnecessary tests across models, 107 target the unchanged channel. While testing an invariant collaborator ensures evidentiary persistence, its cost strictly exceeds its downstream informational value. A substantial portion of tests provides zero expected value of information (EVOI). In 17 sce-narios, neither candidate test reduces downstream costs even before deducting fees, assuming optimal continuation. Gemini nevertheless tests in 12 of these scenarios and Sonnet in 15. Across all 108 unneeded tests, 61 offer zero gross benefit prior to fees (Appendix C.3). In these settings, feedback merely refines posterior beliefs without any capacity to alter the subsequent decision. Cooperation itself serves an exploratory function by generating diagnostic feedback about partner reliability. Among the 50 repeated-cooperation scenarios, 15 (spanning 13 categories) embody an active information-planning trade-off: the optimal first action incurs a higher immediate expected cost to obtain feedback that improves downstream decisions. These cases are identified analyti-cally from reference values, selecting scenarios where no greedy single-stage cost minimizer is optimal over the remaining rounds. Haiku chooses the myopic cost minimizer in all 15 cases, and Sonnet does so in 14 (Table 3). In contrast, Sol selects the optimal exploratory action in all 15; Luna and Gemini do so in 12 each. A route-planning scenario illustrates this dynamic exploration–exploitation trade-off (Figure 3b). Concurrently reserving both advisers’ routes incurs an immediate expected cost of 1.837, marginally lower than relying on the record adviser alone (1.860). However, single-channel delegation isolates whether that adviser was viable, whereas joint reservation reveals only whether at least one route succeeded. Under optimal continuation, absorbing the minor initial cost penalty to isolate feedback yields an expected cumulative cost of 8.681, compared with 89.282 under immediate hedging. Sonnet and Haiku greedily choose the latter, securing marginal immediate savings while forfeiting critical diagnostic feedback. Model behavior diverges sharply when identical diagnostic information is packaged as an explicit audit rather than an emergent byproduct of work allocation. Seven of the 15 blueprints admit matched delayed-feedback counterparts where purchasing an audit is optimal. These workflows share production payoffs and prior state probabilities, differing only in feedback timing and available actions. Luna makes optimal first choices in 4/7 ordinary-cooperation cases and 5/7 audit cases; Gemini does so in 5/7 and 7/7, respectively (Figure 4a). Sonnet displays the starkest contrast, choosing the myopic cost minimizer in all seven ordinary-cooperation cases while buying the optimal audit in all seven audit cases (across all seven pairs; Appendix C.4). This contrast separates two modalities of partner learning: executing dedicated verification actions versus structuring routine task division to harvest diagnostic signals. Sonnet readily pays for explicit audits yet consistently overlooks the informational dividends of cooperative task allocation. Coupled with its unneeded tests in Section 5.2, this dissociation demonstrates why verification frequency alone is an inadequate proxy for trust calibration: true calibration requires aligning information acquisition with cumulative decision utility. 

5.4. Allocating assistance for future cooperation 

In improvement-or-assessment scenarios, models can support or test either channel, or do neither. Providing assistance enhances future capability, but the optimal target depends on how channels are leveraged in downstream work. Several models consistently misallocate support interventions toward suboptimal channels (Figure 4b). Of Sonnet’s nine first-stage errors, eight target channel Q when assisting P is optimal; Gemini makes this error in five of its seven first-stage errors. In an assembly task where channel Q begins with lower baseline reliability and offers a larger nominal gain from assistance (Figure 3c), equal intervention fees nevertheless favor improving P due to its downstream structural importance (12.385 vs. 14.313 for Q). Sonnet erroneously assists Q, focusing on local reliability improvements while neglecting downstream utility. Appendix C.5 details the full error breakdown and analytical derivations. 

5.5. Using explicit reliability information 

Does isolating belief inference from decision-making improve cooperation? On 24 specialized scenarios, we explicitly supply the normative joint distribution over partner states, derived from public task rules and observed history. This provides exact Bayesian belief updates while leaving action selection to the model. Conditioning on identical historical trajectories yields 39–70 paired decisions per model (Figure 4c). Most evaluated decisions are already optimal in both conditions. Performance gains on previ-ously failed instances are largely offset by novel mistakes. Luna rectifies four of its six baseline errors but changes three previously optimal decisions to incorrect choices. Similarly, Haiku repairs six of eight errors while introducing five new mistakes, netting an overall gain of only one additional optimal decision across 59 and 46 paired decisions, respectively (Figure 4c, Appendix C.6). Providing explicit posterior belief states remediates isolated misjudgments, yet its net workflow utility depends equally on preserving baseline competence. ## 6. Discussion 

An agent adopting heightened caution after partner failure may appear to calibrate trust, yet choose safeguards whose cost exceeds their benefit. Decoupling responsiveness to evidence from calibrated reliance is therefore essential. The same principle governs assistance: improving partner capability is justified only when it yields downstream collaborative payoff. Cooperation also governs what an agent can infer. When an action isolates individual contri-butions, the resulting evidence sharpens future delegation; when joint outcomes aggregate contributions, credit assignment becomes harder. Work allocation thus fulfills an informational role alongside immediate production. The contrast between ordinary cooperation and explicit audits demonstrates that learning through work represents a distinct cognitive demand from willingness to verify: structuring informative workflows is an integral facet of trust calibration. Decision support must be evaluated by both errors corrected and competencies preserved. Supplied distributions resolve isolated errors, but repairs and new mistakes occur together. A valuable next step is investigating scaffolding that directly connects candidate actions to ex-pected costs, feedback distributions, and downstream consequences, testing which interventions improve cooperation while preserving existing strengths. 

## 7. Conclusion 

We introduced a benchmark of 324 scenarios across 23 collaboration categories to investigate whether LLMs translate partner evidence into appropriate reliance. Our seven-model eval-uation reveals that responsiveness to evidence frequently coexists with excessive protection, superfluous verification with zero expected value of information, and missed opportunities to learn through cooperation. Assistance is often misdirected toward suboptimal targets, while explicit reliability distributions induce new failure modes alongside targeted repairs. B.2. Prompts and evaluation protocol 

All conditions use the same system prompt: 

System prompt 

You are a language model participating in a controlled collaboration decision experiment. Answer using the current task and actual feedback, following the requested output format. Answer using only the experimental materials supplied in each input. Do not use tools or access files or the network. Follow the requested output format. 

Each scenario runs in a fresh context, and later turns replay the visible conversation. The prompt asks for JSON, and we parse the action from the response, including JSON inside prose or a code block. Any legal action, optimal or not, moves the environment down the corresponding branch. An empty, ambiguous, or unparseable response ends the trajectory and counts as an error. 15 B.3. Scoring and uncertainty 

An action is correct if its expected cost matches the optimum; when several actions tie, each receives credit. Normalized regret skips nodes where all actions have the same value. Blueprint-macro accuracy is the mean of the 74 per-blueprint accuracies. For a controlled-history pair, paired accuracy requires both actions to be optimal, while relation agreement only checks whether the action changes or stays the same as it should. Confidence intervals for the main metrics come from a bootstrap over blueprints (10,000 resamples), keeping all scenarios of a blueprint together and using the same resamples for every model. For proportions in the diagnostic analyses we use 95% Wilson intervals.

Sequential horizon length and extended interactions. Each benchmark scenario evaluates a three-stage sequential decision process. This horizon length is chosen deliberately: three sequential decisions provide a well-controlled, analytically exact horizon that cleanly dissociates myopic safeguard adoption from forward-looking information gathering (e.g., incurring imme-diate verification or cooperation costs to inform subsequent reliance choices), while keeping the underlying partially observable Markov decision process analytically solvable and com-putationally tractable across hundreds of scenarios. Nonetheless, many real-world human–AI and multi-agent collaborations involve longer or indefinite interaction sequences. In extended interactions, additional behavioral dynamics arise, such as cumulative reputation formation, gradual partner drift over extended periods, and progressive renegotiation of collaborative responsibilities. Assessing how language models balance exploration, learning, and safeguard procurement across extended horizons represents a valuable direction for future work. 

Single-trajectory evaluation and deterministic feedback draws. To guarantee strict, controlled comparability across models, each scenario evaluates a single trajectory under pre-sampled, fixed environmental feedback draws at each decision node. This design ensures that every model faces identical objective state transitions and diagnostic observations when taking identi-cal actions, eliminating confounding variance from environmental sampling noise. Furthermore, our statistical confidence is grounded in the benchmark’s breadth, spanning 324 scenarios (972 decision opportunities) across 74 distinct blueprints, with sampling uncertainty quantified through 10,000 bootstrap iterations and Wilson score intervals. Nonetheless, evaluating sin-gle trajectories under fixed draws does not capture generation-level stochasticity or model output variance across repeated trials and varied sampling temperatures. Future iterations could explore multi-trajectory rollouts and temperature sensitivity analyses to characterize within-model behavioral stability across stochastic runs. 

Commercial API opacity and model evolution. Our benchmark evaluates frontier reasoning models accessed through commercial provider APIs. While commercial endpoints grant access to state-of-the-art capabilities, proprietary model services are subject to unannounced backend updates, provider-side prompt transformations, and behavioral drift over time. To ensure full reproducibility despite API opacity, the public benchmark release includes frozen records of the entire experimental workflow: the exact evaluation environments, input prompts, fixed pseudo-random feedback draws, raw model outputs, and automated scoring pipelines.
