Abstract 

Multi-turn agents are commonly evaluated with LLM-based simulated users, which can be overly cooperative and can therefore overestimate an agent’s performance. We here introduce StressSim , an intent-preserving adversarial user simulator framework for stress-testing agents with challenging yet valid user interactions. 

StressSim optimises the simulator’s high-level adversarial plan for each task in-stance. Candidate planner prompts are evolved with a genetic prompt-optimisation loop that uses rollout traces, granular task-completion signals, and validity feed-back to favour conversations that reduce task success without changing the user’s underlying goal. We evaluate the approach on the MultiWOZ dataset and the retail and airline domains from τ -bench with three target LLM agents, com-paring against standard simulators and hand-designed non-collaborative user be-haviours. We demonstrate the effectiveness of StressSim in exposing robustness gaps across frontier LLMs, with τ -bench pass^4 scores decreasing by up to 69 %

on gemini-3.8-flash and 62 % on gpt-5.6-sol . StressSim maintains goal preservation performance (which decreases by at most 10 %), using an optimisation budget of 20 rollouts per task. Sensitivity analysis suggests that increasing the bud-get further decreases task success. Overall our results demonstrate that StressSim 

exposes robustness gaps missed by standard and hand-designed user simulators while maintaining conversation realism. This is achieved through lightweight prompt optimisation alone, without model fine-tuning. 

1 Introduction 

Multi-turn interactive AI agents are typically evaluated using LLM-based simulated users designed to mimic human interactions and provide a controlled environment for testing the agent’s performance [Guan et al., 2025, Ni et al., 2026, Yao et al., 2025]. However, LLMs trained as assistants tend to be overly cooperative and have low diversity, which can lead to a significant performance gap between testing and real-world deployment [Naous et al., 2025, Dou et al., 2025, Zhou et al., 2026]. Recent work has aimed to address this issue by fine-tuning LLMs on user utterances [Naous et al., 2025], as well as incorporating realistic non-collaborative strategies into simulators [Shim et al., 2026], which has shown significant performance degradation in multi-turn agents, suggesting that current simulated user evaluation may be overestimating the robustness of conversational agents. However, existing approaches apply pre-defined and highly engineered strategies for stress-testing multi-turn agents, which may not capture the full range of possible user behaviours and interactions, thereby providing an optimistic estimate of multi-turn agent robustness. Our work aims to address this limitation by introducing an intent-preserving adversarial user simu-lation framework, which optimises a high-level adversarial user policy to generate user dialogues that expose weaknesses in a multi-turn agent. The framework is designed to preserve the specified goals and naturalness of the user. We critically make the distinction that we aim to model a realistic

Our approach is inspired by constrained optimisation techniques to discover semantically equiva-lent counter-examples in the image classification, [Szegedy et al., 2013] [Carlini et al., 2019], text classification [Jin et al., 2020] and question answering [Liang et al., 2025] domains. Our framework includes three primary components: (i) a goal-state tracker to ensure generated dialogues aim to progress the goals of the user, (ii) an adversarial turn level planner that optimises the user policy for a challenging conversation trajectory, and (iii) a user utterance generator that produces natural language utterances for the user. The optimisation process involves genetic prompt optimisation for the adversarial planner, under strict validity constraints on the conversation trajectory. This approach allows us to discover challenging user behaviours that may not have been anticipated by human designers, and to evaluate the robustness of multi-turn agents in a worst-case scenario without relying on pre-defined strategies, providing a more realistic and comprehensive assessment of their performance. Our contributions are summarised as follows: • We formulate multi-turn agent robustness as intent-preserving adversarial user simulation ,distinguishing valid conversational difficulty from invalid task changes. • We propose StressSim , a black-box prompt optimisation pipeline for discovering adversar-ial user policies without fine-tuning or model-internal access. • We show that our optimised adversarial users lead to substantial task-completion degra-dation, by an average of 70% across 3 benchmarks and 3 agent LLMs while maintaining competitive goal preservation, exposing robustness gaps that collaborative and hand-crafted user simulators miss. 

2 Related Work 

Multi-turn agent evaluation. Our work builds on multi-turn evaluation datasets for task-oriented dialogue and agent interaction. MultiWOZ provides a standard setting for multi-domain dialogue state tracking and belief modelling [Budzianowski et al., 2018], while modern benchmarks extend this multi-turn paradigm to interactive agent environments, with tau-bench introducing user-agent-tool tasks with side-effecting APIs [Yao et al., 2025]. We use MultiWOZ and tau-bench to test whether agents remain successful with valid, goal-preserving, but strategically difficult simulated users. 

LLM user simulation and simulator validity. Although LLM-based user simulators provide a scalable evaluation framework, they often suffer from poor controllability and loss of goal fulfilment over long conversations. Methods to address these issues include domain-aware fine-tuning [Sekulic et al., 2024], generative dialogue-act modelling [Lin et al., 2022], prompting and in-context control [Algherairy and Ahmed, 2025, Li et al., 2022], reusable personas [Paglieri et al., 2026], and explicit goal-state tracking [Mehri et al., 2025]. A separate line of work shows that standard LLM simulators are often overly compliant and insufficiently diverse, which can inflate estimates of agent robustness: 2Naous et al. [2025] demonstrate that fine-tuning an LLM specifically to generate more realistic user dialogues significantly degrades measured agent performance, and recent benchmarking efforts quantify this gap by measuring how closely LLM-simulated users align with human behaviour, outcomes, and assistant rankings [Zhou et al., 2026, Dou et al., 2025]. Most closely related, Shim et al. [2026] hand-design non-collaborative user behaviours that degrade agent performance; our work instead optimises for difficult but valid user behaviour under goal-preservation constraints. 

Prompt optimisation. Automatic prompt optimisation treats the natural-language instruction itself as the search variable, using an LLM to propose candidates that are scored on a held-out set [Zhou et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2024]. Evolutionary methods maintain a population of prompts and apply LLM-generated mutation and crossover operators under a fitness-based selection rule [Guo et al., 2024, Fernando et al., 2024], while reflective methods propose edits based on textual feedback from execution traces, [Pryzant et al., 2023, Yuksekgonul et al., 2025]. Recent work on compound LLM systems combines both, with GEPA optimising system prompts through trace-level reflection and Pareto-based candidate selection [Agrawal et al., 2025]. Inspired by this genetic, reflective approach we implement a genetic optimiser loop for the adversarial user simulation. 

Adversarial attacks. Adversarial prompt-search and jailbreak methods, such as SEMA, PAIR, and AutoDAN, show that adversarial optimisation and multi-turn pressure can successfully elicit harmful and prohibited behaviours from LLMs and conversational agents [Feng et al., 2026, Chao et al., 2023, Zhu et al., 2024]. Our setting applies these ideas of adversarial optimisation with validity constraints for stress testing of conversational agents, such that an adversary represents a plausible user rather than a malicious attacker. 

3 User Simulator Formulation 

Multi-turn agent. We consider a task instance x = ( g, s 0, V ), where g is the user’s goal descrip-tion, s0 is the initial environment state, and V is an outcome validator for the given task instance. The evaluated agent A interacts with a user simulator U inside an environment E. A rollout τ produces a trajectory containing user turns, agent messages, tool calls, tool observations, intermediate environ-ment states, and a terminal state. The primary task-completion score is computed by a deterministic function V of the final state sT , which reflects the completion of all user goals. 

Goal following user simulation. We implement the user simulator as three LLM-mediated modules. First, inspired by the goal-state tracker in Mehri et al. [2025], we implement a goal state tracker that decomposes the user goal description g into a finite set of sub-goals 

G(g) = {γ1, . . . , γ m}.

At turn t, the tracker reads the conversation and assigns each sub-goal a status from 

Q = {NOT_ATTEMPTED , IN_PROGRESS , COMPLETED , FAILED , USER_ERROR }.

The resulting goal state ledger rt at the current turn is 

rt = Tθ (ht, G (g)) = {(γi, q t,i )}mi=1 , qt,i ∈ Q .

Second, a turn planner chooses the simulator’s next turn-level intent. Given the goal state rt, dialogue history ht, and a planning instruction prompt π, the planner samples 

zt ∼ Pθ (zt | ht, r t, π ),

where zt specifies what the user should do next: which content to provide, which information to ask for, the persona and tone to adopt, any intended user-side error to inject, and whether the conversation should terminate. Third, a dialogue generator verbalises this plan as the next user utterance: 

ut ∼ Dθ (ut | zt).

3The generated utterance is conditionally independent of the full history given the turn-level plan; the planner is responsible for carrying the stateful reasoning, while the generator is responsible for producing a natural surface form. 

Validity criteria. Distinguishing a task completion error caused by an agent failure from one caused by an invalid user trajectory is a central challenge in optimising an adversarial user-simulator. To obtain realistic failure cases and robustness estimates for the evaluated agent, we define a strict validity criterion for user trajectories to provide suitable constraints for the optimisation process. We decompose validity into two components: goal-information coverage and behavioural validity. For each task instance, we can construct a set of information items I(g) that must be communicated to the agent for it to successfully complete the task. For each required information item i ∈ I(g), we define cov( i, τ ) = 1 if some user turn in τ explicitly states, confirms, or agrees to i before the system supplies that information. 4 Constrained Adversarial Optimisation 

Our method treats adversarial user simulation as a per-example constrained optimisation problem. For each evaluation instance x, we keep the target agent A, environment E, user-simulator architecture, and rollout budget fixed. The optimisation variable is only the adversarial planner instruction π,which controls the turn-level tactics available to the simulated user. The objective is defined over both final and intermediate task completion. In addition to the binary final-state success score S(τ, x ), we allow a task to expose granular deterministic completion scores based on the system state:

where Sj measures whether the agent completed the jth required sub-task. Like the final success score, these intermediate scores are deterministic functions of the current system state for the task instance. They are useful when an agent partially completes a task: for example, it may satisfy some user constraints, complete one booking step, or recover one required field while still failing the final validator. This per-example formulation asks for the strongest valid adversarial planner instruction found for the current task instance under the available search budget. It is therefore closer to a worst-case interaction search than to learning a single global user prompt shared across all tasks. Candidate prompts are represented as reusable planner instructions. A candidate π specifies adversar-ial conversational tactics for the planner, while the hidden user goal and runtime state are provided separately by the simulator. This separation prevents the optimiser from hard-coding task-specific facts into the prompt and keeps the search focused on interaction strategies rather than memorised utterances. We optimise these planner instructions with a simple genetic prompt optimiser inspired by GEPA’s use of trace-level reflection and prompt evolution [Agrawal et al., 2025]. Algorithm 1 (Appendix B) summarises the resulting per-instance optimisation loop. The procedure treats prompts as the population members and uses the reflection LLM only to propose natural-language variants; all selection decisions are made from rollout-derived fitness under the same validity-penalised objective. At generation t, the optimiser selects the highest-fitness valid candidates from Pt as parents. A reflection LLM then mutates each parent using the parent’s score, failed sub-goals, validity failures, dialogue snippets, and planner traces. Mutations are natural-language edits to the planner instruction: for example, they may encourage the user to reveal information later, press the agent on unresolved constraints, or ask for corrections after an invalid intermediate step, while preserving the hidden user goal. Exact duplicate children are removed before evaluation. The remaining children are rolled out on the same instance, scored with the same objective, and merged with the parent population. We use elitist replacement, keeping the best-scoring candidates under the validity-penalised objective for the next generation. After the final generation, the best candidate in the population is selected as π⋆x.

5 Experimental Evaluation 

Benchmarks and LLMs. We evaluate on 50 tasks each from MultiWOZ, a task-oriented dialogue benchmark with state-based task-completion checks [Budzianowski et al., 2018], and on the retail and airline domains of τ -bench, a benchmark for tool-agent-user interaction in real-world domains 5[Yao et al., 2025]. Both benchmarks provide a user goal text and a deterministic validator for task success. We evaluate three target agent LLMs, gpt-5.4-nano [Singh et al., 2026], kimi-k2.6 [Kimi Team, 2025], and gemini-3.8-flash [Google DeepMind, 2026]. We use gpt-5.4-nano as the user simulator LLM for the state tracker, adversarial planner, and user utterance generator, across all experiments. All three target agents and the user simulator LLM are queried with low reasoning effort. We query agent LLMs through OpenRouter, and the user simulator LLM through the OpenAI API. 

Baselines. We compare our optimised adversarial user simulator against a standard user simply ini-tialised with the original user goal. We further compare against four hand-designed non-collaborative user simulators from Shim et al. [2026] that implement specific difficult user behaviours, namely emotional acts, fragment dumping, tangential behaviour, and unavailable-service behaviour. We refer to these baselines with an NC prefix in our experiments. 

Hyperparameters. We run our method for 4 iterations with a budget of 20 total rollouts per task, and use a penalty coefficient of β = 5; we analyse the sensitivity of the optimisation to β and to the rollout budget in Appendix C. The baselines have no tunable hyperparameters.

Human validation of the validity judge. To assess the performance of the validity judge, we conducted a human validation study on 100 randomly sampled conversations from the 3 benchmarks and target LLMs, stratified to include an approximately balanced mixture of conversations marked valid and invalid by the automated judge. Two human annotators were given the same validity rubric used by the LLM judge and independently labelled the behavioural-validity axes, goal-information coverage, and final validity. Table 2 reports Cohen’s κ agreement between the automated judge and each annotator, treating invalid conversations as the positive class; the full per-axis metrics are provided in Table 6 in Appendix F. The results support the reliability of the automated validity judgements. Across the behavioural validity axes, the judge shows high agreement with human annotators, with most F1 scores above 0.85 and high Cohen’s kappa. The main discrepancy appears in goal-information coverage, where we observe low precision but high recall: the automated judge is conservative and tends to mark some conversations as invalid for incomplete goal-information coverage even when human annotators consider them acceptable. This is the safer direction for our evaluation, since it reduces the chance that invalid adversarial conversations are counted as successful.

Analysis of discovered adversarial strategies. To understand the user behaviours discovered by StressSim , we constructed an inductive taxonomy from the optimised planner prompts across all dataset–model settings. We first extracted adversarial strategies (ignoring validity-preserving instructions) from the optimised planner prompts using an LLM. Subsequently, we embedded these strategies and applied k-means clustering to obtain groups of similar strategies, which were then summarised and named by an LLM. The full taxonomy construction procedure is provided in Appendix G. We then performed multi-label classification of every optimised prompt against this taxonomy and report the prevalence of each strategy family per dataset in Table 4 Conditional and staged disclosure is consistently common across both datasets, showing that with-holding information until a specific dialogue stage is a broadly effective source of adversarial pressure. Beyond this shared strategy, the optimiser discovers dataset-specific behaviours: MultiWOZ strate-gies frequently use relational cross-turn references and relative option selection, whereas τ -bench retail strategies instead emphasise coordinating multiple items or actions and managing consent at commitment boundaries. These differences across datasets demonstrate that the optimiser adapts its strategies to the domain and model being tested. 

6 Conclusions 

We introduced StressSim , an intent-preserving adversarial user simulation framework for stress-testing multi-turn agents without changing the user’s underlying task. Rather than relying on a fixed catalogue of difficult user behaviours, StressSim optimises the turn-level planner of a modular LLM user simulator under explicit validity constraints. The framework ensures that an adversarial rollout should lower task completion only while preserving the original goal, providing required information, and remaining plausible as user behaviour. This is achieved via lightweight genetic prompt optimisation, requiring up to 20 rollouts per task instance. Across MultiWOZ and the retail and airline domains of τ -bench, StressSim exposed substantial, previously unseen robustness gaps, decreasing the repeated-success reliability of agents driven by frontier LLMs: on τ -bench retail, pass^4 drops by 69 % for gemini-3.8-flash (from 0.320 to 0.100) and by 62 % for gpt-5.6-sol (from 0.296 to 0.112), while goal preservation decreases by at most 10 % across all settings. A human validation study showed that the automated validity judgements are well calibrated with human annotators. Overall, these results suggest that standard simulated users can overestimate the robustness of multi-turn agents, and that validity-aware adversarial simulations, such as StressSim offer a practical and principled way to measure whether agents can perform under difficult but realistic user behaviour. 87 Limitations 

We note a few limitations of our work. First, our primary experiments are restricted to LLMs with low reasoning budgets. While we show that our approach can expose robustness gaps in even frontier LLMs, we do not evaluate the approach with a large reasoning budget due to cost considerations. Furthermore, we restrict our evaluation to benchmarks with deterministic task rewards derived from environment state, Scenarios which may require the use of other judge models to assess subjective task success could make the optimisation vulnerable to reward hacking of the judge, and it remains unclear how reliably the proposed method would perform in such settings.

9 Ethics Statement 

This work uses adversarial simulation as a controlled diagnostic tool for evaluating multi-turn agents. C Sensitivity to Optimisation Hyperparameters 

We study the sensitivity of the optimisation procedure to its two hyperparameters: the validity penalty coefficient β and the rollout budget per task. Each sweep varies one hyperparameter with the other fixed at its default, and we run the two sweeps on different benchmark and target-agent pairs. We sweep β on MultiWOZ with kimi-k2.6 as the target agent (Figure 3a). The results show the expected but small trade-off between adversarial strength and validity preservation: a lower β

gives the optimiser more freedom to search for failure-inducing user behaviour, reducing pass^4 

from 0.420 at β = 10 to 0.340 at β = 2 , while also lowering first-attempt goal alignment from 0.980 to 0.900. We use β = 5 as a practical middle point that preserves high goal alignment while applying adversarial pressure. We sweep the rollout budget on τ -bench retail with gemini-3.8-flash as the target agent (Figure 3b). Increasing the rollout budget increases adversarial pressure, with varying impact on the different metrics: increasing the budget from 10 to 20 rollouts per task reduces pass^4 

significantly from 0.168 to 0.100, while increasing it further to 40 rollouts yields no further reduction in pass^4 but decreases pass@4 from 0.668 to 0.568. These results suggest that the optimisation is robust to hyperparameter choice, and that the default configuration of β = 5 and 20 rollouts per task provides an effective balance between goal alignment, adversarial pressure, and cost.
