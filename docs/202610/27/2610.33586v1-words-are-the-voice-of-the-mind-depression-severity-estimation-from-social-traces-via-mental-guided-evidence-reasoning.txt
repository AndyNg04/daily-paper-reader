Abstract 

Depression severity estimation is crucial for mental health assessment and intervention, yet traditional approaches depend on clinical inter-views or self-report questionnaires that require users’ active participation. With the widespread use of online social media, users leave rich social traces that may reflect their mental states, motivating the task of social media-based depression severity estimation. Existing efforts have progressed from textual-feature-based classification to LLM-based question-naire completion over standardized scale items, yet they still fall short in user-specific men-tal priors modeling, diversified semantic re-trieval, and fine-grained reasoning over mental evidence. To address these limitations, we pro-pose UMer, a user-specific mental evidence reasoning framework for social traces-based depression severity estimation. UMer first dis-tills a user-specific mental prior from histori-cal social traces to capture the user’s psycho-logical tendencies, behavioral patterns, and self-expression style. It then performs scale-guided multi-query retrieval to construct evi-dence memories from item-relevant posts. Fi-nally, it conducts coarse-to-fine evidence-aware reasoning to distinguish direct and indirect ev-idence and predict item-level scores with in-terpretable rationales. Extensive experiments on real-world benchmarks demonstrate that UMer not only achieves competitive perfor-mance over existing baselines, but also pro-vides evidence-grounded interpretability and generalizes to other scale-based tasks. 

1 Introduction 

Depression has become a growing public health concern in modern society, due to individuals be-ing increasingly exposed to stress from work, study, relationships, and daily life (Magomedova and Fa-tima, 2025). Traditional depression assessment methods, such as clinical interviews and standard-ized self-report questionnaires, are effective but require that users actively seek help (Joiner Jr et al., 2005). Recently, online social media has provided a spontaneous space for people to communicate, express themselves, and share daily experiences, where users leave rich social traces, such as posts, comments, replies, and interaction histories, that may reveal their personality traits, behavioral pat-terns, and psychological states that are difficult to capture through traditional offline assessments, making them a promising source for depression severity estimation (Masood et al., 2019). Thus, researchers have reformulated depression assessment as a social traces-based depression severity estimation (ST-DSE) task, which aims to infer users’ risk levels from their social media posts (Losada et al., 2020; Parapar et al., 2021a). Earlier works typically treated ST-DSE as a text classification task, relying on extracted textual fea-tures from social traces to train supervised clas-sifiers (Ji et al., 2022). Although these methods can achieve relatively high accuracy, they mainly predict depression severity from uninterpretable surface-level textual signals and require sufficient labeled data for effective training. In the era of large language models (LLMs), recent studies have begun to reformulate ST-DSE as a questionnaire scoring problem (Aragón et al., 2024; Ravenda et al., 2025b), where LLMs are prompted to as-sign scores to standardized scale items based on 1arXiv:2610.33586v1 [cs.CL] 24 Oct 2026 users’ social media posts. This transformation pro-vides an evidence-based solution and improves in-terpretability by aligning social media evidence with item-level psychological measurements. How-ever, the following limitations remain: (L1 ) Overlooking the user-perspective nature of self-report assessment . Existing LLM-based meth-ods primarily retrieve posts as supporting evidence for scale-level prediction. However, standardized depression scales are originally designed as self-report questionnaires, where each item reflects how a specific user would assess their own mental state. Therefore, the model should not only retrieve item-specific evidence from users’ social traces, but also capture the user’s overall mental state, self-expression style, and behavioral patterns from these records, thereby fulfilling the task through user-specific self-report simulation. (L2 ) Incomplete semantic coverage of evidence .Existing methods for evidence retrieval often rely on coarse-grained embedding similarity between standardized scale items and user posts. How-ever, scale items are usually written in medical and psychometric language, while users describe their mental states through daily events, fragmented feelings, implicit behaviors, and colloquial expres-sions. Therefore, directly using the original scale item descriptions as retrieval queries may fail to retrieve posts that are psychologically relevant but lexically different (Zeng et al., 2006), resulting in incomplete semantic coverage of evidence. (L3 ) Insufficient fine-grained evidence reasoning .Current LLM-based methods make predictions di-rectly based on retrieved posts, without explicitly assessing the role and strength of each piece of evidence. However, retrieved posts may support a scale item in different ways. For example, some posts directly match the item definition through explicit symptom descriptions or clear keyword-level expressions, while others only provide indi-rect evidence through daily-life events, behavioral changes, contextual self-disclosures, or long-term expression patterns. Such evidence requires struc-tured reasoning over relevance, evidential strength, and user-specific context, rather than simply rely-ing on semantic similarity or keyword overlap. To address these limitations, we propose a User-specific Mental-guided Evidence Reasoning frame-work (UMer). Specifically, UMer is built upon LLM in an agentic paradigm, which follows a three-stage workflow. UMer first distills a user-specific mental prior from the user’s historical social traces to understand the user’s psychological tendencies, behavioral patterns, and self-expression style. It then performs scale-guided multi-query retrieval to obtain item-related posts from multiple semantic perspectives, thereby forming scale-aware mental evidence memories for each user. Finally, a coarse-to-fine evidence-aware reasoning mechanism is uti-lized to guide the LLM to distinguish direct and indirect evidence and predict item-level scores with evidence-grounded rationales. We conduct experi-ments on real-world benchmark datasets, demon-strating not only the effectiveness and interpretabil-ity of UMer compared with existing baselines, but also its generalization ability across different men-tal health computing scenarios. In sum, our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We reformulate ST-DSE as a user-specific self-report inference task, emphasizing that LLMs should reason from the user’s perspec-tive rather than simply mapping retrieved so-cial traces to a specific scale item. To sup-port this, we distill a user-specific mental prior from historical social traces to reflect the user’s inner mental characteristics, enabling the LLM to perform user-perspective mental reasoning. • We propose UMer, a mental-guided evidence-aware reasoning framework for ST-DSE. UMer is equipped with mental prior and men-tal evidence memories constructed through scale-guided multi-query retrieval. It fur-ther performs coarse-to-fine reasoning, dis-tinguishing direct from indirect evidence for interpretable item-level scoring. • We conduct extensive experiments on real-world benchmarks, demonstrating that UMer outperforms existing baselines in de-pression severity estimation while providing evidence-grounded rationales and showing generalization ability across different mental health computing scenarios. 

2 Related Work 

Users’ social traces in social media have increas-ingly been used to model mental well-being and understand health-related outcomes (Chancellor and De Choudhury, 2020). Existing studies on ST-DSE mainly fall into the following two paradigms: 

Conventional Text Classification Methods . Con-ventional works focus on extracting lexical or se-2mantic features from users’ social media histories and train supervised classifiers by treating sever-ity scores as classification labels. For instance, Uban and Rosso (2020) combine psycholinguis-tic, emotion, and sentence-embedding features with SVM and logistic regression classifiers for depression-level prediction. Subsequently, the rise of pre-trained language models shifted the field to-ward contextual semantic modeling, which applies BERT-based classifiers to infer depression sever-ity from users’ social media histories (Martínez-Castaño et al., 2020a). The deployment of domain-adaptive models, such as MentalBERT and Men-talRoBERTa, further shows that continued pre-training on mental-health-related corpora can im-prove mental health risk detection (Ji et al., 2022). In addition, Pérez et al. (2022) develops symptom-specific word embedding models to estimate BDI-II scores, while Pérez et al. (2023) proposes seman-tic similarity models to retrieve posts relevant to depression severity levels, providing interpretable evidence for severity estimation. Although these methods can achieve competitive accuracy, their reliance on supervised training may limit general-ization ability and reduce interpretability. 

LLM-based Questionnaire Completion Ap-proaches . Recent studies have explored the po-tential of LLMs in mental health analysis (Aragón et al., 2024), emotional state understanding, and ex-planation generation from social media posts (Yang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024). For instance, Chim et al. (2024) utilizes LLMs for suicide-risk analysis by identifying risk-indicative evidence and produc-ing interpretable rationales, making timely online risk assessment more feasible. Aragón et al. (2024) assesses depression severity by predicting sentence-level summaries constructed separately for each BDI item. Ravenda et al. (2025b) propose Adaptive Retrieval-Augmented Generation (aRAG) for zero-shot psychological questionnaire completion from social media posts. Ravenda et al. (2025a) investi-gates steering vectors as a lightweight alternative for improving LLM-based mental health assess-ment. However, existing LLM-based approaches still largely rely on direct prompting or coarse re-trieval over social media posts, making it difficult to fully model user-specific mental context and perform fine-grained reasoning over direct and in-direct evidence. Our UMer moves beyond direct questionnaire completion by introducing a user-specific reasoning framework. It distills a mental prior and organizes scale-aware mental memories from users’ historical social traces, and then per-forms fine-grained evidence-aware reasoning to distinguish direct and indirect evidence for inter-pretable item-level scoring at a user perspective. 

3 Methodology 

In this work, we focus on Social Traces-based De-pression Severity Estimation (ST-DSE), which aims to estimate a user’s depression severity by retriev-ing scale item-relevant evidence from his or her historical social traces. Unlike existing efforts, we formulate ST-DSE as a self-report simulation task that infers how a user would respond to standard-ized scale items based on the user’s historical social traces, and propose UMer, a user-specific persona-guided mental reasoning framework. As shown in Fig. 2, UMer follows a three-stage agentic work-flow: 1) mental prior distillation , 2) scale-guided mental memory retrieval , and 3) evidence-aware reasoning . The details of each stage are presented in the following sections. 

3.1 Recursively Mental Prior Distillation 

Since UMer aims to simulate users’ self-reporting process on standardized scale items, it requires the LLM to reason from the user’s perspective. Thus, accurate mental prior are essential for pro-viding user-level priors that help the LLM under-stand individual characteristics and behavioral pat-terns (Jansen et al., 2020). To fulfill this step, we utilize an LLM to summarize the user’s behavioral patterns, personality-related tendencies, and self-expression style from social traces, thereby generat-ing a natural-language mental prior (Zhang, 2024). Instead of directly feeding the entire history into an LLM, which not only introduces substantial computational burden and performance degrada-tion but also makes it difficult to utilize key infor-mation in long contexts (Jiang et al., 2024), we de-sign a recursive summarization mechanism (Wang et al., 2025) to construct the user-specific mental prior through recursive chunk updating. 3.2 Scale-guided Memory Construction 

To mitigate the semantic gap between social media language and clinical criteria, unlike existing meth-ods that retrieve evidence by directly computing the similarity between standardized scale items and user posts, we design a Scale-guided Multi-query Retrieval (SMR) strategy. Specifically, SMR con-sists of two stages: 1) scale-item diversification ,which rewrites standardized scale items into post-oriented semantic probes to remedy the scale–trace semantic gap caused by the mismatch between psy-chometric descriptions and informal user expres-sions, and 2) mental evidence retrieval , which re-trieves candidate item-relevant posts and organizes them into the user’s mental evidence memory for subsequent reasoning.

3.3 Coarse-to-Fine Evidence-aware Reasoning 

After the retrieval stage, we obtain a scale-aware mental evidence memory Eu for each user. Exist-ing methods typically make predictions directly based on the retrieved evidence, but they fall short in fine-grained evidence assessment and in-terpretable rationale generation (Bao et al., 2025; Pérez et al., 2025). Therefore, we design a coarse-to-fine evidence-aware reasoning (EaR) mecha-nism to guide the LLM to assess the retrieved posts before producing the final item-level score. The EaR process is formulated as: 

Du,j , I u,j = Reason θ (dj , Rj , E u,j , Su) , (7) where Du,j and Iu,j denote the direct and indirect evidence, respectively. Specifically, for each scale item ij , the EaR fol-lows three steps. (S1) The LLM identifies direct evidence Du,j from Eu,j , where the posts explic-itly describe symptoms or states that closely match the definition dj of ij . (S2) Equipped with the user-specific mental prior Su, the LLM reasons from the user’s perspective to identify indirect evidence Iu,j ,where the posts do not explicitly mention the tar-get symptom but may imply it through life events, behavioral changes, self-expression patterns, or contextual signals. (S3) The LLM integrates both direct and indirect evidence by jointly consider-ing their relevance, evidential strength, severity-level similarity to the rubric examples Rj , and con-sistency across posts. In a nutshell, this process encourages the model to assess evidence quality rather than simply relying on semantic similarity or keyword overlap.

4 Experiment 

4.1 Experiment Settings Datasets & Metrics . We conduct depression severity prediction experiments on the eRisk 2020 (Losada et al., 2020) and eRisk 2021 datasets (Parapar et al., 2021b). Following the offi-cial eRisk evaluation protocol (Losada et al., 2020), we report two item-level metrics, ACR and AHR, and two questionnaire-level metrics, ADODL and DCHR. In addition, we use the eRisk 2022 (Para-par et al., 2022) and eRisk 2023 (Parapar et al., 2023) datasets to evaluate UMer’s generalization ability. All eRisk data were accessed and used in accordance with the official User Agreement.

Impact of LLM backbones We further analyze the impact of different LLM backbones on LLM-based methods, including aRAG and our UMer, and obtain the following observations: (1) Compared with aRAG, UMer achieves better and more bal-anced performance across all metrics, even when equipped with a lightweight backbone. (3) Larger and smaller open-source backbones exhibit relatively comparable performance, indicating that UMer is not highly dependent on model scale. To further analyze the scoring behavior of different backbones, we visual-ize the estimated score distributions in Fig. 3. Most models tend to favor low-severity responses, espe-cially scores 0 and 1, while smaller models assign relatively more predictions to scores 1 and 2. This tendency may help lightweight models obtain com-petitive questionnaire-level results by producing ag-gregate severity estimates closer to the ground truth. However, their item-level metrics remain weaker than those of proprietary LLMs, suggesting that lightweight models still struggle with fine-grained rubric following and item-level score calibration. In sum, these findings demonstrate the cross-LLM stability of UMer, which mainly stems from its tailored modules: user-specific mental prior distil-lation provides user-level contextual information, scale-guided multi-path retrieval supplies more gen-eralizable and reliable item-relevant evidence, and fine-grained evidence-aware reasoning guides the LLM to interpret the retrieved evidence more accu-rately. 

Human Evaluations Three annotators are asked to independently rate the extracted user-specific men-tal prior on a 1–5 scale based on the corresponding user-specific mental prior, assessing: (1) Objec-tive Accuracy, namely whether the prior accurately reflects objective facts without introducing hallu-cinated information; (2) Completeness, namely whether it covers the important user-specific in-formation without significant omissions; and (3) Subjective Reasonableness, namely whether sub-jective descriptions are rational, unbiased, and ade-quately supported by mental prior. For each metric, we consider the annotators to be in agreement when their ratings differ by no more than one point on the five-point scale. As shown in Tab. 2, all three evalu-ation metrics receive relatively high average scores from the three annotators. Under this one-point tolerance criterion, the agreement rates among the three annotators are 86.3% for Objective Accuracy, 73.1% for Completeness, and 87.9% for Subjec-tive Reasonableness. These results suggest that the extracted mental priors can be evaluated with relatively consistent human judgments.

4.3 Ablation Study 

To analyze the contribution of each component, we conduct an ablation study on the eRisk 2020 dataset. We additionally report item-level Mean Absolute Error (MAE) to quantify the average absolute devi-ation between the predicted and gold BDI-II total scores (Willmott and Matsuura, 2005). As shown in Tab. 3, we draw the following observations: ( O1 )After removing the user-specific mental prior (w/o 

Su), the performance drops substantially across all metrics, demonstrating that user-level mental pri-ors are crucial for user-perspective reasoning. This also supports our user-specific formulation, indicat-ing that depression severity estimation requires not only retrieved posts, but also a holistic understand-ing of the user’s inner characteristics. We further present a case study in Appendix A.1, where we find that our distilled user-specific mental prior pro-vides a comprehensive summary of the user’s long-term social characteristics, such as habitual behav-ioral patterns. This user-level prior helps the model understand the user’s mental tendencies and self-expression style, enabling it to interpret indirect evidence from memories and produce judgments that are better aligned with the user’s self-reported ground truth. ( O2 ) Removing the EaR module (w/o 7EaR) also leads to clear performance degradation, highlighting that fine-grained evidence assessment is essential for reliable item-level reasoning. With-out EaR, the model is more likely to directly pre-dict scores from retrieved posts based on coarse semantic matching or surface-level keyword over-lap, which weakens its ability to interpret implicit mental-state evidence. To further examine whether EaR effectively guides the model to infer indirect evidence by leveraging the user-specific mental prior, rather than simply relying on explicitly re-trieved posts, we employ GPT-4o as an LLM-based judge for automatic evaluation. Specifically, we introduce Content Attention Analysis (CAA) as the judge metric to assess whether the generated ra-tionales mainly rely on the mental prior, retrieved posts, or their joint use. We evaluate UMer on two additional questionnaire-based eating-disorder assessment tasks, i.e., the EDE-Q scoring tasks in eRisk 2022 and eRisk 2023. The user-specific mental prior construction and structured reasoning follow the same pipeline as in ST-DSE, and the detailed reasoning prompt template is provided in Appendix B.3. The ex-perimental results are shown in Fig. 4, where we observe that our UMer achieves balanced and com-petitive performance on both eRisk 2022 and eRisk 2023 EDE-Q scoring tasks. Notably, compared with the task-specific baselines, our training-free framework achieves competitive or even superior performance across several metrics. These results suggest that the proposed user-centered, mental-guided self-reported framework can be effectively transferred to another continuous psychological scale, demonstrating strong generalization ability under zero-training or few-shot settings. 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we formalize social traces-based de-pression severity estimation as a user-centered self-report simulation task. Instead of directly diag-nosing users based on their social media posts, our goal is to use these posts as evidence to sim-ulate how users would report their own psycho-logical states when completing standardized scale items, thereby making the predicted scores bet-ter aligned with users’ self-reported ground truth and enabling a more faithful estimation of their psychological status. To this end, we propose UMer, a user-centered mental evidence reason-ing framework that combines user mental prior construction for capturing user-level psychologi-cal priors, scale-aware evidence retrieval for col-lecting item-relevant posts from multiple semantic perspectives, and evidence-aware structured reason-8ing for distinguishing direct and indirect evidence and performing item-level scoring. Extensive ex-periments on real-world benchmarks demonstrate that UMer achieves competitive performance, pro-vides evidence-grounded interpretability, and gen-eralizes to other questionnaire-based mental health assessment tasks under zero-training or few-shot settings. Limitations and ethical considerations are discussed in the supplementary material. 

6 Limitations 

Despite the effectiveness of our proposed UMer, several limitations remain: (1) Cost and Data Dependency of User Mental Prior . UMer requires distilling a user mental prior from historical social traces. On the one hand, this introduces additional computational cost compared with existing LLM-based methods, since construct-ing a user-level prior from scratch for each user can be expensive. On the other hand, the recursive updating strategy is more suitable for long-term user modeling scenarios. Therefore, sufficient his-torical traces are indispensable for reliable prior construction. Future work should explore simpler and more efficient ways to extract user-level pri-ors, such as lightweight profile summarization or retrieval-based approximation. (2) Dataset Scale and Severity Calibration . The utilized datasets contain a relatively small number of users, and the overall severity distribution is skewed toward moderate cases. This may make the model more prone to underestimating severe condi-tions, especially when strong evidence is scarce or implicitly expressed in social media posts. More robust calibration strategies are needed to reduce systematic underestimation and improve prediction reliability across different severity levels. (3) Clinical Use and Interpretation . The pro-posed UMer is developed to assist preliminary screening and questionnaire-based research, rather than to provide definitive medical diagnosis or clin-ical advice. Although UMer simulates users’ self-reporting process to estimate their psychological states with standardized psychological scales, its outputs should be interpreted as auxiliary signals and should not replace professional assessment by qualified mental health practitioners. 

7 Ethical Considerations 

This work uses social media data for questionnaire-based mental health assessment, which requires careful attention to privacy, data protection, and the boundaries of system use. Users’ posts may contain sensitive personal information, and the generated mental prior should also be regarded as sensitive derived data. Therefore, any deployment of such a system should follow strict data governance princi-ples, including informed consent when applicable, data minimization, anonymization, secure storage, and restricted access. The proposed framework is intended for prelimi-nary screening and research-oriented questionnaire completion, rather than definitive clinical diagno-sis or medical decision-making. Although stan-dardized psychological scales provide a structured way to estimate users’ self-reported psychologi-cal states, model predictions may still be affected by incomplete evidence, biased expressions, and reasoning errors. The outputs should therefore be interpreted as auxiliary signals for identifying po-tential risk and should not replace assessment by qualified mental health professionals. Responsible use also requires preventing over-interpretation of model-generated scores and ex-planations. The system should not be used in high-stakes settings without human oversight, clin-ical validation, and appropriate safeguards. Users should be clearly informed that the framework simulates questionnaire responses from historical posts, instead of directly diagnosing their mental health status.

B.3 Evidence-aware Reasoning
