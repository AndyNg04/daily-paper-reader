# Competitive exploration in structured environments 

James P. Wolfe 1, Joshua B. Plotkin 2, ∗  

> 1Graduate Group in Applied Mathematics and Computational Science, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA, 2Department of Biology, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA
> ∗Corresponding author. Email: jplotkin@sas.upenn.edu October 25, 2026

ABSTRACT Organisms must constantly weigh exploiting the resources they know against exploring for better ones. When foragers compete, the best behavior for each forager also depends on what its competitors do. How does competition change the strategic balance between exploration and exploitation? Here we address this question in a minimal model: two ε-greedy temporal-difference learners on a one-dimensional lattice, who start at a small reward at one end and are unaware of a larger reward at the other. For a lone forager, the optimal exploration rate increases monotonically with the value of the distant reward, and with its distance up to a critical distance. When two foragers share the lattice and split rewards, the choice of exploration rate becomes a game whose Nash equilibria have both foragers exploring, both staying, or one exploring while the other stays (anti-coordination). Under local best-response dynamics, pairs of foragers often converge on both exploring or both staying, even when anti-coordination would be socially optimal. This social dilemma arises for a wide range of parameters, and it persists even in a simplified model without explicit spatial structure. A minimal, naturalistic signal, in which one forager informs the other that it has found the distant reward, can remove many of the dilemmas in which both foragers explore, and can produce a Nash equilibrium whose collective payoff exceeds that of every outcome available without signaling. 

Across species and timescales, organisms must bal-ance exploiting the resources they already know against exploring for better ones they have not yet found. A bac-terium performing chemotaxis alternates between swim-ming up an attractant gradient and tumbling to reorient at random (Berg and Brown 1972); a bee colony works its known flower patches while dispatching scouts in search of richer ones; and a person searching for infor-mation faces the same choice (Pirolli and Card 1999). The tradeoff has been studied for decades in behavioral ecology, where optimal foraging theory asks how an ani-mal should time its departure from a depleting resource and divide its effort among sites of differing quality (Charnov 1976; Stephens and Krebs 1986; MacArthur and Pianka 1966). Whether the relevant timescale is a single lifetime or an evolutionary one, the balance that maximizes long-run reward is difficult to strike, and it depends both on the structure of the environment and on the behavior of others exploiting it. Reinforcement learning provides a formal frame-work for managing the exploration–exploitation tradeoff (Sutton and Barto 2018). An agent that interacts repeat-edly with its environment maintains value estimates for the states and actions available to it, and at each step chooses between the action favored by its current value estimates and an alternative that may prove bet-ter. The tension is sharpest in the multi-armed bandit problem (Robbins 1952), in which an agent chooses re-peatedly among fixed options with unknown reward distributions. Most of the resulting theory pursues re-gret minimization—algorithms whose cumulative re-ward provably stays close to that of the best fixed op-tion (Lai and Robbins 1985; Auer et al. 2002). These guarantees are worst-case: they bound performance across all environments rather than prescribe how much an agent should explore in a particular one. How hu-mans and other animals in fact resolve the exploration– exploitation tradeoff has itself become an active question in cognitive science and neuroscience (Daw et al. 2005; Tavoni et al. 2022). Animal foraging departs from the stylized bandit problem in one important respect: foraging options are not equally accessible. Reaching a distant resource takes time, during which the forager collects nothing, so ex-ploration incurs an opportunity cost in travel time that a bandit model omits. We therefore study reinforce-ment learners in spatially structured environments, in which movement between options is itself costly. We adopt temporal-difference (TD) learning (Sutton 1988), in which an agent revises the value it assigns to a site using the values it has assigned to neighboring sites. TD learning is a standard model of animal reward learn-ing, and it has a well-documented neural correlate: the phasic activity of midbrain dopamine neurons covaries with the reward-prediction error term of the TD up-date (Schultz et al. 1997; Montague et al. 1996), and the same signal has been identified in the human brain (O’Doherty et al. 2003). TD learning has been applied specifically to spatial foraging, such as bumblebees mov-ing among flowers (Montague et al. 1995). How much should a learner explore new territory versus exploit known resources? The simplest rule is 

ε-greedy, governed by a single exploration rate ε: with probability 1 − ε the agent takes the action it currently 1arXiv:2610.34643v1 [physics.soc-ph] 26 Oct 2026 values most, and with probability ε it moves at ran-dom. Despite its simplicity, ε-greedy often matches or outperforms more elaborate algorithms with dynamic exploration rates and stronger convergence guarantees (Vermorel and Mohri 2005; Kuleshov and Precup 2014), and it requires no sophisticated cognition about the environment, unlike dynamic programming (Bellman 1957; Puterman 1994; Sutton and Barto 2018). But sys-tematic guidance on which exploration rate ε performs best in a given environment remains scarce. A fixed rate is also the natural object of study for an evolved forager: an animal may be unable to adjust its explo-ration rate from moment to moment, and instead have an intrinsic rate shaped by natural selection to suit its ecology (Dingemanse and Wolf 2010; Wolf et al. 2007). Consistent with such intrinsic variation, ant colonies of different species differ in their exploitation intensity: species that commit most of their foragers to the better of two feeders are slow to reallocate when the poorer feeder improves, whereas species that spread foragers more evenly shift readily to the improved feeder (Latty and Beekman 2013). In the terms of our model, each species behaves as though it has a different fixed explo-ration rate. Foragers are rarely alone, though, and competition changes what each of them should do (Giraldeau and Caraco 2018). Competitive multi-armed bandit models with shareable rewards address a version of this ques-tion: at the Nash equilibrium of such a game the number of agents at each option is roughly proportional to that option’s value (Xu et al. 2023), recalling the ideal free distribution of classical foraging theory (Fretwell and Lucas 1969; Parker and Sutherland 1986). Our setting is neither ideal nor free: the agents lack perfect infor-mation, and although nothing prevents a forager from traveling to the distant reward, the opportunity cost of doing so can be large. Here we ask what fixed exploration rates are indi-vidually and socially optimal when foragers compete for shared rewards in a spatially structured environ-ment: a one-dimensional lattice with a small, known reward at one end and a larger, undiscovered reward at the other. We first find that, for a single forager, the reward-maximizing exploration rate is intermediate— rising with the value of the distant reward, and rising and then falling with the distance to it. We then find that competition makes the choice of rate a two-player game whose Nash equilibria include anti-coordination, in which two identical foragers adopt opposite behav-iors, and that the Nash equilibria at which both foragers stay or both foragers explore are often collectively worse than anti-coordination, producing a social dilemma. Fi-nally, we find that a simple, cognitively undemanding signal—one forager informing another that it has found the distant reward—can remove the suboptimal equi-librium at which both foragers explore and resolve the dilemma.

Our model represents the essential tension of a forag-ing task: at each moment an agent must decide whether to exploit a resource it already knows or to explore in search of a better one it has not yet found. We situate this decision in a spatially structured environment, so that exploration incurs an opportunity cost—time spent traveling away from a known reward (Fig. 1). An agent begins at a small, familiar reward, unaware of a larger reward far away. A single parameter, the exploration rate ε, governs how readily it explores. Below we specify the environment, the movement policy, and the policy’s 2equivalent descriptions as a temporal-difference learner or as a Markov chain. We represent the environment as a finite, one-dimensional lattice of sites {0, 1, 2, . . . , d}, with d > 0. Two sites contain resources: a small reward at one boundary, r(0) = 1, and a larger reward at the other boundary, r(d) = R > 1; all sites in between are empty, 

r(s) = 0. The lattice length d thus sets the distance an agent must travel to reach the richer resource, and the reward ratio R governs the benefit of discovering the richer reward. At each discrete time step the agent occupies a single site s and accrues its reward r(s);rewards are non-depleting, so an agent collects r(s)

on every step it remains at site s. Movement is local: from site s the agent may step only to a site in its neighborhood n(s) (self-inclusive), e.g. n(2) = {1, 2, 3 }

for d > 2 and n(0) = {0, 1 }.The agent moves on this lattice by an ε-greedy rule. At each step it chooses between two behaviors: • with probability 1 − ε it exploits , moving toward the highest reward it has so far visited; • with probability ε it explores , moving to a randomly chosen neighboring site (excluding its current one). This rule generates a two-phase biased random walk. Before the agent has discovered the far reward, its only known resource is the near one at site s = 0, so exploita-tion biases it leftward: in the interior {1, . . . , d − 1} it steps left with probability 1 − ε/2 and right with proba-bility ε/2. Once it has visited site d, its bias reverses—it steps right with probability 1 − ε/2 and left with proba-bility ε/2—and it is biased toward the richer resource. The exploration rate ε therefore sets the strength of the bias in each phase: an agent that never explores ( ε = 0) remains at the near reward indefinitely, whereas larger 

ε increases the variance of the walk and the probability of reaching site d.The two-phase biased random walk is what atemporal-difference (TD) learner would produce, as we make precise below. The intuition is that, once the agent reaches the far reward, subsequent steps propagate its value backward along the lattice: each visited site’s value estimate is set to a discounted fraction of R,decreasing geometrically with distance from site d.For a sufficiently large discount factor γ, the value estimates of the sites visited since that discovery there-fore increase monotonically toward site d, so that the exploitation move at every interior site points toward it. The two ends of the lattice require rules of their own. Before site d has been found, site 0 is reflecting: at site 

s = 0 the agent stays with probability 1 − ε and steps to site s = 1 with probability ε. After site d has been found, site d is reflecting in the same way: the agent stays there with probability 1 − ε and steps to d − 1 with probability 

ε. After site d has been found, an agent at site s = 0moves to site s = 1 with probability 1, consistent with the interior rule: with probability 1 − ε it exploits by moving toward d, and with probability ε it explores to its only available neighbor, site 1. An agent values immediate rewards more than fu-ture ones, which we encode with a discount factor γ ∈

(0, 1 ): a reward collected t steps in the future is worth a fraction γt of its immediate value. Discounting repre-sents a finite foraging horizon, and corresponds to aver-aging reward over a characteristic time T ≈ 1/ (1 − γ)

(see Markov chain formulation below). Interpretation as a temporal-difference or Q-learning problem 

The movement rules outlined above also arise from temporal-difference learning (Sutton 1988; Sutton and Barto 2018), which is a standard model for how animals and algorithms learn to value their environment. Sup-pose the agent maintains an estimate Q(s) of the long-term value of each site s—the total discounted reward 3it expects starting from s—initialized at Q(s) = 0 every-where. Upon first visiting a site s that provides a reward 

r(s) > 0, the agent assesses its steady-state discounted value r(s)/(1 − γ) and sets Q(s) = r(s)/(1 − γ); that is, on encountering a reward the agent immediately reg-isters its long-run worth. Updating Q-values to account for the discount factor is a modeling choice made with-out loss of generality: an alternative assignment based on instantaneous rewards, Q(s) = r(s), scales every 

Q-value by 1 − γ and so leaves every comparison be-tween sites, and with it the movement rules, unchanged (cf. (Wiewiora 2003)). Because the agent begins at site 0, the rule for as-signing Q-values fixes Q(0) = 1/ (1 − γ) immediately, while all other sites are initialized with Q = 0. The far site keeps Q(d) = 0 until the agent first reaches it: the value Q(d) = R/(1 − γ) is assigned on reaching site d,not in advance. The agent thus has no knowledge of the far reward until exploration reveals it, at which time the agent computes its long-term discounted value. At the empty sites between the two rewards ( s ∈{1, 2, . . . , d − 1} where r(s) = 0), the agent updates its estimate by temporal-difference learning, which we call state-value Q-learning: 

Q(s) = r(s) + γ max {Q(s′) : s′ ∈ n(s)}.This recursion—a site’s value equals its immediate re-ward plus the discounted value of its best neighbor—is the standard Bellman update used in Q-learning. Two choices adapt it to our setting. First, because the re-ward at each site is fixed rather than noisy, the agent overwrites its old estimate outright rather than aver-aging old and new; in the language of Q-learning, the learning rate is α = 1. Second, the agent stores a sin-gle value per site rather than a separate value for each available move, because a site’s location already deter-mines which moves are possible. The agent updates only the value of the site it occupies, so value estimates propagate one site at a time as the walk proceeds. Applying the Bellman update repeatedly as the agent moves produces Q values that are monotone across the sites visited in each phase of the walk. Before the agent has visited site d, each site it has visited has value Q(s) = γs/(1 − γ): the first step sets Q(1) = γ/(1 − γ) from the reward at site 0, the next sets Q(2) = γ2/(1 − γ), and so on. Sites not yet visited—including site d—remain at their initial value 

Q = 0. Once the agent has reached site d, a similar sequence of updates proceeds inward from the far reward: in the high-discount regime each subsequently visited site is updated to Q(s) = γd−s R/(1 − γ), so that walks are biased towards the distant reward. The movement of the agent, even outside the high-discount regime, can be reduced to a Markov chain on 2 (d + 1) states. The Markov chain describes the same movement as the TD learner, but it gives the agent’s expected reward exactly, without simulating many runs. At d = 8 and R = 5, the Markov chain prediction matches the mean reward of 5000 TD-learner runs ( SI Appendix , Fig. S1). Two competing agents 

We now add a second, identical agent to the same en-vironment. When both agents occupy the same site at the same time, they split its reward. We assume R > 2, so that an agent’s share of the far reward, R/2, still exceeds the near reward r(0) = 1, which is necessary for an agent that has discovered site d to stay biased towards it even when it must share. 4Under the TD dynamics, an agent that has found site d remains biased toward it from every site when-ever γd R/2 > 1. # Results 

An intermediate exploration rate is optimal for distant, valuable rewards 

What fixed exploration rate ε maximizes a single agent’s expected discounted reward, π(ε)? Except in extreme regimes, the expected discounted reward is non-monotonic in the exploration rate, with one local maximum at ε = 0 and another at an intermediate exploration rate ε > 0 (Fig. 2A). Which of the two exploration rates is the global reward maximizer, ε∗,depends on the relative value of the distant reward, R,and the distance to reach it, d.Pure exploitation ( ε∗ = 0) is optimal when the dis-tant reward is small or far away (e.g. R = 2.5, d =

10): the opportunity cost of travel outweighs the gain from reaching site d. Increasing the value of the dis-tant reward ( R = 5, d = 10) or shortening the distance (R = 2.5, d = 5) moves the optimal exploration rate to the interior, so that some exploration is optimal. As the distance d grows, this interior optimum shifts to larger values of ε, and less of the agent’s total reward is collected from the distant site s = d.The intuition is a tension between finding the distant reward and exploiting it. Some exploration is needed to reach site d at all, and the farther it is, the more exploration this requires. But the exploration rate is fixed, so an agent that explores enough to reach site 

s = d also steps away from it at that same rate, forfeiting reward in the process. The optimal exploration rate balances these two effects; when the reward is too small or too distant for the gain from reaching d to outweigh the loss due to continued exploration, the optimum is 

ε∗ = 0. A sharp boundary separates the parameters at which exploiting is optimal from those at which exploring is optimal (Fig. 2B,C). For fixed distance d, the optimum stays at ε∗ = 0 until the reward benefit R crosses a critical value R∗(d), and then it jumps to a positive rate and increases gradually. For fixed benefit R, the optimal exploration rate increases with d up to a critical distance 

d∗(R), and then it drops discontinuously to ε∗ = 0. The critical reward ratio R∗(d) increases with the distance 

d, faster than linearly beyond the shortest distances, and it decreases as the foraging horizon lengthens ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2). Away from the boundary between exploiting and exploring, the optimal exploration rate is robust to ran-dom variation in the distance to the distant reward. When the distance is random, d ∼ Poisson (λ) + 1 with mean distance λ + 1, the optimal exploration rate closely matches that of the static environment with d = λ + 1; near that boundary the two optimal rates differ, in ei-ther direction depending on the discount factor (see 

SI Appendix , Fig. S3). Hence a single exploration rate, chosen for the mean distance, is close to the best fixed rate for a distance that varies about that mean, except near the boundary between exploiting and exploring.

> Strategies and payoffs

How should an agent explore when it must compete with another agent for the same rewards? Rewards at a shared site are split, and so the payoff to a focal agent with exploration rate ε1 depends not only on the dis-tance d to receive a reward benefit R, but also on the exploration rate ε2 of the other agent. The choice of ex-ploration rate therefore defines a symmetric two-player game with continuous strategies: each agent’s strategy is its exploration rate ε ∈ [0, 1 ], its payoff is the expected discounted reward π(ε1, ε2; d, R, γ) it collects, and the distance d, reward benefit R, and discount factor γ en-ter as exogenous parameters. We ask what exploration rates two self-interested agents will tend to adopt—and whether or not those rates maximize the payoff the two of them collect together. Player 2 therefore faces an en-vironment equivalent to the single-agent problem with the distant reward benefit doubled, R′ = 2R, and total payoff scaled by a factor of 1/2. A competitor that finds and exploits the distant re-ward lowers the value of that reward to the other agent, since the two players split it whenever both are there. And just as a single agent’s optimal exploration rate drops discontinuously to zero when the reward benefit falls below the critical value R∗(d), player 2’s best re-sponse drops discontinuously—from an intermediate rate to ε2 = 0—as ε1 increases. The discontinuity occurs at the value of player 1’s exploration rate ε1 at which the interior local maximum of player 2’s reward curve falls below its value at zero exploration, ε2 = 0, so that the global reward maximizer changes from an intermediate rate to ε2 = 0 (Fig. 3B). Hence player 2’s best response has the same structure as the single-agent optimum—an interior exploration rate that drops discontinuously to zero—with the competitor’s exploration rate ε1, rather than the reward ratio R, as the quantity that crosses the threshold. 

Nash equilibria 

A Nash equilibrium is a pair of exploration rates at which neither player can increase its payoff by unilat-erally changing its own rate, namely an intersection of the two best-response curves, where BR (ε1) = ε2 and BR (ε2) = ε1. Because the game is symmetric, player 1’s best-response curve is player 2’s reflected across the diagonal ε1 = ε2. For the example parameters of Fig. 3 (distant reward benefit R = 2.1, distance d = 4) there are three Nash equilibria: one in which both players explore at the same intermediate rate ( ε1 = ε2 > 0), and a pair related by exchanging the players’ labels, in which one player stays at the near site while the other explores ( ε1 = 0 and ε2 > 0, or vice versa). We refer to the symmetric equilibrium as “both explore” and to the two asymmetric equilibria as “anti-coordination,” since the players adopt opposite behaviors. Across a sweep of the exogenous parameters, the set of Nash equilibria takes one of four forms: {both explore, anti-coordination}, {both explore}, {anti-coordination}, and {both stay} (Fig. 5, top row). Only the set {both ex-plore, anti-coordination} contains Nash equilibria of two types, and this set occurs only in a narrow interval of re-ward ratios at the border between the anti-coordination and both-explore regions. At distance d = 4 and dis-count factor γ = 1 − 1/5000, for example, both the symmetric equilibrium and the anti-coordination pairs are Nash equilibria at reward ratio R = 2.1 (Fig. 3), whereas at R = 2.15 the anti-coordination pairs are no longer Nash equilibria. In particular, when both agents staying is a Nash equilibrium it is the only one, because staying is then the best response to every exploration rate of the competitor ( SI Appendix , section S5). 7Competition thus produces a qualitatively new out-come: at an anti-coordination equilibrium, two identical agents adopt different behaviors—one stays while the other explores. Moreover, the Nash equilibria are of-ten multiple, so the game’s parameters alone do not determine which behaviors two self-interested agents will adopt. Which equilibrium a pair of agents reaches— if any—depends on how they adjust their exploration rates over time, the question we take up next. 

> Locally stable equilibria

Which strategy pairs do agents reach when they ad-just their exploration rates incrementally, and do those pairs coincide with the Nash equilibria? Finding a Nash equilibrium requires each agent to compare its payoff across the entire space of alternative exploration rates, and to perform this computation from the perspective of the opponent as well. Organisms may lack the cogni-tive capacity for this global comparison; more plausibly, they adjust behaviors incrementally, by small changes tested over the course of many games, or by mutations of small effect across generations. We therefore consider myopically updating agents, who after each game shift their exploration rate by a small amount in the direction that increases their payoff. After each game, each player either keeps its exploration rate or changes it to the neighboring value just below or just above. Each player chooses, among these three neighboring rates, written N(εi),the rate that maximizes its payoff against the other player’s current strategy; player 1, for example, adopts argmax ε∈N(ε1) π1(ε, ε2). For most values of the exoge-nous parameters this dynamic has four “locally stable equilibria” (sometimes called local Nash equilibria, in continuous games)—strategy pairs at which neither player can increase its own payoff by a local adjustment (Fig. 4, bottom row). We interpret these locally stable equilibria as the possible outcomes of an evolutionary, or myopic, process. Locally stable equilibria fall into three types: both agents staying, both agents exploring, and anti-coordination. At the “both explore” outcome (ε, ε), the common exploration rate lies close to the exploration rate at the interior local maximum of the single-agent reward curve, for the same distance d and reward bene-fit R (SI Appendix , Fig. S4). When the distant reward is small and far away (high d, low R), the “both explore” outcome disappears, leaving three locally stable equi-libria; in still more extreme regimes anti-coordination disappears as well, leaving “both stay” as the sole evo-lutionary outcome. Hence the number of behaviors a myopic pair can converge on falls as the distant reward becomes smaller or more distant—“both explore” is lost first and anti-coordination next—and wherever more than one outcome remains stable, the outcome the agents reach depends not only on the parameters of the environment but also on the exploration rates they begin with.

Strict dilemmas usually involve a conflict of in-terest 

Most strict social dilemmas are conflicts of interest be-tween the two agents: no alternative locally stable equi-librium makes both agents better off. Reaching a col-lectively better outcome requires one agent to change its behavior, and that agent receives a lower payoff: if both agents stay at the Nash equilibrium, it must start exploring; if both explore, it must stop. A mechanism that relies on each agent’s self-interest therefore cannot reach the better outcome in these dilemmas. We distinguish collective from Pareto dilemmas, sepa-rately for each of the three kinds of social dilemmas we have defined. A collective dilemma requires only that the collective payoff of the suboptimal pair (ε1, ε2) fall short of that of an alternative pair (˜ε1, ˜ ε2),

π(ε1, ε2) + π(ε2, ε1) < π(˜ε1, ˜ ε2) + π(˜ε2, ˜ ε1).A Pareto dilemma requires in addition that each agent individually receive a higher payoff at the alternative strategy pair, 

π(ε1, ε2) < π(˜ε1, ˜ ε2) and π(ε2, ε1) < π(˜ε2, ˜ ε1),so that the alternative strategy pair Pareto-dominates the suboptimal one. Every Pareto dilemma is there-fore also a collective dilemma. Ties are excluded: the strategy space is continuous, so exact equalities are non-generic. The game of Fig. 3 is a strict Pareto dilemma: both agents receive more at each anti-coordination Nash equilibrium than at the Nash equilibrium at which both explore. Pareto dilemmas of the first and second kinds are nearly universal, but strict Pareto dilemmas are rare ( SI Appendix , Table S1). Strict Pareto dilemmas resemble a Prisoner’s Dilemma in that each agent individually would receive a higher payoff at an alternative, dynam-ically achievable outcome. Such dilemmas can occur when the Nash equilibria are of the both-stay or the both-explore type, and in either case they lie on the border with anti-coordination in the plane of reward ratio R and distance d.The intuition for the case in which both agents ex-plore at the equilibrium is that the agent that must stop exploring gives up the distant reward entirely. At the symmetric equilibrium both agents explore, and each collects some share of what the distant site pro-vides. Reaching anti-coordination requires one of them to lower its exploration rate to zero, after which it never reaches the distant site at all and its payoff consists en-tirely of the near reward, which it now shares only on the time steps when its competitor happens to return there. That agent gains the share of the near reward it used to give up and loses everything it used to collect from the distant site. The change to anti-coordination raises that agent’s payoff only when, at the symmetric equilibrium, exploring earns it little more than staying would, which is why the strict dilemmas that admit a Pareto improvement lie along the border with anti-coordination rather than in the interior of the region where both agents explore.

# Discussion 

Organisms must constantly weigh exploiting the re-sources they know against exploring for better ones, and the balance that serves them best is rarely obvious. A forager that explores too much never remains long enough at any resource to profit from it, whereas one that explores too little stays at a poorer resource while a richer one goes undiscovered. The problem becomes harder still when other foragers share the environment, because the best behavior for each forager then depends on what its competitors do. We have asked how a sim-13 ple, evolutionarily plausible forager should resolve this tradeoff. Rather than model an algorithm that tunes its exploration over time, which demands a cognitive so-phistication many animals lack, we take the exploration rate to be a single fixed parameter, an intrinsic ε-greedy tendency shaped by natural selection to suit an animal’s ecology. When two foragers share a lattice and split rewards, the choice of exploration rate becomes a symmetric two-player game with social dilemmas of three nested kinds. In the mildest and most pervasive kind, some locally stable equilibrium is collectively suboptimal; in the most demanding kind, a strict social dilemma, a Nash equi-librium at which both agents stay or both explore is collectively worse than an anti-coordination outcome, in which one agent explores while the other stays at the near reward. Most strict dilemmas are conflicts of interest: the better outcome would lower the payoff of one agent, the one that would have to start exploring when both stay, or to stop when both explore. Only rarely does the alternative Pareto-dominate the equilib-rium, leaving both agents better off, as in a Prisoner’s Dilemma. A minimal signal, one agent informing the other that it has reached the distant reward, removes many of the dilemmas in which both agents explore at the equilibrium, most often those in which the alterna-tive Pareto-dominates. The behaviors the model produces have counterparts in foraging animals. The anti-coordination outcome re-sembles the division of effort documented when ant colonies exploit two feeders, in which some individuals commit to a known source while others continue to range, and colonies of different species differ in how they divide this effort, the kind of intrinsic variation our fixed exploration rate represents (Latty and Beek-man 2013). The signal in our model also has natural counterparts: foragers routinely respond to cues that a competitor has located food, whether an actively pro-duced pheromone or the observation of another individ-ual moving toward a resource (Giraldeau and Caraco 2018). Our results suggest that such communication is valuable not only because it conveys where food lies, but because it can replace an equilibrium that is col-lectively worse than an achievable alternative with a socially better division of effort. Among ecological models, the anti-coordination equilibrium resembles the producer–scrounger game, in which some individuals locate resources and others exploit what those individuals find (Barnard and Sibly 1981; Giraldeau and Caraco 2018); but the agent that stays in our model collects a separate resource rather than its competitor’s discovery, and a reward at a site both agents occupy is split. McNamara and Houston (McNamara and Houston 1985) bring learning into op-timal foraging theory for a single forager; our signaling model adds the minimal social element to that setting. Real foragers often search by committing to bouts of directed travel, as in intermittent-search and Lévy-walk models (Bénichou et al. 2011; Viswanathan et al. 1999), and bumblebees combine reward learning with strong spatial fidelity to familiar patches (Biernaskie et al. 2009; Crall et al. 2018); these studies concern the efficiency of search, a separate matter from the tradeoff that ε-greedy represents. Our results relate to several literatures that have developed largely apart from one another. Within re-inforcement learning, most treatments of exploration specify how it should change over time rather than what constant rate is best: regret-minimizing bandit algorithms (Lai and Robbins 1985; Auer et al. 2002), the Gittins index for discounted bandits (Gittins 1979), and the annealing schedules of stochastic optimization, which lower a temperature parameter that controls ex-ploration (Kirkpatrick et al. 1983). The schedules that can be proven sufficient are typically more conservative than what performs well in practice (Auer et al. 2002; Kuleshov and Precup 2014). Dynamic programming solves the underlying problem exactly (Bellman 1957; Puterman 1994; Sutton and Barto 2018), but it requires a model of the environment held in advance, including the reward at unvisited sites, which is what exploration would discover, and it is unlikely that animals, or hu-mans deciding in natural settings, perform such a com-putation. Dynamic programming and heuristics such as optimistic initialization (Machado et al. 2014) also return a policy rather than an exploration rate, and so do not say how often a cognitively simple agent should explore. Empirical comparisons find that ε-greedy per-forms close to the best available algorithms (Vermorel and Mohri 2005; Kuleshov and Precup 2014), but they tune the exploration rate separately for each problem and do not relate it to the structure of the environment. Our single-agent analysis supplies that relation for one spatial environment: the optimal rate increases with the value of the distant reward, and increases and then falls to zero with the distance to it. The standard multi-armed bandit problem has a sin-gle learner. Its multiplayer extensions, in which several learners compete, typically assume competitive exclu-sion: two agents that choose the same option collide and gain nothing (Boursier and Perchet 2024; Rosenski et al. 2016; Liu and Zhao 2010). Xu et al. (2023) instead allow rewards to be shared, as we do, and find a Nash equilibrium in which the number of agents at each op-tion is roughly proportional to its value, as in the ideal free distribution of foraging theory (Fretwell and Lucas 1969; Parker and Sutherland 1986). In those models an agent chooses an option directly; in ours its strategy is 14 an exploration rate and travel is costly, so the agents are not distributed in proportion to resource value, and the Nash equilibria instead fall into three discrete types: both agents exploring, one exploring and one staying, and both staying. Our assumption of a fixed exploration tendency re-lates our model to work on how nervous systems control exploration: dopamine signals the reward-prediction er-ror of temporal-difference learning (Schultz et al. 1997) and has been proposed to encode the opportunity cost of time (Niv et al. 2007), the cost that travel imposes in our model. How the brain arbitrates between habitual and deliberative control, and how animals weigh the opportunity cost of persistence while foraging, are ac-tive questions (Daw et al. 2005; Constantino and Daw 2015). Rather than ask how a single agent should time or direct its exploration, we ask what fixed rate is best. Our analysis of which outcomes are reached, as dis-tinct from which are optimal, places the model within evolutionary game theory: we ask not only whether a strategy pair is a Nash equilibrium but whether myopic, incremental adjustment converges on it (Smith and Price 1973; Hofbauer and Sigmund 1998). Certain forms of reinforcement learning converge, in a suitable limit, to the replicator dynamics (Börgers and Sarin 1997), so an exploration rate adjusted within a lifetime and one shaped by selection across generations obey related dy-namics. Our fixed exploration rate can then be treated as an evolved trait, and the consistent, heritable differ-ences in exploration documented as animal personality (Dingemanse and Wolf 2010; Wolf and Weissing 2010) as the variation on which selection would act. We have restricted our analysis to a one-dimensional lattice, whereas animals often forage in two or three di-mensions. Because the two agents move independently and interact only by splitting rewards, each agent’s payoff equals its solitary payoff reduced by half of the reward it collects when it shares a site with its competi-tor ( SI Appendix , section S4). The solitary payoff and the shared reward are both determined by how often a lone agent occupies each reward site at each time, so the envi-ronment enters the game only through those two single-agent quantities. We conjecture that the same qualitative behavior arises in higher-dimensional lattices, in other graphs, and in environments with clustered rewards, with the exploration rates at which an agent reaches the distant reward rescaled by how long discovery takes. The two-site model of SI Appendix , section S7, which retains distance only through a hitting probability and a hitting time and reproduces the dilemma ( SI Appendix ,Fig. S8), is the simplest such case. An unbiased random walk in three or more dimensions is transient, but our walk is biased toward the highest reward found and the lattice is finite; confirming the conjecture in higher dimensions remains for future study. A second limitation concerns the search rule. An 

ε-greedy forager does not commit to a bout of explo-ration: after a single exploratory step it exploits again with probability 1 − ε, which tends to return it toward the reward it just left, so that at the short distance exam-ined in SI Appendix , Fig. S9, an agent rarely reaches the distant site before returning to the near one unless its exploration rate is high. Osband et al. (2019) make the same point about ε-greedy in a different setting. Real foragers sometimes commit to bouts of travel, and a rule that commits, such as a Lévy walk (Viswanathan et al. 1999), may perform better even among rules with a single fixed parameter. We retain ε-greedy because our question concerns the exploration–exploitation tradeoff rather than the efficiency of search, and because it is the simplest rule with which to study that tradeoff. We have also held the environment fixed. In reality an organism may meet similarly structured environ-ments repeatedly, and an exploration rate shaped by selection would reflect that distribution of environments; nor can an agent know that its environment is static, or that a higher reward exists at all, and that uncertainty is part of what makes a nonzero exploration rate worth-while. If a patch is exhausted and foraging restarts at a fixed rate κ, so that both agents return to the near reward with no knowledge of the distant one, the two agents face exactly the same game as in the static model with the discount factor reduced from γ to γ(1 − κ), as long as that factor remains in the high-discount regime, so the equilibria and the social dilemma are those of the static model with a shorter foraging horizon ( SI Appendix , section S6). Our comparison across discount factors therefore already spans patches from long-lived to quickly renewed. Environmental change that shifts reward values during foraging, while leaving the agents’ positions and value estimates intact, remains to be ana-lyzed. Finally, we have treated only two competing agents. A population model with heritable exploration rates would show whether the socially suboptimal equilib-ria we identify are the ones that evolve, and whether signaling can itself be favored by selection. Costly dis-covery and shared rewards are the rule rather than the exception when animals forage together, so both the collectively suboptimal exploration we identify and the minimal signals that can remove it may be widespread in nature.

Numerical computation of equilibria 

Every payoff we report is computed by solving the lin-ear system above rather than by simulating the agents, so the payoff at any given pair of exploration rates is exact up to floating-point error. The only approximation in our results is the discretization of the strategy space: best responses, Nash equilibria and locally stable equi-libria are located on a grid of exploration rates in [0, 1 ],and the local best-response dynamics move by one grid step. The grid spacing therefore sets both the resolution at which we locate an equilibrium and the size of the adjustment against which a locally stable equilibrium must be stable. Under the local best-response dynam-ics both agents update simultaneously, each moving to whichever of its current rate and the two neighbor-ing rates pays most against the other agent’s current rate, and to the lower rate when two pay equally; a strategy pair is locally stable if neither agent’s payoff increases with a one-step change, and a trajectory that enters a cycle is assigned the first pair of the cycle it reaches. We classify a strategy pair as both agents ex-ploring if the two rates differ by at most one grid step, as anti-coordination if exactly one rate is zero, and as asymmetric otherwise. ## Competitive exploration in structured environments 

# S1 Variable distance to the distant reward 

In this section we relax the assumption that the distance to the distant reward is fixed. Foraging animals might encounter many different environment structures, and so we consider the possibility that d is not one fixed distance but rather a random variable dV ∼ Poisson (λ) + 1. In general, the optimal exploration rate when dV ∼ Poisson (λ) + 1 is close to the optimal exploration rate in the static environment d = λ + 1, although the explore vs. stay boundary is not quite the same (Fig. S3). For larger discount factors (longer foraging horizons), there is a region where the optimal exploration rate in a static environment is positive, but zero in a variable environment. The opposite is true for smaller discount factors (shorter foraging horizons): there is a region where the optimal exploration rate in a variable environment is positive, but zero in the static environment. For smaller distances d, a variable environment encourages slightly larger optimal exploration rates. Intuitively, an agent might want to explore more when there is a possibility of larger, but still manageable, distances, to ensure it reaches the reward in the most likely landscapes. This correspondence suggests that an exploration rate suited to the mean distance is close to the rate that is optimal when the distance varies, except near the boundary between exploring and not exploring, where the two environments disagree.

# S3 Proof that there is always a dilemma of the first kind when d ≥ 2

A dilemma of the first kind exists whenever some locally stable equilibrium is collectively worse than a strategy pair the agents could reach instead, so it suffices to exhibit one such point and one such alternative. We take both agents staying as the point and anti-coordination as the alternative. First, the “both stay” strategy (ε1, ε2) = ( 0, 0 ) is locally stable; the same argument establishes that ε = 0 is a local maximum in the single-agent case. Second, any anti-coordination strategy has a higher collective payoff than both agents staying. 

S3.1 Both stay is locally stable 

Local stability at (0, 0 ) means that a small amount of exploration does not pay. We establish local stability by comparing what an exploring agent forgoes against what it can gain. In particular, we consider a lower bound on the expected opportunity cost, a minimal expected opportunity cost, and we consider an upper bound on the expected rewards of exploration. An upper bound on the expected rewards of exploration minus the minimal opportunity cost amounts to an upper bound on the difference π(ε) − π(0). We will find ε > 0 such that this difference is less than zero. 

Theorem 1. Let R 0, Rd > 0 and γ ∈ (0, 1 ). When d ≥ 2, the point (ε1, ε2) = ( 0, 0 ) is locally stable. # S7 A two-site model without explicit spatial structure 

We construct a simplification of the model that discards explicit spatial structure and retains only two quantities through which distance acts: the probability that an exploring agent reaches the distant site, and the expected time it takes to get there. At the one parameter combination we examined, the simplification reproduces the four locally stable equilibria, their ranking by collective payoff, and the shape of the basins of attraction, which suggests that the dilemma does not require the lattice itself. We consider only two sites, indexed 1 and 2 with associated rewards r1 < r2. The agents start at site 1. The strategy used by the agent is the following: • With probability 1 − ε, move toward the best reward the agent has seen. (Moving toward the best reward it has seen may mean staying at the current site, or trying to go to the other site, which arises only if the agent has seen the higher reward and is currently at the site with the lower reward.) • With probability ε, explore, i.e. try to go to the other site.
