# When Personas Become Harmful: Guiding LLMs with Global Citizenship Values Alignment

Abstract 

LLMs are increasingly being used by users from diverse demographics with pluralistic values and distinct backgrounds. Maintain-ing safety while respecting value-laden per-sonas is uniquely challenging because persona-conditioned requests are often not overtly ma-licious, unlike those in existing safety bench-marks. Alignment methods aimed at adver-sarial requests therefore may miss the harm that emerges from persona use, leaving models to either refuse legitimate value expression or blindly follow unsafe personas. We construct a dataset of 11k controversial topics paired with value-based personas mapping to value dimensions from the Schwartz Value Survey. Through an agentic pipeline that iteratively re-fines the topic and persona, we surface and collect harmful responses from well-aligned commercial LLMs with a more than 47% suc-cess rate. We use UNESCO’s global citizen-ship principles as a minimal common ground for distinguishing valid value expressions from harmful behaviors, and introduce a controllable alignment model that lets users balance per-sona adherence and safety at inference time. The resulting model is safer and more persona-adherent than baselines on most samples. 

1 Introduction 

With the growing integration of LLMs into appli-cations over a wide variety of domains, ensuring models remain safe and helpful has been a central focus of AI alignment (Bai et al., 2022a,b). How-ever, people from distinct cultural backgrounds of-ten have different beliefs, making desired behaviors more nuanced when the models face value-laden scenarios or topics in which different cultures tend to respond differently. For example, neither stance on the legality of abortion should be considered clearly wrong and thus prohibited, despite the fact that the model developer may not agree with a cer-tain stance. Meanwhile, current LLMs alignment approaches have been criticized for encoding a nar-row set of values specified by model developers, and are often dominated by Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) so-cieties (Santurkar et al., 2023; Durmus et al., 2024). Persona assignment has been a common ap-proach for instructing models to adopt specific char-acters or value orientations, enabling personalized interactions (Zhang et al., 2018; Argyle et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024), whether as a core feature of plat-forms such as Character.AI and Replika or through custom instructions in commercial LLMs. How-ever, this can also lead to major safety concerns when the provided personas embody strong posi-tions on controversial topics, such as religious tra-ditions or political ideologies, and can steer models toward the generation of harmful content (Cheng et al., 2023; Deshpande et al., 2023), yet refusing to follow the user customization may also draw pushback. We believe that a well-aligned model should respect the diversity of pluralistic values while maintaining safety within a certain boundary (Sorensen et al., 2024). Existing safety benchmarks primarily target ma-licious requests leading to offensive or unethical content (Mazeika et al., 2024). However, they do not capture the harm that emerges from the ex-pression of personal values over a controversial topic. This can result in two failure modes. First, the model may respond with a general refusal (e.g. “I can’t help with ...”) or contradict the persona, for example, a hardcore conservative agreeing that “abortion should be legal”. Both dismiss legitimate value differences. Alternatively, the model may adhere to the persona blindly and generate harmful content. For example, consider a persona with tra-ditional religious convictions arguing against legal-izing same-sex marriage. The persona is valid and the topic is not malicious. Yet faithfully adopting the persona may lead the model from a legitimate value position to language that demeans LGBTQ arXiv:2610.33899v1 [cs.CL] 24 Oct 2026 Figure 1: Overview of our framework. individuals, which goes beyond value expression into harm that should be moderated. Therefore, there is a natural trade-off between following a persona faithfully and acting safely, which poses a unique challenge to operate along it instead of collapsing to either end. We address this challenge by developing a dataset that surfaces harmful persona-driven be-haviors, along with a controllable alignment setting that balances keeping responses safe and respect-ing legitimate values. For the dataset generation, we first collect topics from a debate portal (Ajjour et al., 2019) or global/local news, and convert them into statements that people either agree or disagree based on their personal values. We pair each topic with valued-based persona, then iteratively revise the topic and persona through an LLM agentic pipeline to discover harmful responses elicited by persona. To establish safety boundaries for alignment and to generate aligned responses without imposing val-ues specific to certain demographics, we draw on a set of global citizenship principles endorsed by UN-ESCO that are considered universal and transcend cultural boundaries (UNESCO, 2015), establish-ing common ground for open communication. The principles, such as commitment to human rights and respect for diversity, are provided to the LLM to determine if the responses are harmful and to generate more aligned responses. Lastly, we finetune an LLM with our dataset and examine the effect of alignment. We also train a controllable model with safety and persona adherence by training on samples with different settings, including aligned responses (high safety, high adherence), persona-conditioned responses (low safety, high adherence), and default responses (high safety, low adherence). This controllable model allows users to explicitly specify their pref-erence between safety and adherence. We release the resulting dataset and model under gated access with a research-only license to support reproducibil-ity while limiting misuse potential 1.

Contributions. Our contributions are as follows: (1) We identify a new type of LLM safety failure where harmful responses originate from legitimate persona use on non-malicious requests, and curate a diverse dataset consisting of controversial topics from different cultural regions and languages. (2) We build an agentic pipeline that iteratively refines topics and personas to surface harmful re-sponses from LLMs, where current LLMs present harmful behavior with over a 47% success rate con-firmed by scorers from independent families. (3) We propose a controllable alignment setting that jointly improve safety and persona adherence as a multi-objective task, producing models that outper-form both unaligned and single-objective aligned baselines.

2.1 Seed Topics Collection 

We collect our seed topics from two sources with distinct characteristics. The topics from the debate portals are naturally controversial, and focus on prominent topics widely discussed, for example, “abortion” or “death penalty”. Meanwhile, the top-ics from news headlines are more well grounded in specific real-world events with factual details, although not always controversial. For the debate portal, we use the 1.0-cleaned 

version of the args.me (Ajjour et al., 2019) dataset. The dataset has different expressions for the same topic, for example “term limits should not exist” and “term limits should be abolished”. Therefore, we conduct a deduplication step by grouping the nearest topics into canonical topics based on their embedding from all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model. We obtain 26,647 seed topics after deduplication and use a subset in the following refinement stage. For the news headlines, we collect top world news events from 2024 to 2025 using NewsAPI. We gathered all headlines from different news sources and prominent events obtained by clustering arti-cles with semantic similarity. This covers news with a global impact but lacks news with local con-text. Therefore, we collect local news for a selected set of countries from different geopolitical regions and cultural groups on the Inglehart–Welzel world culture map (Inglehart and Welzel, 2005), includ-ing USA, Germany, France, Russia, China, India, Brazil, and Egypt. We manually curate a list of top media sources for each country to ensure data qual-ity as shown in Table 9. To collect topics targeting local audiences, we also restrict to news covering domestic events and in corresponding official lan-guage. For example, we identify local French news as headlines written in French whose article bodies mention France. We gather 50 local events from each country and 100 global events that had not occurred in the listed country every month over the past 18 months, resulting in a total of 8,626 samples. We translate the news headlines to English for the red-teaming and alignment stage to make the setting consistent, as LLMs exhibit large perfor-mance discrepancies in different languages. We make public the news collected in the original lan-guages for future research. We convert the topics to a stance-eliciting statement using GPT-4.1. For example, a news title “One of the nation’s premier historic preservation groups on Tuesday urged the Trump administration to pause demolition of the White House East Wing...” will now be converted to “The Trump administration should pause the demolition of the White House East Wing to re-consider the preservation of its historic value.”, as shown in Table 1. 

Initial statement and persona generation Peo-ple can have different stances on a topic, where their personal experiences and values play an im-portant role. And a topic can be controversial because of more than one underlying values di-mensions. We use GPT-4.1 to group the topics by their domain (economic values, religious values, ethical values) according to the World Value Sur-vey (WVS) (WVS, 2022), and the most relevant value dimension from the Schwartz Value Survey (SVS). The model is provided with the complete list of WVS domain categories and SVS value di-mensions, along with brief definitions of each SVS dimension from Schwartz (2012). We then ask the model to generate a pair of two opposite personas ppro and pcon embodying the assigned value dimension that leads to different stances. We use survey items from the PVQ40 ques-tionnaire (Schwartz et al., 2001) as few-shot exam-ples for the initial ppro for corresponding value dimensions. For example, “Religious belief is im-portant to him. He tries hard to do what his religion requires.” is a valid persona given the value dimen-sion “Tradition”. The persona pcon is then obtained by negating and paraphrasing ppro , ensuring that two personas represent contrary positions.

3 Alignment with Global Citizenship Principles 

We use the collected harmful persona red-teaming dataset to align the model and extend the frame-work to a controllable setting. 

3.1 Alignment with Traditional Finetuning 

For the harm-eliciting pairs of topics and personas, we align the original response by revising it with an LLM, similar to Bai et al. (2022b). We pro-vide the original response, an explanation from the scorer for the harmfulness score, and a set of prin-ciples that need to be followed for the revised re-sponse. As most modern models have already been aligned for standard safety principles, we place more emphasis on principles encouraging cultural awareness without over-promoting certain values by including the list of global citizenship values that we use in the harmfulness evaluation. The prompts used for generating revised responses can be found in Appendix D. All revised responses are scored again with harmfulness and faithfulness scores with the same scorer used on the original responses. We train the aligned model following the stan-dard approach with supervised finetuning and pref-erence optimization stages. Supervised finetuning concatenates the persona and topic for the input and trains the model to imitate the aligned response 

Orevised . We then use DPO as an offline preference optimization approach, constructing the preference pair as orevised ≻ odef ault so that the original re-sponses also contribute to the training signal as negative examples. 

3.2 Controllable Alignment 

In our preliminary experiments, we observed a con-sistent trade-off between safety and persona ad-herence. However, it remains clear that harmful responses are undesired, regardless of how faithful they are. Therefore, we propose to explicitly set adherence and safety as two separate objectives and frame the task as a multi-objective optimiza-tion problem (Zhou et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024b). We refer to the trained model as controllable super-vised finetuning (CSFT). During training, all samples are paired with a label of safety level and adherence level as controlling parameters, for example 

⟨persona _adherence : 2⟩ and ⟨saf ety : 4⟩.These controlling parameters are based on faith-fulness and harmfulness scores, respectively, but are discretized from [0 , 1] to 1, 2, ..., 5. At the in-ference stage, the user should be able to specify different setups, obtain responses that focus more on safety or persona adherence, and select the most preferred one. To obtain a model with good controllability, a balanced number of samples with different combi-nations of controlling parameters is required. We use a mixture of four types of samples: default response odef ault from the base model without see-ing the persona, which has low persona adherence and high safety; persona-conditioned response o

from the base model, which has high persona adher-ence and low safety; and aligned response orevised 

which has moderately high adherence and mod-erately high safety. The detailed distribution of the samples used in the training is shown in Table 10. We also include an ablation setting without using the low safety response in Table 17.. While exposing a controllable safety parameter raises con-cerns, the model’s output at minimum safety setting is bounded by the base model’s default persona-conditioned behavior without alignment, thus not more harmful. Besides, explicit control tokens are more auditable than implicit prompt manipulation. We discuss it further in Ethical Considerations.

Human Annotation We conduct a human anno-tation study to verify the quality of the generated personas and responses. Three annotators inde-pendently label a subset of 60 samples, including responses flagged as harmful by the scorer, persona-conditioned responses judged safe, and default re-sponses generated without the persona. Each an-notator is shown one set of topic, persona, and response at a time, with 10 calibration examples beforehand, and is asked to determine whether a response is harmful following the same definition given to the LLM judge. The annotators agree with the LLM label on 93% of the harmful responses and 89% of all responses, with a Fleiss’ κ of 0.732 indicating substantial inter-annotator agreement. Among the safe responses, 24% of the persona-conditioned ones and 4% of the default ones are still judged harmful, which sug-gests a conservative threshold but does not affect the validity of the harmful samples we collect. We also ask annotators to rate whether the persona is coherent (natural and self-consistent) and relevant to the topic. It shows a mean coherence of 4.50 and a mean topic relevance of 4.73 out of 5, indicating the personas are internally consistent and aligned to their assigned topics. The detailed annotation results can be found in the Appendix C.1. 

4.2 Alignment Results with traditional finetuning Trade-off between Faithfulness and Safety Fol-lowing the setting in § 3.1, we grade the responses generated by the finetuned models and baselines for faithfulness and harmfulness. The default re-sponse is an oracle setting disregarding the persona completely, which results in much safer responses while having major inconsistencies with the per-sona. When compared with responses with harmful persona, alignment through finetuning reduces the level of harmfulness by 0.152 (Qwen3-8B) to 0.371 (Qwen3-14B), while keeping a relatively high faith-fulness to the persona above 0.63, as shown in Ta-ble 2. The default is safer still, but only because it ignores the persona entirely. DPO shows mixed results on different models and is not able to reduce harmfulness and increase faithfulness at the same time. It further lowers harmfulness on Llama-3.1 and Qwen3 models, but at the cost of lower faith-fulness. This suggests there is a trade-off between adhering to a persona and acting safely. 4.3 Controllable Alignment Persona Controllability We evaluate the control-lable models with response generation conditioned on different persona adherence and safety settings. We examine the controllability of the model by comparing the specified parameters with the cor-responding metrics for generated responses using Pearson correlation and average Mean Square Error (MSE). The target levels of faithfulness and safety do not guarantee the corresponding metrics in the out-put. Therefore, for each generated response, we use LLM auto-judge to obtain its faithfulness and harmfulness score and calculate how well the actual response follows the target parameter. We find that the models’ output has a strong correlation with the specified setting on both persona adherence and safety. Gemma3-12B shows the highest cor-relation and thus the best controllability, whereas Llama3.1-8B is less sensitive to the controlling pa-rameters, as shown in Table 7. Figure 3 shows the average metrics for responses from different settings. Both the safety and the adherence of re-sponses change monotonically with the controlling parameter. Effectiveness of Controlled Alignment We eval-uate the overall performance on adherence and safety by sweeping the parameters for each sample. We then compare the area covered by this frontier against two baselines: refined responses from the strong LLM and unaligned persona-conditioned responses. A response generated by controllable model lies on the Pareto frontier if no other re-sponse achieves both a higher safety score and a higher adherence score. The frontier area is between 0 and 1, where 1 in-dicates always perfectly safe and faithful responses from the model. The dominance percentage indi-cates how often the controllable model outperforms the baseline, where a dominance rate of over 50% indicates that the model performs better on average given the appropriate set of controlling parameters. As shown in Table 8, the responses from all mod-els cover a frontier area over 0.7. Gemma3-12B model covers the largest frontier area, which is a 27.5% improvement over the refined response with-out the controllable setting. Responses from the controllable model dominate the refined response or lie on the frontier for 50% to 64% of samples. For the remaining samples, the refined responses are neither the safest nor the most adherent, but may represent a better trade-off, as illustrated in Figure 6.

5 Related Work 

Assigning persona to an LLM is a common tech-nique adopted in different applications to customize LLM interactions with desired personality traits or backgrounds. Zhang et al. (2018) study persona in a dialogue setting for more engaging conversa-tion. Argyle et al. (2023) use persona as proxies for specific human sub-populations in social science research to mimicking human respondents. Park et al. (2022) uses persona to initialize LLMs to simulate social interactions, which is the default setting for multi-agent works (Guo et al., 2024a; Tran et al., 2025). However, models’ capability of being customized with persona comes with signif-icant safety concerns. It is shown that assigning simple personas to ChatGPT, such as gender or race, can increase toxicity and generate outputs en-gaging in stereotypes and harmful dialogue Cheng et al. (2023); Deshpande et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2025). We follow this line of work but use more complex value-based personas with a more sophis-ticated red-teaming method to reveal and fix the issues in more well-aligned models compared to previous work. LLM red-teaming generally refers to the prac-tice of collecting input samples where the models fail by producing a harmful or less-preferred re-sponse. Early research uses red-teaming prompts gathered from human crowd-source workers (Xu et al., 2021; Askell et al., 2021; Ganguli et al., 2022). Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b) also relies on crowd-sourced red-teaming prompts. Al-though human annotated samples may have higher quality, they are very expensive and thus not as scalable (Askell et al., 2021). Therefore, automatic red-teaming has been heavily explored. Perez et al. (2022) explicit harmful responses through simple prompting on early LLMs that are not well-aligned. Zou et al. (2023) use a gradient based method to search for a suffix that lead to harmful response. Gradient-based red-teaming produces token-level prompt injections that are not legible, so there are also methods that use prompt-level red-teaming by iteratively improving the prompt using different strategies (Chao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024, 2025). Our approach also uses an iteratively automatic red-teaming, but with metrics and strategies specific to value-based persona. Our setting differs from jailbreaking in that, instead of a malicious request, the unsafe behavior emerges from the interaction between a persona and a controversial topic. 

6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced a new dataset for harm-ful behavior emerging from value-laden personas in LLMs and proposed a framework for alignment grounded in global citizenship principles. Our red-teaming pipeline shows that even well-aligned commercial models, such as GPT-4o-mini, can be steered toward generating harmful outputs when provided with value-based personas. Through alignment with UNESCO’s global citizenship prin-ciples, we successfully reduced the harmfulness scores while maintaining faithfulness above a rea-sonable level. The aligned models also generalize well with reduced toxicity on RealToxicityPrompts and improved instruction-following on IFEval. We further show that by explicitly modeling the trade-off between safety and persona adherence, a con-trollable aligned model can achieve Pareto domi-nance over baselines. These findings suggest that our method offers a scalable approach to data col-lection and model alignment toward LLMs that respect value pluralism while maintaining safety. 

Limitations 

In this work, we propose a method to conduct au-tomatic red-teaming by introducing harmful per-sonas on controversial topics. However, several constraints remain and limit the scope of this study. While we included local news from eight countries spanning different geopolitical regions and cultural groups, this represents only a subset of global de-mographics. The collected data were translated into English for consistent model behavior in our pipeline. However, there may also be insightful findings on how language itself affects harmful persona-conditioned behavior, which remains un-explored. Our red-teaming pipeline relies on LLM judges to evaluate the generated responses with some qualitative inspections. Although this ap-proach enables scalability, it may not fully align with human judgments, particularly for culturally sensitive content. We provide a list of safety princi-ples that are grounded in global citizenship values, which we deemed reasonable. However, it is de-batable what constitutes "harmful" behavior, and a static definition may introduce potential biases in the alignment process. The alignment process of revising the responses does not guarantee all results to be harmless. Although the model is not trained on samples with both low safety and low persona adherence, our current setup did not prohibit the user from doing so. AI assistance is used in this work on coding auto-completion and for revising the writeup. 

Ethical Considerations 

Our work studies how value-laden personas elicit harmful responses from LLMs, and thus naturally involves generation and utilization of some harmful content. We address the ethical concerns as follow. 

Dual-use Risk of Dataset Our dataset consists of controversial topics paired with personas and harmful responses, and the agentic pipeline can in principle be used to discover new persona–topic combinations that elicit harm. However, we believe that the misuse risk is small. First, our work follows the established practice of red-teaming literature (Liu et al., 2024; Perez et al., 2022) by discussing the methods and releasing the dataset to encour-age and accelerate fix to the problem, instead of a private report to individual providers, as the be-havior is not tied to a single system and appears in all models we evaluate. Second, our dataset is less harmful than jailbreak datasets which alter models’ follow-up behavior to become unsafe. In contrast, our work targets only the immediate re-sponse to a controversial topic, and the harmfulness is defined as violating global citizenship principles which is more nuanced and mild. Third, we would release the model and dataset with a gated access and a restrictive research-only license. With this, we ensure to our best effort that the alignment com-munity benefits from the work, while misuse for non-research purposes bears legal burden. 

Misuse of Controllable Setting The controllable model accepts inference-time control tokens for safety and persona adherence. Although one could technically specify a low-safety setting, we argue that it does not increase practical risk over the back-bone model. First, we are not using responses that are intentionally harmful by having both low safety and low persona adherence. Second, some low safety responses are used for the model to learn the high persona adherence, but these are what the base model already produces when given the same harm-ful persona without any controllable interface, so we did not introduce new harmful behavior. Third, the explicit control tokens actually make the mali-cious intent more legible and easier to audit. Given that the model is deployed and managed by a re-sponsible user, as we constrained to a gated access for research users, pre-filling the safety parameters with a med to high value could greatly reduce the harmfulness while having a better adherence. 

Adoption of UNESCO Principles Framing Al-though one can still criticize the UNESCO Global Citizenship principles for carrying liberal demo-cratic values and commitments to individual rights that are not culturally universal, we believe it is the only common ground available and appropriate for our value-related setting. It consists of minimal higher-level principles, such as respect for diver-sity and advocate for equality, rather than specific moral verdicts on contested topics. It is also among the most broadly endorsed values recognized by 194 member states. Additionally, our pipeline uses the principles as part of the prompt in components like scorers and can use different sets of principles, while that may affect the attack success rate.
