Abstract 

Large language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate human decision-making, yet their outputs often under-represent human behavioural heterogeneity. We in-vestigate whether conditioning LLMs on Social Value Orientation (SVO, a measure of how individuals value their own outcomes relative to others’) can better reproduce human behaviour in a sequential social dilemma. Using experimental data from two variants of the Centipede Game (CG) as reference, we compare the default behaviour of eight LLMs with behaviour generated after conditioning them on SVO profiles drawn from the human sample. We find that the default strategies vary substantially across models, but are gen-erally distant from the reference distributions. However, conditioning them on SVO pro-files systematically steers their strategic behaviour, improving alignment with the human reference by up to 70% with respect to the default. Across models, higher induced SVO values decrease the probability of stopping the game, reproducing the relationship between prosociality and cooperation observed in human behaviour. Together with the sensitivity of LLMs’ elicited SVO to prompt and order effects, these results suggest that the usefulness of SVO for behavioural simulation does not depend on LLMs possessing stable social pref-erences, but rather on their ability to map social preferences onto corresponding strategic choices. 

# 1 Introduction 

Since the public release of ChatGPT in 2022, the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) has dramatically surged. Although these models operate by predicting the next token in a sequence given a specific context (or prompt ), their textual output has become virtually indistinguishable from humans [12]. Notably, recent works have argued that LLMs appear to simulate complex features of human cognition, such as theory of mind [24, 13], emotional intelligence [20], and even deceptive mechanisms [9]. For these reasons, even though LLMs are neither sentient nor intentional, an increasing corpus of research is investigating their capabilities as strategic decision-makers in both indi-vidual and interactive tasks [11, 5, 2]. Remarkably, LLMs appear to be capable of predicting the outcome of social science experiments and show signs of social preferences, reciprocity, and inference of the beliefs and intentions of others in economic contexts [4, 10]. On the other hand, current concerns include the LLMs’ tendency to understate the diversity of human re-sponses and to exhibit systematic biases toward groups that are inaccurately or inadequately represented in their training data [1, 25]. In addition, results may vary depending on factors 1arXiv:2610.01667v1 [cs.GT] 1 Oct 2026 such as task-specific setting, framing, prompt design, and model configuration [21, 22]. Thus, it remains unclear to what extent these systems can constitute a robust proxy for human samples in behavioural and social sciences [7]. A potential avenue to address these issues consists in explicitly conditioning the model on certain variables such as psychological traits and demographics [3]. Indeed, if prior infor-mation on the population-level distribution of these variables is available, it may be possible to construct a population of LLM-based agents representing distinct personas , thus approxi-mating the heterogeneity observed in human samples. For example, recent research [18] has explored how certain personality traits, measured through the Big Five framework, can influ-ence cooperation in a social dilemma. The study assessed LLMs’ personality scores, examined strategic behaviour with and without explicit personality information, and tested the effects of independently setting each personality dimension to extreme values. Here, we go beyond this methodology by asking not only (1) how the decision-making of an LLM can be steered to obtain a diverse range of strategic behaviours, but also (2) in which cases they can align with a known human sample, through an analysis grounded on experimental data. The present study builds on a previous behavioural experiment [19] about a sequential social dilemma known as the Centipede Game (CG) [17]. In this task, two players take turns to decide how to split a shared resource (see Figure 1 and section 4). At each decision node, the focal player can either stop the interaction and receive a larger share of the resource (action Take ) or proceed to the following turn and pass this decision to the co-player (action Pass ). The dilemma arises particularly when the shared resource increases with each step, forcing each player to choose between one’s immediate personal gain and the greater long-term benefit available to both players. Since the CG describes turn-based mixed-motive interactions, it is particularly relevant in the context of LLM-based research. Indeed, these systems are frequently used as conversational chatbots, and users engage in repeated exchanges with them to achieve a personal goal. However, prompts often entail a cost for the user, while the LLM consumes varying resources to generate responses that may help the user reach their goal. The sequential nature of the conversation, along with its costs and its uncertain gains, make the CG a suitable approximation for these interactions. The behavioural experiment on which the present analysis is based compared the outcomes of two under-explored variations of a linearly-incremental CG, namely the zero-end CG (where the final step is a deadline leading to zero payoffs for both players, n = 164) and the fair-end CG (where the last split is a fair division of the shared resource, n = 162) [19]. The findings suggest that the prospect of a fair outcome, even though uncertain, prompts subjects to go beyond self-interested behaviour. Furthermore, cooperative behaviour in the CG is correlated with individuals’ prosociality, measured through the Social Value Orientation (SVO) [15], a resource allocation task that measures how much weight a person gives to the welfare of others in relation to their own, thus corroborating the idea that social preferences constitute an important driver of human behaviour in this type of social dilemmas. Although there is broad consensus that SVO is associated with cooperation in social dilem-mas because it captures how individuals subjectively value outcomes, it has not yet been applied in the context of the conditioning and alignment of LLMs in strategic interactions [16, 6]. The present study aims to bridge this gap by investigating whether inducing a particular SVO profile in an LLM can steer its behavioural choices in the CG accordingly. Specifically, our objective is the comparison between the default behaviour of different LLMs playing the CG without any conditioning and the behaviour obtained after prompting the LLM with several SVO profiles retrieved from the experimental data. We expect that, in the latter case, the outcome distribution at the population level, obtained by aggregating all behavioural profiles according to the preva-2lence of their respective SVO values in human data, is more closely aligned with the diverse human reference than the default distribution. In particular, we hypothesise that this increased diversity stems from the fact that a prosocial conditioning leads to higher-payoff outcomes in the CG, while an individualistic conditioning shifts the LLM’s behaviour towards the rational but sub-optimal outcome in the CG. We tested eight LLMs (four closed models of the GPT family, two versions of Qwen-3.5, Llama-3.3 and Gemma-4). All models, used through the Python library litellm 1, were employed without reasoning and all results are based on the top-5 log-probabilities of the first generated token (see section 4 for more details). Our prompts fall into two main categories, labelled as blinded and unblinded [8]. In the blinded system prompt the LLM is told to be aparticipant in a behavioural experiment , and to choose the action it prefers the most. In the unblinded system prompt, instead, we make the objective of our analysis explicit by imposing the LLM to be an expert in predicting human decision-making in behavioural experiments ,and to predict the most preferred choice by a participant in our experiment (all prompts are included in Appendix A). This choice is motivated by the fact that, while blinding 2 prevents experimental design from influencing human participants’ decisions, it may create confounds in LLM simulations by forcing the model to infer missing context differently across treatment conditions [8]. Our contributions are the following. We analyse the LLMs’ default behaviour at each stage of both the zero- and fair-end CGs. We find a diverse range of default behaviours: some models follow rational self-interested strategies, while others deviate from the latter by displaying vari-ous degrees of cooperation. Notably, these behaviours remain relatively stable across game and prompt types. However, a common feature of these strategies is the higher degree of determin-ism compared to the human reference. The SVO conditioning procedure allows us to partially overcome this limitation. Indeed, through SVO conditioning, the LLM-generated output sys-tematically gets closer to the human experimental reference relative to the default behaviour in the CGs, with Qwen3.5-27B and GPT-5.4 Mini achieving the best fit in the zero- and fair-end CGs, respectively. The overall improvement is particularly relevant in the fair-end case. However, many models preserve their characteristic behaviour, often remaining further from the human distribution than the baseline threshold set by a random agent that selects each ac-tion with equal probability. The improvement obtained through SVO conditioning is due to the ability of LLMs to map the induced SVO profile to strategic choices in the CG. Indeed, we find that higher SVO values (i.e., more prosocial profiles) lead to a lower probability of stopping at each step of the game. Nevertheless, when SVO in LLMs is elicited through the original task, results appear to sensitive to the prompt type and subject to order bias, suggesting that their SVOs are closer to task-conditioned profiles than to stable human-like social preferences.

## SVO conditioning drives LLMs towards more representative behaviour in the CG. 

Having established the default behaviour of eight LLMs in the CGs, we now investigate the effects of SVO conditioning on the behavioural alignment of these models. As stated in the Introduction, we repeatedly condition LLMs on several SVO profiles retrieved from a human dataset and ask them to make decisions in the CGs in accordance with these profiles (see sec-tion 4 for more details). In the context of the CG, prosocial individuals tend to be less inclined to choose Take at a given node, whereas individualists may pursue a more self-interested strat-egy, thereby increasing the chances of stopping the game early. SVO conditioning thus aims to induce representative behaviour in the CG by exploiting the potential correlation between an individual’s social preferences and their strategic behaviour. If such relationships are suf-ficiently represented in an LLM’s training data, the model may be able to reproduce them, thereby generating responses that more closely resemble human behaviour. Figure 3 shows that SVO conditioning indeed results in a systematic reduction in mean absolute error (MAE) 4 with respect to the human experimental reference. Here, the MAE is computed between the take probabilities generated by the LLMs and those estimated from the human reference data. The improvement is particularly large in the fair-end CG (right panels), with an average relative reduction in MAE of 36% (standard deviation σ = 0.21), than in the zero-end CG (left panels), where the average relative reduction is 25% ( σ = 0.16). One possible explanation for this difference is that the LLMs’ default behaviours in the fair-end game are more dissimilar to the human reference ( μbaseline , f air = 0.46, σbaseline , f air = 0.20) than in the zero-end game ( μbaseline ,zero = 0.32, σbaseline ,zero = 0.08), leaving greater room for improvement under SVO conditioning. Through this procedure, the gap between game types decreases substantially ( μcond , f air = 0.28, σcond , f air = 0.16; μcond ,zero = 0.24, σcond ,zero = 0.07). The results are typically consistent across prompt variants. Finally, for both games, the lowest MAE ( ≈ 0.1) is obtained by SVO conditioning: Qwen3.5-27B with unblinded prompts in the zero-end CG, and GPT-5.4-Mini with blinded prompts in the fair-end CG. The optimal terminal node distributions are compared to the human reference in the bottom panels of Figure 3. Despite the improvements achieved through SVO conditioning, in the zero-end CG, a ran-dom agent that chooses Take with 50% chance at each decision node more closely approximates the human distribution than all but two models, Qwen3.5-27B and GPT-5.4-Mini (see the red dotted vertical line in the top panels). In the fair-end case, on the other hand, most models outperform the random player, with the exception of Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma-4-31B, and GPT-5.4-Nano. This finding suggests that the utility of conditioning LLMs on social preferences is highlighted by the presence of the fair outcome of the fair-end CG.

## LLMs’ elicited SVO is sensitive to prompt type and shows signs of order bias. 

The results of the previous section suggest that, when LLMs are explicitly conditioned on SVO, they appear to adapt their output in the CG accordingly, with prosocial profiles leading to more cooperative choices in the game. Motivated by this effect, we elicit the LLMs’ inherent social preferences to examine if they can explain the default output observed in the CGs. We then esti-mate the default SVO distributions of the LLMs (that is, their SVO distributions when no SVO conditioning is applied) using the original Slider Measure developed by Murphy et al. [15]. Specifically, each distribution is obtained by aggregating independent responses across the six primary items of the Slider Measure (see section 4 for more details). On average, however, we do not detect any effect between the elicited default SVO and the default output in the CGs (see Figure 10 in Appendix F). In this section, we further explore the limitations of using this task to draw meaningful conclusions about LLMs’ inherent social preferences. In particular, we investigate whether LLMs are robust to changes in how the SVO task is presented. For each LLM, the SVO distribution is computed under four conditions, combining two prompt types (blinded and unblinded) with two orderings of the nine response options within each item of the task (standard and inverted). The comparison between blinded and unblinded prompts aims to capture sensitivity to prompt type, while reversing the original option order allows us to test for potential order bias. 8Figure 5 reports the shifts in mean SVO induced by these two manipulations. The left panel compares the blinded condition, in which the LLM is asked to make its preferred choice as a participant, with the unblinded condition, in which it is asked to predict the choice a participant would prefer. On average, SVO tends to be higher under the unblinded prompt, suggesting that the elicited social preferences depend on whether LLMs are asked to choose for themselves or predict the preferences of others. This tendency is not uniform across models. Most models exhibit relatively small shifts, whereas GPT-5.4 is particularly sensitive to unblinding, shifting from a self-interested to a prosocial SVO when options are presented in the standard order. The right panel of Figure 5 compares the standard and inverted ordering of the response options. Since this manipulation leaves the underlying allocation choices unchanged, invariance to op-tion ordering would place observations along the diagonal. Instead, inverting the option order tends to lower SVO on average, indicating a systematic order effect. These effects are again heterogeneous, as both Gemma-4-31B and GPT-5.4-Nano have a lower average SVO under the inverted option order, while GPT-5.4 shifts in the opposite direction. Nevertheless, all points in the scatter plots fall in the range defined by the modes of the human distribution obtained in the experimental reference (see the empty circles on the diagonals).

We investigated whether SVO can be used to condition LLMs to generate more representative behaviour in a sequential social dilemma. Using human experimental data from two variants of the CG as a reference, we compared the default behaviour of eight LLMs with behaviour ob-tained by conditioning the same models on SVO profiles observed in the human sample. First, we show that the default behaviour of LLMs differs substantially across models. While some models follow almost deterministic quasi-rational strategies, others exhibit varying degrees of cooperation. Within each model, however, strategies remain relatively stable across the two game variants and prompt types. This contrasts with the human reference, where individuals are influenced by the different terminal outcomes of the two games. In addition, LLM be-haviour is considerably more deterministic than human behaviour, resulting in default outcome distributions that remain relatively distant from the human reference. These differences motivated us to investigate whether conditioning LLMs on a relevant source of individual variation (here the SVO) could provide more representative behaviour. Our results show an important distinction between eliciting social preferences from LLMs and using those preferences to condition their behaviour. We found that SVO elicited directly from the models is sensitive to prompt framing and option order, suggesting that it should not be interpreted as a stable, human-like preference. In contrast, when models are conditioned on the SVO profiles observed in the human sample, their output systematically becomes more aligned with the human reference. In particular, we showed that the models reproduce the association between social preferences and strategic choices in the CG found in human data: higher induced SVO values generally lead to more cooperative behaviour in the CG. This distinction suggests that the usefulness of SVO for behavioural simulation does not depend on LLMs possessing stable social preferences themselves, but rather on their ability to map preference profiles onto corresponding strategic behaviour. Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. First, our analysis is restricted to SVO and two variants of the CG. Whether the same approach generalises to other psycho-logical dimensions, strategic interactions, and behavioural domains remains to be established. Second, the effectiveness of SVO conditioning depends on a human reference distribution from which the profiles are drawn. Third, substantial differences remain across models, and im-proved alignment is not universal. In some conditions, SVO-conditioned models remain further from the human reference than a simple random agent baseline. Future work should investi-gate when behavioural associations transfer across tasks and models, and whether combining multiple empirically grounded individual characteristics can reproduce richer forms of human behavioural diversity.

## 4.2 Tasks 

4.2.1 Centipede Game 

The CG is a 2-player sequential task. The key feature of this task is that the payoffs are struc-tured such that if the focal player passes the shared resource to the co-player, who then ends the interaction, the former receives a lower share than they would have received by ending the interaction themselves. However, continuing for one additional step leads to a higher pay-off for the focal player. This structure therefore entails a conflict between long-term (mutual, cooperative) and immediate (individual, competitive) interests. In our case, the presence of a deadline at the end of the zero-end CG promotes the com-petitive tendency of anticipating the co-player. The equal division at the end of the fair-end CG, instead, encourages both players to engage in further exchanges to reach the final node. Nonetheless, standard game-theoretic reasoning based on backward induction yields the same subgame-perfect equilibrium in both scenarios: the game terminates at the initial decision node. In both CGs, a fully rational and self-interested player is expected to choose Take at every turn, anticipating that an equally rational opponent will follow the same strategy.
