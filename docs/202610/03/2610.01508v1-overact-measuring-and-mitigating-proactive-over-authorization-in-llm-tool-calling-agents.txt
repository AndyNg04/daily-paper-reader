Abstract 

LLM agents with tool-calling capabilities can access external services and private user data, but they may retrieve more information than a user’s request explicitly requires. We study this behavior in structured tool-calling agents and term it proactive over-authorization . This setting differs from filesystem-level coding agents because the main risk is unnecessary access to private data. We introduce O VER -ACT , a controlled benchmark spanning eight privacy-sensitive domains with deterministic, judge-free scoring, together with an interpre-tive decision-theoretic framework that yields three testable predictions. Across seven mod-els from four families, all models significantly exceed authorized scope. Request specificity is the strongest predictor of severity, over-authorization grows sublinearly with tool-pool size, and decoding temperature has little ef-fect. These patterns are consistent with a cost-asymmetry account, suggesting that over-authorization arises more from structural de-cision tendencies than from decoding random-ness. We also propose S ELF AUDIT , a zero-shot inference-time method that generates request-grounded justifications and filters unjustified calls before execution. Ablation shows that explicit filtering is the main driver of scope re-duction. S ELF AUDIT reduces privacy-oriented excess by 43% without oracle knowledge. 

1 Introduction 

LLM-based agents increasingly interact with exter-nal services through structured tool calls (Schick et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2023). Re-cent surveys document the rapid growth of these systems and the privacy and security concerns they raise (Qin et al., 2025; Xi et al., 2025; Das et al., 2025). In this setting, a user issues a natural-language request and the agent autonomously de-cides which APIs to invoke, creating a failure mode:

the agent may access data beyond what the request explicitly requires. Consider a user asking a bank-ing agent to “ check my balance ”. Under a con-servative interpretation, the minimally sufficient response is a single get_checking_balance 

call. In practice, however, an agent may also retrieve transaction history, savings details, or card information, each exposing personal data not strictly required by the request. Similarly, in healthcare, asking “ When is my next appoint-ment? ” may trigger retrieval of medication lists, lab results, or diagnosis history, creating tension with data-minimization principles (Goldsteen et al., 2022). We study this behavior as proactive over-authorization , defined as expansion of the agent’s action scope beyond what the user’s words explic-itly entail. Unlike generic tool overuse, the main concern here is unnecessary access to private data. Existing agent safety benchmarks focus mainly on catastrophic or adversarial failures, such as jail-breaking (Wei et al., 2023), prompt injection (Zhan et al., 2024), and malicious misuse (Ye et al., 2024); recent work also evaluates broader forms of agentic harmfulness (Andriushchenko et al., 2025). Tool-calling benchmarks, by contrast, primarily measure task success and tool-use correctness without pe-nalizing excess retrieval scope (Patil et al., 2025; Mialon et al., 2024), although recent work has be-gun to examine unnecessary or overeager tool use (Xu et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2026). Concurrent work studies a related phenomenon in filesystem-level coding agents, where agents perform out-of-scope actions on benign tasks (Qu et al., 2026). We focus on the complementary setting of structured tool-calling APIs that access private user data. Our question is different from prior work: not whether an agent can use the right tools, but whether it can avoid plausible but unnecessary ones when those tools expose private data. To study this question, we introduce O VER -ACT , a controlled benchmark of 720 episodes span-arXiv:2610.01508v1 [cs.CR] 1 Oct 2026 ning eight privacy-sensitive domains, each anno-tated with a benchmark reference minimal tool set. OVER ACT supports deterministic, judge-free eval-uation: over-authorization is measured by com-paring the tools an agent actually calls with the tools minimally required, without any LLM-based scoring. This setup adopts a deliberately conser-vative benchmark criterion, enabling reproducible and privacy-protective measurement of scope ex-pansion. It is useful to distinguish three notions of scope. Benchmark-authorized scope is the set of tools entailed by the user’s literal words under our conservative policy. User-preferred scope is what a given user would actually want. Deployment-safe scope is what a production policy or compliance regime permits. Our empirical claims are anchored to benchmark-authorized scope; the other two no-tions provide important context, but we do not claim that benchmark excess always corresponds to user dispreference or deployment harm. We develop an interpretive decision-theoretic ac-count of tool selection. Its core intuition is that asymmetric omission and commission costs induce a low effective inclusion threshold, making seman-tically related but unnecessary tools more likely to be called. This account yields three qualitative predictions: over-authorization should increase as requests become less specific, grow sublinearly with tool availability, and remain relatively stable across decoding temperatures. We test these pre-dictions across seven models from four families in more than 90,000 core experimental runs, and find that over-authorization is widespread and broadly consistent with these regularities. We also compare five prompt-level interven-tions. Among them, only exact permission lists serve as an oracle upper bound, substantially reduc-ing scope without lowering task completion. We therefore explore S ELF AUDIT , an inference-time method that generates request-grounded justifica-tions for planned tool calls and filters unjustified actions before execution. Ablation shows that ex-plicit filtering, rather than structured justification alone, drives most of the reduction. Overall, our contributions are as follows: 1. We define proactive over-authorization un-der a conservative privacy-first benchmark criterion and present an interpretive cost-asymmetry account that organizes three em-pirical regularities into testable predictions. 2. We introduce O VER ACT , a controlled evalu-ation protocol with judge-free scoring across eight privacy-sensitive API domains. 3. We provide large-scale evidence that over-authorization is widespread, evaluate prompt-level interventions, and present S ELF AUDIT ,an oracle-free proof of concept showing that explicit self-filtering, rather than structured reasoning alone, drives scope reduction. 

2 Related Work 

Our work relates to three lines of research: tool-calling evaluation, agent safety and privacy, and alignment dynamics. 

Tool-calling agents and evaluation. LLMs are in-creasingly deployed as tool-calling agents (Xi et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025), with representative frameworks including Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023), ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), and ToolLLM (Qin et al., 2024). Existing bench-marks mainly evaluate task success or tool-use cor-rectness, including AgentBench (Liu et al., 2024), BFCL (Patil et al., 2025), GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024), and ToolTalk (Farn and Shin, 2023). Recent work has also studied unnecessary tool use, includ-ing training-time reward shaping for efficient tool calling (Xu et al., 2025) and agents’ tendency to prefer external tools over internal knowledge (Zeng et al., 2026). Recent efforts to build smaller agentic models through synthetic environments (Lyu et al., 2026b) and student-centered distillation (Lyu et al., 2026a) complement this line by improving tool-use capabilities at lower cost, while multi-agent reasoning frameworks (Zhang et al., 2025) study how agent collaboration shapes tool selection. Our focus is narrower and complementary: we study 

scope excess among otherwise plausible tool calls, where the concern is unnecessary access to private data. Whereas prior work mainly asks whether a tool call is needed at all, we ask which additional 

tools are called beyond the minimal sufficient set. 

Agent safety, privacy, and trustworthiness. 

Agent safety work has largely focused on catas-trophic or adversarial failures, such as jailbreak-ing (Wei et al., 2023), prompt injection (Zhan et al., 2024), and malicious misuse (Ye et al., 2024). Related benchmarks include Agent-SafetyBench (Zhang et al., 2024), ToolEmu (Ruan et al., 2024), R-Judge (Yuan et al., 2024), and AgentHarm (An-driushchenko et al., 2025). Broader work studies trustworthiness and privacy more generally, includ-ing training-data extraction (Carlini et al., 2021), data minimization (Goldsteen et al., 2022), text sanitization (Chen et al., 2023; Awon et al., 2025), and comprehensive surveys of generative-model trustworthiness (Fan et al., 2025). Agent resilience against transient failures has also been studied in multi-agent settings (Zhang et al., 2026), which is complementary to our focus on scope expansion rather than agent failure. A related systems line enforces least-privilege constraints through exter-nal mechanisms such as programmable privilege-control policies (Shi et al., 2025), prompt-flow integrity checks (Kim et al., 2025), and formal access-control frameworks (Li et al., 2025). These methods assume a pre-specified permission bound-ary and enforce it at the system level. By con-trast, O VER ACT is diagnostic: it measures the ten-dency toward scope expansion when no external constraint is imposed. Concurrent with our work, Qu et al. (2026) introduce OverEager-Gen, a bench-mark for out-of-scope actions by filesystem-level coding agents on benign tasks. The settings are complementary. They study coding agents acting over filesystems, whereas we study structured API tool calls that access private user data. We also fo-cus on benchmark-defined minimal scope, an inter-pretive cost-asymmetry account, and an oracle-free inference-time mitigation. 

Alignment dynamics and interpretive mod-els. Preference-alignment methods such as RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022) and related optimization ap-proaches (Rafailov et al., 2023) are central to mod-ern LLM behavior (Bai et al., 2022). Recent work studies unintended effects of these training regimes, including sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2024; Shapira et al., 2026), reward tampering, and other misalign-ment behaviors (Denison et al., 2024; Perez et al., 2023). Our framing is related in spirit: proactive over-authorization can be viewed as an action-level form of over-helpfulness expressed through tool selection rather than text. Formal analyses of re-ward misspecification and Goodhart effects (Pan et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2023) further motivate this perspective. We use a lightweight interpretive decision-theoretic account to organize empirical regularities rather than to claim a verified causal mechanism (Russell and Norvig, 2020). 

3 Decision-Theoretic Framework 

This section presents an explanatory abstraction rather than a validated causal account of internal model computation. We formalize tool selection as a cost-sensitive decision problem, derive an in-clusion threshold from asymmetric error costs, and use it to motivate three testable predictions.

3.3 Evaluation Metrics 

We use three metrics to quantify the amount and severity of over-authorization. Scope Inflation Ra-tio (SIR) is SIR = |Aactual ||Aminimal | .

It measures excess scope as a multiplicative fac-tor. SIR = 1 .0 means the agent called exactly as many tools as the minimal set; it does not by itself indicate perfect compliance, because a set of the same cardinality may omit a required tool while including an unnecessary one. We therefore read SIR together with the Task Completion Rate (TCR), which detects omitted required tools, and additionally report exact-set match and normalized excess as complementary metrics in Appendix B.3. SIR = 3 .0 means the agent called three times as many tools as needed. Because many episodes have 

|Aminimal | = 1 , ratio values can be magnified; we also report absolute excess counts and the Privacy Violation Score (PVS) in Appendix B.3, which support the same qualitative conclusions. TCR is TCR = |Aactual ∩ Aminimal ||Aminimal | .

Despite its name, TCR measures recall of the anno-tated minimal tool set: it does not check argument correctness, execution success, or whether the re-turned information actually satisfies the request. We therefore interpret it as a minimal-tool recall 

proxy for task completion; genuine end-to-end task-completion evaluation with executed tool outputs is a natural extension. TCR separates scope reduction from task failure: an agent that omits required tools scores low on TCR regardless of how many excess tools it calls. PVS is PVS = X

3.4 The O VER ACT Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluate the framework under a deterministic protocol. 

Domains and tools. The protocol spans eight privacy-sensitive domains: banking, healthcare, e-commerce, email/calendar, HR systems, smart home, travel booking, and social media. Each do-main contains exactly six tools with full param-eter schemas and PII annotations, for a total of 48 tools (Appendix B.1). The domains are cho-sen to balance practical relevance with variation in intra-domain semantic coherence; per-domain breakdowns appear in Appendix B.5. 

Episode construction. Each domain contains 30 episodes at each of three specificity levels, precise ,

moderate , and vague , yielding 720 episodes in total. This manipulation directly tests Prediction 2. All metrics are computed from set operations on tool-call sets, with no LLM-based judgment. 

Annotation. Two trained annotators independently labeled the minimal tool set for the original 240 episodes under the stated policy (Appendix B.2), achieving Cohen’s κ = 0 .91 before consensus. The benchmark was then expanded to 720 episodes by paraphrasing while preserving each minimal tool set. The high agreement indicates that the policy is consistently implementable. The 22 dis-agreements (9.2%) were resolved through struc-tured discussion; most involved scope-boundary ambiguities for moderately specific requests. Most policy-flagged excess calls are judged unwanted or neutral by human annotators (Table 5), espe-cially for precise requests, suggesting reasonable alignment between the conservative benchmark cri-terion and user judgments. Tool presentation order is randomized per episode, and position effects are negligible.

4.2 Over-Authorization Across Models (RQ1) 

Table 1 shows that proactive over-authorization appears in every tested model: all seven signifi-cantly exceed authorized scope, and none achieves mean SIR ≤ 1.0 under baseline conditions. 4.5 Intervention Effectiveness (RQ3) 

We compare prompt-level interventions in Figure 4 and Table 2. A clear pattern emerges: interventions that constrain the agent’s action space, especially permission lists and clarification, reduce scope in-flation more effectively than those that merely re-state or narrow the request. Exact permission lists are the only intervention that substantially reduces SIR without lowering task completion, but they require oracle knowledge of the minimal tool set. Clarification achieves the lowest SIR among non-oracle interventions but incurs a large TCR penalty, because our benchmark records no tool call when the agent asks a clarifying question instead of act-ing. In interactive settings, clarification may there-fore be more competitive than its benchmark TCR suggests. Intent Echo is also informative: restating the user’s intent before acting does not reduce ex-cess scope and can slightly worsen it, suggesting that the problem is not simply surface-level intent misunderstanding. Permission lists are effective only when they are exact. We vary the number of extra permitted tools beyond the minimal set and find that models exploit any slack roughly in proportion to the num-ber of extra tools allowed (Figure 5), while task completion remains nearly constant. Approximate permission lists therefore offer limited protection: effective scope control requires knowledge of the exact minimal set rather than a broad superset. Full per-model results are reported in Appendix C.2.

What kinds of excess calls occur? A rule-based taxonomy applied to all baseline excess calls re-veals four recurring patterns: Exploratory (50%), 

Anticipatory (21%), Completionist (20%), and Con-firmatory (9%). Exploratory calls dominate: agents most often retrieve additional information from the same semantic neighborhood as the request rather than invoking unrelated tools. Over-authorization therefore usually reflects local scope expansion rather than arbitrary tool misuse. 

When is excess harmful? Recall the three notions of scope introduced in §1: benchmark-authorized scope (the conservative, literal-interpretation base-line), user-preferred scope (what a given user would actually want), and deployment-safe scope 

(what a compliance regime permits). Benchmark-authorized excess subsumes the other two, but not all benchmark-authorized excess is unwanted, and not all unwanted excess is harmful. 5 Conclusion 

We study proactive over-authorization , measured under a deliberately conservative benchmark cri-terion, as a form of scope expansion in which tool-calling agents access more information than a user’s explicit request strictly requires. We present an interpretive decision-theoretic account that moti-vates three qualitative predictions. Under this crite-rion, over-authorization across seven models from four families is widespread, increases sharply as re-quest specificity decreases, grows sublinearly as the tool pool expands, and remains stable across decod-ing temperatures. These results suggest that the be-havior is systematic rather than a sampling artifact. We introduce O VER ACT , a controlled benchmark with deterministic, judge-free scoring across eight privacy-sensitive domains. We also present S ELF -AUDIT , a proof-of-concept inference-time mitiga-tion that does not require oracle knowledge. Its ablation shows that the main driver of scope reduc-tion is an explicit removal step rather than struc-tured justification alone, pointing to self-filtering as a starting point for oracle-free mitigation. 

Limitations 

OVER ACT is a diagnostic benchmark that uses controlled tool schemas with six tools per domain. These choices enable deterministic measurement, but the absolute magnitude of over-authorization in production settings may differ. Our ecological val-idation experiment (Appendix D.2) extends pool sizes to 10, 15, and 20 with richer schemas, but larger tool pools may introduce additional forms of over-authorization not captured here. Our decision-theoretic account is calibrated post hoc and is in-tended as an interpretive model. The PVS metric treats all PII categories equally, although some ex-cess accesses, such as financial versus preference data, are clearly more consequential than others; a severity-weighted metric would better capture this distinction. Finally, our benchmark adopts a strict privacy-first authorization criterion. This enables deterministic evaluation, but it may classify as ex-cess some actions that would be considered helpful in other deployment contexts. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work does not involve real user data or de-ployed decision-making systems. All models were accessed through API endpoints under standard terms. Our goal is to support the development of more privacy-respecting tool-calling systems. There are also dual-use considerations. Character-izing systematic patterns in tool selection could help an adversary anticipate what information an agent is likely to retrieve, although such behavior is already observable through ordinary API use. Conversely, scope-minimization strategies such as SELF AUDIT may increase over-filtering, especially for vague requests whose intended scope is gen-uinely broad. We therefore view O VER ACT as a diagnostic benchmark rather than as a prescription that all excess actions should always be eliminated.

B.2 Annotation Guidelines 

This section documents the protocol used to con-struct the benchmark reference minimal tool sets in O VER ACT .

Annotation principles. 

1. Literal interpretation. Only tool calls di-rectly entailed by the user’s explicit words are treated as authorized. Anticipated needs, contextually helpful extensions, and common-sense elaborations are treated as excess. 2. Minimal sufficiency. Among all tool sets sufficient to satisfy the request, the smallest one is selected. If multiple sets have the same cardinality, the one that accesses fewer PII categories is preferred. 3. Conservative boundary. In ambiguous cases, the smaller set is adopted, biasing toward ex-cess classification rather than permissive ex-pansion. 

Annotation procedure. Two trained annotators independently annotated all original 240 episodes. Each annotator received: (i) the user request, (ii) the six tool schemas for the domain, and (iii) the annotation principles above. Pre-consensus inter-annotator agreement was high (Cohen’s κ = 0 .91 ), and post-consensus annotations cover all original 240 episodes; paraphrased variants inherit the same minimal tool sets. 

Disagreement analysis. The 22 initial disagree-ments (9.2% of episodes) fell into three categories: • Scope boundary (14 cases): whether a mod-erately specific request authorizes retrieval of related metadata. • Composite intent (5 cases): whether a com-pound request authorizes multiple operations. • Implicit dependency (3 cases): whether a logically prerequisite tool call should count toward the minimal set. All cases were resolved in favor of the conservative interpretation. 

B.3 Metric Design Considerations SIR as a ratio metric. The same absolute excess can imply different relative scope inflation depend-ing on the size of the minimal set. For example, one extra tool yields SIR = 2 .0 when |Aminimal | = 1 ,but SIR = 1 .33 when |Aminimal | = 3 . For episodes with larger minimal sets, the main conclusions are unchanged under both ratio-based and absolute-excess formulations. 

Absolute excess counts. In absolute terms, the mean baseline excess is about 0.7 additional tool calls per episode. Because many episodes have |Aminimal | = 1 , this absolute magnitude is small, and PVS provides a complementary severity-weighted view of the privacy-oriented excess. The qualitative patterns reported in the main text (the cross-model gradient, the specificity effect, and the saturation behavior) are supported by absolute-excess and PVS analyses as well as by SIR. 

Severity-weighted PVS. PVS treats all accessed PII categories equally. This simplifies annotation, but it does not distinguish between categories that may differ substantially in downstream privacy con-sequence. A severity-weighted variant could better reflect differential risk, but it would require an ad-ditional layer of normative judgment outside the scope of the present benchmark.

B.4 Human Evaluation of Excess Actions 

Our automatic metrics classify every tool call be-yond the annotated minimal set as excess. To assess whether these calls are also perceived as unnec-essary by users, we conduct a human evaluation study. We sample 100 baseline episodes with at least one excess tool call (SIR > 1.0), stratified by specificity: 20 precise, 35 moderate, and 45 vague. The sample covers all eight domains. For each excess call, annotators answer: Would you, as the user who made this request, have wanted this additional action to be taken? Responses are col-lected on a three-point scale: Unwanted , Neutral ,or Wanted . Table 5 shows that 71.2% of excess calls are judged unwanted and 17.0% neutral, while 11.8% are judged positively. The unwanted rate is highest for precise requests and lowest for vague requests, mirroring the specificity gradient in the main experiments.

C.3 Reproducibility Details 

All experiments use the official API endpoints of the four providers, accessed during June–August 2026 (the period preceding the August 2026 sub-mission). Each configuration is run for three rep-etitions with tool presentation order randomized per episode; all randomized procedures (including the cross-domain tool padding in Appendix D.1) use seed 42. Decoding settings: temperature is 

{0.0, 0.7, 1.0} for the temperature ablation (Ap-pendix D) and 0.7 otherwise; all other sampling parameters use provider defaults. We use each provider’s native tool-calling mode, collecting the full tool-call plan before execution where the API supports it. API requests use retry with exponential backoff on transient errors; rate-limit and HTTP errors trigger re-runs so that every reported con-figuration attains the intended three repetitions. Malformed or empty tool-call responses are dis-carded and re-issued; in the ecological validation (Appendix D.2) this affected 12 .0% of attempted runs, spread evenly across pool sizes. D Supporting Prediction Analyses 

D.1 Tool-Pool Experiment Details 

Our baseline domains use six tools, which imposes a hard ceiling of SIR = 6 .0. The empirical mean SIR (1.72) reaches only 29% of this ceiling, sug-gesting that the observed saturation pattern is not a metric-ceiling artifact. Tool-pool sizes are varied over 

{4, 6, 12 , 24 , 36 , 48 }. For pools larger than the domain’s native set, we pad with cross-domain tools drawn deterministically (seed 42) from other domains. At pool sizes 36 and 48, most available tools are semantically unrelated to the query domain. The saturation observed at these larger pool sizes (SIR = 2 .00 at pool 36 vs. 1.97 at pool 24) suggests that irrelevant cross-domain tools do not strongly attract excess calls, consistent with the relevance-threshold account. The monotonic growth from 4 to 24 tools followed by saturation is consistent across pool-size comparisons. 

D.2 Ecological Validation with Extended Schemas 

A potential concern is that our baseline evalua-tion uses six tools per domain with short descrip-tions, whereas larger registries may expose agents to more complex schemas (e.g., nested parameters, enums, authentication tokens, pagination). To test robustness, we generate 14 additional tools per do-main with extended schemas and evaluate at pool sizes {10 , 15 , 20 } using within-domain distractors. We run two models (Qwen3.6-Plus, DeepSeek-v4-Pro) on moderate and vague episodes with three repetitions, yielding 2,880 attempted runs; 2,534 completed runs are retained after discarding mal-formed or empty tool-call responses (12.0% drop, evenly spread across pool sizes). Table 9 reports the results. SIR increases from 3.60 at 10 tools to 4.21 at 15 tools ( p=0 .02 ), then plateaus: 15 vs. 20 tools yields no significant dif-ference ( p=0 .21 ). The saturation pattern observed in the main experiment (§4.4) therefore replicates with richer schemas, and the plateau appears at an even smaller pool size. Absolute SIR values are higher than in the cross-domain padding ex-periment because within-domain distractors carry greater semantic relevance. E.1 Cross-Model Agreement Analysis 

We compute pairwise Jaccard similarity on excess tool sets across model pairs (Table 4). Agreement is high (mean J = 0 .764 over all 21 model pairs), indicating that different models over-authorize in similar semantic directions. The most frequently shared excess tools are same-domain tools semantically close to the user request, such as get_transactions in Bank-ing and get_devices in Smart Home. A tool-level logistic regression on the same 240-episode sample, predicting whether a non-minimal tool is called in excess on a given run, identifies cosine similarity as the only significant predictor (β = 10 .31 , p < 0.001 ); tool-description length and parameter count are not significant after con-trolling for similarity. The model achieves AUC 

= 0 .75 , indicating that semantic proximity is the most informative observable predictor of excess selection, with richer schemas contributing little additional explanatory power in this sample. 

E.2 Reasoning Trace Analysis 

Across 160 vague episodes with Qwen3.6-Plus and Qwen3.6-Flash (80 per model, sampled from the 240 vague episodes; extended thinking enabled), trace length does not correlate with SIR (Spearman

F SELF AUDIT and Mitigation Details 

F.1 Minimal Removal-Instruction Baseline 

To test whether the full S ELF AUDIT pipeline adds value beyond a simple minimization instruction, we evaluate a one-sentence removal prompt that tells the agent to call only tools strictly required by the user’s exact words, with no structured plan-ning or per-tool justification stage. This minimal instruction reduces SIR by 24% relative to base-line, confirming that an explicit removal step is the primary active ingredient and that the structured protocol is not necessary for a sizable share of the gain. 

F.2 Aggregate S ELF AUDIT Results 

Table 10 reports the full S ELF AUDIT before/after comparison on moderate and vague episodes across the four high-inflation models (Qwen3.6-Plus, Qwen3.7-Max, DeepSeek-v4-Pro, Kimi-K2.7-Code). These four are selected for the ab-lation because they exhibit the highest baseline SIR on the moderate and vague subset; GLM-5.2, despite a high overall SIR, is excluded from the ablation subset for consistency with the ini-tial pilot study, and its S ELF AUDIT results are re-ported separately in Table 11. DeepSeek-v3 and Qwen3.6-Flash are excluded for low baseline in-flation and unreliable multi-step instruction fol-lowing respectively. SELF AUDIT reduces scope inflation by 26% and PVS by 43%, with a mod-erate effect size ( d = 0 .36 ). The reduction is larger for vague requests than for moderate ones, where lexical grounding removes a greater share of marginal tool calls. S ELF AUDIT occasionally over-filters tools that were in fact needed, espe-cially when the request is paraphrastic or when the lexical link between request and tool descrip-tion is indirect. In our benchmark, 92% of tool descriptions share at least one content word with typical domain requests, making lexical ground-ing generally viable; for APIs with more abstract naming conventions, stronger semantic matching would likely be needed.
