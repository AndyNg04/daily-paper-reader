## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) have shown great potential as general-purpose user simulators for interactive systems. Prior studies have demonstrated that training on high-quality data can substantially improve model reasoning and downstream task performance. However, the upper bound of user simulation is still constrained by the model’s reasoning paradigm: native chain-of-thought often degenerates into shallow pattern matching and fails to adequately model the behavioral factors underlying real user decisions. In this paper, we propose OmniSimulator , a novel training framework designed to improve the predictive ability of small LLMs for realistic user simulation on short-video platform environments. Specifically, OmniSimulator organizes behavior-oriented reasoning through a structured schema with five dimensions, carefully selects data from real user-behavior scenarios, and introduces a teacher model to generate high-quality reasoning traces under the proposed schema, helping small models learn behavior-relevant reasoning patterns rather than relying on superficial behavioral correlations. Based on this process, we construct 9,881 high-quality training instances from three months of historical interaction trajectories of 200 real users, averaging about 50 distilled examples per user. Experimental results show that OmniSimulator improves the average score of Qwen3-8B by 34.33%, achieves performance competitive with Claude-Sonnet-4.5, and supports smaller-scale, self-hosted deployment. These results suggest that learning structured behavior-oriented reasoning patterns is a promising direction towards scalable and high-fidelity LLM-based user simulation 1.

Recent studies have shown that training on high-quality data can substantially improve reasoning ability and downstream task performance of LLMs, suggesting that post-training and supervision quality are critical for building stronger simulators. However, real user simulation remains far from solved. On challenging real-world benchmarks such as OmniBehavior [9], even frontier models achieve limited performance, while also exhibiting systematic biases such as overestimating positive actions, generating overly optimistic utterances, and collapsing different users into an average persona [6, 9]. Moreover, although closed-source models such as Claude-Opus-4.5 [2] provide stronger performance, their cost becomes prohibitive when conditioning on long interaction histories, making them difficult to deploy at industrial scale. A difficulty of user simulation is that real behavior is not generated by direct surface matching between historical interactions and the current scenario. Instead, user actions are mediated by latent mental states, such as stable preference, recent interest drift, transient fatigue, emotional tendency, and momentary decision style. In other words, predicting whether a user will click, purchase, continue watching, or say a particular utterance requires the model to first recover why this user is likely to act in that way at the current scenario . This observation naturally suggests that reasoning capability counts. If the model can explicitly infer the hidden state underlying the next action, it should in principle make more faithful predictions than a model that only performs shallow behavioral imitation. Chain-of-thought (CoT) therefore appears to be a natural candidate for improving user simulation, since it provides an intermediate reasoning process between observed history and predicted behavior. However, our empirical analysis reveals that simply enabling native thinking mode brings almost no benefit for user simulation: across Qwen3 models, the performance of thinking and non-thinking settings is often nearly identical, and in some cases the thinking variant even performs worse. A closer inspection of native CoT outputs explains why: existing models rarely use reasoning to reconstruct the user’s latent mental state; instead, they tend to produce generic justifications, shallow pattern matching, or direct guesses based on superficial correlations. This suggests that the bottleneck is not merely whether the model generates more reasoning tokens, but whether its reasoning follows a behavior-oriented structure. 2Motivated by this, we propose OmniSimulator , a novel training framework designed to improve small LLMs for real user simulation on short-video platform environments. The key idea is to teach the model to follow behavior-oriented structured reasoning rather than free-form reasoning. Specifically, OmniSimulator introduces a structured CoT schema that forces the model to infer latent user states through five dimensions: User Profile Grounding , Historical Behavioral State Inference , Content Relevance Alignment , Decision Mode Identification , and Final Decision Declaration . On top of this schema, we build a task-aware causal distillation pipeline: a teacher model (GPT-OSS-120B [1]) generates multiple reasoning traces for each prediction point under task-specific prompting strategies, and only traces consistent with ground truth are retained through rejection sampling. Following this process, we construct 9,881 high-quality distilled training instances from three months of interaction histories of 200 real users. Experiments show that OmniSimulator substantially improves small models over zero-shot and free-CoT baselines, enables Qwen3-8B to achieve performance competitive with strong proprietary models such as Claude-Sonnet-4.5 [3], and alleviates overestimation bias while supporting smaller-scale deployment. These results suggest that the upper bound of LLM-based user simulation is constrained not only by model scale, but also by the reasoning supervision paradigm. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify a key weakness of existing LLM-based user simulators: native CoT struggles to predict realistic user behavior, yielding only marginal improvements in behavioral prediction over non-thinking settings. • We propose OmniSimulator, a novel training framework that combines a structured five-dimensional CoT schema with a task-aware causal distillation pipeline, enabling small models to learn behavior-oriented reasoning patterns for user simulation. • We construct 9,881 high-quality distilled training instances from real-world long-horizon user trajectories, and OmniSimulator substantially improves small LLMs, achieves performance com-petitive with strong closed-source baselines and mitigates overestimation bias while supporting efficient small-model deployment. 

## 2 Methodology 

In this section, we first formulate user behavior simulation on short-video platforms, and then present 

OmniSimulator , our training framework for improving small LLMs on realistic user simulation. The central idea is that real user behavior is not generated by direct surface matching between historical interactions and the current scenario. Instead, it is mediated by a latent decision process shaped by stable preference, recent interest drift, transient affect, and the decision regime at the current moment. Existing LLMs, even with native thinking mode, often fail to reconstruct this hidden process and instead rely on shallow behavioral priors. To address this issue, OmniSimulator explicitly teaches the model to infer latent user states before predicting behavior. The framework consists of two tightly coupled components: (1) a structured chain-of-thought schema that organizes reasoning into a fixed sequence of human-like internal deliberation steps, and (2) a task-aware rationale distillation pipeline 

that converts teacher-generated reasoning traces into high-quality supervision for student models.

2.3 Overview of OmniSimulator 

Figure 1 illustrates the overall framework. For each prediction point, we first provide the teacher model with the user’s historical interaction trajectory and the current scenario context. The teacher is required to reason under a structured schema that explicitly reconstructs the user’s latent mental state. To increase diversity, we sample multiple reasoning traces for each example with stochastic decoding. Depending on the task type, we apply different prompting strategies to control the degree of access to ground truth. The resulting traces are filtered by rejection sampling, and only those whose final answers are consistent with the observed outcome are retained as supervised fine-tuning data. Finally, the student model is trained to generate both the structured reasoning trace and the final prediction. This design follows a simple principle: rather than only teaching the model what to predict, we also provide structured rationale supervision for how the prediction may be derived from behavioral evidence. In this way, latent-state reconstruction becomes explicit, learnable, and distillable. 

Structured Chain-of-Thought Schema. The first stage of OmniSimulator is a structured CoT schema tailored for user simulation. Our design is motivated not only by the empirical analysis above, but also by cognitive theories of human behavior. Prior work suggests that behavior is mediated by stable person-level characteristics, dynamically activated cognitive-affective states, and their interaction with situational cues [17]. In parallel, dual-process accounts further distinguish between reflective, deliberative decision making and more impulsive behavior pathways [21]. Native thinking mode alone is therefore insufficient, because it leaves this behavioral reasoning path under-specified for human behavior simulation tasks. To address this issue, we require the model to generate reasoning within a <think> block following five fixed dimensions: 41. User Profile Grounding: The model first interprets stable user characteristics reflected in long-term history, such as age, gender, educational background. This step provides the global behavioral background of the user. 2. Historical Behavioral State: The model then infers the user’s dynamic internal state from recent and long-horizon interactions. This includes long-term interest continuation, short-term preference drift, current fatigue level, and emotional tendency. Compared with the previous step, the focus here is not the user’s general profile, but the user’s state from the history sequences at the current moment. 3. Content Relevance Alignment: Next, the model matches the inferred user state with the current scenario and content. This step examines whether the current item is aligned with the user’s active interests, intent, mood, or aversion, and serves as the bridge between historical state and prediction item. 4. Decision Mode Identification: The model further determines whether the current behavior is primarily driven by rational decision or random decision . Rational decisions are mainly explained by interpretable factors such as fatigue levels in this session, while random decisions are more influenced by curiosity or stochasticity. This distinction is essential for modeling negative outcomes and low-confidence actions. 5. Final Decision Declaration: Before closing the </think> block, the model should explicitly state its final decision, and this declaration is required to be fully consistent with the final output answer. This design reduces reasoning-answer mismatch and improves the controllability of the generated traces. With this schema, OmniSimulator decomposes user simulation into the following process: 

(Hut , x ut+1 ) → rut → yut+1 , (4) where rut denotes the structured reasoning trace. In practice, rut serves as a textual proxy of the unobserved latent state zut . Compared with unconstrained CoT, this schema forces the model to move from observable behaviors to hidden user states, and then from hidden user states to final actions. 

Task-aware Rationale Distillation. Structured reasoning alone is not sufficient if teacher-generated traces are noisy. We therefore design a task-aware rationale distillation pipeline to obtain high-quality supervision for student training. For each prediction item, we use a teacher model (GPT-OSS-120B [1] in our experiment) to rollout N

candidate reasoning traces under the structured schema. Formally, given an example (Hut , x ut+1 , y ut+1 ),we sample 

Rut = {rut, 1, r ut, 2, . . . , r ut,N } (5) with relatively high temperature. This design allows the teacher to generate multiple candidate rationales for each prediction instance. However, different task types have different output spaces and different risks of information leakage, so we use different prompting strategies for different tasks. • Binary tasks: For binary prediction, we use forward-only reasoning . The teacher is not given the ground-truth label, and must predict the outcome solely from historical evidence and the current scenario. This avoids hindsight bias, since directly injecting the correct label would encourage the teacher to rationalize the answer after the fact. • Continuous-value tasks: For continuous prediction, exact-value sampling is more difficult because the target space is broad and numerically sensitive. Pure forward reasoning often yields semantically reasonable but poorly calibrated values. To stabilize teacher generation, we provide a coarse range hint derived from the ground truth, rather than the exact answer itself. This provides task-specific guidance for generating candidate rationales with more appropriate numerical scales. • Text-generation tasks: For textual simulation, namely next utterance prediction, the answer space is open-ended and highly diverse. Unconstrained multi-sample generation is therefore both costly and unstable. We directly inject the ground-truth utterance and require the teacher’s reasoning to converge to this answer. In this setting, the role of the reasoning trace is to explain why the observed utterance is consistent with the available behavioral history and the current conversational context. 5After teacher generation, we apply rejection sampling and retain only those traces whose final prediction is consistent with ground truth: 

eRut = {r ∈ R ut | Ans( r) = yut+1 }, (6) where Ans( r) denotes the final answer implied by trace r. For each instance with accepted candidates, we select the trace with the highest teacher mean token log-probability, breaking ties by shorter length, to construct the supervised training data. If none of the sampled traces matches the observed outcome, the example is excluded from the training set and stored separately for analysis. In practice, this mainly occurs on difficult binary examples. This design deliberately prioritizes supervision quality over raw sample coverage.

Instead of only optimizing the final prediction, it encourages the student model to learn an internal decision process that first reconstructs the user’s latent state and then derives behavior from it. As a result, the student is trained to imitate human-like deliberation rather than only mimic surface-level action patterns. The student model receives the user profile, historical interaction sequence, and current scenario context. It then generates a structured reasoning trace under the predefined schema and outputs the final prediction. No manually constructed external annotation, or additional structured memory is required. All latent-state reconstruction is performed directly from the observed trajectory. This design preserves the end-to-end simplicity of LLM-based user simulation while improving faithfulness to real behavior. In particular, because the model is explicitly trained to reason about latent mental states, it is better positioned to capture negative feedback, temporary preference shifts, and user-specific heterogeneity, which are often suppressed in standard simulation pipelines. 

## 3 Experiments 

We conduct experiments on realistic multi-scenario user simulation tasks to address three research questions: Q1: How effectively does OmniSimulator improve small LLMs across diverse user behaviors and scenarios? Q2: How competitive are OmniSimulator-trained small LLMs compared with frontier LLMs and human baselines? Q3: Which design choices in the reasoning schema and training pipeline contribute most to the performance gains? 

3.1 Experimental Setup Benchmark. We conduct experiments on OmniBehavior [9], a real-world benchmark for LLM-based user simulation built from long-horizon, cross-scenario, and heterogeneous behavior traces. The benchmark is constructed from three months of complete interaction trajectories of 200 representative users on a short-video platform, covering video browsing, live streaming, advertisement, e-commerce (including customer service dialogues), and search scenarios. Prior analysis on OmniBehavior shows that realistic user decisions are inherently long-horizon and cross-scenario, and that current frontier LLMs still exhibit a substantial capability gap on this benchmark. 

Training data construction. Our training data is distilled from the same realistic platform setting as OmniBehavior, but from users and trajectories excluded from the benchmark. Additional Training Controls. To further examine the contributions of reasoning supervision and rejection sampling, we conduct additional comparisons on Qwen3-8B. Metrics. Following OmniBehavior, we categorize user behaviors into 3 types. For binary behaviors (e.g., like, share), we formulate simulation as a classification problem and report the F1-score. For continuous behaviors (watch duration), standard absolute error introduces bias toward longer videos. To mitigate this, we report a normalized score defined as 100 × (1 − NMAE) , where NMAE normalizes the prediction error by the video’s duration and higher scores indicate better performance. Finally, for textual behaviors (e.g., customer service dialog), we utilize Claude-Sonnet-4.5 as judge to quantify the performance of the simulator which measures four criteria (intent fidelity, persona mimicry, knowledge boundary, and semantic alignment). We further validate the textual evaluation using two additional LLM judges and a blinded paired human evaluation, with detailed results provided in the appendix. 

3.2 Main Results OmniSimulator delivers large and consistent improvements across all models . On Qwen3-8B, our method improves the average score from 30.82 to 41.40, a relative improvement of 34.33%, and 7the same trend holds across all smaller backbones. The consistent gains over both zero-shot inference and SFT with free CoT suggest that the improvement comes not merely from additional supervision, but from combining structured rationale supervision with answer-consistency filtering. Table 1 reports the results of different training settings on OmniBehavior benchmark. We can see that: native thinking alone is insufficient for realistic user simulation. Across all model scales, zero-shot inference with and without thinking mode yields nearly identical performance, confirming that simply allocating more reasoning tokens does not address the core difficulty of the task. While SFT with free CoT brings moderate gains over zero-shot inference, its improvements remain unstable across scenarios and behavior types, suggesting that unconstrained reasoning traces do not reliably capture the latent decision process of real users. 

The strongest gains occur on binary behaviors, and the resulting small models become competi-tive with frontier baselines . The largest improvements are observed in video, live, and advertisement binary prediction, where shallow priors tend to over-predict positive actions. For instance, on Qwen3-8B, OmniSimulator improves Video Binary from 17.29 to 30.24, Live Binary from 20.29 to 32.40, and Ads Binary from 22.96 to 40.97. As a result, Qwen3-8B trained with OmniSimulator reaches an average score of 41.40, achieving performance competitive with Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (40.49) and substantially outperforming the zero-shot Qwen3-235B baseline (32.11), while nearly matching GLM-4.7 (41.46), with baseline scores taken from OmniBehavior [9]. Together, these results suggest that for realistic user simulation, the key bottleneck lies less in model scale itself than in the reasoning paradigm used to reconstruct user behavior.

In this sense, reducing overestimation bias is not merely a desirable property of the simulator, it’s one of the direct reasons why the model becomes more accurate.

3.5 Training Strategy Analysis 

To further understand the sources of OmniSimulator’s improvements, we compare alternative training strategies on Qwen3-8B, as shown in Table 3. Answer-only SFT yields only marginal improvements despite using the same retained training examples, suggesting that the gains cannot be explained by supervised learning on selected behavior labels alone. While synthetic rationales provide stronger su-pervision, their performance remains below OmniSimulator, highlighting the importance of explicitly guiding reasoning toward behavior-relevant user states rather than relying on generic explanations. Moreover, removing rejection sampling leads to a noticeable performance degradation, indicating that structured reasoning alone is insufficient without reliable supervision signals. Together, these findings suggest that the effectiveness of OmniSimulator stems from the combination of behavior-oriented reasoning structure and quality-controlled distillation, rather than simply increasing the amount of training supervision. In conclusion, the most important takeaway from our experiments is that the main bottleneck of realistic user simulation is not whether the model can think, but whether it can reconstruct the user’s latent state in a behaviorally meaningful way . Native thinking mode brings almost no gain, indicating that simply increasing the amount of free-form reasoning is insufficient for the human behavior simulation task. Free-form CoT supervision is somewhat more helpful, but its improvements remain limited and unstable, suggesting that unconstrained rationales still fail to faithfully capture the internal 9process that drives real user behavior. By contrast, once reasoning is explicitly constrained to model user profile, dynamic historical state, content alignment, and decision mode, even small models can improve consistently across scales. This also explains why OmniSimulator is particularly effective on binary behaviors, where the challenge is to distinguish genuine intent from overly optimistic priors. More broadly, these results suggest that the performance ceiling of current LLM-based user simulators is determined less by model scale alone than by whether the reasoning process can recover the latent psychological factors underlying user decisions. 

## 4 Related Work 

4.1 LLMs for Human Behavior Simulation 

Large language models have recently become a promising paradigm for human behavior simula-tion. Prior work has explored LLMs as user simulators in settings such as social simulation [23], recommender systems [5, 10, 7], and interactive behavior modeling [31, 19, 23]. Compared with traditional simulators based on fixed rules or narrow action spaces, LLM-based methods provide a unified interface for modeling heterogeneous actions and natural-language responses, making them suitable for personalized and context-dependent simulation. However, most existing studies focus on synthetic or relatively controlled environments, while paying less attention to realistic long-horizon user logs in which behavior is shaped by evolving latent states rather than static personas alone. Our work builds on this line of research, but focuses on improving small models for real-world user simulation through explicit latent-state reconstruction. 

4.2 Chain-of-Thought and Structured Reasoning 

Chain-of-thought (CoT) has been widely used to improve reasoning in large language models by introducing intermediate deliberation before the final answer [25, 24]. Subsequent work has shown that reasoning traces, structured rationales, and teacher-generated thoughts can serve as effective supervision for improving smaller models [11, 16]. Related efforts on structured prompting further suggest that reasoning quality depends not only on having intermediate steps, but also on how these steps are organized [8]. Our work is closely related to this direction, but differs in a key aspect: instead of applying free-form CoT to generic reasoning tasks, we design a structured schema tailored to user simulation, where the central challenge is to recover the latent mental state underlying the user’s next behavior. 

## 5 Discussion 

Conclusions. We presented OmniSimulator , a training framework that improves small LLMs for realistic user simulation through structured latent-state reasoning and task-aware rationale distillation. Experiments across binary, continuous, and textual behaviors show that OmniSimulator consistently outperforms zero-shot and free-CoT baselines, substantially reduces overestimation bias, and enables an 8B model to achieve performance competitive with Claude-Sonnet-4.5. Further ablation and analyses highlight the benefits of combining behavior-oriented reasoning supervision with answer-consistency filtering. These results suggest that the key challenge of current user simulation lies not in model scale alone, but also in the reasoning supervision paradigm used to model behavior-relevant user states. We hope this work encourages future research on more generalizable and more faithful LLM-based user simulators. 

Limitations. The current study evaluates held-out users from one short-video platform within a temporally overlapping collection window, and therefore does not establish temporal or cross-platform generalization. Moreover, the structured reasoning traces provide behavior-oriented supervision rather than direct evidence of users’ actual cognitive processes. In particular, the distilled reasoning traces may still inherit part of the teacher model’s own biases, while target-derived guidance for continuous and textual tasks introduces a risk of post-hoc rationalization. Although our ablations support the effectiveness of the full framework, the respective contributions of the schema and filtering have not been fully isolated under strictly matched conditions.
