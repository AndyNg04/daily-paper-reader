# Multimodal Interaction Estimation for Contact-Aware Adaptive Impedance Control 

Mateusz Jaszczuk, Paris Perdikaris and Nadia Figueroa 

Abstract — With advancements in learning-based policies, robots are increasingly being deployed in human-centric en-vironments, such as manufacturing floors and homes, where safe and precise operation requires compensating for un-known loads while remaining compliant during human in-teractions. Reliably distinguishing between these interaction types is challenging with a single sensing modality, as force signals cannot separate payloads from human pushes, and vision is susceptible to occlusions. We propose a task-agnostic 

interaction estimation and adaptive control framework that addresses this challenge by fusing force history and 3D point cloud through a transformer-based classifier. Trained with modality dropout for occlusion resilience and fine-tuned from simulation to hardware, the classifier achieves 91.89% ac-curacy and maintains above 90% under complete occlusion of one modality. The classifier drives a system identification framework that selectively compensates for unknown payloads and a variable-impedance controller that switches between adaptive and reactive modes, while simultaneously estimating interaction forces for downstream tasks, such as human intent estimation. We validate the framework under external pertur-bations and unknown loads across single-arm manipulation, deployment with an imitation-learning-based policy, intent-driven target switching, and zero-shot bimanual deployment on two Franka arms lifting a box of unknown mass, demonstrating generalization to closed kinematic chains and unseen setups, enabling safe and precise deployment. I. I NTRODUCTION 

Collaborative robots operating in human-centric environ-ments must simultaneously maintain precise trajectory track-ing and respond safely to human contact. While compliant behavior can be achieved through impedance control [1] by reducing control gains or passivity-based techniques [1]–[12] these approaches rely on accurate knowledge of the robot’s dynamics and struggle with unknown payloads and unmod-eled actuator dynamics. Both of which degrade tracking performance and can result in unsafe behaviors. PID-based controllers can minimize tracking errors, but they sacrifice compliance to human touch. Humans, by contrast, seamlessly distinguish externally applied loads from contact perturba-tions, adjusting gravity compensation in real time [13], [14]. For example, when two coworkers collaboratively carry a heavy assembly part while remaining responsive to each other’s pushes and redirection. Achieving this, however, re-quires resilience to visual occlusions and force ambiguity. Just as humans rely on both touch and vision to understand interactions, achieving interaction-awareness in robots requires multimodal percep-tion to classify the sources of external forces, even under occlusions, thereby enabling selective load compensation without sacrificing responsiveness to human contact, which is essential for safe human-robot collaboration. 

Related Work Physical human-robot interactions and precise impedance control require robots to reliably interpret contact events and adapt their behavior accordingly, which spans force estimation, interaction detection, and compli-ant control. Human contact detection from proprioception alone has been extensively studied, and typically requires several estimation-control layers and an accurate dynamics model [17], limiting its robustness when the dynamics are unknown, for example, when manipulating an unknown payload. Adaptive human-robot interaction strategies have proposed using passive dynamical systems (DS)-based con-trol [18], [19], enabling intuitive human-guided control. More recently, [20] demonstrated that proprioception alone can enable an intrinsic robotic sense of touch, accurately localizing contact points on the robot’s surface without tac-tile skin, enabling intuitive contact-based interaction. While effective, these methods respond to contact without explicitly arXiv:2610.24235v1 [cs.RO] 16 Oct 2026 classifying its source online , for example, distinguishing whether an external force is due to a human push or to an attached load. External payload estimation methods [21], [22] enable robots to manipulate heavy external loads, but typically cannot reliably distinguish different types of forces, which is the core objective of this work. Further, contact-rich manipulation methods [23]–[27] demonstrate effective force-aware policies but focus on objectives for learning motion policies rather than on understanding the source of interaction and estimating force. 

Contribution We propose a task-agnostic adaptive con-trol framework for online interaction classification and force estimation that distinguishes unknown payloads from human perturbations applied anywhere on the robot in real time and adapts gravity compensation and compli-ance accordingly. At its core, we developed a lightweight transformer-based multimodal classifier that fuses force his-tory derived from proprioceptive sensing with a 3D point cloud, enabling an information-rich representation of the interaction (i.e., human perturbation or applied external load) experienced by the robot, thereby outperforming single-modality baselines by combining evidence from both inputs. To ensure robustness to sensor occlusion, we implemented modality dropout, enabling accurate classification from either modality alone, achieving over 90% accuracy even with full occlusion of one sensor. To enhance generalization, we pretrain the network in simulation, then fine-tune it on a small real-robot dataset, achieving 91.89% classification accuracy on real hardware, outperforming thresholding-based and MLP baselines, and generalizing to an unseen bimanual setup, and enabling zero-shot payload adaptation of an imitation learning policy not originally trained to handle loads. The classifier drives a system identification frame-work that selectively compensates for unknown loads and a variable-impedance controller that switches between adaptive and reactive modes, while estimating interaction forces for downstream tasks, such as human intent estimation. Problem The performance of impedance controllers relies on accurate system dynamics equations, as any unknown payload will result in uncompensated τext , compromising tracking performance [17]. While existing frameworks [21], [31], [32] can adapt to unknown loads attached to the end-effector, they are not designed to disambiguate load and interaction-related forces, leading to unsafe behaviors when the two coincide. Further, while impedance controllers allow compliant responses to perturbations, they require low impedance gains to achieve this, which degrades tracking. Therefore, to allow precise execution and compliance, we need to distinguish Fload from Fint and compensate only for 

Fload to avoid instabilities and poor tracking. 

Goal Therefore, we aim to classify interaction events as either unknown payloads or perturbations in real time, compensating for unknown payloads and measuring effec-tive interaction force (Fload , F int ). We further want to use the interaction estimation to actively adjust the controller’s impedance γd(·), enabling precise tracking in reactive mode and compliant behavior when human perturbation is detected. III. I NTERACTION CLASSIFIER 

To distinguish human perturbations (Fint ) from external loads (Fload ), we design a transformer-based classifier that fuses proprioceptive and visual inputs to estimate the current interaction, inspired by transformer-based multimodal fusion approaches in robotics [33]–[36]. By aggregating information from both modalities, the framework maintains high accuracy even when an interaction is not fully observable from a single modality, i.e., due to occlusion or ambiguous forces.

1) Force History Encoder: To process the Fext history, we designed a force encoder that produces a compact latent force representation for the transformer module. The force history Fext ∈ R25 ×3 (∼ 250 ms) is passed through a two-layer 1D Convolution Network [37] with channel dimensions 

3 → 32 → 32 and kernel size 5, followed by attention pooling [38] for adaptive feature extraction, and an MLP with architecture 32 → 16 → 64 and ReLU activation. Finally, we apply an output projection and Layer Normalization [39] to extract the latent force representation, xF ∈ R64 .

2) Point Cloud Encoder: To process visual information, we employ a Point Cloud encoder [40] that extracts a latent 3D representation of the scene. A raw point cloud is cropped and furthest-point sampled to R1024 ×3, then passed through the MLP with an architecture of 3 → 64 → 128 → 256 

and ReLU activations, followed by max pooling to aggregate global geometric features. Finally, we apply output projection and Layer Normalization [39] to ensure the latent 3D repre-sentation, xPC ∈ R64 , is compatible in scale and dimension with the representations produced by other encoders. 

3) Transformer: To learn latent multimodal interaction representations, we design a transformer-based architec-ture [38] that integrates visual and proprioceptive cues (Fig 2). The transformer receives the Force, the Point Cloud, and learnable Interaction Classification Tokens, which are jointly passed through a Multi-Head Self-Attention (4 heads) to model cross-modal dependencies, followed by Layer Nor-malization [39], an MLP with architecture 64 → 128 → 64 

with ReLU activations, and a second Layer Normalization, with Residual connections around both attention and MLP modules. The learnable prediction token, having attended to both modalities, is then extracted from the transformer output and passed through the output projection to yield the latent multimodal interaction representation 2, xMM ∈ R64 .

4) Classification Head: We pass the interaction repre-sentation xMM ∈ R64 through an MLP with architecture 

64 → 128 → 256 → 1 and ReLU activations, followed by a sigmoid function and max pooling to produce the final interaction classification prediction, ˆyint .

f (x) = −(x − x∗) subject to perturbations and loads. IV. A DAPTIVE IMPEDANCE CONTROLLER 

The adaptive impedance controller uses the interaction-classification output, ˆyint ∈ [0 , 1] , to (i) set the controller’s compliance and to (ii) compensate for unknown loads via the RME’s model-mismatch estimate [21]. Because the classifi-cation is stochastic, we use the most conservative (compliant) of the last 250 predictions 6 as the safety buffer, preventing mode changes due to a ”false reactive” classification, while introducing minimal delay, which we did not observe to negatively impact the system during hardware deployment. 

Adaptation Goals: When manipulating an unknown pay-load, the robot should be in a (stiff) reactive mode 

allowing for payload estimation updates. Conversely, when a human perturbation is detected, the robot should be in a (soft) compliant mode , passive to the human touch.

B. Unknown Load Manipulation with Human Interactions 

To examine the controller’s accuracy during continuous adaptation, we deploy it on the Franka arm and perturb the robot as it manipulates an unknown payload. The framework distinguishes load- and interaction-induced forces online, adjusting impedance parameters and gravity compensation in real time, allowing the robot to maintain low tracking error while remaining compliant under contact. Fig. 6 shows the robot tracking an equilibrium point across a series of human-applied perturbations under varying unknown loads, comparing our adaptive controller against fixed-gain stiff and soft controller baselines. After each perturbation, our controller quickly returns to the equilibrium and has lower residual error than the baselines (the average tracking errors after recovering from perturbations are shown in Table V). The stiff controller (γd = 2.4) achieves relatively low tracking error, but offers no compliance during interaction, while the soft controller ( γd = 1.0) remains compliant but recovers slowly and drifts under unknown loads. Our adaptive controller combines the benefits of both, remaining compliant during contact while precisely tracking equilib-rium once the interaction ends.

E. Dynamics Mismatch Imitation Learning Policy 

To evaluate our framework with an imitation learning policy, we deploy the pretrained EMP policy [46] for the pouring task with an unknown 0.85 kg payload . We com-pare the performance of stiff ( γd = 2 .4) and soft ( γd =1.0) controllers against our adaptive impedance controller (with the NN classifier fine-tuned only on real-robot data to improve sim-to-real transfer to an unseen setup 8). As shown in Fig. 9, the soft controller yields to the applied payload and fails to complete the task in all three trials, diverging from the trajectory and colliding with the table. The stiff controller improves tracking; however, it fails to pour the payload in 2 trials due to collision with the pot and inability to complete the task (pouring failure), as it cannot compensate for unknown dynamics. The adaptive controller, which adjusts impedance and gravity compensation online, most closely follows the EMP trajectory (Table VI) and succeeds in all three trials, allowing the controller to safely and precisely execute the learned policy with unknown loads, while maintaining compliance to human perturbations. Note that the EMP policy, as with other pure-motion-based approaches, was not trained for unknown, heavy payload ma-nipulation. It was trained to perform a pouring motion with the cup holding toy blocks of less than 120g. Thus, we are showing that our framework can improve the performance of motion-based imitation learning policies on OOD (out-of-distribution) dynamics. VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We propose a novel interaction estimation and adap-tive control framework that enables collaborative robots to compensate for unknown heavy objects in real time while estimating human interaction forces, thereby enabling precise and adaptive physical interactions and outperforming single-modality baselines. We evaluated our method in a series of experiments across single and bimanual tasks, demonstrating fast and safe adaptation to constantly changing dynamics and interaction conditions, while tracking accurately.

In our future work, we aim to improve the controller’s performance by fine-tuning on more diverse datasets (across multiple locations and users) to enhance generalization and develop morphology-specific model checkpoints (single-arm, bimanual, mobile manipulation). A current limitation is that the interaction classifier assumes that load and perturbations will not occur simultaneously; when they do, the robot enters a compliant mode and delays mismatch compensation until the perturbation ends. While safe, this might compromise system performance in complex interaction scenarios, which we plan to address in our future work.
