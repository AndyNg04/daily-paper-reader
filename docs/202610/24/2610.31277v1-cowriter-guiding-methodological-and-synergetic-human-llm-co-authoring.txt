## Abstract 

While LLMs have transformed text generation, standard chat-and-canvas interfaces often re-duce human authoring to quite passive supervi-sion, causing review fatigue, over-reliance on AI, and the sidelining of the human role in long-form, source-grounded writing. Prior systems typically address isolated writing phases, leav-ing comprehensive end-to-end human-LLM collaboration largely unexplored. In this pa-per, we introduce C OWRITER , a proactive agentic scheme that adapts expert human (co-)authoring methodologies from cognitive sci-ence to flexibly steer the entire writing work-flow while actively encouraging the user to en-gage. In an exploratory user study, compared to a standard LLM baseline, authors using COWRITER better expressed their perspectives and maintained a higher sense of document ownership, supported quantitatively by 2.2×

more successful agent engagements. Further-more, external reader judges rated the resulting texts higher in “human touch” qualities like storytelling and integrating unique personal ex-periences. Overall, our results demonstrate that proactively engaging users through estab-lished human practices is beneficial for effec-tive LLM-supported co-authoring, providing useful methodologies, techniques and insights for future research on collaborative agents. 1

## 1 Introduction 

Writing has been a fundamental human activity for millennia. Recently, though, Large Language Mod-els (LLMs) have reshaped how we create texts, making LLM-assisted writing one of the most widespread uses of these models (Chatterji et al., 2025). Nowadays, authors increasingly rely on LLMs, through standard chat or interactive canvas interfaces, to alleviate demanding authoring tasks like synthesizing sources and drafting large blocks

Yet, such LLM-boosted text generation often comes at the expense of sidelining the human role and effectiveness in the authoring process. This comes with over-reliance on LLM outputs along with challenges like verifying text faithfulness and review fatigue (Buçinca et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025). Indeed, few recent works explored im-proving human-LLM co-authoring processes, ad-dressing concrete aspects such as pre-writing brain-storming (Jiang et al., 2024), outlining (Zhang et al., 2026) and turn-based initiative-taking (Shao et al., 2026). However, an agentic scheme that actively engages the human throughout the entire co-authoring process, from brainstorming to re-viewing, remains under-explored. In this paper, we propose a novel approach to-wards bridging these gaps in the complex setting of long-form, source-grounded authoring. Our research hypothesis is that effective synergetic 

human-LLM co-authoring may be achieved by properly adapting established expert methodolo-gies for human-human co-authoring. To explore it, we present C OWRITER , an agentic scheme follow-ing the seminal cognitive-science work of Flower and Hayes (1981) and its prominent extension to writing teams by Lowry et al. (2004). These works formalize distinct writing activities (such as brain-storming, outlining, drafting, and reviewing) and, for teams, collaborator roles (e.g., leaders, writ-ers, and reviewers). Our adaptation steers the writ-ing process flexibly through these activities, while keeping the human author fully engaged and influ-ential all along. To assess the utility of C OWRITER for hu-man users, we devise a comprehensive evalua-tion scheme that involves author-centric assess-ments alongside objective measurements of the co-authoring session trajectories (e.g., writing-activity coverage and user engagement) and the eventual written document. ## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Human-LLM Co-Authoring 

Assisting humans to write and express knowledge has long been a research goal, predating LLMs by decades (Engelbart, 1962). Unlike fully auto-matic text generation, human–LLM co-authoring combines the strengths of LLMs with human con-tributions, perspectives, and editorial control. Many recent LLM-powered writing assistants fo-cus on inline editor assistance, including autocom-plete, rewriting, and local text suggestions (e.g., Yuan et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023; Hoque et al., 2024; Yeh et al., 2024). These systems are largely orthogonal to our work, as such assistive features can naturally be incorporated into our broader co-authoring scheme. Closer to our setting are recent agentic co-authoring systems, in which an LLM actively par-ticipates in writing. Jiang et al. (2024) focuses 2primarily on pre-writing exploration and discov-ering “unknown unknowns.” Shao et al. (2026) studies turn-based collaborative environments built around a chat interface, shared document canvas, and source materials. Others explore alternative interfaces, such as the tree-structured document representation of Zhang et al. (2026). We focus on proactive agentic behavior that or-chestrates an established authoring methodology while sustaining user engagement, thereby miti-gating the “human-sidelining” effect that writers report (Zhao et al., 2025; Reza et al., 2025). Specif-ically, our work targets source-grounded factual au-thoring, which requires continued human involve-ment in activities such as claim verification, per-spective shaping, and narrative steering. 

2.2 Human (Co-)Authoring Methodologies 

Research in cognitive science and collaborative writing has extensively studied how skilled authors operate, providing a strong empirical foundation for our method design. Single-author writing is widely modeled as an iterative process of plan-ning, translating, and reviewing, coordinated by a central monitor (Flower and Hayes, 1981), rather than a linear progression toward a final document. During this process, an expert writer engages in 

knowledge-transforming , i.e., reshaping content according to goals and audience, rather than sim-ply knowledge-telling (Bereiter and Scardamalia, 1987). Specific stages within this process are asso-ciated with better documents, including rich initial 

goal setting to define the task (Flower and Hayes, 1980), dedicated pre-writing brainstorming (Gra-ham and Perin, 2007), and formal outlining prior to drafting (Kellogg, 1988). In multi-author settings, poorly organized col-laboration often becomes inefficient and frustrating (Ede and Lunsford, 1990). To structure effective and coherent collaboration, Lowry et al. (2004) syn-thesized empirical studies of real writing teams into a foundational taxonomy of expert collaborative writing activities and flows. Notably, this taxon-omy formalizes distinct activities (brainstorming, outlining, drafting, reviewing, revising, and copy-editing) alongside specific roles, such as leaders, writers, and reviewers (Posner and Baecker, 1992). Directly operationalizing these expert method-ologies, our agentic workflow (§3) adapts the taxon-omy of Lowry et al. (2004) into a structured LLM-based co-authoring workflow, guided by three spe-cialized agentic components. 

## 3 COWRITER Co-Authoring Architecture 

This section introduces C OWRITER , an agent-based platform for the co-authoring task (§3.1) de-signed to lead a methodological co-writing process (§3.2; Figure 1). The system includes a main agent to direct the workflow and engage the human co-author (§3.3; Table 1), a monitor to oversee the writing process (§3.3.2), and a reviewer agent to provide external “objective” feedback (§3.4). We then deploy C OWRITER (§4) to assess the benefits of this guided approach (§5). 

3.1 COWRITER Task and Environment 

We extend the collaborative human-agent author-ing task from prior work (Jiang et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2026) to a source-grounded setting, where we focus on authoring long documents with ex-plicit structure (i.e., hierarchically titled sections) whose content relies substantially on given (and cited) source documents. To maintain faithfulness to the sources and enable effective content verifi-cation (Gao et al., 2023), we expect that any in-formation requiring citation (e.g., not insights and perspectives of the author, or functional statements; Zhang et al., 2025) would explicitly reference sup-porting evidence in the source documents. The C OWRITER environment employs an aug-mented chat-and-canvas layout (left part of Fig-ure 1). Similar to standard chat-assisted writing, it includes a chat-based message queue for human-agent communication, and a canvas for joint docu-ment authoring. In addition, following Shao et al. (2026), the UI also includes a source explorer to view information about the source documents (UI screenshot in App. A). Notably, the canvas sup-ports concurrent document updates by the user and the agent at any moment. In contrast to turn-based co-authoring approaches (Shao et al., 2026), this allows the agent to proactively act upon user edits.

3.3 LEADER Agent 

The L EADER is the primary agentic collaborator — it reads sources and revises the document, while using conversation to proactively guide and engage the user through the co-authoring workflow. 

3.3.1 Core Functionality 

The agent’s core is a 7.7k-token system prompt 

(full prompt in Figure 28, App. B) implementing an agentic workflow that adapts the (human) co-authoring process and writing activities formulated by Lowry et al. (2004). Its steps ( 1 – 7 ) are de-picted in Table 1. Primarily, the L EADER is instructed to proac-tively guide the user through the co-authoring ac-tivities, rather than simply following user instruc-tions, as the writing-activity coverage results in §5.1 show. At the same time, it is directed to com-ply with ad hoc user requests even when they de-viate from the workflow ( ∗ in Table 1). specifying how to interact with the sources, can-vas, and chat (e.g., exploring sources or updating the document outline); and (d) invites , which are proactive suggestions to encourage step-relevant user engagement (see below). Finally, when the user edits the document, the L EADER proactively acts upon the revisions rather than waiting for ex-plicit instructions. 3.3.2 The M ONITOR 

During complex authoring processes, both human writers and LLM-based agents can drift off-track, necessitating a governing element. To maintain focus, human cognitive models describe a cen-tral monitor (Flower and Hayes, 1981), and re-cent agentic systems employ mechanisms to mod-erate the process (Jiang et al., 2024; Balepur et al., 2025). Inspired by these concepts, the MONITOR 

is an LLM-based component embedded within the LEADER . It observes environment events — such as document updates and user messages — and tracks progress along the workflow steps. To maintain visibility of the workflow progress, the M ONITOR maintains a Writing Log widget (example in App. C), providing structured context to both the user and the agent. The log displays: (a) a checklist of visited workflow steps (Table 1); (b) the explicit writing goal captured from the user; and (c) a concise timeline of events occurring dur-ing the session. Using the state captured in the Writing Log, the MONITOR triggers the L EADER with four types of 

system reminders to ensure workflow adherence: (1) the current status of the authoring process, e.g., word count and work-in-progress markers; (2) un-fulfilled questions or requests from either the user or the L EADER ; (3) the next candidate workflow steps; and (4) short feedback flagging key diver-gences from the system prompt guidelines. Further details are in App. D. 

3.4 REVIEWER Agent 

Delegating critique to a separate agent follows prior multi-agent writing systems that decouple genera-tion from review to reduce confirmation bias and identify a broader range of issues (Bae and Kim, 2024; Hong et al., 2024). In the collaborative-writing taxonomy of Lowry et al. (2004), a reviewer evaluates the document without participating in its writing. Accordingly, R EVIEWER simulates an ex-ternal reviewer from the target audience. Invoked on demand by L EADER , R EVIEWER 

reads only the current document and the Writing Goal section of the Writing Log . It does not access the chat history or activity log, making its feedback independent of the authoring process. The agent can additionally retrieve relevant source content to verify claims or identify missing information. REVIEWER returns a concise structured review containing bulleted lists of strengths , issues , and 

suggested fixes . L EADER then surfaces the review in the chat for collaborative action. Additional technical details are provided in App. E. 

## 4 Experimental Setup 

Our experiments evaluate whether guiding users through a methodical workflow leads to an effec-tive collaborative writing process and produces a source-grounded document that users can confi-dently approve. We describe our user study com-paring C OWRITER against a baseline (§4.1), fol-lowed by our evaluation dimensions for both the co-authoring sessions and the final outputs (§4.2). 

4.1 User Study Design Task setup. The study simulates a realistic long-form writing task requiring prolonged human-agent interaction. Participants collaboratively authored a 750–1000 word blog post on a specified topic, grounded in provided sources. To elicit meaningful, active user contributions, we instructed participants to act as a popular blogger , tasking them with combining factual claims from the sources with their own perspectives , personal experiences , and 

illustrative examples (full details in App. F). 

Data. We adapted four everyday-life blog topics from the DREsS dataset (Yoo et al., 2025) (e.g., 

“The Impact of Smartphones on Family and Friend Communication” ), manually chosen to satisfy three criteria: (i) they are easy to understand and suffi-ciently engaging for non-expert writers, (ii) all top-ics encourage participants to contribute personal perspectives and experiences, and (iii) they are comparable in complexity and writing difficulty. For each topic, we manually curated ten relevant diverse academic research papers. To keep the read-ing load manageable, each source was required to be academically credible, relevant, permissively licensed, and under 20 pages (see App. G). 

Baseline system. We compared COWRITER 

against a control baseline that simulates a stan-5dard LLM-assisted setup of a chat, a document canvas, and uploaded sources, excluding the com-ponents supporting our co-authoring workflow and user engagement. To ensure a fair comparison, both systems share the exact same environment and underlying model. 3 To mitigate possible con-founding variables, we stripped our collaborative mechanisms: we modified the L EADER prompt to remove the workflow and engagement instructions, and disabled the other two agentic components, the 

Writing Log , and localized attribution. 

Participants and procedure. Fourteen graduate students with academic writing experience partic-ipated in a within-subjects study. 4 Following an orientation and 15 minutes of guided practice per system, participants completed two writing ses-sions on different topics — one with C OWRITER 

and one with the baseline. To encourage realistic iterative writing and source exploration, each ses-sion was intended to last up to 75 minutes, with the second session occurring within 48 hours of the first. We counterbalanced both the system order and the topic-to-system assignment, ensuring that all topics appeared approximately equally often in both conditions. Participants completed post-session questionnaires after each task, followed by a final comparative survey upon finishing both. Recruitment and procedural details are in App. H.

For both dimensions, we utilize authors’ questionnaire ratings alongside quantitative mea-surements and external judgments. This section outlines these aspects; full results are in §5.

## 5 Results and Analysis 

We next compare C OWRITER to the baseline sys-tem in terms of guiding the writing process and maintaining user engagement (§5.1), process effi-ciency (§5.2), and the quality of the authored docu-ments (§5.3). Despite the modest cohort size, clear trends stand out, with multiple statistically signifi-cant results (detailed results in App. M).

Overall, the figure clearly indicates that C OWRITER -guided sessions closely follow the established co-writing methodology, unlike the baseline sessions guided by a “bare” LLM. 5.3 Document Quality 

We next assess the impact of C OWRITER ’s method-ological workflow on the quality of the co-authored documents, as evaluated by the participating au-thors, independent human raters, and LLM judges. 

Author perception. The 3 bottom bars of Fig-ure 3 show a clear and statistically significant trend of higher perceived ownership by authors of doc-uments written with C OWRITER . This includes 

self-expression (feeling the text reflected their own style or captured their intended key points), as well as verifiability and integrity (recall C OWRITER ’s localized citations, which are easier to verify). Ac-cordingly, for an overall sense of ownership (will-ingness to publish the document under the author’s name), 11 participants favored C OWRITER , vs. one favoring the baseline ( p = 0 .006 ). In conclusion, documents co-authored with C OWRITER are per-ceived by authors as significantly more authentic to their personal voice, easier to verify, and ultimately more of their own. 

Reader perspective. We employed human and LLM raters to assess the authored documents in-dependently of the co-authoring process, on four key dimensions established in Lee et al. (2025), depicted in Figure 4. Overall, across all four di-mensions and the 19 individual evaluation ques-tions, documents co-authored with C OWRITER 

were rated higher than or similarly to those pro-duced with the baseline, which aligns with the au-thors’ own positive assessments. Most notably, human raters preferred COWRITER documents for both Linguistic Fluency and Interestingness , with respective preference rates of 43% and 30% higher than for the baseline. The top five questions favoring COWRITER (Figure 10) mostly relate to author self-expression and a more human writing style, which was also noticeable to external human raters, in sync with authors’ own perceptions. Similar trends appear in the LLM-judge evaluation (Figure 11 and Figure 12), including corresponding preferences for storytelling , use of personal experiences , and unique ideas or perspectives from the author .These results link the authoring process to docu-ment outcomes. A structured process with greater human engagement yields documents that authors and external raters (human and LLM) find more nat-ural and reflective of the author, without sacrificing other quality measures. Human co-writing methods thus transfer well to human-LLM schemes, while human involvement remains valuable. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We introduced C OWRITER , a proactive agentic framework that shifts human-LLM co-authoring from passive text generation to an actively guided, methodical workflow. By adapting expert human writing methodologies, C OWRITER leads the en-tire authoring process while actively eliciting user engagement. Our evaluation demonstrates that this approach significantly improves both the co-authoring process and the final output: C OWRITER 

improved the overall writing experience, increased engagement and ownership, and produced docu-ments preferred by authors, human annotators, and LLM evaluators over a strong control baseline. By demonstrating the value of proactive and methodi-cal guidance, this work provides a foundation for designing user-centered agents and harnesses for a wider range of complex collaborative tasks. 8Limitations 

Although the population size and demography is limited in our user studies (with recruitment con-ducted through universities for students and grad-uates), this is common practice in controlled NLP and HCI user studies. Relatedly, the study scope was limited to a moderate number of writing topics (as is common in comparable studies). Conducting large-scale controlled co-authoring studies is inher-ently expensive and time-intensive, as each session requires long interactive writing periods, extensive source materials, and detailed post-hoc evaluation. Such studies are particularly challenging to scale in academic research settings with limited resources and outreach. Nevertheless, the study was designed with counterbalancing across systems and topics, and combined user feedback, human document as-sessment, and automatic evaluation to provide mul-tiple complementary perspectives on system be-havior. Broader deployment studies across more diverse populations, professional writing settings, and writing genres (e.g., expert or creative writing, where authors may prefer less guidance) would help further validate the generality of our findings and the significance of results. Our implementation used claude-sonnet-4.6 

as the underlying LLM backbone. Some observed behaviors may therefore depend on characteris-tics of this specific model family. However, our work primarily studies co-authoring workflows, in-teraction design, and agentic behavior rather than model-specific capabilities. We therefore expect the proposed principles and workflow structure to transfer to other modern LLMs with comparable instruction-following and tool-use abilities. 

## Ethical Considerations 

Human involvement. The study was approved by the institutional review boards (IRBs) of the participating universities. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty to their accumulated compensation. Participants were recruited via university-wide ad-vertisements and were compensated according to local standards, independent of performance. Session logs, including interactions with the sys-tem and produced documents, were recorded with participant awareness for research purposes. All collected data was anonymized, and no identifying participant information is reported in this paper. 

Inaccuracies of LLM generated output. Partic-ipants were instructed that LLM-generated content may be imperfect and should be treated as assistive material requiring verification. However, the goal of the study was to evaluate collaborative writing behavior in a realistic setting. Therefore, imper-fections in model outputs were expected and con-sidered part of the experimental conditions rather than an anomaly. The resulting documents were created solely for experimental purposes and were not intended for external use. 

Data. The study used academic source docu-ments with appropriate licenses permitting their use in research. These sources did not contain sen-sitive personal information. 

Use of AI. We used Claude Code as a coding assistant while developing C OWRITER system and environment, as well as formatting and suggest-ing content for the paper. We used ChatGPT 5.4 

and C OWRITER for assisting in paraphrasing some parts of the text while writing this paper to improve clarity and coherence. Any use of AI was followed by manual verification and editing to maintain cor-rectness and authenticity.

Agent execution. At the start of a co-authoring session, the underlying LLM is given the system prompt, and the agent greets with a predefined mes-sage to the user. Concur-rent document updates (by both L EADER and the user) are handled with the Yjs protocol that man-ages concurrent updates to a shared data structure (the document) in a conflict-free approach (Nico-laescu et al., 2015). 21 H User Study Details 

Ethics approval. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) committees of the participating universities. We ob-tained approval from each committee before recruit-ing participants. All participants gave informed consent before taking part and could withdraw at anytime from the study. 

Recruitment. We recruited participants through a public call distributed to Masters and PhD stu-dents at two universities. Eligibility required some prior experience using AI tools and having written at least one multi-page academic document in En-glish (e.g., for a course or a research project). Four-teen participants (P1–P14) completed both writing sessions and all questionnaires. 

Participant background. Before the study, par-ticipants completed a pre-study survey covering their writing experience, use of AI writing tools, academic background, and general attitude towards AI. The latter was measured with the four-item AI Attitude Scale (AIAS-4; Grassini, 2023). Figure 9 summarizes the responses of the 14 participants. 

Counterbalanced assignment. Each participant completed one session with the baseline system and one with C OWRITER , on two different topics drawn from the four topics in Table 4. To avoid bi-asing the participants, the systems were introduced as Systems A and B. We counterbalanced the sys-tem order and the topic–system pairing across par-ticipants. Each topic was assigned to exactly 7 of the 14 participants and appeared in both conditions. Table 5 gives the exact per-participant assignment. 

Procedure and logging. The orientation session was conducted over virtual communication. For each writing session we recorded the complete ac-tivity log, including the full chat between the par-ticipant and the system and the final document, which forms the basis of the analyses reported in this paper. 

Compensation. Participants were compensated following the IRB-approved guidelines of their in-stitution. At one university, compensation was an hourly rate of approximately $25–$33, determined by the participant’s academic degree. At the other university, a fixed payment of e100 was paid for the full study of approximately 5.5 hours (orienta-tion, two writing sessions, and questionnaires).

## I User Study Questionnaires 

We developed the questionnaire statements to tar-get our co-authoring process. ## K Session Annotation Details 

This appendix details the conversation annotations underlying the process metrics (§4.2), including the LLM procedure, human validation, and inter-rater agreement. 

LLM annotation. We annotated each session message by prompting an LLM (claude-opus-4.8 with high reasoning ef-fort) once for each metric: 1. Writing activities: The model labels each message with the single most prominent writ-ing activity from the seven predefined activi-ties (Table 1), or “other” if none apply (prompt in Figure 23). 2. Engagement phrases: The model extracts every engagement expression verbatim from each message, classifying its type (question, choice, action request, or next-step proposal) and whether it was followed up (i.e., di-rectly addressed in the collaborator’s next turn; prompt in Figure 24). These message-level annotations (activity type, total engagement expressions, and successful follow-ups) are then aggregated per system to cal-culate the metrics in §4.2. 

Inter-rater agreement with human annotation. 

Two human annotators, both PhD students experi-enced in English academic writing, independently labeled 118 messages across four sessions (two per system) using the guidelines provided to the LLM. Annotation process. Five annotators rated the 30 sampled document pairs. Three of them had also participated in the writing study. Annotate engagement events in a CoWriter study session. An engagement is a message segment that invites, requests, or prompts the other party to respond or act — beyond merely answering what was just asked. Both agent and user engagements are annotated. 

What counts as an engagement 

A chat-message segment (agent or user) matching one of the four types below. One message may contain several engagements — one row per engagement, each judged for follow-up independently. Split into separate rows only when the parts can be followed up independently; a single sentence expecting one answer is one row. 

Not an engagement: 

• Plain compliance ("Done, fixed the typo"), status updates, acknowledgments ("Noted."), content delivery with no prompt attached. 

• An affirmative reply to the other party’s offer or proposal ("yes, go ahead") — that is an answer (a follow-up), not a new engagement. Only asks going beyond accepting what was offered get their own row. 

• Rhetorical or formulaic sign-off questions expecting no specific answer ("How does that sound?" appended to a delivery). 

Every chat message emits at least one row. If a message contains no engagement, emit the minimal row {msg_id, engager, engagement: false} — so coverage of all messages is verifiable. 

Engagement types 

• question — asks something to be answered in words: perspective, preference, factual information, or clarification ("What angle matters most to you?", "why is this sentence in italics?"). 

• choice — presents 2+ explicitly enumerated alternatives that resolve one concrete decision ("keep or drop section 5?", "title A / B / C, or something else").

Follow-up 

Next turn is the other party’s next contiguous block of chat messages, before the engaging party speaks again. If the other party never takes a next turn (e.g., session ends), followed_up: false .

followed_up is True only when that next turn directly and explicitly takes up the engagement, per its type: 

• question : directly answers the question as asked. 

• choice : selects one (or more) of the offered alternatives, or supplies its own alternative resolving the same decision .

• next_step_proposal : proceeds to the proposed step or acknowledging it directly.

Stop Drilling, Start Playing: What Science Says About Raising Smart Kids 

1. Hook: Play Is Brain Training 

Picture a group of preschoolers playing Simon Says. They’re laughing, squirming, desperately trying not to move when the rule says stop. It looks like pure fun — and it is. But here’s what’s also happening: those children are actively training their prefrontal cortex, the part of the brain responsible for focus, impulse control, and decision-making. Neuroscientists have found that the prefrontal cortex is refined through play, and that play stimulates the production of proteins responsible for the growth of new neurons and synapses 594561:L53 . In other words, play is not the opposite of learning. It may be the most powerful form of it — and the research is unambiguous about why that matters for your child’s future. 

2. The Science: What Play Does to the Developing Brain 

So what exactly is happening in that game of Simon Says? The answer lies in a set of cognitive skills called executive function 

(EF) — the brain’s "air traffic control system," responsible for focus, working memory, impulse control, and the ability to switch between tasks, critical to healthy development through adolescence and into adult life 781094:L53-55 . Here is the crucial finding: a meta-analysis of 150 studies found that self-regulation at age 4 is positively associated with academic performance in math and literacy, and negatively associated with substance abuse and criminal behaviour 781094:L63 ,and is also linked to social competence and pre-academic skills including literacy and numeracy 640485:L63 . A child’s temperament matters too: 26 out of 27 studies confirmed a significant relationship between temperament and school readiness, with emotion management identified as key to children’s ability to concentrate and succeed 244377:L227 . And beyond academics, a positive psychology intervention showed that fostering positive emotions and social relationships in early childhood significantly increased well-being and positive approaches to learning 585439:L55 . Play — especially adult-guided, intentional play — is one of the most powerful ways to build all of these skills, because the prefrontal cortex is refined through play, stimulating the production of proteins responsible for new neuron and synapse growth 594561:L53 .The science is unambiguous: play does not interrupt development — it drives it. 

3. The Tension: Play vs. Formal Learning — A False Choice 

So if play is this powerful, why are so many children being pushed into formal academics earlier and earlier? There is a growing trend to introduce structured reading and mathematics in preschool — often at the expense of play. (Apparently, someone decided that finger-painting and building block towers were not "serious" enough. The blocks, for the record, disagree.) In a misguided effort to increase school readiness, some education systems are introducing basic academic skills earlier in preschool at the expense of whole-child development through playful pedagogies 594561:L65 — and the evidence suggests this strategy backfires. A study of 6,039 students in Turkey found that students who started school before 72 months (6 years) consistently scored lower on national exams than those who began at a developmentally appropriate age 108418:L169 . A study of 1,391 preschoolers in China found an optimal enrollment age — both very early and delayed enrollment produced worse outcomes 379724:L256-258 . This concern is global: researchers across more than 20 countries have noted that an ever-increasing focus on direct instruction is not developmentally appropriate 176374:L41-43 . The good news: play and structure are not enemies. Playful pedagogies can be more effective than traditional approaches in fostering social, emotional, cognitive, and creative skills 594561:L106 , and the most effective educators balance structured guidance with child-directed activity and independent exploration 594561:L122-126 . The goal is not to choose between play and learning — it is to integrate them wisely. 

4. How I Almost Got It Wrong 

I’ll be honest — I used to be that parent. My son Arbel was four years old, and every time I picked him up from gan (kindergarten), I’d feel a quiet pang of guilt watching him race back to the Lego corner instead of sitting down with a workbook. His friend Eden was already tracing letters. Another kid, Yoav, could count to thirty. And there I was, watching Arbel build an elaborate spaceship out of mismatched blocks, narrating an entire story to himself in a serious tone I’d never heard him use before. I started pushing — gently at first, then less gently. Flashcards before dinner. Letter tracing on Saturday mornings. Arbel would comply, quietly, and then go straight back to his blocks the moment I turned around. It wasn’t until I stumbled onto the research behind this post that I understood what I had almost taken away from him. That spaceship wasn’t a distraction. It was a classroom. 

5. Practical Takeouts: What You Can Do Today 

The research is clear — but what does it look like in practice? A Canadian program called Building Brains and Futures (BBF) identified 10 simple, evidence-based games — each just 5 minutes long — that measurably improve executive function, language, and motor skills in preschoolers after just 6–8 weeks 781094:L67-69 . The best part: you already know most of them. Simon Says builds impulse control; Red Light/Green Light trains self-regulation; pretend play (taking on roles like "doctor and patient") develops working memory and flexibility; and block building strengthens both motor skills and problem-solving 781094:L112-144 . You don’t need a curriculum — you need 5 minutes and a willing child. Parents also play a more powerful role than many realize: parental belief that play is genuinely developmental is a significant predictor of children’s inhibitory control, even after accounting for age and socioeconomic status 836326:L200-201 . Children who frequently engage in fine motor activities at home (drawing, cutting, building) show stronger visual-spatial working memory 836326:L202 , and engagement in home learning activities — reading together, counting, singing, and playing with building toys — predicts school readiness regardless of parents’ education level or language background 389714:L47-48 . So the next time your child asks to play instead of practice their ABCs, consider this: they might already be doing exactly the right thing.

• Source-grounding: use provided source files as the foundation for writing. When writing based on sources, cite to specific lines with <deeplink> tags (see Citation Format section). 

• Citation standard: cite all information that is not trivial or common knowledge — specific claims, data, definitions, quotes, and non-obvious arguments. Every non-trivial claim must have a <deeplink> citation (see Citation Format section). Each cited source must actually support the claim it’s attached to. 

• Fluidity: allow skipping steps and looping back to any activity at any time. 

• Take time on important decisions that deserve multiple turns: stance, exploring angles during Brainstorming, choices with multiple viable options. Keep the user engaged and encourage them to contribute.

Citation Format 

Every non-trivial claim must cite a source. Citations render as clickable links in both the document and chat. 

Reference syntax (same for document and chat): #<source-file-id>:L<start>-<end> — plain line numbers (e.g., 

L23-30 , NOT L23-L30 ). When a claim is strengthened by more than one source, stack their refs in a single href separated by 

; (e.g. #482910:L23-30;#540112:L22-28 ); the UI renders this as numbered markers next to the claim. Prefer multiple refs over a single ref whenever convergent sources are available — it visibly strengthens the claim. File ids come from 

list_source_files ; prefer granular line ranges over whole-file refs. 

• Document format: <deeplink href="#. . . ">claim words</deeplink> — wrap the claim’s own words. 

• Chat format: markdown link [claim words](#. . . ) . For a whole-file source brief in chat, use [source title](#<source-file-id>) .

Important rule: the visible text inside a link is always the claim’s own words (or the source title for a whole-file brief) — never the file id, the line range, a #. . . href string, or any footnote-style marker like [1] . File ids are internal; never surface them to the user in chat or in the document.

Chat Message Rules 

Brevity: Go straight to the point. Aim for 1–2 sentences per chat message; ≤60 words is the target, ≤100 words is the hard cap. Lead with the answer, proposal, or question — not preamble or reasoning. If you can say it in one sentence, don’t use three. (Word limits do not apply to XML in tool calls, update_document content, or structured option lists like Brainstorming’s 5–12 options.) 

Tone: be direct and objective. No superlatives, praise, or emotional validation ("Smart choice", "Great question", "Excellent pick"). When the user makes a choice, acknowledge briefly ("Noted." / "OK," / just name the choice) and proceed. 

Do not: 

• Restate what the user just said. 

• Open with filler or praise: "Great!", "Sure!", "Smart pick!", "That’s a good question", "Excellent choice". 

• Evaluate why a user’s choice is good — just act on it. 

• Thank the user repeatedly. 

• Narrate what you’re about to do before doing it. 

• Recap at the end ("In summary...", "To recap..."). 

• Expose internal reasoning or tool-call details. 

• Directly refer to the system reminders or the Writing Log in chat messages — act on their content silently. 

Focus each message on ONE of: 

• A question the user needs to answer. 

• A proposal or set of options needing the user’s pick. 

• A status update at a natural milestone (e.g., "Drafted §2 intro"). 

• An error or blocker. 

• One paragraph / outline chunk you drafted. 

At most 1 question or 3 options per turn. Keep each question, suggestion, or choice to one short sentence. Do not stack multiple workflow steps into one message. If the user skipped something important (a Goal aspect), briefly remind them — one line. 

Invite user participation: every reply should close with one of: 

• Choices — present options for the user to pick; always include "or suggest your own". 

• Question — ask something open-ended that draws on the user’s perspective or experience. 

• Review — ask the user to review, approve, or give their opinion on what you just did. Small localized edits (typo fix, style tweak without meaning change): just do it, briefly mention what you did, and propose an alternative if applicable. Bigger changes not yet discussed (rewriting a section, changing structure): ask before doing. When drafting, include 1–2 alternative phrasings for key sentences. 

Chat formatting: Chat messages are rendered as Markdown (GitHub Flavored). Use *italic* , **bold** , - lists , 1. numbered lists , > blockquotes , and headings ( ## ). Do NOT use XML tags ( <strong> , <emphasis> , etc.) in chat — XML is only for document tool calls. To update the document, use update_document — do not paste long drafts into chat. Use 

[TBD] markers for missing info. 

Citing sources in chat: follow the Citation Format section — wrap the claim’s words in a markdown link. Never surface file ids to the user. 

Emphasis: Use bold and nested lists to highlight questions, options, and key information the user needs to focus on. 

Language: Write the document and chat messages in the same language the user uses. If sources are in a different language, translate when incorporating them. 

Clean output: Show only clear natural language. Never expose internal tool calls or technical reasoning in chat. 

Plain language: Do not assume the user knows how the app works, what the writing process involves, or any terminology. Use natural language: say "let’s think together about the topic" not "brainstorming phase", say "writing goal" not "rhetorical problem", say "let’s plan the structure" not "outlining activity."
