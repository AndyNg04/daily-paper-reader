## ABSTRACT 

Agentic recommendation pursues shopper goals through sequences of context-dependent decisions. Developing and evaluating such policies requires faithful recommendation world models that capture both the interactive shopping envi-ronment and users’ behavioral responses over long interactions. Existing re-sources rarely provide long-horizon, fine-grained real-user trajectories or support trajectory-level evaluation and offline validation against production A/B results. We introduce USER ARENA , a real-world e-commerce interactive benchmark con-taining 64,729 session trajectories from 11,010 users, spanning 566,102 items and 13 action types. USER ARENA pairs these trajectories with an interactive environment and supports offline validation against outcomes from a controlled production A/B test, enabling five-level evaluation from output validity and lo-cal decisions to sequential behavior, personalization, and outcomes. To improve simulator fidelity over these long-horizon interactions, we further propose On-

Policy Trajectory Distillation ( OPTD ), which uses trajectory-level intent to pro-vide dense teacher targets on student-generated rollouts. Experiments show that OPTD achieves the strongest overall fidelity among the evaluated simulators under USER ARENA ’s evaluation protocol and most closely reproduces the production policy effect in offline simulation. 1

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in language and multimodal agents create new opportunities for agentic recom-mendation, in which an agent pursues a shopper’s goal through a sequence of context-dependent decisions (Liu et al., 2026; Shang et al., 2025). Post-training and evaluation of the underlying recom-mendation policy require a scalable recommendation world model that simulates how the shopping environment and its users respond to each policy action (Ie et al., 2019). An executable environment models what the policy can observe and change, while a user simulator models how shoppers re-spond to those changes (Yao et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2024). Together, they support offline and counterfactual evaluation as well as reinforcement-learning-based optimization, reducing reliance on costly online experiments (Shi et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023). Existing resources provide rich behavioral data and interactive evaluation for such world models. Amazon Reviews (Hou et al., 2026) contains ratings and review text, Qilin (Chen et al., 2025) adds multimodal content and app-level sessions, and OmniBehavior (Chen et al., 2026) captures longitudinal traces. RecBench+ (Huang et al., 2026) and OPeRA (Wang et al., 2026) move from offline records toward interactive evaluation. Yet two crucial gaps remain: At the data level, ex-isting datasets rarely provide long-horizon trajectories that align real-user profiles, visual observa-tions, product context, and actions across repeated sessions. At the evaluation level, next-action agreement measures step-wise choices in isolation but does not assess whether simulated trajecto-ries preserve a user’s behavioral tendencies over long horizons. To address these limitations, we introduce USER ARENA , an interactive benchmark combining a production-grounded shopping environment with real-user trajectories recorded on one of Asia’s largest e-commerce platforms (Figure 1). It contains real-user data comprising 11,010 users, 566,102 items, and 64,729 session trajectories and covers a complete action space of 13 action types. Each trajectory aligns user profiles, visual observations, product context, and actions at every step. For long-horizon evaluation, U SER ARENA links sessions from the same user into cross-session trajectories. It also supports offline validation by comparing the same recommendation-policy pair in simulation with outcomes from a controlled production A/B test. We evaluate simulators at five levels: output validity ( L0 ), local behavioral fidelity ( L1 ), sequential behavioral fidelity ( L2 ), per-sonalization fidelity ( L3 ), and outcome fidelity ( L4 ). The protocol traces fidelity from output validity and local decisions to long-horizon behavior, personalization, and aggregate outcomes. Beyond constructing the benchmark, we also address the challenge of learning faithful user simula-tors. Simulator design has progressed from hand-crafted and log-driven models to LLM-based mod-els (Ie et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2024), yet reproducing user be-havior over long rollouts remains difficult for three reasons. (i) Behavioral miscalibration: learned simulators can deviate from real user behavior (Zhang et al., 2024; Yoon et al., 2024). (ii) Sparse supervision: exact next-action targets penalize plausible alternatives and overlook trajectory-level quality (Wang et al., 2025c; Zhang et al., 2026b). (iii) Exposure bias: teacher forcing condi-tions only on ground-truth prefixes, creating a mismatch with autoregressive inference (Dai et al., 2021; Chi et al., 2022). To address these challenges, we propose On-Policy Trajectory Distillation (OPTD ), which distills teacher guidance on current-policy responses at real states (Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). This on-policy supervision trains on student-generated prefixes, while trajectory-level intent guides teacher targets toward coherent long-horizon behavior. In summary, our contributions are threefold. (1) We introduce U SER ARENA , a real-world e-commerce interactive benchmark pairing long-horizon user trajectories with an interactive envi-ronment and a five-level protocol spanning environment compliance, local decisions, sequential be-havior, personalization, and outcomes. (2) We propose OPTD, which uses trajectory-level intent and dense teacher targets to guide student-generated rollouts toward coherent long-horizon behavior. (3) We benchmark representative text- and GUI-based simulators for effectiveness and efficiency and characterize U SER ARENA ’s behavioral composition and interaction horizons.

Second, its evaluation should go beyond next-action agreement to assess complete trajectories and whether simulator-based policy comparisons agree with real-user outcomes in production. Existing resources cover these requirements partially. E-commerce datasets contain real preferences and interaction logs but rarely retain the visual context of each decision (Harper & Konstan, 2015; Hou et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). Interactive platforms enable closed-loop evaluation but depend on user dynamics whose fidelity remains insufficiently established (Ie et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023). Recent benchmarks improve behavioral grounding through visual shopping traces or longitudinal records (Wang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a), yet none combines real step-level trajectories, visual context, a full shopping action space, long-horizon cross-session behavior, and production A/B validation (Table 1). To bridge this gap, U SER ARENA combines a production-grounded shopping environment, fine-grained real-user trajectories, and an evaluation protocol spanning trajectory fidelity and production validation. The environment reconstructs production recommendation-feed and item-detail pages with 13 action types, from feed navigation and product inspection to add-to-cart and purchase. It updates the visible feed by cropping the corresponding viewport from the pre-rendered full feed as the simulator scrolls. Clicking any currently exposed item, including one not clicked by the real user, opens an interactive detail page reconstructed from that item’s pre-rendered views and recorded product content. Following the observation–action formulation of interactive agents (Yao et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025), each recorded decision aligns the shopper’s profile, visual observation, visible product context, and action. The benchmark contains 

11,010 users, 64,729 sessions, 577,670 actions, and 566,102 products and 6 user attributes. Across these trajectories, 73,717 item clicks span 41 product categories (Appendix Figure 4). For long-horizon evaluation, U SER ARENA organizes 59,378 chronologically ordered sessions from 5,768 users into cross-session trajectories. They average 10.29 sessions and 86.36 actions per user, testing whether simulators preserve preferences and behavioral tendencies across repeated visits. Appendix Table 4 compares their interaction horizons with related benchmarks. For production A/B validation, U SER ARENA compares the same recommendation-policy pair offline in simulation with controlled production A/B outcomes. 2.2 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

As illustrated on the right side of Figure 1, U SER ARENA evaluates simulator fidelity at five levels of increasing scope: from whether its outputs can be parsed to whether its trajectories, personalized behavior, and session outcomes match those of real users. We use two evaluation modes. (1) Replay uses the recorded state at each step of a real session to pre-dict the logged next action. This isolates local next-action fidelity without accumulating simulator-induced state changes. (2) Rollout lets the simulator select actions and determine subsequent states until the session ends. It tests whether free-running simulated sessions reproduce real trajectories and aggregate outcomes. We evaluate L0 under both modes, L1 under Replay, and L2–L4 under Rollout. ## 3 ON-P OLICY TRAJECTORY DISTILLATION 

To model long-horizon shopping behavior in U SER ARENA , we propose On-Policy Trajectory Dis-tillation (OPTD). OPTD extracts a trajectory-level intent from each complete training trajectory and provides it as privileged context to a frozen teacher. The intent conditions dense token-level teacher supervision throughout each student response. A per-token trust region limits how far these targets depart from predictions based only on the student’s observable state. Figure 2 contrasts the train– test mismatch and myopic targets of SFT, the coarse response-level credit assignment of GRPO, and OPTD’s dense, trajectory-aligned teacher targets at every generated token. 3.1 TASK SETUP 

Problem formulation. USER ARENA records each shopping session as a trajectory. At every step, the user observes a page and responds to it. Let τu denote the trajectory of user u, xt the state at step t, and y⋆t the user’s real response at that step. The state xt contains the current page and interaction history before the response. The response y⋆t is a token sequence containing a short rationale followed by one structured interface action. We denote its token at position k by y⋆t,k ,its prefix before that position by y⋆t,<k , and its token length by |y⋆t |. A user simulator is a policy 

πθ (y | x) that generates response y from state x, where θ denotes the trainable student parameters. 4On-policy sampling. We treat πθ as the student and optimize it with token-level guidance from a teacher on the student’s own responses. In contrast, standard teacher forcing on y⋆t (Bengio et al., 2015; Ranzato et al., 2016) conditions every prediction on a ground-truth prefix. At inference, the student instead conditions on its own partial output, so errors can move it to prefixes absent from teacher-forced training (Dai et al., 2021; Chi et al., 2022). To expose training to these self-generated prefixes, OPTD adopts on-policy distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). At each training step, we draw a real state xt from the empirical training distribution D and perform a one-step rollout by sampling a complete response from the current policy. 

yt = ( yt, 1, . . . , y t, |yt|) ∼ πθ (· | xt), (1) where yt,k is the generated token at position k, |yt| is the response length, and yt,<k =(yt, 1, . . . , y t,k −1) denotes its prefix before position k. A teacher with privileged trajectory-level context scores each position of yt, and the student learns from the resulting distributions on its own prefixes rather than only recorded prefixes. This reduces the mismatch between teacher-forced training and autoregressive inference. 3.2 TRAJECTORY -L EVEL INTENT SUPERVISION 

To guide the student toward behavioral patterns that unfold across an entire session, we provide the teacher with a trajectory-level intent extracted from the complete training trajectory. 

Intent extraction. We use a large language model as an extractor E that maps the trajectory to an abstract summary of the shopper’s intent, 2

zu = E(τu), (2) where zu is a structured record of complementary typed anchors capturing trajectory-wide prefer-ences, concerns, information needs, comparison criteria, and conditional decision tendencies. To keep zu from serving as an answer key for individual steps, the extraction and validation rules exclude concrete actions, specific products and identifiers, exact prices, trajectory outcomes, future-page content, and the step indices supporting the summary. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS 

We evaluate every simulator in two settings. The single-session setting evaluates each user’s tra-jectory within one session. The cross-session setting evaluates a chronologically ordered trajectory spanning multiple sessions from the same user, emphasizing long-horizon behavioral prediction across visits. We report both settings in Table 2. 

Single-session results. The single-session block of Table 2 shows that OPTD ranks first on all eight reported L1–L4 metrics, spanning local and sequential behavioral fidelity, personalized population fidelity, and outcome fidelity. This broad improvement aligns with its training design: trajectory-level intent guides token-level targets on the student’s own rollout distribution, while the trust region and grounding term keep this guidance close to behavior supported by observable states and recorded responses. Customer-R1’s profile-conditioned interaction history is consistent with its strong local prediction, while STA’s multimodal state context and reinforcement-learning-tuned checkpoint align with its strong sequential and population-level fidelity. A/B Agent remains competitive on ACR error, but its lower Replay and Rollout valid-action rates suggest less reliable transfer to the fixed USER ARENA action protocol, limiting downstream fidelity. 

Cross-session results. The cross-session block of Table 2 uses the same summary metrics. Among all methods, OPTD ranks first on five of the eight L1–L4 metrics and second on two more. It achieves the strongest L2 sequential behavioral fidelity, with the lowest decision-mix divergence and trajectory-length error, together with the highest L3 personalized population fidelity. These results demonstrate OPTD’s gains in preserving long-horizon trajectory structure and personalized behav-ioral patterns across sessions. Its leading L1 accuracy separately indicates the strongest step-wise ac-tion prediction under Replay. To assess whether simulator-based policy com-parisons reflect real-user responses to recommen-dation changes, we conduct an offline validation against outcomes recorded for the control and treatment arms of a production A/B test. We eval-uate the same pair of ranking policies in each simulator and compare the simulated policy ef-fect with the recorded production effect. In sim-ulation, we assign a frozen pool of 10,000 users evenly to the two policy arms and hold the users and product catalog fixed across all simulators. Because the ranking policies alter item exposure, we measure their effect using the between-arm difference in page-view click-through rate ( ∆PV-CTR). The effect ratio divides the simulated ∆PV-CTR by its online value, and the effect error is the absolute deviation of this ratio from 1. Among the evaluated user simulators, OPTD most closely reproduces the online policy effect, reaching an effect ratio of 0.713 ×. sets the grounding weight to zero. (5) w/o SFT initializes the teacher and student from the base model. Figure 3(a) reports one representative metric from each behavioral fidelity level L1–L4. First, removing any component reduces performance on at least one selected metric, showing that each contributes to behavioral fidelity. Second, the w/o SFT variant degrades most markedly across the selected metrics, indicating that the warm-up establishes the instruction-following and action-generation abilities required for subsequent trajectory distillation. 

Turing-style human evaluation. Automatic metrics quantify agreement with recorded behavior, but they do not directly test whether a complete simulated trajectory appears human to an observer. We therefore define a source-blinded Turing-style evaluation (Turing, 1950) that compares OPTD with the text-based Customer-R1 (Wang et al., 2025c) and two GUI-based simulators, STA (Zhang et al., 2025) and A/B Agent (Zhang et al., 2026a). For each user context, three annotators inde-pendently compare an anonymized, order-randomized real–simulated pair and identify the machine-generated trajectory or choose Unsure . Each simulator is evaluated on 200 trajectory pairs sampled from the held-out test set. Figure 3(b) shows that OPTD has the lowest Human win rate and the highest Model win rate among the four simulators. Appendix C.4 provides the complete protocol and experiment analysis.

User simulator development. Early systems represented users through configurable or parameter-ized dynamics, including RecSim (Ie et al., 2019), RecoGym (Rohde et al., 2018), and the prob-abilistic RecSim NG framework (Mladenov et al., 2021). Log-driven approaches then learned in-teraction dynamics from observed behavior: Virtual-Taobao (Shi et al., 2019) combines generative customer modeling with adversarial imitation, while the Generative Adversarial User Model (Chen et al., 2019) learns user dynamics and rewards for model-based policy training. IRecGAN (Bai et al., 2019) incorporates generated trajectories into adversarial user-model learning. More recent LLM-based simulators, including RecAgent (Wang et al., 2025a), Agent4Rec (Zhang et al., 2024), and SUBER (Corecco et al., 2024), increase the expressiveness of profile-conditioned behavior, while Customer-R1 (Wang et al., 2025c), Shop-R1 (Zhang et al., 2026b), and RecVerse (Tang et al., 2026) apply reinforcement learning to shopping simulation. Despite this progress, two optimization challenges remain. (1) Sparse rewards: most methods use exact-match rewards, treating plau-sible alternatives and invalid behaviors alike. (2) Exposure bias: teacher forcing trains only on ground-truth prefixes, leaving models unprepared for self-generated prefixes and vulnerable to com-pounding errors at inference. OPTD addresses both through dense distributional supervision on student-sampled responses and trajectory-level intent for long-horizon behavioral guidance. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced U SER ARENA , an interactive benchmark combining a production-grounded shopping environment with fine-grained, long-horizon real-user trajectories. We also developed a five-level protocol that evaluates simulator quality from environment compliance and local decisions to se-quential, personalized population, and outcome fidelity. For this long-horizon setting, we proposed On-Policy Trajectory Distillation (OPTD), which uses trajectory-level intent to guide dense teacher supervision on the student’s own rollouts. OPTD achieves the strongest overall fidelity under this protocol and most closely reproduces the production policy effect in the controlled comparison. As future work we will develop a leaderboard platform around U SER ARENA to support standardized and continuously updated evaluation of user simulators. B.1 BASELINE DETAILS 

We compare against eight representative user simulators while preserving each method’s observation modality, context construction, memory mechanism, and training objective. All methods use the common U SER ARENA environment, action space, source test split, and output schema. 1. RecAgent (Wang et al., 2025a) conditions an LLM agent on a user profile and hierarchical sen-sory, short-term, and long-term memories. The memories are updated only with interactions that have already occurred. 2. Agent4Rec (Zhang et al., 2024) represents a user through profile, memory, and reflection mod-ules. Retrieved memories and a compact reflection on prior interactions inform each action. 3. SimUSER (Bougie & Watanabe, 2025) constructs a profile from pre-session history and updates category preferences from executed interactions. Our adapted version retains one profile update per session and one action prediction per decision. 4. Shop-R1 (Zhang et al., 2026b) is a text-based simulator trained with supervised fine-tuning fol-lowed by reinforcement learning. It receives the textual page state and interaction history but not the user profile. 5. Customer-R1 (Wang et al., 2025c) extends the Shop-R1 training recipe with the shopper profile, recent clicks and purchases, and the executed interaction history. 6. AlignUSER (Bougie et al., 2026) augments supervised training with counterfactual actions and teacher-generated comparison rationales. Its evaluation prompt contains only the profile, past interactions, and current state. 7. A/B Agent (Zhang et al., 2026a) is a GUI-based simulator that combines the current screenshot with a user profile, retrieved long-term memory, working memory, and a fatigue mechanism. 8. STA (Zhang et al., 2025) is a GUI-based simulator that predicts a rationale and structured action from the current screenshot, textual page state, and interaction history. We use its reinforcement-learning-tuned checkpoint. B.2 DATA PROCESSING AND IMPLEMENTATION 

Data processing. We normalize the raw interaction logs into the 13-action U SER ARENA space and reconstruct the state immediately preceding each recorded decision. The visual observation and textual page state are drawn from the same pre-action interface state. We stratify users into training, validation, and test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio using seed 42, assigning all sessions from each user to the same split for both single- and cross-session experiments. Training and validation examples may be resampled to balance action coverage, whereas every test trajectory is retained once and is never resampled. Replay reconstructs each recorded state independently, while Rollout starts from the same initial context and thereafter advances only with actions accepted by the environment. 

Implementation details. To control for backbone capacity, all methods in the main comparison, including the prompting-only baselines, use Qwen3.5-2B (Team, 2026) with a maximum context length of 32k tokens. This shared-backbone setting isolates differences in prompting, memory, ob-servation modality, and post-training strategy from differences in model scale. We standardize the interaction interface of every trainable baseline and OPTD. Their base prompts use the same task instruction, action definitions, and response schema, while retaining any method-specific context described in Appendix B.1. We use Qwen3.5-397B (Team, 2026) to generate a short first-person rationale for each recorded action from the corresponding user context, interaction history, and cur-rent state. Each SFT target pairs this rationale with the recorded structured action. The rationale encourages the model to summarize relevant evidence from the user context, interaction history, and current state before selecting an action, and using this format for every trainable method keeps the supervision interface consistent. We cap each GUI image at 200,704 input pixels. For OPTD, we perform an SFT warm-up on the same interaction format so that the resulting checkpoint can follow task instructions and generate a short rationale followed by a valid action from the current state. We then freeze this checkpoint as the teacher and initialize the student from it. We use ROLL (Wang et al., 2025b) for distributed training, vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) for on-policy generation, and 18 Megatron-Core (Shoeybi et al., 2019) for student optimization and teacher inference. OPTD train-ing uses BF16 precision, tensor and sequence parallelism, distributed optimization, and activation recomputation to reduce GPU memory use. Replay uses greedy decoding and Rollout samples with temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95. Generation is capped at 512 tokens per decision. We cap Rollout at 31 executed actions for single-session evaluation and 42 for cross-session evaluation, one more than the maximum recorded action count in each setting. A trajectory is marked as truncated when it reaches the corresponding limit without terminating. B.3 TRAINING HYPERPARAMETERS 

All trainable baselines and OPTD use a peak learning rate of 2×10 −8. For GRPO-trained baselines, each rollout batch contains 64 samples organized into groups of eight responses per prompt. We use an initial KL-controller coefficient of 0.001 and a KL-loss coefficient of 0.01, with temperature 0.99 and top-p 0.95 for rollout generation. For OPTD, we optimize the student with Adam and zero weight decay, using linear learning-rate warmup followed by linear decay. We set the Jensen– Shannon mixing weight to β = 0 .5, which yields the standard Jensen–Shannon divergence, and the grounding weight to γ = 0 .1. C.1 PRODUCTION A/B V ALIDATION DETAILS 

Section 4.3 validates simulator-based policy comparisons offline against outcomes recorded in a production A/B test. The production reference is computed from outcomes recorded over a fixed observation window in two production traffic buckets, one serving the control policy and the other the treatment policy. We evaluate the same pair of policies in simulation using a fixed pool of 10,000 users and a frozen product catalog. Each policy is assigned exactly 5,000 users, and every simulator receives the same user assignment and product catalog. We compare five task-specific checkpoints, including OPTD, STA, Customer-R1, Shop-R1, and AlignUSER, with the Qwen3.5-2B base model and two general-purpose foundation models, GPT-6-Sol and GPT-6-Luna. 3

We compute PV-CTR within each policy arm as the total number of click actions divided by the total number of exposure page views. This definition matches the production metric and counts repeated exposures to the same item. For both the recorded production results and the offline simulation, we define the policy effect as ∆PV-CTR = PV-CTR treatment − PV-CTR control . The effect ratio divides the simulated policy effect by the production policy effect, and the effect error is the absolute deviation of this ratio from 1. Table 3 reveals three findings about simulator calibration to the production policy effect. First, OPTD provides the closest match, achieving an effect ratio of 0.713 × and an effect error of 0.287, which is 50.2% lower than that of the next-closest simulator. In contrast, the other task-specific simulators substantially attenuate the policy response, with effect ratios ranging from 0.020 × to 0.297 ×. The untuned Qwen3.5-2B model reaches only 0.004 ×. Second, the general-purpose GPT-6-Sol and GPT-6-Luna models overestimate the production response, reaching 1.576 × and 2.154 ×,respectively. This result indicates that general-purpose model capacity, without task-specific be-havioral calibration, does not directly translate into realistic sensitivity to ranking-policy changes. Third, all evaluated simulators retain some gap from the production reference. Even OPTD, the closest method in this comparison, underestimates the production effect by 28.7%.
