> ABSTRACT

In a hierarchical multi-agent system, a Leader assigns tasks and subagents carry them out. Jointly training the Leader and the subagents is difficult because a final answer gives only indirect feedback to the subagents. An LLM judge can score the performance of the subagents, but these scores depend on the judge’s own assessment of the quality of subagents’ responses. We present MultiAgent-OPD, which combines on-policy distillation with reinforcement learning to jointly train a shared-parameter Leader–Searcher–Answerer team end to end. We first train a Teacher in a Searcher–Answerer workflow using answer correctness. During team training, the frozen Teacher scores student-generated search and summary tokens to provide local process supervision for the Searchers, while the Leader learns from team success and the Answerer from candidate correctness. Across seven datasets, the trained system reaches 38.68% macro avg@4, exceeding Prompted Critic by 4.06 percentage points and C-MAS + RL, the trained cascaded multiagent system, by 2.61 percentage points. The team also solves more questions within four attempts. 

1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) for LLM-based multi-agent systems faces a central challenge: the final outcome reflects the team’s combined decisions, making it difficult to assess individual intermediate actions. As agents specialize in planning, information acquisition, and reasoning, effective training requires signals that reflect their different roles in a shared objective. Recent advances in collaborative post-training span discussion-based RL [Park et al., 2025], credit propagation through sequential reasoning [Motwani et al., 2025], and agent-wise advantage normalization [Feng et al., 2026]. This difficulty is particularly pronounced in Leader–Subagent systems, where a Leader decomposes a task, delegates its execution, and integrates subagent reports. Kimi K2.5’s PARL addresses the challenge by optimizing the Leader with frozen subagents [Kimi Team, 2026], so subagent adaptation stays outside joint training. M-GRPO enables joint optimization by combining final-answer and format rewards with LLM-based evaluations of subtask execution [Hong et al., 2025]. Such evaluations provide local supervision, but their reliability depends on the judge’s assessment capabilities, which are subject to biases and reasoning errors [Zheng et al., 2023]. Moreover, a

single subtask score offers limited guidance on the sequence of search decisions that produced the report. Jointly optimizing the hierarchy calls for informative feedback at the level of subagent execution. We match learning signals to each role’s relation to the final task objective. Outcome rewards align the Leader’s coordination decisions with team success and directly evaluate the Answerer’s predictions. Searchers, in contrast, acquire intermediate evidence whose downstream value depends on later selection and reasoning. A useful report may receive no positive outcome reward when another role fails to use it. This motivates us to introduce a complementary source of supervision for Searchers: a policy trained to acquire and summarize evidence guides them locally, while outcome rewards train coordination and answer generation. We introduce MultiAgent-OPD, a two-stage framework that integrates on-policy distillation (OPD) into end-to-end RL for hierarchical multi-agent systems (Figure 1). The first stage trains the Teacher in a simpler Searcher–Answerer workflow with rewards on answer correctness. In the second stage, the frozen Teacher provides token-level supervision on Searcher trajectories sampled from the hierarchical student’s current policy. Teacher–student log-probability differences yield local advantages for query generation and evidence summarization [Agarwal et al., 2024, Gu et al., 2024], while the Leader and the Answerer keep outcome-based RL objectives. Because the Teacher conditions on the subtask and the interaction history of the student, the feedback follows the states that the team reaches as its policies evolve. On seven question-answering datasets, MultiAgent-OPD achieves 38.68% macro avg@4, the highest among the evaluated systems. The score exceeds Prompted Critic (Appendix C.4) by 4.06 percentage points and C-MAS + RL, the cascaded multi-agent baseline, by 2.61 percentage points. It also exceeds the five RL-based search agents included in the comparison. Diagnostic comparisons examine the roles of direct Searcher supervision and task-specific Teacher training. Together, these results characterize how a policy learned in a simpler workflow can supply process supervision for subagents in a jointly trained hierarchy. We make three contributions.  

> ▶

We propose MultiAgent-OPD, a framework for end-to-end hierarchical multi-agent training that combines local Searcher supervision with outcome-based optimization of the Leader and the Answerer.  

> ▶

We formulate Searcher process supervision as on-policy distillation from a task-trained Teacher, which provides token-level guidance on the student’s evolving subtask trajectories.  

> ▶

We report higher macro avg@4 across seven datasets than the included cascaded, hierarchical, and RL-based search baselines, and provide diagnostic comparisons of the Searcher learning signal, Teacher training, and direct subagent supervision.

2 Related Work  

> Multi-agent learning signals and credit assignment.

MAPoRL trains agents through discussion, ReMA separates planning from reasoning execution, and MHGPO uses heterogeneous rollout groups for multi-agent search [Park et al., 2025, Wan et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2026]. Dr. MAS uses agent-wise advantage normalization and verifier-controlled Search–Answer calls [Feng et al., 2026]. PARL trains a Leader with frozen subagents [Kimi Team, 2026]. M-GRPO jointly trains main and subagent models with outcome, format, and expert LLM scores [Hong et al., 2025]. Our method jointly trains the hierarchy with OPD from a task-trained Teacher for Searcher supervision and outcome-based RL for the Leader and Answerer.  

> Multi-agent systems.

Agent harnesses such as Claude Code, Codex, DeepSeek Harness, and Kimi Code support task delegation to subagents and integration of their results [Anthropic, 2026, OpenAI, 2026, DeepSeek, 2026, Moonshot AI, 2026]. Anthropic’s research system similarly coordinates parallel searchers [Anthropic, 2025]. These systems motivate our Leader–Subagent structure, and we study how to train the cooperating roles end to end.  

> On-policy distillation.

GKD and MiniLLM train on student-generated sequences using teacher probabilities [Agarwal et al., 2024, Gu et al., 2024]. Recent work shows how dense OPD feedback supports reasoning and preserves learned behavior [Lu and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025]. SOD adjusts distillation strength at each tool-use step based on teacher–student divergence [Zhong et al., 2026], while MAD-OPD uses multi-agent debate to improve teacher supervision [Wang et al., 2026]. OPID derives hindsight skills from completed on-policy trajectories and uses them for self-distillation alongside outcome optimization [Yang et al., 2026]. SD-Search adds step-level supervision at query positions through hindsight self-distillation, where a single model acts as both student and outcome-conditioned teacher [Ma et al., 2026]. OPDSearch+ distills a frozen off-the-shelf teacher on live search trajectories and then refines the student with RL [Ye et al., 2026]. Both train a single search agent without task delegation. In contrast, we use a frozen, task-trained Searcher–Answerer policy to guide Searcher tokens on delegated subtask histories, while the Leader learns from team success and the Answerer from candidate correctness. Applying a task-trained teacher to delegated subtasks within a jointly trained hierarchy distinguishes our approach from prior work on OPD for search. 

3 MultiAgent-OPD 

MultiAgent-OPD trains the Leader–Searcher–Answerer team in two stages and gives each role a learning signal that matches the relation of the role to the final outcome (Section 1, Figure 1). Stage I trains the simpler Searcher–Answerer workflow, in which the report goes straight to the Answerer, and freezes the trained policy as the Teacher (Section 3.2). Stage II trains the team with role-specific advantages, in which the Searchers learn from the Teacher through on-policy distillation and the Leader and the Answerer learn from team rewards (Section 3.3).

4 Experiments 

We evaluate MultiAgent-OPD on open-domain question answering, training on NQ and HotpotQA and testing on 5,000 fixed questions from the seven benchmarks in Table 1 [Kwiatkowski et al., 2019, Joshi et al., 2017, Mallen et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2018, Ho et al., 2020, Trivedi et al., 2022, Press et al., 2023]. We compare MultiAgent-OPD with the cascaded and hierarchical baselines in the table and with five RL-based search agents, Search-R1 [Jin et al., 2025], DeSA [Wang et al., 2025], Tree-GRPO [Ji et al., 2026], MemSearcher [Yuan et al., 2026] and Dr. MAS [Feng et al., 2026]. We build all systems on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 [Qwen Team, 2025] and connect them to the same retriever, which returns three passages per query (Appendix B). The cascaded and hierarchical systems use inference limits of 5 and 12 environment steps, respectively (Appendix B.2). We run each system four times per question, with one Answerer candidate per request, and score each final answer by normalized exact match. We report avg@4, the mean score over the four runs, and pass@4, the fraction of questions answered correctly at least once. avg@4 estimates the single-attempt success rate and requires no answer-selection rule, while pass@4 is the coverage upper bound across the four attempts. The primary metric is macro avg@4, which weights the seven datasets equally (Appendix A). 

4.1 Main Results 

Table 1 compares correctness across datasets for the cascaded and hierarchical systems and the five RL-based search agents.  

> The base hierarchy outperforms the base cascaded workflow.

Both base systems directly use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 without task-specific training. H-MAS, the hierarchical Leader–Searcher–Answerer system, reaches 28.60% macro avg@4 versus C-MAS base’s 25.09%, a gain of 3.51 percentage points. Macro pass@4 rises from 34.02% to 39.32%. H-MAS has higher avg@4 on five of seven datasets, including all four multi-hop datasets, with the largest gains on Bamboogle and 2WikiMultiHopQA.  

> C-MAS + RL has a higher score than Prompted Critic.

C-MAS + RL, the Stage-I policy evaluated in its cascaded workflow, reaches 36.07% macro avg@4. Prompted Critic, which trains the hierarchy with an M-GRPO-like combination of team rewards and a prompted judge of Searcher reports [Hong et al., 2025], reaches 34.62%, 1.45 percentage points lower. Their macro pass@4 values are nearly tied (45.97% and 45.96%), so the observed difference is in average correctness across attempts. This comparison motivates evaluating local Searcher supervision within the hierarchy.

5 Conclusion 

MultiAgent-OPD jointly trains a hierarchical multi-agent team using a task-trained Teacher for local Searcher supervision, team success for the Leader, and candidate correctness for the Answerer. It achieves higher seven-dataset macro avg@4 and macro pass@4 than Prompted Critic, C-MAS + RL, and the included RL-based search agents, while using more searches and input tokens. A policy trained in a simpler workflow can thus guide subagents during joint training. 

Limitations 

Scope and uncertainty. We evaluate cascaded and hierarchical systems and five RL-based search agents built on Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 on a fixed English open-domain QA set. Four attempts per question and question-bootstrap intervals characterize variation within the evaluated checkpoints, not variation across independently trained runs. Macro averaging weights the seven datasets equally, and normalized exact match does not detect  

> 10 MultiAgent-OPD Technical Report · October 2026

semantic overlap or pretraining contamination. Systems run at their own workflow budgets. We do not compare them under a matched retrieval or token budget.  

> Mechanism.

OPD supplies teacher-conditioned local process supervision, not a causal estimate of a token’s contribution to the final answer. The teacher can make errors, full-question teacher training differs from delegated-subtask use, and shared role parameters couple updates across roles. Reverse-KL training can reduce exploration, and teacher and student require compatible token vocabularies. The diagnostic comparisons retain Leader and Answerer outcome objectives, so they do not separately evaluate those objectives.  

> Reproducibility.

The anonymous artifact provides the scored evaluation records, aggregation script, result file, and evaluation protocol needed to reproduce the reported summary statistics. Recorded token counts measure inference workload. 

Ethical Considerations 

We use public QA datasets and a text retriever. Retrieved material can be inaccurate or biased. Exact match measures answer correctness. Further checks are needed to assess whether evidence supports the answer, whether sources are credited, and whether the system is safe to use.

A.3 How We Count Resource Use 

Each workflow record contains prompt/response token totals, search actions, returned passages, Searcher reports, Leader subtasks, and a duration value. For each turn, the recorder applies the chat template to the assistant sample and cuts it to the input and output limits. It then adds the token counts across turns. This may count the same history more than once. Output counts include EOS tokens. These counts let us compare recorded workload across systems. They do not measure unique context, exact service usage, or total training cost. Searcher-action counts include nonempty queries recorded with a complete search tag. We separately count search actions that return passages. A returned passage may still be irrelevant or lack the needed evidence. The Searcher–Answerer workflow has no Leader, so the JSON value of zero for task assignments means this field does not apply. It is not a measurement of Leader behavior. We also keep separate report and task-assignment counts. Costs average the four attempts within each question, then questions within each dataset, then datasets equally. Relative changes are calculated from unrounded values. Pooled descriptive statistics are kept separately. Batch duration is not a per-question response time measurement.

This supports the interpretation that final-answer rewards did not sustain retrieval in this configuration; it does not isolate the contribution of each training choice.

B Systems and Training Details 

> B.1 Compared Systems

The compared systems vary architecture, task-specific training, and Searcher supervision. C-MAS base and H-MAS base use the base model without task-specific training. C-MAS + RL jointly trains the Searcher and the Answerer with outcome-based RL in an implementation adapted from Dr. MAS [Feng et al., 2026]; it evaluates the Stage-I policy in its original cascaded workflow. Prompted Critic trains the hierarchy with an M-GRPO-like combination of local and team rewards [Hong et al., 2025]. The judge prompt appears in Appendix C.4. Its Searchers receive 0 .3𝑅 EM + 0.5𝑅 judge + 0.2𝑏 𝑆 , where 𝑅 judge is a binary report score prompted from the shared checkpoint. Its Leader and Answerer use the same outcome objectives as in MultiAgent-OPD (Eq. (6)); the two trained hierarchies differ only in the Searcher learning signal. Published M-GRPO instead trains separate role models and aligns variable subagent trajectories. MultiAgent-OPD trains the same hierarchy with token-level Searcher OPD from the frozen Stage-I Teacher and outcome-based RL for the Leader and the Answerer. Neither trained hierarchy requires the Teacher or a judge at inference. H-MAS base is not trained and therefore receives no Leader reward. The three diagnostic variants in Table 3 retain the full configuration’s Leader and Answerer objectives while changing the Searcher path. The RL-based search agents use the same backbone and protocol as our systems. Search-R1 trains a single agent with outcome rewards to interleave reasoning and search calls. DeSA first trains search with a retrieval-recall reward and then trains answering with an outcome reward. Tree-GRPO samples trajectories as trees over agent steps and derives step-level advantages from outcome rewards. MemSearcher keeps a compact memory across turns and trains the agent with multi-context GRPO. Dr. MAS trains a hierarchical team with agent-wise advantage normalization. 

> B.2 Training and Inference Settings

Training uses 79,168 Natural Questions and 90,447 HotpotQA examples [Kwiatkowski et al., 2019, Yang et al., 2018]. Training uses batch size 32, five trajectories per question, five PPO minibatch updates, and learning rate 10 −6, with veRL and SGLang [Sheng et al., 2025, Zheng et al., 2024]. Stage I allows five environment steps; Stage II allows 12 steps and up to five Leader delegation rounds. All training configurations, including the three diagnostic variants, use five Answerer candidates per request. The full method and its ablations share the same reward and advantage normalization and other training settings. Answerer rewards are normalized within the parent workflow, as defined in Eq. (1). Evaluation uses one candidate per answering request for all systems. The four evaluation runs per question are independent workflows, each using this single-candidate answering rule. Evaluation uses vLLM in float16, temperature 0.6, top-𝑝 = 0.95, an 8,192-token input limit, and a 512-token output limit per call. Cascaded and hierarchical systems keep their respective 5- and 12-step limits. Following Dr. MAS [Feng et al., 2026], we use E5 [Wang et al., 2022] as the retriever. All systems use the same retriever, returning three passages per query. Saved trajectories provide search and token counts, completion statistics, and evidence-use examples. 

> B.3 Evaluation Provenance and Statistics

The main comparison uses a fixed 5,000-question manifest and four stochastic workflows per method. Appendix A gives the dataset composition, question-overlap check, answer extraction rule, and question-bootstrap procedure. Checkpoints are saved every 100 steps. Trained systems in the main comparison use checkpoints nearest in step to the validation peak; all four rows of Table 3 use step 500. Checkpoints and the OPD coefficient were fixed before their respective test evaluations. The anonymous artifact includes question–replica scores and a Python/NumPy script for recomputing macro metrics and paired intervals.
