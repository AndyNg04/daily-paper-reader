With the rapid advancement of wireless technologies, in-air handwriting recognition based on radio frequency (RF) signals emerges as a promising solution for human-computer interaction. However, existing methods often constrain writing gestures to predefined planes, impose strict requirements on writing order and direction, and limit recognition tasks to a small set of digits or letters. To overcome these limitations, we propose 𝑚 2𝑉 𝐿𝑀𝑠 , a modality mapping architecture, which serves as a bridge between the millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar sensing technologies and large vision-language models (VLMs). Based on the proposed architecture, a three-dimensional (3D) in-air handwriting word recognition system named mmPencil is developed. Specifically, we design a multi-stage spatial trajectory reconstruction algorithm that extracts frequency-domain features to identify handwriting regions and achieve high-precision reconstruction of 3D word trajectories. Furthermore, we introduce a novel spatial-to-visual mapping algorithm, which bridges the gap between spatial trajectory information captured by mmWave radar and vision-language representations, providing a foundation for cross-modality understanding in the large vision-language model. As a result, mmPencil tackles the limitations of current solutions that are heavily reliant on handwriting styles and environmental factors, expanding RF-based in-air handwriting recognition to more complex word-level scenarios. We collect and release a 3D mmWave handwriting dataset comprising 200 distinct words (ranging from 2 to 9 letters), contributions from 12 users, and 22 different writing scenarios, totaling 7,664 samples with an overall size of 31.66 GB. 1 Introduction 

With the rapid development of information technology, human-computer interaction (HCI) has become in-creasingly important in fields such as smart devices [25], industrial internet of things (IoT) [24], and assistive technologies [31]. Traditional interaction methods rely on input devices like touchscreens, mice, and keyboards. However, these methods perform poorly on devices with limited screen sizes (e.g., smartwatches) or without dedicated input interfaces, and they are not always user-friendly for specific groups such as the elderly and people with disabilities. As a result, innovative interaction technologies, such as in-air writing recognition[1], voice input [7, 17], and visual gesture recognition [27], have garnered growing attention. However, voice and visual-based methods can leak sensitive personal information, such as voice tone, conversation content, or facial features, posing privacy risks. In contrast, in-air writing recognition based on radio frequency (RF) signals offers the advantages of non-contact and privacy protection, which allows the user to write in the air across various scenarios. It overcomes many limitations of traditional approaches and has become an ideal choice for achieving natural human-computer interaction. Unlike common non-interactive sensing tasks such as vital signs monitoring [30, 33, 42, 60], material detection [5, 40, 41, 43], and human activity recognition [3, 37, 56], in-air writing recognition is a more fine-grained sensing task. By analyzing reflected signals from the target, it deciphers handwriting content and provides richer semantic information. In-air writing recognition has been extensively studied in the literature. Early approaches utilized acoustic devices [9, 10, 38, 58] to extract distinctive acoustic patterns related to writing trajectories, enabling the classification of letters or words. However, this line of methods is highly susceptible to environmental noise. Solutions based on RF signals commonly utilize Wi-Fi channel state information (CSI) [14, 28, 51, 61] to detect fine finger movements by leveraging detailed subcarrier information. However, Wi-Fi signals are highly affected by severe multipath effects and have limited sensing resolution. Similarly, RFID-based approaches [36] require a large number of tag arrays, which makes them difficult to deploy in real-world scenarios. In contrast, mmWave radar has recently gained widespread attention for its rich sensing capabilities, including range, angle, and velocity, along with its high resolution. Beyond in-air writing recognition, it has demonstrated outstanding performance in applications such as heartbeat monitoring [4, 65, 66], vibration detection [18, 57], and facial recognition [53, 55, 63]. To extract handwriting trajectory information, existing radar-based studies can be roughly divided into two categories, i.e., the model-driven method and the data-driven method. Model-driven methods construct physical models [20, 29] between the signals and the handwriting trajectory to perform handwriting tracking and recognition. This line of studies, while eliminating the need for pre-training on collected data, struggle to handle characters written in non-predefined orders and are heavily reliant on specific scenarios. On the contrary, data-driven methods transform RF signals into trajectories or spectrograms and build classifiers based on long

However, RF signals are highly susceptible to interference from factors such as writing position, direction, and individual writing habits. The scarcity and low quality of the RF sensing data, along with the diversity of handwriting content, greatly limit the development of RF-based in-air handwriting recognition. Recently, foundation models (FMs), represented by large language models (LLMs) and large vision-language models (VLMs), have demonstrated unique capabilities in processing sensing data from the physical world, offering new opportunities for in-air writing recognition. For instance, the Tent system [70] integrates IoT sensor signals with text embeddings to enable zero-shot human activity recognition, while Penetrative AI [54] connects LLMs with the physical world, empowering them to process and interpret sensory data. Similarly, the FM-Fi system [48] introduces an innovative cross-modal system to transfer knowledge from vision-FMs, which facilitates human activity recognition based on RF signals. However, the powerful capabilities emerging from FMs such as VLMs have not yet been applied to in-air writing recognition. Although there has been work on enhancing wireless sensing using LLMs or VLMs, achieving writing-style-independent in-air handwriting recognition via the mmWave radar and the large vision-language model is still a non-trivial issue, due to the following three major challenges. First, realistic in-air writing usually occurs in 3D space, making it difficult to accurately reconstruct writing trajectories from composite RF signals, even with a mmWave radar featuring a 4GHz bandwidth and an antenna array. Second, the reflected signals of handwriting movements depend on not only the word being written, but also factors such as writing position, direction, and order, resulting in significant variability even within samples of the same word. Third, the RF data differs significantly from the text and image data, which inherently possess abundant semantic information. This disparity restricts the efficacy of VLMs when directly handling RF signals. To address the aforementioned challenges, we develop a system named mmPencil , which is a pioneering effort in harnessing the powerful generalization capability of VLMs to achieve writing-style-independent recognition of in-air handwriting words. Specifically, a modality mapping architecture is proposed to bridge the mmWave data and VLMs, which consists of four components: data collection, trajectory reconstruction, data mapping, and instruction fine-tuning. First, we collect and release a 3D mmWave in-air handwriting word dataset. Afterwards, an environment-robust multi-stage spatial trajectory reconstruction algorithm is designed, which effectively mitigates the interference caused by variations in handwriting positions. In particular, by exploring the range-azimuth and range-elevation profiles refined through spectral refinement, we accurately capture the spatial trajectory of handwriting movements at each moment. Outlier removal is performed on the obtained trajectory sequence, followed by smoothing using a 3D Kalman filter. Next, we design a spatial-to-visual mapping algorithm to achieve handwriting recognition independent of writing styles, such as direction and order. Through direction-corrected Principal Component Analysis (PCA), we map the 3D trajectories into 2D images while preserving the shape features of the handwriting words. This mapping algorithm bridges the gap between wireless signals and visual images, enabling VLMs to intuitively interpret and process RF signals. Finally, we put forward an instruction fine-tuning approach to improve handwriting word recognition. A clear prompt is designed to guide the model in extracting word from image, while strictly limiting the output format. To sum up, the main contributions of this study are as follows: 

• We propose a modality mapping architecture, 𝑚 2𝑉 𝐿𝑀𝑠 , which serves as a bridge between mmWave data and VLMs, facilitating the transfer of the generalization capability of VLMs to RF sensing applications. To foster further exploration in this emerging field, we collect and release a 3D mmWave in-air handwriting dataset 1 containing 7,664 samples of 200 different words, 12 users, and 22 writing scenarios. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to apply VLMs for RF-based handwriting recognition.

In general, existing studies can be categorized into two groups, the first being model-driven methods that aim to establish a mapping between signal variations and handwriting actions. For example, the FingerDraw system [51] employs a CSI-quotient model to eliminate noise using the ratio of CSI signals between two antennas. This enables precise tracking of finger movements at sub-wavelength scales and, through signal segmentation and trajectory reconstruction algorithms, achieves two-dimensional (2D) trajectory reconstruction of finger movements. However, the limited bandwidth of Wi-Fi signals constrains FingerDraw’ s performance in fine-grained finger-tracking tasks. method using a single mmWave radar. By leveraging advanced signal processing techniques, it achieves millimeter-level precision in handwriting trajectory reconstruction. The HandGest system [61] utilizes Dynamic Phase Vector (DPV) and Motion Rotation Variable (MRV) as gesture features, applying a hierarchical perception system for phased feature extraction and classification. This robust system enables finger tracking and air handwriting recognition without requiring large-scale data collection. The GWrite system [28] leverages CSI temporal features and time-reversal techniques, combining feature extraction and geometric analysis to achieve through-wall gesture recognition. However, its recognition capability for complex curved handwriting trajectories remains limited. The AirWrite system [20] introduces a handwriting recognition algorithm based on vector matrix generation and string matching. Without pre-training, it relies solely on simple string similarity calculations to identify handwriting trajectories. Nevertheless, this approach struggles with unseen letter writing-order, limiting its application in practical scenarios. Although these systems have achieved commendable results, they confine handwriting actions to 2D planes, whereas handwriting often occurs in 3D space. The CentiTrack-3D system [14] addresses this limitation by integrating Angle of Arrival (AoA), Time of Flight (ToF), and variations in reflection path length to construct a high-precision 3D spatial gesture tracking model. However, its performance remains sensitive to the accuracy of initial position estimation, posing challenges for broader applications. The other group is data-driven methods, which rely on large volumes of labeled data to train deep neural networks, enabling them to learn and extract unique patterns from hand movements. For example, the RF-finger system [36] utilizes gesture reflection features captured in three frames (corresponding to the initial, middle, and final phases of a gesture) and trains a CNN model to achieve fine-grained gesture recognition with high precision. Arsalan et al. [1] propose an in-air writing recognition system based on multiple mmWave radar devices, which combines trajectory time series and 2D images, leveraging LSTM-CTC and DCNN models for character classification with notable performance. Focusing on two-finger character input, the IndexPen system [45] combines a hybrid architecture of CNN and LSTM to fully exploit the range-doppler and range-azimuth-elevation features of the mmWave radar and successfully realizes the effective extraction of spatial and temporal information. Similarly, the MMHTSR system [6] introduces an air-handwriting trajectory detection and reconstruction system, utilizing advanced signal processing techniques and Bidirectional-LSTM to achieve high-accuracy handwriting trajectory recognition and gesture classification. However, these CNN- and LSTM-based methods face limitations in generalizing to unseen complex writing movements and adapting to user-varying writing style preferences, significantly hindering their applicability in real-world scenarios. In contrast, mmPencil combines the advantages of model-driven and data-driven methods. It employs a multi-stage spatial trajectory reconstruction algorithm that effectively eliminates the influence of factors such as writing position, direction, and speed. Additionally, by leveraging a spatial-to-visual mapping algorithm, it capitalizes on the ability of VLMs to perceive the physical world. Even in the presence of significant varying of handwriting styles and environmental factors, mmPencil maintains a high sensing performance. 

3 Preliminaries 

In this section, we will introduce the preliminaries that form the basis for designing the proposed architecture and algorithms. Specifically, we first delve into the principles of multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) antenna arrays based on time-division multiplexing (TDM). Subsequently, we explore the key techniques for range estimation and multi-dimensional angle estimation. 

3.1 Antenna Array 

Unlike traditional pulse radars, mmWave radars emit signals with a frequency that varies linearly over time, thereby referred to as Frequency-Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radars. By analyzing the ToF and Doppler shift of the received signals, FMCW radars can simultaneously measure the range and velocity of a target.

However, relying solely on range and velocity information is insufficient to distinguish multiple targets. To address this limitation, FMCW radars employ MIMO antenna arrays, which enable spatial separation of targets through angle estimation. The core concept of MIMO antenna arrays lies in combining the signal paths of multiple transmit (Tx) and receive (Rx) antennas to form an equivalent virtual antenna array. The number of antennas in this virtual array is equal to the product of the physical Tx and Rx antennas. This design significantly enhances the angle resolution and target detection capability of radars without a substantial increase hardware costs. As shown in Fig. 1, the transmit antennas emit signals in chronological order in TDM mode. The receive antennas capture the echo signals reflected from the targets. By jointly processing the received signals, target signals can be effectively separated across both temporal and spatial dimensions.

4 Overview of the 𝑚 2𝑉 𝐿𝑀𝑠 Architecture 

Due to the abundance of large-scale image-text data, VLMs possess outstanding generalization capabilities in sensing and understanding the physical world. To unlock this great potential of VLMs in the domain of RF sensing, we propose a modality mapping architecture that connects mmWave data and VLMs, as shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, the proposed 𝑚 2𝑉 𝐿𝑀𝑠 architecture consists of four modules: data collection, trajectory reconstruction, data mapping, and instruction fine-tuning. In particular, the trajectory reconstruction module effectively eliminates the influence of environmental factors and writing position, while the data mapping module mitigates the impact of writing order and direction. We will provide detailed descriptions of these two modules in §5 and §6, respectively. 

Data Collection. 𝑚 2𝑉 𝐿𝑀𝑠 is built on a commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) 60 GHz mmWave radar. The radar is equipped with a MIMO antenna array comprising 3 transmitting antennas and 4 receiving antennas, offering a 120 ◦ azimuth field of view and a 60 ◦ elevation field of view. We place the radar horizontally on a desktop or similar platform to capture the writing movements. 

Trajectory Reconstruction. We design a set of trajectory reconstruction methods that effectively reduces the impact of variations in writing position on air handwriting recognition. First, we calculate the vector mean of time-domain data to suppress environmental clutter caused by multi-path effects. Next, we propose a multi-stage spatial trajectory reconstruction algorithm. Using range-azimuth and range-elevation profiles after spectrum refinement, we dynamically extract the center coordinates of target clusters, accurately capturing the spatial (i.e.,

Data Mapping. We design a spatial-to-visual mapping algorithm. Through direction-corrected PCA, we map 3D trajectories to 2D images while preserving the shape characteristics of handwriting words. Instruction Fine-Tuning. We design an instructional prompt to clearly guide the VLM in extracting text from images while strictly constraining the output format. The content of the prompt is as follows: "<image> Here is a picture containing a handwriting word. Please identify the word in the image. Ensure that your output is a correctly spelled English word. Note: Only provide the word itself, without any additional content or explanation." Using clear instructions, we define the VLM’s task objective as accurately recognizing handwriting words from images. Additionally, we incorporate the principles of constraint-based prompts [62], strictly limiting the output to "a single correctly spelled English word" and excluding unnecessary content or explanations. This design combines task instructions with output constraints, effectively helping the VLM adapt to handwriting-based text recognition tasks and improving the simplicity and accuracy of the output.

Since direct leakage (within 20 cm) and environmental noise (beyond 100 cm) tend to remain constant throughout the handwriting process, subtracting the mean vector can effectively eliminate these non-motion components. 

5.2 Multi-Stage Spatial Trajectory Reconstruction 

As we know, a FMCW radar is capable of detecting the relative range, azimuth and elevation angles of the target. Furthermore, based on the multi-stage spatial trajectory reconstruction algorithm, we can not only effectively suppress environmental interference, but also accurately estimate the finger position at each moment with super resolution. As a result, handwriting word recognition can be achieved regardless of the writing position.

Although spectrum refinement and trajectory point localization techniques are applied to enhance signal quality during data processing, the generated trajectory sequences inevitably exhibit fluctuations and high-frequency noise. These fluctuations result not only from the complexity of in-air writing but also from environmental disturbances and inherent uncertainties in signal processing. To optimize trajectory sequences, we introduce a 3D Kalman filter. Traditional filtering techniques, such as the Savitzky-Golay filter [32], rely on fixed window sizes to smooth data. However, these methods often cause detail loss when processing signals of varying lengths. This issue becomes particularly evident in character shape restoration. Additionally, windowing strategies distort periodic strokes, especially in letters like "o", "e", and "a", leading to compression or deformation during smoothing. To address these challenges, we adopt the dynamic prediction and update capabilities of the Kalman filter [47], and the smoothed trajectories of the example words are shown in Fig. 6(c) and 6(d). In our implementation of Kalman filtering, we typically set the observation covariance to 1 and the transition covariance to 1 × 10 −3. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the transition covariance significantly affects the quality of trajectory reconstruction. In particular, a smaller transition covariance tends to over-smooth the trajectory, thereby removing fine-grained details of the word writing process. In contrast, a larger transition covariance retains more details but also introduces considerable noise and clutter. This 3D Kalman filtering approach fully leverages the recursive computation of the Kalman filter, effectively smoothing the three-dimensional trajectory data while preserving the original shape of the characters and avoiding distortion issues common in traditional methods.

6 Data Mapping from Spatial Trajectory to Word Image 

In-air writing trajectories in space tend to be more arbitrary and irregular than those written on a plane. These trajectories can be influenced by hand movements, postures, or spatial orientations. Directly projecting them onto fixed coordinate axes may lead to significant distortion of the word’s shape, posing challenges in maintaining the original structure. To address this issue, we employ PCA [49] to extract the principal directions of the spatial trajectory and transform it into an image, which helps retain the overall word shape effectively. Specifically, we first convert the writing trajectories from spherical coordinates to Cartesian coordinates, where each trajectory point x𝑖 = [𝑥 𝑖 , 𝑦 𝑖 , 𝑧 𝑖 ]⊤ is represented as 𝑥 = 𝑟 · sin (𝜃 ) · cos (𝜙 ), 𝑦 = 𝑟 · cos (𝜃 ) · cos (𝜙 ), 𝑧 = 𝑟 · sin (𝜙 ).Given the centralized handwriting trajectory sequence X = [x1, x2, . . . , x𝑛 ]⊤ ∈ R𝑛 ×3, we compute the covariance matrix C and perform eigenvalue decomposition as: 

CV = VΛ , Λ = diag (𝜆 1, 𝜆 2, 𝜆 3), 𝜆 1 ≥ 𝜆 2 ≥ 𝜆 3, (13) where Λ is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues, and V = [v1, v2, v3] is the matrix of eigenvectors. In normal handwriting, the principal directions of the trajectory are usually related to writing habits, with the primary direction representing horizontal extension (left-to-right writing) and the secondary direction reflecting vertical variations. Thus, we select the eigenvectors v1 and v2 corresponding to the two largest eigenvalues 𝜆 1

and 𝜆 2 to construct a 2D projection basis: 

P = [v1, v2] ∈ R3×2. (14) These two principal writing directions precisely capture the characteristics of writing. Specifically, the largest eigenvalue corresponds to the primary horizontal writing trend, while the second largest eigenvalue corresponds to the secondary vertical variation trend. The trajectory points projected from space to plane can then be expressed as: 

X2𝐷 = XP , X2𝐷 ∈ R𝑛 ×2, (15) where 𝑛 represents the total number of trajectory points, and X2𝐷 represents the generated word image. However, PCA determines the principal directions based on the statistical variance of spatial data, neglecting the sequential or temporal nature of handwriting trajectories. This limitation may result in dimensionality reduction results that deviate from typical writing habits, such as left-to-right progression, potentially resulting in horizontally or vertically mirrored trajectories. These distortions can impede the recognition of handwriting trajectories. In practical handwriting scenarios, words are typically written starting from the top-left corner and progressing towards the bottom-right corner. Based on this observation, we propose a post-processing adjustment method to correct the orientation of PCA results, ensuring that the writing direction aligns with common conventions and eliminating potential mirroring distortions. This adjustment process involves analyzing the starting and ending points of the PCA-reduced trajectory to determine the overall writing direction. Specifically, we compute the horizontal and vertical components of the trajectory’s direction vector. If the horizontal component indicates a right-to-left progression, the trajectory is flipped horizontally to achieve left-to-right alignment. Similarly, if the vertical component indicates an upward trend, the trajectory is flipped vertically to conform to the top-to-bottom writing habit. Examples of gradient color visualization images are shown in Fig. 8. The direction-corrected PCA-based data mapping algorithm reconstructs the overall word shape and eliminates distortions caused by irregular spatial writing directions, such as slanted writing. It ensures that the generated trajectory follows logical writing habits, e.g., left-to-right progression, and aligns with visual conventions. By rectifying the trajectory and converting it into an image, we mitigate the impact of writing order on word recognition. This approach circumvents the obstacles that time-series-based techniques like LSTM frequently encounter.

To validate the performance of 𝑚 2𝑉 𝐿𝑀𝑠 , we develop a prototype in-air handwriting recognition system named 

mmPencil . Specifically, a commercial mmWave radar is used to collect sensing data samples of 3D mmWave handwriting words, and a series of experiments are conducted to evaluate the accuracy and robustness of mmPencil

7.1 Experiment Setup 

7.1.1 Hardware Implementation. To implement mmPencil , we use the IWR6843ISK mmWave radar, which can transmit FM continuous wave signals in the frequency range of 60 to 64 GHz, and the DCA1000EVM for data collection. It is equipped with 3 transmitting antennas (one of which is arranged at different heights) and 4 receiving antennas, with a peak gain of 7 dBi, and can provide a horizontal FoV of 120 ◦ and a elevation FoV of 60 ◦. We set the frequency slope to 66.011 MHz/ 𝜇 s, and set the sampling rate to 2000 ksps. At the same time, each frame consists of one chirp with 108 ADC samples per chirp, resulting in a range resolution of 4.2 cm. In addition, the mmWave data collection module is deployed on a mini-PC running Windows 10, with a CPU frequency of 2.8 GHz. We use a GPU server equipped with 8 A100 GPUs (each with 40 GB of memory) for fine-tuning the VLMs.

In other words, users retain the freedom to adhere to their individual writing styles and habits without any limitations. 8.2 Zero-Shot Recognition of Unseen Words 

Given the vast number of words, it is impractical to collect and annotate RF sensing data for every possible word or writing style. Hence, achieving zero-shot recognition of handwriting words becomes essential. Fortunately, the use of VLMs allows for the inference of unseen words by deeply understanding handwriting trajectories and semantic information. Specifically, VLMs excel at aligning visual information with the semantic space of language models through cross-modal learning. As a result, the mmPencil system is capable of predicting the structure and meaning of unseen words via semantic associations, facilitating zero-shot recognition. To evaluate the zero-shot recognition potential of mmPencil , we conduct an exploratory experiment. We fine-tune the VLMs using 180 word categories from the 200-Word 4-User Dataset and evaluate the models with the remaining 20 unseen words. Specifically, we perform instruction fine-tuning on both the Qwen2-VL and 

LLaVA-NeXT models. Moreover, we observe significant performance variations across different unseen words. Specifically, words like "that", "do", "seems", and "an" are easily recognized with an accuracy exceeding 70%, while words like "god", "almost", "sir", and "old" pose challenges for recognition. 8.3 Real-Time Processing Feasibility 

To evaluate the real-time processing feasibility of the mmPencil system in practical applications, we conducted the analysis based on the 50-Word 12-User Dataset under cross-user evaluation settings, focusing on the trade-offs between computational overhead, inference time, and recognition accuracy, to discuss the practicality of the methods. The overall system latency is primarily attributed to two stages, including the stage of trajectory reconstruction from mmWave signals and the stage of word inference by the classification model on the generated images. The experimental results are presented in Table 4. In the trajectory reconstruction stage, under the standard spectral refinement (SR) strategy, the average reconstruction time per letter is about 468.30 ms. To reduce the latency, we have tested a moderate SR strategy, where the degree of spectral refinement is appropriately reduced, decreasing the reconstruction time to 155.65 ms, at the cost of a slight drop in accuracy. During the inference stage, different models exhibit significantly different inference speeds. Specifically, ResNet-101 , as a lightweight classifier, achieves a fast inference time of only 10.05 ms per sample, with an accuracy of 84.13%. In contrast, GOT 

takes a longer inference time (697.69 ms per sample) due to the absence of vllm [19] acceleration, even though it achieves a better recognition performance (92.50%). It is worth noting that GOT has a relatively small parameter size (500M), and once vllm-based acceleration is implemented, its inference time is expected to decrease to a level comparable to ResNet-101 . For the Qwen series models, benefiting from vllm acceleration, Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen2-VL-7B take inference times of 32.44 ms and 185.56 ms per sample, respectively, while maintaining relatively high accuracies of 80.50% and 89.50%. To sum up, the mmPencil system, configured with Qwen2.5-VL-3B 

and the moderate SR strategy, achieves a favorable balance between recognition accuracy and computational latency. It is important to emphasize that mmPencil performs recognition at the word level, with trajectory reconstruc-tion and word typically triggered only after the entire word is written. Based on our experience in data collection, the natural writing time per letter usually ranges between 2 and 3 seconds. Consequently, despite the system’s end-to-end latency may exceed 200 ms, it is generally tolerable when compared to the user’s natural writing rhythm, ensuring a smooth user experience. We must acknowledge that as we prioritize maximal recognition accuracy, the proposed solution exhibits inherent limitations in real-time processing latency. 8.4 In-Air Handwriting Sentence Recognition 

In this study, we have realized word-level handwriting recognition using mmWave radar technology and VLMs. As a result, a logical next step for improvement is advancing towards sentence-level recognition of in-air handwritings. It holds substantial promise for enhancing human-machine interaction, facilitating innovative applications in areas such as education, healthcare, and smart devices. Apparently, sentence-level recognition presents greater complexity and challenges than word-level recognition, due to the intricate semantic structures and contextual information embedded within sentences. Specifically, the challenge mainly lies in extracting meaningful sentence-level features from complex trajectories captured by the mmWave radar. It necessitates efficient segmentation techniques and sophisticated model architectures capable of capturing both local word-level features and global semantic information. Vital to this process are VLMs, which excel at aligning cross-modal data, thereby mapping the captured handwriting trajectories to semantic spaces at the sentence level. Additionally, the acquisition and exploitation of large-scale and diverse RF sensing data through techniques such as data augmentation could play an important role in further enhancing the recognition performance. 

9 Conclusion 

In this paper, to achieve writing-style-independent 3D handwriting word recognition, we proposed a system named 

mmPencil based on mmWave radar and VLMs. Specifically, we designed a multi-stage trajectory reconstruction algorithm that fully leverages the rich spatial information of mmWave signals, enabling the extraction of 3D trajectories that are independent of writing position and direction. Additionally, we proposed a spatial-to-visual mapping algorithm to convert the spatial trajectory into 2D visual image that can be processed by VLMs. We collected a mmWave 3D handwriting word dataset and fine-tuned two leading VLMs. Extensive experiments demonstrated that mmPencil can achieve accurate word-level handwriting recognition and exhibit high robustness to a wide range of variations, including different users, varying word lengths, diverse writing styles (ranges, angles, speeds, sizes, directions), and environmental conditions and user actions. Finally, we evaluated the real-time processing feasibility of mmPencil and explored its potential in zero-shot recognition of digits, symbols, unseen words and sentence-level recognition.
