# 1 Introduction 

Multimodal Foundation Models have expanded from image and text understanding to video and audio [1, 65]. Their evaluation spans video understanding and increasingly examines people in egocentric and social settings [7, 15, 27, 37, 39]. Here, recognizing actions is not enough: models must interpret social cues, infer psychological states and needs, and make interaction judgments appropriate to the context [40, 50]. Inferring latent states from observable cues is a central problem in social cognition [49]. We ask: Can multimodal foundation models understand human states and needs from observable cues and make appropriate interaction judgments? 

Existing benchmarks and embodied systems address aspects of this question, from perception and social reasoning [34, 40] to personalization and embodied decision making [18, 32, 67, 72]. Broader benchmarks [45, 50] still differ in capability definitions and assessment criteria, making failures difficult to compare. Aggregate accuracy leaves two diagnostic questions unanswered: where do models struggle between social observation and response selection, and when do they select the right option without adequate cue support?

Challenge 2: Psychological constructs and observable cues (Section 3.3). Mental states and support needs are latent. Questions must anchor interpretations in observable visual and conversational cues without unsupported attribution. 

Challenge 3: Cue support beyond option matching (Section 3.5). Assessment must check cue support and logical consistency of explicit rationales, even when the selected option is correct. To address these challenges, we introduce PsycheMME , a benchmark informed by psychology for multimodal evaluation of human understanding and interaction. Drawing on Social Information Processing [13] and the Perception-Action Cycle [21], we define eight diagnostic dimensions spanning perceptual grounding, psycho-logical understanding, needs, interaction decisions, memory, and risk (Challenge 1). Independent answering with proposed references withheld and expert review ground psychological QA in observed cues (Challenge 2). Reasoning-Verified assesses answer correctness, rationale coverage, Evidence Support, and Logical Integrity, separating response success from justification quality (Challenge 3). Our main contributions are as follows: 

• A diagnostic benchmark informed by psychology. PsycheMME contains 1,000 questions from 630 episodes across eight capabilities: understanding (G, W, N), response selection (T, S, A), and contextual regulation (M, R). 

• Independent curation and rationale scoring. Agent roles isolate independent answering from refer-ence proposals, followed by unanimous expert review. Reasoning-Verified assesses explicit rationales for cue support and logical coherence alongside answer correctness. 

• Diagnostic findings across 17 MLLMs. Weak or contradicted support labels occur in 49.74% of correct Timing answers, while open Need full credit rates differ by 40 percentage points between Gemini 3.6 Flash and Claude Opus 5. Voting, input, history, and SFT/GRPO analyses examine answer selection and adaptation.

2.1 Multimodal Foundation Models 

Multimodal Foundation Models extend learning from images and text [1] to video, audio, and reasoning over long contexts [62, 65]. Magma further connects multimodal understanding to action [66]. Beyond scene perception, PersonaVLM explores personalized memory, MindPower connects reasoning about human states to embodied actions, and FAMER addresses implicit user desires through communication and memory [42, 63, 72]. These systems motivate evaluation of how social observations, personal history, and inferred needs inform response choices. 

2.2 Multimodal Benchmarks of Human Understanding and Interaction 

Multimodal benchmarks assess understanding and interaction. PCA-Bench evaluates how perception and cognition inform action, localizing errors within this chain [10]; Human Behavior Atlas integrates psycho-logical and social tasks [45]; HumanSense covers perception, contextual understanding, and empathetic re-sponses [50]. Social Genome evaluates reasoning traces grounded in multimodal social cues [40]. Other benchmarks assess video understanding [12, 39, 48], embodied decision making [68], egocentric memory and assistance [15, 64, 67], human perception [7, 37], and social interaction and reasoning [23, 29, 30, 34, 35, 43]. EgoPro-Bench and LiveProBench examine interaction timing [19, 54], while related efforts assess social ap-propriateness [9], implicit norms [73], and embodied safety [69]. PsycheMME brings these judgments into a shared setting for video QA, distinguishing response timing, content, and action while assessing the support for explicit explanations. 

2.3 Evaluation Informed by Psychology 

Models of affect, Theory of Mind (ToM), and social cognition in direct interaction inform the assessment of human states [49, 56, 60]. Social Information Processing (SIP) links cue encoding and interpretation to goal clarification and response selection [13]. Research on empathy, interpersonal emotion regulation, and matching support to circumstances informs response assessment [14, 16, 71]. FANToM, EmoBench, and ToMBench adapt psychological constructs for model evaluation [11, 31, 58]. These perspectives inform Psy-cheMME’s task definitions; its rubric for cue support and logic links answer credit to contextually supported explanations. 3.2 Capability Taxonomy Informed by Psychology 

Social Information Processing [13] and the Perception-Action Cycle [21] motivate three diagnostic domains (Fig. 2): human understanding (G, W, N), interaction decisions (T, S, A), and context and constraints (M, R). Detailed definitions and requirements for observed cues appear in Appendix B. 

Perceptually Grounded Mentalizing. Social Grounding (G) associates speech, gaze, gestures, and actions with participants and interaction relations. Psychological Understanding (W) uses multimodal cues and context to infer psychological states, possible causes, and changes. Need Inference (N) identifies desired support, distinguishing explicit requests, inferred needs, and willingness to receive help [4, 14]. 

Situated Empathic Responding. Interaction Timing and Attunement (T) judges whether and when to re-spond, wait, listen, or refrain [59]. Empathic Verbal Responding (S) selects response content, strategy, and tone [14, 16, 71]. Embodied Social Action (A) selects physical or nonverbal behavior appropriate to the person’s state, need, and context. 4Contextual and Normative Modulation. Interaction and Profile Memory (M) applies prior exchanges and individual preferences to current judgments [42]. Risk, Boundary, and Autonomy Regulation (R) assesses potential harm, drawing on embodied safety evaluation [69], and additionally considers consent, boundaries, and autonomy in proposed interventions [5, 57].

Video Collection. We collect videos of social interactions from six public datasets (Ego4D [27], EgoCom [44], MERCI+ (the extended MERCI release [2]), CASTLE [55], EasyCom [17], and IndEgo [8]) and public vlogs, interviews, and recordings of daily life. Human screening assisted by VLMs retains clips with observable social cues and adequate visual quality. 

Multi-Agent QA Generation. The question construction framework in Fig. 3 combines question generation, independent answering, verification, and refinement. Separating question proposal from independent answer-ing reduces the risk of accepting a proposal based on its own justification. The Question Agent proposes a question, candidate answer, options where applicable, and supporting cues. The Answer Agent examines the video again using only the question, options, and public context, without access to the proposed answer, cues, or private rationale. It independently answers or flags ambiguity and insufficient support. The Critic Agent checks both paths for answerability, cue support, and logical coherence. Proposal defects return to the Question Agent; reasoning defects trigger another examination. Psychology Knowledge guides the agents’ construct alignment and inference boundaries; items failing after two refinement rounds are discarded. A

Shared State with access control based on agent roles enforces information isolation and tracks verification feedback, refinement history, and taxonomy coverage to guide generation toward underrepresented capabili-ties (Appendix C.2). 

Quality Review. For benchmark construction, five researchers with psychology backgrounds independently review question validity, answer correctness, and psychological consistency. Only unanimously approved items enter the frozen benchmark. Data for fine tuning are separately filtered and kept disjoint from benchmark items.

3.5 Evaluation Protocol 

We report Raw Answer and Reasoning-Verified scores. Choice answers match the reference option or complete option set exactly; a fixed semantic judge evaluates open answers. Reasoning-Verified additionally considers rationale coverage, support from reference cues, and logical integrity: 

si = aicieiℓi/λ 2, (2) where ai ∈ [0 , 1] denotes answer correctness, ci ∈ { 0, 1} indicates an informative rationale, and ei, ℓ i ∈ [0 , λ ]

measure support from reference cues and logical integrity, respectively. With λ = 1 .2, full Verified credit requires a correct answer, an informative rationale, strong cue support, and fully coherent logic ( ei = ℓi = 1 .2). Normal support and acceptable logic ( ei = ℓi = 1 .0) receive 1/1.44 ≈ 69 .44% credit. The rubric grades justification quality beyond answer correctness. Both metrics are macro averages across eight capabilities (Appendices D.4 and D.5). 

3.6 SFT and GRPO on PsycheMME 

We apply SFT followed by GRPO to Qwen3.5-4B using LoRA [28] on the language backbone, with the visual encoder and multimodal aligner frozen. SFT trains for one epoch on 3,657 video QA examples with 32 sampled frames, ASR, and available interaction history. Targets contain the answer and one annotated observation; other rationale fields are null. Training uses AdamW [38]. GRPO [61] starts from SFT and samples eight responses per question from 2,842 single-choice examples. Its binary reward requires a correct answer that satisfies the specified response requirements: 

r(o) = 1[correct( o)] · 1[valid( o)] , (3) We use clipped GRPO with advantages normalized within each group and KL regularization against the fixed SFT reference, excluding groups with zero variance following the filtering criterion used in DAPO [70]. Training performs 32 updates over 256 distinct questions. Reasoning verification is used only for evaluation; details appear in Appendix E. 

# 4 Experiments 

64.1 Experimental Setup 

Models and Protocol. We evaluate 17 frozen MLLMs: 8 proprietary and 9 open-weight models (Table 2), on the same 1,000 questions without adaptation to the benchmark. A common input policy uses each model’s supported media interface: native video and native audio take priority, with sampled frames (1 FPS, at most 64) and ASR as the respective fallbacks. Memory questions include relevant history. Input details and separately evaluated SFT/GRPO checkpoints appear in Appendices D.1 and E. 

Metrics and Scoring. Models produce an answer and structured rationales. Raw Answer measures cor-rectness; Reasoning-Verified additionally considers rationale coverage, cue support, and logical integrity. Both use macro averaging across eight capabilities. Closed answers use deterministic matching; Qwen3.8-27B judges open answers and rationales. Scoring and paired bootstrap procedures over episodes appear in Appendix D.5.

4.3 Ablations and Validity Analysis 

Multimodal Cues. Full video and ASR improve Raw scores over Question-only by 12.50/12.43 points for Qwen3.5-9B/MiniCPM-V4.5 (Table 3). Visual-only retains most Full accuracy; adding ASR gives another 3.10/3.98 points. Removing video (ASR-only) reduces Grounding by 30.65/45.16 Raw points. Using a single midpoint image reduces Qwen’s Grounding by 12.63 points and Timing by 8.33 points (Appendix I). 

Historical Context. On 40 Memory questions, matched history raises Qwen3.5-9B Raw/Verified from 42.50/25.28 to 82.50/74.55, while mismatched history averages 45.00/23.87 (Table 4). The gains depend on relevant history; results for each seed appear in Appendix J. 

Task Adaptation. Qwen3.5-4B improves from 47.68/37.71 Raw/Verified macro scores to 72.58/52.13 after SFT and 73.19/52.62 after SFT+GRPO (Table 2). Gains vary by capability: SFT raises Grounding and Risk Raw scores by 27.96/27.40 points, but Timing falls from 40.28 to 31.94 and recovers only to 34.72 after GRPO (Table 13). GRPO adds 0.61 Raw and 0.49 Verified points, leaving a gap of 20.57 points between Raw and Verified. These results leave temporal grounding and rationale quality unresolved. 

Human Baseline. Five annotators independently answer 300 randomly sampled questions (1,500 responses), achieving 85.27% Raw micro and 84.82% macro accuracy (Table 5). Timing accuracy is 79.43% on this sample (Appendix K). 

Human-Judge Agreement. Five psychology experts independently review 300 randomly sampled Judge cases: 1,352 of 1,500 assessments endorse the presented decisions (90.13%; Appendix L). Weight sensitivity analysis supports the Timing retention finding (Appendix G.4). 

# 5 Conclusion 

PsycheMME unifies eight capabilities informed by psychology in a benchmark of 1,000 questions grounded in video. Its evaluation protocol assesses answers alongside support from reference cues and the logical integrity of explicit rationales. Across 17 models, Timing remains difficult, similar Need scores accompany differences in answer completeness, and voting can override correct minority answers. Input and history controls re-veal contextual dependence. These findings describe behavior by capability and justification quality beyond aggregate accuracy. The resulting diagnostics support comparisons of human understanding and interac-tion judgments while exposing how answer completeness and rationale support shape reported performance. The benchmark can guide future improvements in answer completeness, temporal grounding, and response selection informed by context. 9Limitations 

PsycheMME evaluates human-centered reasoning in recorded social interactions; real-time interactive settings remain unevaluated. While the benchmark covers diverse scenarios, its evaluation remains bounded by the represented social and cultural contexts. Future work could expand contextual and cultural diversity, explore streaming and interactive evaluation, and incorporate more fine-grained human assessments of explanation quality. 10 References

# B Psychological Foundations and Capability Definitions 

This section expands the eight capabilities in Sec. 3.2, specifying their theoretical foundations, target judg-ments, and requirements for observed cues. Schematic interaction examples illustrate the distinctions between these judgments. 

B.1 Theoretical Foundations and the Eight Capabilities 

The taxonomy connects social observation to contextual response selection. Social Information Process-ing distinguishes cue encoding and interpretation from goal clarification and response selection [13]; the Perception-Action Cycle describes how perception and temporally organized information inform action [21]. We operationalize these principles through judgments with explicit targets and requirements for observed cues. 

Perceptually Grounded Mentalizing distinguishes observable cues, psychological interpretations, and support needs. Situated Empathic Responding distinguishes intervention timing, verbal response, and physical or non-verbal action, informed by empathy and interpersonal emotion regulation [16, 71]. Contextual and Normative Modulation addresses historical information and constraints affecting either domain. The eight capabilities separate six judgment targets and two forms of contextual modulation. G, W, and N address what is observed, how the person’s state is interpreted, and what support is needed; T, S, and A address when to respond, what to say, and what action to take. M and R specify how relevant history and consent or safety constraints shape those judgments. A question’s requested judgment and contextual requirements determine its capability assignment, enabling diagnosis at each of these points in social under-standing and response selection. G/W/N/T/S/A/M/R denote Grounding, Understanding, Need, Timing, Response, Action, Memory, and Risk throughout. 

B.2 Capability Definitions 

B.2.1 Perceptually Grounded Mentalizing 

This domain relates observable interaction structure to human states and needs through cues about each person and the supplied context. 17 Social Grounding (G). Grounding identifies participants, observable behavior, and interaction relations: who speaks, who is addressed, and whose gesture or action is described. Speech, expression, gaze, and move-ment provide cues specific to each person; turn structure and spatial context distinguish speakers, addressees, and targets. Associating a cue with the correct participant establishes what was observed. Explaining the person’s underlying state instead belongs to Understanding. 

Psychological Understanding (W). Understanding concerns psychological states and their possible causes or changes during an interaction. Theory of Mind motivates the distinction between observed behavior and attributed mental states [49]. Judgments integrate dialogue, behavior, and context to interpret converging or conflicting cues. The target is an interpretation of the person’s state supported by observed cues within the supplied interaction. 

Need Inference (N). Need identifies what a person wants to change, what support may be relevant, and whether they would accept it. Requests, hesitation, refusal, repeated difficulty, and affective reactions inform these judgments. Distinguishing practical help, acknowledgment, or space requires cues indicating what support the person wants. N thus distinguishes possible support needs from willingness to receive help; T, S, and A address when and how to respond. 

B.2.2 Situated Empathic Responding 

This domain evaluates intervention opportunity, verbal content, and behavioral form as distinct aspects of response appropriateness. 

Interaction Timing and Attunement (T). Timing judges whether to respond now, continue listening, wait, or refrain. Conversational turn-taking motivates attention to ongoing speech and participation [59]; PsycheMME also considers activity, urgency, pauses, and changes across the interaction. Turn structure and current activity establish whether a pause offers an appropriate intervention opportunity. 

Empathic Verbal Responding (S). Response selects what to say and how to say it when a verbal intervention is appropriate. Content, strategy, and tone should fit the person’s state, need, and context, consistent with research on matching support to stressful circumstances [14]. Suitable responses include acknowledgment, validation, clarification, advice, or a restrained reply. S evaluates the fit of response content, strategy, and tone to the situation, complementing N’s support need and T’s intervention opportunity. 

Embodied Social Action (A). Action selects appropriate physical or nonverbal behavior, including gaze, gesture, interpersonal distance, assistance, approach, retreat, and handover. Judgments combine the person’s state and need with physical and social context. Recognizing an action already performed belongs to G; choosing a subsequent action belongs to A. The evaluation target is the appropriateness of the behavioral choice. 

B.2.3 Contextual and Normative Modulation 

Memory provides continuity across interactions; Risk constrains otherwise feasible or helpful responses. 

Interaction and Profile Memory (M). Memory uses relevant supplied history to interpret the current person and situation. Interaction memory includes prior events, utterances, requests, refusals, and outcomes; profile memory includes individual preferences, habits, boundaries, and choices, connecting to personalized multi-modal reasoning [42]. Available history establishes individual preferences and constraints. These categories organize the historical information supplied to Memory questions. 

Risk, Boundary, and Autonomy Regulation (R). Risk addresses harm, consent, personal boundaries, and autonomy. Embodied evaluation focused on safety motivates attention to action consequences [69]; Psy-cheMME also considers expressed preferences and refusals. Constraints may require changing, delaying, or abandoning an intervention. N identifies support needs; R checks the proposed intervention against consent and other relevant constraints. 18 B.3 Examples of Capability Distinctions 

Observation, state, need, and timing. Someone frowns while attempting a task and says, Let me finish explaining before you help . G identifies the actor and speaker; W interprets the supported state; N identifies support needs; T favors listening before intervening. 

Recognition and response selection. For an offered object, identifying the giver and recipient targets G; choosing an acknowledgment targets S; deciding whether to accept or step back targets A. Response choices also depend on requests and refusals. 

History and present constraints. If history records a preference for verbal guidance, M applies it while S and A select a fitting response. A present refusal invokes R, making current consent decisive for the proposed intervention. 

B.4 Cue Grounding and Diagnostic Interpretation 

Expressions, utterances, and actions may support multiple psychological accounts. Valid interpretations fit the supplied situation; responses require cues supporting their timing, content, and form. Requests and refusals establish willingness to receive support, while relevant history establishes individual preferences. The capability definitions are implemented through questions with specified targets, reference cues, and explicit review criteria. Five researchers with psychology backgrounds independently review question valid-ity, reference correctness, and psychological consistency; benchmark inclusion requires unanimous approval (Appendix C.4). Table 9 specifies the cue requirements and review criteria for each capability. Capability profiles summarize performance on these target judgments under the shared scoring protocol in Sec. 3.5 and Appendix D.5. They locate differences in observation, psychological interpretation, support inference, response selection, use of history, and respect for consent and safety constraints. 

# C Benchmark Construction and Quality Review 

C.1 Source Composition and Cue Preparation 

PsycheMME contains 630 video episodes from eight source groups (Table 6). An episode may support several questions, yielding 1,000 questions in total: 890 choice questions and 110 open questions. These statistics describe the final benchmark after screening and quality review. Capability counts and question format distributions appear in Table 7. The video sources include Ego4D [27], EgoCom [44], MERCI+ [2], CASTLE [55], EasyCom [17], and In-dEgo [8]. Episode selection prioritizes social relevance, observable behavioral cues, and adequate video quality. Each question identifies a target person, interaction, and relevant time window, with its reference answer grounded in the source cues. For example, speaker labels require visual corroboration before they can establish a person’s identity; psychological causes and preferences likewise require contextual support. These criteria implement the requirements for cue grounding in Appendix B.4. 

C.2 Question Construction with Independent Answering

With the proposed answer and its supporting rationale withheld, this agent provides an independent answer or identifies ambiguity and insufficient observable support. The Critic Agent evaluates answerability, cue support, and logical coherence by comparing the proposed and independent answers, following the use of multiple answers in multiagent reasoning [20]. C.3 Iterative Question Refinement 

Critic feedback determines whether a candidate needs a clearer question or a renewed examination of the cues. Ambiguous referents, unsupported premises, and defects in answer options prompt question revision; reason-ing errors prompt another independent answer. Every revised question is answered again without revealing the proposed reference. Candidates that remain unresolved after two refinement rounds are discarded. Shared State coordinates feedback and tracks capability coverage during construction. Coverage guides further question generation toward underrepresented capabilities, while the same answerability and criteria for cue support apply to every candidate. 

C.4 Human Quality Review 

As described in Section 3.3, all benchmark items require unanimous approval from five researchers with psy-chology backgrounds. Reviewers check question validity, reference correctness, and psychological consistency using the capability definitions and cue requirements in Table 9.

D.4 Judge Instructions and Rubric 

The Judge receives the question, applicable options, reference answer, textual descriptions of reference cues, and the model’s answer and rationales. It assesses explanation support against these cues and checks logical consistency. Reasoning-Verified measures the quality of explicit justifications relative to annotated reference cues and the final answer. Multiple psychological interpretations are accepted when supported by the available cues. Answer correctness and rationale quality are assessed separately. Open answers receive semantic credit in 

{0, 0.5, 1}: full credit for a correct answer, partial credit for a materially correct but incomplete answer, and zero for incorrect or unsupported answers or responses that do not address the question. Evidence Support concerns whether the explanation’s observations and inferences are grounded in the refer-ence cue annotations. Logical Integrity concerns whether the active rationale fields are mutually compatible and support the final answer. An internally consistent explanation may lack support from observed cues, while grounded statements may combine into an inconsistent conclusion. Rationale coverage is evaluated separately. The Judge assigns Evidence Support and Logical Integrity labels with a short explanation for each assessment. # E SFT and GRPO on PsycheMME 

E.1 SFT and GRPO Training Configurations 

Both adaptation stages update LoRA parameters in the language backbone of Qwen3.5-4B while freezing the visual encoder and multimodal aligner. LoRA is applied to the linear projections in the language model with rank 8, scaling α = 32 , and dropout 0.05. SFT trains on 3,657 video QA examples for one epoch (458 updates). Each example contains 32 sampled frames with a maximum image dimension of 256 pixels, ASR, and public history where available. Targets contain the answer and one annotated observation; unused rationale fields are null. The loss is computed over assistant tokens. Training uses BF16, fused AdamW, a constant learning rate of 5 × 10 −6, weight decay 0.1, no warmup, a batch size of one with eight gradient accumulation steps, and a maximum sequence length of 4,096 tokens. GRPO starts from the SFT checkpoint and uses it as a fixed reference. Its training pool contains 2,842 single-choice examples with the same 32-frame representation. For each question, eight responses are sampled at temperature 1.0 and top-p = 0 .9, with at most 768 completion tokens. The binary reward in Eq. 3 requires both exact answer correctness and compliance with the response requirements. Evidence Support and Logical Integrity scores are used only for evaluation. The GRPO objective uses rewards normalized within each group, clipping ϵ = 0 .2, and KL regularization with β = 0 .03 against the fixed SFT reference. Training uses a constant learning rate of 5 × 10 −7, no warmup, and seed 42. Groups with constant rewards or truncated responses are excluded. Each update uses eight distinct questions and 64 sampled responses, giving 32 updates over 256 distinct questions. 

E.2 Checkpoints and Evaluation Scope 

The adaptation comparison evaluates Qwen3.5-4B Base, SFT at step 458, and GRPO at step 32 on the same 1,000 benchmark questions. Evaluation uses 1 FPS with at most 64 frames, without the reduction in image size used during training, together with greedy BF16 inference and a 768-token output limit. All three models use the same rules for scoring answers and Qwen3.8-27B rationale Judge. Raw/Verified macro scores are 47.68/37.71 for Base, 72.58/52.13 for SFT, and 73.19/52.62 for GRPO (Ta-ble 13). Figure 9 shows the overall scores and Raw profiles across the eight capabilities. SFT improves several 25 capabilities, but Timing remains below the Base score after both adaptation stages. This comparison does not isolate the effects of temporal sampling from the training objectives. 

E.3 Training and Evaluation Data 

To prevent data leakage, we exclude benchmark questions and video episodes from training. We further split the data by source video and remove clips that share the same source with the development and held-out sets. This ensures that the SFT training data does not overlap with the benchmark or checkpoint screening set. Checkpoint screening and final evaluation use independent question sets: the screening set is used for model selection, and the evaluation set is used to report the results in Table 13. This adaptation comparison is separate from the 17-model foundation evaluation.
