# Legal Guidance in the Wild: How Users Seek Legal Help in Real-World LLM Conversations 

Amirreza MOHSENI a, Gerasimos SPANAKIS aa Maastricht University, Maastricht, The Netherlands 

Abstract. People ask conversational AI models about their legal rights, obligations, procedures, and personal situations that may have legal consequences. To study this behavior, we first need to distinguish requests for legal guidance from other legally themed discussions and unrelated conversations. We contribute a multi-axis taxon-omy of legal queries capturing whether legal guidance is sought, how a request is formulated, and which legal domains it concerns; apply it to 2,122 naturally occur-ring WildChat-1M conversations; and create a human-reviewed gold evaluation set of 200 conversations. All annotations, model checkpoints, and evaluation code are publicly available. In this enriched sample, 71.1% of conversations containing legal content seek personalized or practical guidance. Rights-and-options advice is the most common primary request type among legal-content conversations, and 25.3% of guidance-positive conversations span multiple legal topics. The gold evaluation set supports a two-stage comparison of ModernBERT-base and ModernBERT-large against a TF–IDF cascade. Our framework supports large-scale studies of legal guidance in conversational AI, and the public release of the annotated data facili-tates reproducible research and future model development. 

Keywords. legal natural language processing, conversational AI, legal guidance seeking 

1. Introduction 

People use conversational AI to seek both legal information and advice about what ac-tion to take [1]. In our dataset, these questions span topics such as “Immigration & Cit-izenship,” “Employment & Labor,” “Housing & Property,” “Business & Commercial,” and “Consumer & Personal Finance.” Prominent legal NLP benchmarks evaluate legal-document understanding, retrieval, answer generation, or expert-defined reasoning tasks [2,3,4,5]. They reveal less about the more basic question faced by a general-purpose assistant: when is a person actually seeking legal guidance, and how are such requests formulated in the wild? Legally themed text is not synonymous with guidance seeking. A user may ask for a statute to be translated, discuss a fictional dispute, request an academic explanation, or describe a personal situation while seeking concrete rights, options, or next steps. We therefore study a hierarchical classification problem over real LLM conversations: the

The distinction matters because public-facing LLMs can confidently produce unre-liable legal information [6]: a compact guidance detector could support large-scale ex-traction for empirical research or, after calibration and user testing, trigger a notice that generated output is not a substitute for professional advice. This framing parallels recent work on how users seek health information from conversational systems [7]. We make four contributions. First, we develop a taxonomy for identifying and char-acterizing requests for legal guidance in multi-turn conversations. Second, we apply it to 2,122 naturally occurring WildChat-1M conversations spanning both guidance-seeking and non-guidance cases and analyze how requests are formulated, when they first arise, and which legal domains they concern. Third, we release a human-reviewed gold evalua-tion set of 200 conversations and supporting artifacts. 1 Fourth, we compare a lexical cas-cade with ModernBERT-base and ModernBERT-large [8], evaluating them on the gold evaluation set only after finalizing all modeling decisions using separate silver-labeled development data. 

2. Related Work Legal information needs. Prior work has examined both observed requests to legal-help AI and users’ expectations of such systems. Kuk and Haraˇ sta study queries submitted to a GPT-4 legal assistant and distinguish requests for legal information from requests for advice about what action to take [1]. Hagan uses interviews and design sessions to examine when people would use legal-help AI and which qualities and safeguards they expect from it [9]. Steenhuis and Westermann use LLMs to support civil legal-aid intake by checking eligibility and generating follow-up questions [10]. We study an upstream and broader task in naturally occurring conversations: identifying whether users seek legal guidance and how they formulate those requests. 

Behavior in real-world conversations. WildChat makes it possible to study interactions with conversational models at scale [11]. A close methodological analogue is What’s Up, Doc? , which filters large conversational datasets and applies a clinician-driven taxonomy to how and why users seek health information across specialties [7]. We adopt the same user-centered perspective for law, but separate legal content from guidance seeking and add hierarchical topic classification, supporting both descriptive analysis and smaller classifiers that apply the taxonomy without repeatedly invoking a frontier model. 

Legal NLP. Prominent benchmarks evaluate legal-document understanding or expert-defined reasoning tasks [2,3]. A complementary line of work starts from citizen or lay legal questions. BSARD maps French questions to relevant Belgian statutory provisions, and Finding the Law studies improved statutory retrieval in the same citizen-question set-ting [4,12]. LLeQA pairs expert-annotated questions with long-form answers grounded in legislation [5], while GerLayQA pairs German civil-law questions from laypeople with lawyer-authored answers and statutory references [13]. These resources provide useful evidence about question language, legal-topic distributions, retrieval, and answer generation in fixed-question settings. We instead examine the distributions of guidance

Topic taxonomies provide useful structure for legal datasets [14], but assigning an inter-action directly to a legal field misses whether the user wants explanation, drafting, proce-dural steps, or advice about rights and options. Strong benchmark performance also does not make a public model a reliable legal adviser: Dahl et al. document frequent legal hal-lucinations and confident acceptance of false premises [6]. Our classifier does not assess answer correctness; it identifies conversations in which stronger safeguards, specialized routing, or human escalation may be warranted. 

3. Data and Task 

3.1. Dataset construction, taxonomy, and silver annotation 

The conversations originate from English-language WildChat-1M interaction logs [11]. We used OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Terra, accessed through the OpenAI API at high reasoning effort, for an initial screening pass to identify clearly unrelated material and conversa-tions potentially containing legal content [15]. The retained sample contains 2,122 con-versations, combining conversations initially identified as containing legal content with an equal-sized random sample from those classified by Terra as non-legal. These initial labels determined sample composition and are distinct from the annotations used in our analysis. This design allows building classification models; however, the label distribu-tion cannot estimate prevalence in WildChat. The taxonomy captures three aspects of a conversation. Its legal relevance axis dis-tinguishes legal content from personalized or practical guidance seeking. Its interaction 

axis records user context, drafting intent, guidance onset, and request types: rights-and-options advice, information or explanation, procedural assistance, document drafting, document review, and other requests. Its domain axis assigns legal topics. We adapted the topic and subtopic structure of existing literature [4,12]. The domain axis concerns 13 substantive legal topics and a residual “O THER ” category. Each conver-sation receives one primary topic representing its predominant legal issue and optional secondary topics for additional issues. The domain axis is flat; the classification pipeline first detects guidance seeking and then predicts a primary topic. The other taxonomy fields support descriptive analysis. Following screening and sample selection, we annotated all 2,122 retained conversa-tions with OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol, accessed through the OpenAI API at medium reason-ing effort under the taxonomy [15]. Sol annotated every retained conversation, including the non-guidance cases; Terra’s screening labels were not used as the final silver labels. These Sol annotations form the silver-labeled dataset: 1,367 conversations contain legal content and 972 seek legal guidance (45.8%). All classifiers and descriptive analyses use these annotations, with human-reviewed labels replacing them for gold evaluation. Only user messages are presented, in chronological order; assistant messages are excluded be-cause we study how users phrase legal requests. The dataset, annotation prompt, JSON schema, and run configurations are available through the project resources. 2

3.2. Human review and gold evaluation set 

Two reviewers with backgrounds in computer science independently examined all 200 conversations in the gold evaluation set. Each reviewer worked on a separate worksheet and recorded whether the silver guidance and primary-topic labels were correct, adding a corrected label and short note where needed. Reviewing the silver labels rather than labeling each conversation from scratch allowed both reviewers to assess the full set, but may have introduced anchoring; we return to this in the Discussion. We measured agreement on the 200-conversation gold evaluation set using the avail-able paired judgments. Guidance agreement was 96.5%, with Cohen’s κ = .927. Topic agreement, measured over binary judgments of whether the silver topic was correct, was 94.9%, with κ = .561. The lower topic κ reflects judgment imbalance: reviewers marked the silver topic as correct in 94% of judgments, and κ is sensitive to such imbalances [16]. Overall, the reviewers reached full agreement on 186 conversations (93.0%). They discussed the remaining 14 cases (7.0%) and reached consensus; these consensus deci-sions became the final labels. We use only the silver development set for training and model selection and reserve the gold evaluation set for final evaluation. 

3.3. Task and metrics 

We formulate the task as a two-stage classification pipeline. In Stage 1, the model iden-tifies whether a conversation contains a request for legal guidance. In Stage 2, conver-sations identified as positive in Stage 1 are classified into one of 14 legal topics. Each conversation receives one of 15 final labels (either one of the 14 topics or “N O GUID -

> ANCE

”), so errors at either stage affect the final label. We report accuracy and macro-F1 for guidance detection, topic classification, and end-to-end evaluation. Macro-F1 gives equal weight to each class, which is important because several topics are rare; the sup-plementary material provides additional guidance metrics and confidence intervals. The primary goal of the manual annotation was to verify whether each conversation seeks legal guidance. Because guidance requests span many legal topics, the gold eval-uation set provides more limited evidence for topic evaluation: its 84 guidance-positive conversations cover 12 of the 14 topics, with no examples of “Torts & Civil Liability” or “O THER ,” and five topics have five or fewer examples. We compute gold topic macro-F1 over only the 12 observed classes and provide bootstrap intervals and paired significance tests in the supplementary material. 

4. How Users Seek Legal Guidance 

Of the 2,122 conversations in the silver-labeled dataset, 1,367 (64.4%) contain some form of legal content. Among those 1,367 conversations, 972 (71.1%) seek personalized or practical legal guidance; these guidance requests account for 45.8% of the dataset. The remaining 395 conversations discuss legal issues without seeking guidance, and 755 contain no legal content. These percentages should not be interpreted as prevalence esti-mates for WildChat because the sampling process deliberately included more conversa-tions initially identified as legal. 

Request types. Among the 1,367 legal-content conversations, “rights-and-options ad-vice” is the most common primary request type (712; 52.1%), followed by “information or explanation” (293; 21.4%), “procedural how-to” (180; 13.2%), “document drafting” (137; 10.0%), “other request” (26; 1.9%), and “document review” (19; 1.4%). “Rights-and-options advice” concerns the user’s own situation (e.g., “Can my landlord evict me for this?”); “information or explanation” asks how a legal concept or system works (e.g., “What is the difference between civil and criminal law?”); and “procedural how-to” re-quests ask for concrete steps, forms, or deadlines (e.g., “How do I file an appeal?”). “Doc-ument drafting” asks the model to produce or revise legally operative text (e.g., “Draft a termination clause”), whereas “document review” asks it to analyze an existing docu-ment (e.g., “What does this clause mean?”). The residual “other request” category covers legal-content requests outside these groups (e.g., “Create a quiz about contract law”). Requests to create quizzes or similar study materials fall under this residual category, while requests to explain legal concepts fall under information or explanation. A conversation can receive more than one request-type label. When both primary and secondary request-type labels are counted, 62.5% of conversations include “rights-and-options advice” and 36.6% include “information or explanation.” Thus, users most often ask what they can or should do in their particular situation. Explanations also ap-pear in more than one third of conversations, often alongside a request for practical guid-ance. 

Topics and guidance onset. Among the 972 guidance-seeking conversations, “Immigra-tion & Citizenship” is the largest primary topic (14.1%), followed by “Criminal Law” (11.4%), “Intellectual Property” (11.3%), “Business & Commercial” (11.0%), and “Tax Law” (10.8%). The distribution has a long tail: “Civil Rights & Constitutional” accounts for 0.3% of primary labels and “Torts & Civil Liability” for 1.2%. In total, 25.3% of guidance-positive conversations receive more than one topic. 5. Classification Models and Early Prediction 

5.1. Experimental setup 

We compare two classifier families: logistic regression using word- and character-level TF–IDF features, and ModernBERT encoders in base and large configurations [17,8]. We chose these models with consumer-device use in mind and aimed for an approach that could process entire datasets quickly. TF–IDF with logistic regression provides a lexical baseline, while ModernBERT provides a contextual language-model approach. The base and large configurations allow us to examine whether increased model size consistently improves classification performance. All classifiers receive only user turns in chronological order. The training and validation splits have similar guidance rates (46.0% and 46.2%), and both contain examples from all 14 topics. Because the topic distribution is highly imbalanced, we use class-weighted topic training and macro-averaged evaluation. To support guidance detection as a conversation unfolds, we also train binary prefix models. A conversation prefix contains all user messages up to a given turn, whereas the full-conversation models are trained only on complete conversations. 5.2. Evaluation 

We calibrated the guidance models on the silver validation split using the thresholds in Table 3 and froze all model-selection decisions before evaluating on the gold evaluation set. The final-turn results use this locked evaluation; the exploratory turn-1 and turn-2 analysis applies the same frozen prefix checkpoints and thresholds to shorter inputs. We report 95% bootstrap intervals from 2,000 conversation-level resamples and use exact paired McNemar tests to compare system errors. Full reliability procedures and results are provided in the supplementary material.

6.3. Statistical reliability and silver-label effects 

No exact paired comparison reaches p < . 05, and the bootstrap macro-F1 intervals over-lap. The clearest performance advantage of dense models over TF–IDF is in topic clas-sification, where ModernBERT-base and ModernBERT-large improve macro-F1 by 8.7 and 11.2 points, respectively. Model size was not predictive of performance across the tasks. The base model led the large model by 2.9 macro-F1 points on the silver validation set for topic classifica-tion, while the large model led by 2.5 points on the gold topic task. The large model also scored highest on full-conversation guidance detection and end-to-end classification. These mixed results do not show a consistent advantage for either model size. 

7. Discussion 

The results suggest that ModernBERT helps most with topic classification. Guidance re-quests often contain clear words and phrases associated with options, forms, deadlines, or document drafting, which may explain why TF–IDF remains competitive. Topic clas-sification requires separating related and sometimes overlapping areas of law, and both ModernBERT models perform better on this task. However, the base and large models perform differently across the silver and gold datasets, and the differences between them are not statistically significant. The errors also show a practical trade-off. ModernBERT-large misses the fewest guidance requests but also incorrectly classifies the most non-guidance conversations as guidance requests. This may be acceptable for a simple caution notice, but it would increase workload if flagged conversations are sent to human reviewers or specialists. The decision threshold should therefore match the intended use. Moreover, 25.3% of guidance-seeking conversations span multiple topics, while the classifier assigns just one. Consequently, some topic classification errors may stem from the presence of multiple topics, rather than incorrect predictions. Several design choices limit the scope of our findings. Training and development la-bels come from one language model, and reviewers assessed pre-annotated labels, which may have introduced anchoring effects [18]. Both reviewers had computer-science back-grounds, and the topic labels were not independently checked by a legal expert. Request types, secondary topics, and uncertainty flags were not human-validated. The enriched sampling design supports classifier development but not prevalence estimates for Wild-Chat. The gold evaluation set contains only 84 guidance-positive conversations across 12 observed topics, and the models were trained using a single random seed. The taxon-omy’s focus on English, its jurisdiction-agnostic design, and the reduction of multi-topic requests to one primary routing label further limit transfer to other settings. Guidance onset was not human-validated, so the initial-turn analysis measures whether eventual guidance seeking is predictable from initial turns rather than whether a model detects its true onset. The conversations may describe sensitive legal circumstances despite WildChat’s anonymization procedures [11]. In this paper, we report aggregate results and do not reproduce conversation excerpts. The classifier identifies requests that may need safe-guards or specialized treatment; it does not determine whether a claim is valid or an an-swer is correct. Calibration, user testing, and access to human support would therefore be necessary before deployment. 

8. Conclusion 

We introduce a taxonomy and classification benchmark for studying legal guidance seek-ing in general-purpose LLM conversations. In this enriched sample, silver labels identify guidance seeking in 45.8% of conversations. Rights-and-options advice is the most com-mon primary request type among legal-content conversations, and 25.3% of guidance-positive conversations span multiple topic labels. On the gold evaluation set, ModernBERT-large achieves the best performance over-all; however, the gains are not uniform: TF–IDF performs similarly to dense models on binary guidance detection, whereas dense models lead on topic classification. No paired comparison reaches significance at this sample size, so the score differences should be interpreted cautiously. Future work should extend this taxonomy, test its robustness across languages and legal systems, and explore how classifier outputs might inform real-time caution notices or routing decisions in deployed conversational AI systems. More broadly, we hope the public release of the annotated dataset, taxonomy, and evaluation code supports both further research into legal help-seeking behavior and the development of practical safe-guards in deployed systems.
