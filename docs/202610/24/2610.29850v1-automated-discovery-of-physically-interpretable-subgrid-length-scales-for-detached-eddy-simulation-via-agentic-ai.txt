Traditional turbulence-model development relies heavily on expert knowledge and is time-
consuming. This study develops an agentic AI framework, DEScover (automated Discovery
of DES closures), to explore subgrid length scales for detached eddy simulation (DES).
The user provides only a modeling idea: the length scale is the grid width normal to
the local vortex axis. Four large language model (LLM) agents then propose, review,
implement, and evaluate candidate formulations and generate scripts for CFD simulations.
The Discovery Agent infers the vortex axis from various vortex-identification methods.
The candidates are tested on four cases spanning canonical and engineering flows: a flat
plate, a backward-facing step, a free shear layer, and an iced airfoil. For all test cases, the
best candidate consistently outperforms the standard length scale based on the maximum
cell size. Its accuracy is similar to, or slightly better than, that of the expert-designed
shear-layer-adapted length scale. Each agentic discovery run takes about 25 minutes on
average, excluding the CFD simulations. These results show that LLM-based reasoning
can significantly accelerate the discovery of physically interpretable turbulence models and
reduce reliance on manual expert formulation.
1. Introduction
Turbulence modeling is of fundamental importance in computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
and has long attracted extensive research attention. Traditionally, the development of
turbulence models has relied heavily on both a deep physical understanding of turbulent
flows and substantial empirical knowledge. Representative advances include wall-modeling
approaches in large eddy simulation (LES), such as integral wall models based on ordinary
differential equation (ODE) formulations (Yang et al. 2015), log-layer mismatch correction
and fluctuating wall-stress modeling (Yang et al. 2017), semi-locally scaled eddy-viscosity
formulations for high-speed flows (Yang & Lv 2018), and eddy-conductivity closures for
wall heat flux prediction in high-speed regimes (Chen et al. 2022). Additional developments
include new length-scale formulations in detached eddy simulation (DES) (Zhou et al.

capability and applicability of turbulence models for complex flow configurations.
In recent years, the integration of machine learning (ML) and data-driven techniques
has emerged as a revolutionary paradigm for turbulence modeling, aiming to overcome the
inherent limitations and empirical assumptions of traditional Reynolds-Averaged Navier-
Stokes (RANS) models and Large Eddy Simulations (LES). Generally, recent advance-
ments in ML-based turbulence closures can be categorized into five major methodologies.
The first relies on data assimilation and field inversion frameworks, which indirectly
calibrate empirical coefficients or introduce spatially varying correction source terms
into existing transport equations to enhance predictive accuracy (Rinc´on et al. 2025; Bin
et al. 2023, 2024; Hu et al. 2025). Notably, recent strides have successfully extended these
frameworks from steady states to time-accurate unsteady flows and automated aerodynamic
shape optimization (Fang & He 2024; Li et al. 2026). The second approach involves direct
corrections targeting the anisotropy tensor of Reynolds stresses, where novel architectures
like Tensor Basis Neural Networks (TBNNs) are utilized to explicitly preserve Galilean
invariance and physical symmetries (Ling et al. 2016). Subsequent extensions successfully
resolved the numerical instability of embedding these learned structures back into CFD
solvers (Wu et al. 2018), and further generalized the invariant tensor architectures to
turbulent scalar and heat flux modeling (Milani et al. 2021). To address the “black-box”
nature and lack of interpretability of deep neural networks, the third category leverages
symbolic regression and gene expression programming to discover explicit, algebraic
turbulence formulations directly from high-fidelity data (Yang et al. 2025b). Notably,
this data-driven technique has been successfully utilized to discover explicit correction
functions for the baseline shear-stress-transport (SST) model, significantly balancing the
interpretability and generalization capability of RANS closures (Wu & Zhang 2023).
In the fourth category, Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) (Raissi et al. 2018)
has gained substantial traction, wherein fundamental conservation laws and boundary
conditions are embedded into the networkvia customized loss functions or hard-constrained
architectures (Yang et al. 2019). This paradigm has been robustly applied to derive data-
driven subgrid-scale closures for LES via physical deconvolution frameworks (Maulik et al.
2018) as well as solving unclosed RANS systems directly via automatic differentiation of
partial differential equation residuals (Eivazi et al. 2022). Beyond these predominantly
supervised paradigms, the fifth category leverages Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)
to resolve the “a priori vs. a posteriori” discrepancy by framing the CFD solver as the
environment. This methodology has been pioneered to automatically discover dynamic
subgrid-scale closures for LES (Novati et al. 2021), construct generalized near-wall models
(Bae & Koumoutsakos 2022), and recently advanced into high-performance computing
(HPC) platforms through a solver-agnostic, open-source SmartFlow framework (Xiao
et al. 2025). Despite this progress, data-driven turbulence models still have limitations.
First, most of them need a large amount of high-fidelity data for training. Such data are
expensive to obtain for complex flows with high Reynolds numbers. Second, neural-
network-based turbulence model augmentations are often black boxes, making their
predictions difficult to interpret physically and limiting their portability across CFD
solvers. Although symbolic regression and gene expression programming can produce
explicit mathematical expressions based on flow features, the mathematical relationships
among these features are primarily determined through data-driven fitting rather than
physical reasoning. Consequently, the resulting formulations may still lack clear physical
connections to established turbulence theories.

The rapid development of large language models (LLMs), trained on vast amounts
of source code and scientific literature, has opened a new paradigm for automated
scientific discovery. Early studies mainly focused on reducing the operational barrier of
CFD by allowing users to describe simulation tasks in natural language. Representative
systems, such as OpenFOAMGPT (Pandey et al. 2025), OpenFOAMGPT 2.0 (Feng et al.
2026), Foam-Agent/Foam-Agent 2.0 (Yue et al. 2025a; Pan et al. 2026), FoamGPT (Yue
et al. 2025b), CFDagent (Xu et al. 2025), ChatCFD (Fan et al. 2025), FlamePilot (Xiao
et al. 2026), and natural-language-to-simulation intermediate-representation frameworks
(Shenoy & Frankel 2026), demonstrate that LLM-based agents can automate case setup,
mesh generation, solver configuration, error correction, post-processing, and visualization
across OpenFOAM-based and other CFD environments. A related line of work extends this
capability to aerodynamic and multidisciplinary design optimization, where multi-agent
systems such as specialized aerodynamic design agents (Lee et al. 2025), AirfoilAgent (Fan
et al. 2026), iDesign (Fang & He 2026), MDO agents (Guo et al. 2025), vision-language
models for engineering design evaluation (Picard et al. 2025), AI design agents for aesthetic
and aerodynamic car design (Elrefaie et al. 2025), and risk-aware set-based engineering
design agents (Kumar & Karniadakis 2026) couple LLMs with CAD, CFD, optimization,
and knowledge-retrieval tools to support geometry generation, design-space exploration,
and iterative performance improvement. A deeper and more impactful application lies in
employing LLMs to propose, refine, and discover new turbulence modelling paradigms—a
direction that has only recently begun to be explored. Two complementary paradigms
have recently emerged for LLM-assisted turbulence modelling. Zhang et al. (2025)
embedded LLMs within an evolutionary search framework to automatically discover
algebraic Reynolds-stress corrections, treating the LLM as an expression generator guided
by a quantitative fitness function. Yang et al. (2025a) adopted a contrasting approach,
engaging DeepSeek-R1 as a reasoning collaborator through iterative human–AI dialogue,
successfully developing near-wall models for LES under adverse pressure gradients, system
rotation, and surface roughness. These works demonstrate that LLMs can contribute to
turbulence modelling as physics-aware reasoning agents.
In this paper, a multi-agent framework, termed DEScover (automated Discovery of
DES closures), is proposed to systematically explore candidate subgrid length scales for
detached eddy simulations (DES). The primary contribution of this work is the automation
of the turbulence model development process, from an initial modeling idea to explicit
mathematical formulations, code implementation, and a posteriori CFD evaluations across
multiple flow cases. Compared with existing data-driven turbulence model augmentation
methods, the proposed agentic-AI-driven approach generates explicit formulations with
clearer connections to underlying turbulence physics. Four flow cases are considered for
validation: a flat plate, a backward-facing step, a free shear layer, and an iced airfoil. The
flat-plate case assesses whether the candidate length scales preserve accurate predictions
for attached flow. The backward-facing step and free-shear-layer cases evaluate their ability
to alleviate the delayed onset of Kelvin–Helmholtz (K–H) instability. The iced-airfoil case
represents a high-Reynolds-number engineering flow and provides a more challenging test
of the generalization capability of the candidate length scales.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. § 2 introduces the methodology. § 3
presents the results and discussion. Finally, § 4 provides the conclusions of this study.

2.2. The DEScover Framework
2.2.1. Overall Architecture
This section presents an automated framework for turbulence modeling. The proposed
framework is multi-case and operates in a closed-loop manner. DEScover targets the
model closures of DES. In the present study, it is applied to the subgrid length scale of
IDDES. The workflow itself does not depend on a specific turbulence model. Its extension
to RANS and LES models is left for future work. The overall architecture consists of
several tightly coupled modules, as illustrated in figure 4. The framework consists of
four LLM-based agents and one script-based module. The four agents are the Discovery
0 X0-8


Journal of Fluid Mechanics
Agent, the Review Agent, the Code Implementation Agent, and the Evaluation Agent. The
fifth module is the CFD Simulation Orchestrator. It is a Python script and does not use
an LLM. It runs the CFD simulations of the case sequence and collects the results. All
modules are automatically invoked through Python scripts to ensure the reliable operation
of the framework. The overall workflow is as follows. First, the user defines a prompt and
submits it to the Discovery Agent. The Discovery Agent then generates candidate models
according to the user-defined prompt. Next, the Review Agent examines the candidate
models to ensure their logical consistency and dimensional correctness. The reviewed
models are then transferred to the Code Implementation Agent. After code generation, the
CFD Simulation Orchestrator applies the candidate models to multiple benchmark cases.
To reduce the total execution time of the CFD Simulation Orchestrator, each candidate
model is assigned to an independent but identical case sequence, [𝑐1, 𝑐2, 𝑐3, ..., 𝑐𝑛], and all
sequences are executed in parallel. When all CFD simulations are completed, the Evaluation
Agent analyzes the simulation results and determines whether a satisfactory model has been
obtained. If a candidate model meets all the predefined evaluation criteria, it is selected as
the final output. Otherwise, feedback is generated and returned to the Discovery Agent to
refine the original user-defined prompt. This iterative process continues until a satisfactory
model is obtained.
The agents do not rely only on the general knowledge of the LLM. Each agent follows a
task-specific instruction file, called Agent Skill. The skill bounds the behavior of the agent
and makes its output more robust and repeatable. Each skill defines four items: the input
files, a step-by-step procedure, the rules that must be followed, and a fixed output format. For
the Discovery Agent, the input is the user prompt (figure 5). Each alternative method must
be written as a separate proposal. Each proposal must follow a fixed template. The template
contains the full expression, a table of variable definitions, a step-by-step explanation, the
applicability conditions, and the advantages and limitations. For the Review Agent, the
instructions are the four review criteria given in § 2.2.2. For the Code Implementation
Agent, the instructions require the agent to first search the OpenFOAM source code for an
existing equivalent method. An existing method is not implemented again. Instead, it is
used as a template for the coding style and the class structure. The agent must write the
code only into a specified directory. It must also add a header that links the code to its
proposal file and to the template file. The agent then checks whether the code is consistent
with the formula (§ 2.2.3). For the Evaluation Agent, the instructions specify the evaluation
criteria of each case and the format of the feedback file comments.md (§ 2.2.5). The CFD
Simulation Orchestrator does not use an LLM. Its behavior is fully fixed by the Python
script.
2.2.2. Intelligent discovery
Discovery Agent. To initiate the DEScover workflow, a user-defined prompt is provided
to the Discovery Agent. In the prompt, the length scale is defined as the grid size in the
direction orthogonal to the vortex axis, i.e., equation (2.8). Figure 5 shows the specific
prompt content. Based on the prompt, the Discovery Agent performs literature analysis
and physical reasoning (figure 4) to identify methods that determine the local vortex-axis
direction n𝑣from the resolved velocity field, including vortex-identification criteria that
were not originally formulated for directional identification. Each method is formulated as
a candidate length scale of the form of equation (2.8), together with the fallback treatment
required in regions where the vortex axis is undefined. In subsequent iterations of the
closed loop, the feedback from the Evaluation Agent (§ 2.2.5) is appended to the prompt,
so that the Discovery Agent can refine the existing candidates or propose new ones.

Review Agent. After the Discovery Agent proposes new length-scale candidates, the
Review Agent evaluates them before implementation in OpenFOAM. The Review Agent
examines and refines each candidate based on four criteria. (1) Internal logical consistency.
This criterion ensures that each candidate is logically consistent throughout the proposed
formulation. For example, if a proposal uses the maximum eigenvalue, the corresponding
formulation should also use the maximum eigenvalue rather than incorrectly referring
to the minimum eigenvalue. Any inconsistency is identified and corrected before further
implementation. (2) Dimensional homogeneity. This criterion ensures that every proposed
expression is dimensionally consistent. The dimensions on the left-hand side and right-
hand side of each equation must be identical. Any expression that violates this requirement
is considered invalid and must be revised to satisfy dimensional consistency. (3) Locality.
vortex axis direction is a pointwise function of the local velocity gradient tensor, n𝑣=
𝑓(∇𝒖), independent of any spatial, temporal or global averages.The subgrid length scale
represents the local resolution capability of the grid. A non-local definition would make
it depend on the averaging procedure, on the choice of averaging domain and on the
convergence of flow statistics, and n𝑣would be ill-defined during the initial transient of
0 X0-10


Journal of Fluid Mechanics
a simulation. Locality also lets each cell be evaluated independently, so the formulation
requires no global reductions or additional stored mean fields and is directly compatible
with parallel computation on arbitrary unstructured meshes. (4) Physical uniqueness.
vortex axis direction is the intrinsic rotation axis (or swirling-plane normal) defined by
a single vortex identification method, rather than an ad hoc combination of directions
from different methods. Ad hoc combinations, weightings or interpolations of directions
obtained from different methods are excluded. The definition of 𝛥⊥rests on the premise
that n𝑣is a physical rotation axis, because 𝛥⊥is meant to measure the grid spacing in the
plane perpendicular to that axis. A blended direction generally corresponds to no physical
axis. It also introduces non-unique modelling choices, such as the form of the weighting
function or switching thresholds, that cannot be justified on physical grounds.
2.2.3. Code Implementation in OpenFOAM
Rather than requiring the LLM to generate OpenFOAM code from scratch, we supply an
existing IDDES length scale implementation as a reference template. The new length scale
expression is implemented by inheriting from this base class, leveraging both the object-
oriented structure of C++ and the modular turbulence model framework of OpenFOAM.
This design significantly reduces the implementation burden and minimises the risk of
code hallucination, as the LLM need only modify the length scale computation within
a well-defined, pre-validated code structure. Specifically, for each candidate, the LLM is
prompted to generate the C++ code that computes the vortex-axis direction n𝑣from the
velocity gradient tensor ∇U, including the fallback treatment, and evaluates equation (2.8)
using the cell centroid-to-face-centroid vectors r 𝑓. The generated code is inserted into the
designated length-scale function of the template class. A Python validation script checks
for syntactic correctness and the absence of division-by-zero or imaginary-valued sub-
expressions, and a dry-run compilation is then performed with the standard OpenFOAM
wmake build system before the code is used in the CFD simulations.
This approach is consistent with the natural-language-to-intermediate-representation
philosophy advocated by Shenoy & Frankel (2026), where the language model infers high-
level intent rather than generating unconstrained executable code. By confining the LLM to
a well-defined function slot within a validated template, the risk of silent semantic errors
and hallucinated solver directives is greatly reduced. However, code that compiles and
runs may still implement a wrong formula. To detect such errors, an additional consistency
check is performed after the code is implemented. The generated code is compared with the
formula in the corresponding proposal file, term by term. The check covers the definition
of the vortex-axis direction n𝑣, the fallback treatment, and the evaluation of equation (2.8).
Any mismatch is returned to the Code Implementation Agent for correction. Only the code
that passes this check is used in the CFD simulations.
2.2.4. Hierarchical multi-case simulation module of DEScover
In this task, a hierarchical, multi-case CFD simulation module is established in DEScover.
The module consists of several benchmark cases with increasing levels. For a specified
turbulence modeling task, a case sequence [𝑐1, 𝑐2, 𝑐3, ..., 𝑐𝑛] should be provided. The
computational cost of the cases should generally increase along the sequence. The cases
should be carefully selected to evaluate different aspects of candidate models. Typical
cases include a flat plate for assessing attached-flow prediction, a backward-facing step
for evaluating flow separation, a free shear layer for assessing the K–H instability, and an
airfoil for evaluating curved-surface and high-Reynolds-number effects. These benchmark
cases are organized into a hierarchical evaluation framework. A candidate model is first
evaluated using the case 𝑐1. Only candidates that satisfy the 𝑐1 evaluation criteria are
0 X0-11


J. Chen, X. I. A. Yang and P. He
allowed to proceed to the next level, namely the case 𝑐2. Models that fail at any level
are excluded from subsequent evaluations. Therefore, a successful candidate model must
sequentially pass the cases [𝑐1, 𝑐2, 𝑐3, ..., 𝑐𝑛]. This hierarchical mechanism provides an
efficient assessment of the generalization capability of candidate models. In addition, it
significantly reduces the computational cost of CFD simulations, since models that fail at
an early stage are not evaluated in the subsequent, more computationally expensive cases.
2.2.5. The closed-loop capability of DEScover
In this task, the closed-loop capability of the DEScover framework is developed. The
closed-loop process is achieved by introducing an Evaluation Agent. If all candidates fail
to satisfy the CFD evaluation module, the Evaluation Agent provides detailed feedback
and instructs the Discovery Agent to refine the prompt. More specifically, the Evaluation
Agent generates a markdown file named comments.md. The first row of comments.md
records the evaluation result, indicating either Pass or Fail. The remaining rows provide
detailed failure comments based on the CFD evaluation criteria. This feedback enables
the Discovery Agent to generate more reliable and physically feasible candidate ideas in
the next iteration. The updated prompt is then used to generate new candidate ideas. This
iterative process continues until a candidate satisfies the CFD evaluation criteria. Once a
candidate passes the CFD evaluation criteria, the Evaluation Agent terminates the iterative
loop and outputs the best candidate. No further refinement is required.
2.3. Numerical Methodology
2.3.1. Solver and Time Integration

4. Conclusion
This paper develops a multi-agent framework, DEScover, for systematically exploring
candidate subgrid length scales for detached eddy simulation (DES). The framework is
built on a turbulence-modeling concept that defines the local length scale from the grid
width normal to the local vortex axis. Four flow cases are used for validation: a flat plate,
a backward-facing step, a free shear layer, and an iced airfoil. The principal findings are
summarized as follows:
(1) DEScover reduces the reliance on extensive expert knowledge in turbulence-model
development. Traditional model development requires substantial expertise and time to
formulate explicit mathematical expressions. DEScover leverages LLM-based reasoning
to reduce this reliance and automatically generate multiple candidate formulations. This
approach enables a faster and more systematic exploration of turbulence-modeling formu-
lations.
(2) The proposed length scales are effective across a range of diverse flow cases. Among
the candidate length scales, 𝛥𝑅provides the most consistent improvement across all tested
cases. It maintains acceptable predictions for the flat-plate flow and alleviates the delayed
onset of Kelvin–Helmholtz instability in the backward-facing step, free-shear-layer, and
iced-airfoil cases.
(3) The systematic exploration of length scales reflects a progressive understanding of the
underlying physics. The vorticity-based length scale provides an intuitive approach by using
the cell width normal to the vortex axis. However, vorticity can be contaminated by shear
effects, leading to inaccurate vortex-axis identification and requiring a correction of the
direction. 𝛥𝑆𝐿𝐴successfully incorporates this directional correction. With more accurate
vortex-axis identification, such a correction is no longer necessary, as demonstrated by the
current 𝛥𝑅.
Although DEScover has demonstrated its capability to explore new length scales for DES,
several limitations remain. First, DEScover currently relies on OpenFOAM, which can be
computationally expensive for systematic DES exploration. Faster solvers, such as JAX-
based solvers, could substantially accelerate the exploration process. Second, DEScover is
applied only to the subgrid length scale of DES in this study. Its workflow does not depend
on a specific turbulence model. Further studies are needed to extend it to RANS and LES
models. Such studies will show whether the framework can also support more conventional
turbulence-model development and reduce the reliance on expert knowledge.
