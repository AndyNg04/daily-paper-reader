## Design and Cross-Domain Evaluation of an Agent Execution Harness 

Saurav Joshi Manas Gupta Ashwin Venkatesh Raman Akshay Budhkar 

Spine AI 

Abstract 

Completing complex tasks with language-model agents requires coordinating planning, execution, and review so the final result ad-dresses the user’s original objective. We present an agent execution harness that keeps the original objective separate from a revisable plan and an execution record. A planner as-signs scoped tasks, workers produce outputs and artifacts, and a reviewer examines results against the original request before submitting or requesting additional work. Reusable domain-specific instructions guide these roles and are refined during construction using task inputs and execution trajectories, without official grad-ing feedback. We evaluate complete harness configurations across terminal tasks, clinical responses, application building, and scientific questions. On Terminal-Bench 2.1, the harness passes 83 of 89 instances (93.26%); on Health-Bench Professional, a Fable-configured system achieves a length-adjusted rubric score of 69.63 across 525 tasks. In a 53-task paired Health-Bench comparison, the Fable-configured har-ness achieves an aggregate score 19.89 points higher than direct completion, despite a −1.46 -point median task-level change; mean inference cost rises from $0.29 to $2.13 per task. We combine these outcomes with latency, docu-mented instruction revisions, and reviewer de-cisions. Evaluating outcomes alongside execu-tion trajectories and resource use clarifies how coordination supports task completion, where it introduces regressions, and at what cost. 

1 Introduction 

An agent can complete an assigned step without fulfilling the user’s original objective. For exam-ple, a database query may return the expected rows with incorrect column types, or an application may appear functional until a reload exposes an unmet authentication requirement. Such cases illustrate the distinction between local execution and task completion. As tasks move through planning, ex-ecution, and review, a system must preserve the original request and the evidence needed to de-cide whether a result should be submitted or work should continue. Coordination and verification fail-ures make this an important challenge for agent systems (Cemri et al., 2025). Tool-using agents support iterative reasoning and action (Yao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), while multi-agent systems provide role assignment, orchestration, and artifact handoff (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Fourney et al., 2024). We study how these capabilities are integrated into an execution protocol. A plan can change as new work is identified, whereas the user’s original ob-jective must remain the reference for completion. A worker’s reported success must therefore be as-sessed in relation to the overall result. We present an agent execution harness that co-ordinates planning, worker execution, and review through three elements: the original objective, a revisable graph of scoped assignments, and an ac-cumulating execution record. A planner assigns work and dependencies; workers execute those as-signments and record outputs and artifacts; and a reviewer examines the available results against the original objective before submitting or request-ing bounded follow-up work. Benchmark-specific adapters connect this protocol to different tools, environments, and submission requirements. Reusable domain-specific instructions guide the planning, execution, and review roles. During con-struction, a setup agent refines these instructions using task inputs and execution trajectories with-out official reference answers or grading feedback. The resulting instructions specialize the common execution protocol without changing its underlying structure. We evaluate complete harness configurations across terminal tasks, clinical responses, appli-cation building, and scientific questions using 1arXiv:2610.25568v1 [cs.AI] 18 Oct 2026 benchmark-native metrics. We supplement these outcomes with fixed-task reference comparisons, cost and latency measurements where available, and analyses of documented instruction changes and reviewer behavior. Paired clinical comparisons show substantial improvements alongside regres-sions and increased resource use. Retrospective terminal episodes show review identifying errors and directing focused repairs, while a contrasting virtual-machine episode shows additional verifi-cation disrupting a working environment. These analyses connect benchmark outcomes to observed execution behavior and resource costs (Ma et al., 2024). We investigate three research questions: 1. Cross-domain outcomes and resources: 

What task outcomes do complete harness con-figurations achieve across different domains, and what model expenditure and latency ac-company them where measured? 2. Task-level comparisons: How do harness configurations compare with reference con-figurations on fixed task subsets, and what improvements, regressions, and unchanged outcomes emerge in paired clinical compar-isons? 3. Execution behavior: What do recorded ex-ecution trajectories, reviewer decisions, and instruction revisions reveal about how the har-ness handles incomplete work, directs correc-tions, and encounters failures? This paper makes three contributions to the de-sign, domain specialization, and empirical evalua-tion of agent execution systems: 1. An agent execution architecture that spec-ifies interfaces linking the original objective, revisable work graph, and artifact record to dependency-aware execution, benchmark-specific submission, and bounded reviewer-directed continuation. 2. A cross-domain empirical evaluation of complete harness configurations across four task domains, combining benchmark-native outcomes, fixed-task comparisons, and avail-able model cost and latency measurements. 3. An analysis of execution behavior using doc-umented instruction revisions, reviewer deci-sions, and task-level successes and failures to examine how the execution protocol operates in practice, including effective corrections and unsuccessful verification. 

2 Method 

The harness coordinates scoped planning, artifact-producing workers, and review against the original objective. Domain-specific instructions specialize these roles within a shared execution protocol. 

2.1 Goal, work graph, and execution record 

A mission consists of the original objective C,a work graph Gt = ( Vt, A t), and an execution record St at round t. The objective consists of the user’s task request and benchmark instructions and serves as the reference for completion. The graph describes proposed work; the record contains task states, worker outputs, and artifact references. Keeping the objective separate from the revisable graph allows subsequent planning and review to return to the original request. The state transition is

An agent runtime is the execution backend, such as Claude Code or Codex, that manages a selected model’s session and tool calls; the model is con-figured separately. The routing policy ρ selects agent runtimes and applicable capabilities for the proposed work. The decision dt requests submis-sion, further work, or stopping. Figure 1 shows the objects and feedback paths. Each graph node specifies a scoped brief, an agent runtime, applicable capabilities, and depen-dencies. Before execution, validation checks re-quired fields, unique identifiers, dependency ref-erences, acyclicity, configured size bounds, and permitted agent runtimes and capabilities. Invalid submissions return actionable errors so the plan-ner can revise the graph. Graph validation governs executable structure, while review assesses task coverage and output quality. Dependencies define execution order: a task be-comes ready when its prerequisites have completed successfully. Failed prerequisites block dependent work. Independent ready tasks may execute concur-rently, and follow-up work extends the graph while 2preserving completed results. Benchmark-specific scheduling is described in Appendix B. 

2.2 Task context and artifact handoff 

Workers receive their task brief, role instructions, and relevant task materials. Results from prerequi-site tasks are passed as summaries and references to files or artifacts in the shared workspace. Work-ers can inspect those artifacts for additional detail, allowing each assignment to use prior results with-out copying complete upstream execution histories into its prompt. The execution record links an assignment to its status, worker output, changed files, and produced artifacts. These links give review a concrete basis for inspecting the work behind a completion claim and for specifying any additional work needed. Follow-up workers can use the same recorded out-puts and artifact references to continue from the existing state. 

2.3 Review, continuation, and submission 

The reviewer receives the original objective and available task results. It assesses the outputs and artifacts against the request, then either submits the result or proposes follow-up tasks with actionable briefs. Follow-up work uses the same graph vali-dation and execution process as the initial assign-ments. A configured limit on continuation rounds controls how much additional work a run can re-quest. Benchmark adapters connect review to each do-main’s submission interface. HealthBench review forks the planner’s session and can make minor inline answer edits or request a follow-up worker; grading uses the finalized response. In Terminal-Bench, review and repairs share the task container, whose final state is graded. Appendix B details context initialization, closed-book access, and exe-cution settings. Official benchmark grading occurs after execu-tion finishes. During review and continuation, the system uses the original request, available outputs, and ordinary tool feedback; official reference an-swers and grader verdicts remain outside this deci-sion loop. 

2.4 Specialization during construction 

Planner, worker, and reviewer skills are reusable textual instructions. A setup agent conducts trial ex-ecutions on a subset of benchmark inputs and exam-ines the resulting outputs, role trajectories, and tool feedback to revise those instructions. For example, revisions can ask the planner to scope a clinical assignment without pre-solving it, or ask review to re-derive relevant facts from the original con-versation. Official reference answers and grading feedback are excluded from this construction pro-cess. Before promotion, we manually review the instruction edits to check that they encode general procedures rather than instance-specific answers or grading information. The finalized instructions are then used for full-benchmark evaluation, covering both inputs en-countered during setup and the remaining bench-mark inputs. Cross-family reuse experiments apply the same instruction content unchanged with dif-ferent role models, without another setup run or model-specific retuning. 

3 Experimental Setup 

3.1 Domains and native outcomes 

We evaluate complete harness configurations with their selected role models across four domains. Terminal-Bench 2.1 contains 89 command-line tasks whose official executable checks evaluate the resulting environment (Merrill et al., 2026; Terminal-Bench Team, 2026a). HealthBench Pro-fessional (HBP) contains 525 clinician-facing con-versations with task-specific physician-authored weighted rubrics (Hicks et al., 2026). ViBench evaluates applications built from product require-ments using human-authored browser plans and reports Plan Pass@1 (ViBench, 2026). DrugDis-coveryBench contains 82 scientific tasks; we report full-credit outcome pass rate under its weighted rubric judge (Akyürek et al., 2026). These benchmarks assess distinct completion targets: an environment state, a multi-criterion clin-ical answer, an application workflow, and a scien-tific result. 

3.2 Evaluation and comparison configurations

Full-benchmark evalua-tions use instructions refined in grade-blind trials and cover construction inputs and remaining tasks. Skill-reuse evaluations use these instructions un-changed with different role models, without an-other setup run. Supplementary slice experiments cover Terminal-Bench 2.1, HBP, and SWE-Bench Pro (Deng et al., 2025). 3.3 Scoring and resource measurement 

Each benchmark uses its native metric. HBP com-putes signed rubric coverage, applies the official response-length adjustment, and clips the aggregate mean to the native score range. Appendix A gives the scoring formula and implementation details. Performance, model-inference cost, and latency 4refer to the same evaluated instances within each configuration. Model-inference costs combine worker and planning/review usage, with model-based grading reported separately (Kapoor et al., 2025, 2026). Latency follows each benchmark’s execution interface: HBP uses mission-event dura-tion, excluding separate grading and provisioning; Terminal-Bench uses Harbor wall time, including preparation, execution, verification, and teardown. We report task-level mean latency. Appendix F pro-vides latency distributions, cost calculation details, and measurement scope. 

4 Results 

4.1 Cross-domain outcomes 

Table 1 summarizes the complete configurations using benchmark-native metrics. Terminal-Bench records 83 passes and six failures (93.26%). The 525-task Fable-configured system achieves a length-adjusted HBP score of 69.63. Terminal and HBP references come from the model release and system cards (Anthropic, 2026b; OpenAI, 2026a,b); Sol Ultra is a multi-agent sys-tem, not a single-worker Sol baseline. ViBench supplies its own references (ViBench, 2026). DrugDiscoveryBench references use mini-SWE-agent (Akyürek et al., 2026; Scale AI, 2026). Pub-lished values provide context for the evaluations rather than a claim about the current benchmark ranking. 

4.2 Slice comparisons and paired outcomes 

Table 2 compares baseline execution with Fable-and Opus-configured harnesses on fixed bench-mark subsets, including the previous-generation Opus 4.8 configuration. These fixed-task com-parisons assess whether the harness adds value over reference execution and whether a previous-generation worker can match or exceed the newer-generation reference. The terminal slice records complete task coverage across the reference and both harness configurations: each passes all nine tasks. In the earlier reported SWE-Bench Pro slice, the Fable-configured harness achieves 82.4% versus the Fable reference’s 81.1%, while the Opus-configured harness matches that reference at 81.1%. Table 3 reports aggregate scores and paired task-level changes on the same 53 tasks. The Fable-configured mean increases by 19.89 points, while the median paired change is −1.46 . The five tasks with the largest gains account for 12.83 points of the mean difference. The Opus-configured mean decreases by 1.06 points, with a median paired change of −0.05 . Reporting both mean and median makes the concentration of gains visible alongside the typical task-level change. To distinguish rubric satisfaction from the ef-fect of response length, we compare each harness answer with the direct answer on the same task, before and after the native length adjustment. Be-fore adjustment, the Fable-configured harness earns more rubric points on 17 tasks, fewer on four, and the same on 32; the Opus-configured harness earns more on nine, fewer on nine, and the same on 35. After adjustment, the Fable-configured har-ness scores higher on 20 tasks and lower on 33; the Opus-configured harness scores higher on 24 and lower on 29. The length adjustment can there-fore change the numerical ordering of answers even when their rubric scores are equal. 

4.3 Direct skill reuse 

The domain instruction content was reused un-changed with other role-model families, without another setup run. Kimi K3 achieves 61.55 on HBP. On ViBench, GLM-5.2 achieves 71.62%; the publicly reported GLM-family score available at the time of evaluation was 66.2% for GLM-5.1 (ViBench, 2026). The same instructions ran with Kimi K3 and GLM-5.2 without retuning.

5.3 Review-guided verification and repair 

Terminal-Bench trajectories illustrate how re-view identifies errors and directs focused repairs. In gcode-to-text , the worker reconstructs em-bossed text from a printer toolpath but writes an uppercase Z where the rendered glyph is lower-case. The reviewer independently rasterizes the original G-code and inspects the disputed charac-ter. It requests a focused correction that preserves the remaining text and output format. The follow-up worker changes the casing and checks the final file’s contents and bytes; the official verifier returns a pass. In query-optimize , review finds that two columns are text rather than integers despite match-ing printable query results. A follow-up worker adds the required casts, preserving all 500 result rows; the final artifact also passes. Appendix D.1 describes the reviewer checks and follow-up repairs for both tasks. Other domains illustrate task-specific verifi-cation. In ViBench Notes, a verification task exercises unlock, edit, cold reload, and re-authentication; the harness artifact hides note data after reload, unlike the reference artifact. DrugDis-coveryBench checks distinguish an intact epoxide from a reacted covalent adduct and the requested neutral formula from a bound protonation state. Appendix E preserves these illustrations. This task-dependent execution pattern is also vis-ible at the mission level: ViBench uses one worker node in each of its 24 application missions, while DrugDiscoveryBench’s 13 two-task missions add a second node only after the first terminates. The scientific follow-ups therefore represent sequential correction or recovery rather than parallel decom-position (Appendix B). The Windows virtual-machine episode provides a contrasting regression. Keyboard and reboot tests disturb a working desktop, prompting a restoration task. The repair worker restores reference frame-buffer pixels but reports that the guest interface re-mains wedged, although final official checks pass. This contrast motivates verifying the requested functionality, not only a visual match. Useful review converts a discrepancy into a bounded repair; narrow checks can miss functional problems, while disruptive checks can damage a working artifact, consistent with prior analyses of agent failures and self-correction (Cemri et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024). Execution interfaces and coordination. Modu-lar model/tool architectures and reasoning–action loops established agent interfaces (Karpas et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023); ReWOO separates plan-ning from observations (Xu et al., 2023). SWE-agent, OpenHands, and Agentless show the impor-tance of execution interfaces and structured repair (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a; Xia et al., 2025). Our proto-col connects an executable work graph and artifact record to review against the original objective, with scoped continuation and benchmark-specific sub-mission. HiAgent and StateM study context man-agement; scaling analyses and Murakkab examine coordination costs (Hu et al., 2025a; Qin et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025; Chaudhry et al., 2026).

Review and empirical evaluation. Self-Refine and Reflexion use model feedback; self-correction can fail without useful feedback (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024). MAST analyzes multi-agent failures (Cemri et al., 2025). Our comparisons and execution cases exam-ine both corrections and regressions. SWE-bench, Terminal-Bench, and tool–agent– user benchmarks assess operational outcomes; HBP and MDIA address clinical response eval-uation and multi-agent reasoning (Jimenez et al., 2024; Merrill et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2025; Hicks et al., 2026; Cruz and Rey-Blanco, 2026). Agent-Board, AI Agents That Matter, HAL, reliability studies, and log analysis motivate finer-grained ev-idence and resource reporting (Ma et al., 2024; Kapoor et al., 2025, 2026; Rabanser et al., 2026; Kirgis et al., 2026). Our cross-domain evaluation examines these execution interfaces through task outcomes, reviewer interventions, and resource measurements. 

7 Conclusion 

An agent’s final submission depends on more than whether its workers finish their assignments. We present an execution harness that preserves the orig-inal objective throughout planning, execution, and review. Scoped assignments, artifact records, and bounded follow-up work make explicit what was attempted, produced, and assessed for submission. Reusable domain-specific instructions specialize this protocol using execution trajectories without official grading feedback. Our evaluation across terminal tasks, clinical responses, application building, and scientific ques-tions reveals capabilities and limitations. Paired clinical comparisons show improvements along-side regressions and persistent failures, while coor-dination adds cost and latency. Execution records clarify these outcomes: in a G-code decoding task, review caught a letter-casing error and directed its correction; in a virtual-machine task, additional verification disrupted a working environment. Doc-umented instruction revisions illustrate how execu-tion procedures can be adapted. These findings do not imply that additional agents or review necessarily improve outcomes. Rather, they highlight submission and continuation as explicit decisions grounded in the original ob-jective and available evidence. Evaluating these decisions alongside task outcomes and resource costs shows what the execution architecture accom-plishes, where it falls short, and at what cost. 

Limitations 

System-level evaluation. Our experiments eval-uate complete harness configurations, combining 8planning, execution, review, and domain-specific instructions. Isolating the contribution of each component or additional inference would require controlled ablations. The HBP and terminal eval-uations primarily exercise planning, scoped exe-cution, and review with one worker; workloads with multiple independent subtasks would provide a broader assessment of parallel coordination. Pub-lished provider results serve as contextual refer-ences for systems evaluated under their respective configurations. 

Exposure and repeatability. Grade-blind con-struction and full evaluation can share task inputs. The evaluation therefore characterizes specialized configurations on the full benchmark population. Single evaluations and retrospective case selection do not establish repeated-run reliability or phe-nomenon prevalence (Rabanser et al., 2026). 

Scoring and resource scope. Benchmark scores characterize performance under the evaluated task and grading protocols. HBP measures rubric-based response quality. Cost estimates cover worker and planning/review model usage, with grading reported separately; setup and infrastructure are excluded from these estimates. Latency follows benchmark-specific execution boundaries. Review can overlook requirements or introduce regressions, making task-aligned acceptance checks an impor-tant part of the execution policy. 

Deployment and artifact sharing. Deployment beyond benchmarks calls for task-specific valida-tion and domain-expert oversight in high-stakes clinical or scientific settings, together with ap-propriate safeguards for shared-workspace edits. Sharing research artifacts—including adapters, do-main instructions, prompts, and execution records— should follow applicable benchmark licenses and protect sensitive information through appropriate anonymization and redaction.
