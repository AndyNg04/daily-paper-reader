## Abstract 

A feed ranker scores candidates with a value model , a weighted sum of engagement predictions. Each prediction conflates a baseline ,what this viewer, item or request would produce anyway, with the incremental amount by which this candidate exceeds it. The literature mostly removes the baseline. We argue that a multi-signal value model should keep the raw prediction and add the incremen-tal part as a separately weighted term. On Instagram Feed, three debiasing families deployed independently, for viewer habit, item popularity and request context, are one parameterised operator, and a design procedure picks a point in it. Media-type (format) bias goes instead to a debiased label chosen by the same procedure. In a controlled four-arm ablation of deployed Feed terms (neither term, each alone, both), the two-term arm beats both single-term arms at production weights on point estimates in 11 of 13 judged (term, metric) pairs. Of the 26 direct contrasts, 17 are significantly for it and only 1 against it, on impressions. The ablation also suggests that the offline diagnostic should screen signals, not rank them. Such terms have served Instagram Feed production for most of a year. Beyond Feed, Meta, whose apps serve billions of people, has adopted the operator across surfaces, apps and both ranking stages. Its launches there drive sessions and daily active users, the core visitation and retention metrics.

## 2 Related Work 

Removing the baseline. Most debiasing work treats the baseline as a confounder to remove. Inverse propensity weighting corrects selection bias in recommender feedback [33] and examination bias in learning-to-rank [1, 3, 16]. Logit adjustment subtracts a scaled log class prior [26], and causal methods deconfound popularity and related biases [8]. Industrial rankers remove the baseline at serving time. PAL and YouTube’s shallow tower drop their learned position part [14, 42], LAFB divides each rating prediction by its mean given viewer–item familiarity [31], and AlignPxtr replaces each by its bias-conditional deviation or quantile [24]. Label-side methods, DVR and RAD, instead train on duration- or cohort-relative watch-time targets [25, 43]. 

Keeping part of it. PDA multiplies a forecast popularity factor back in at inference [40], TIDE keeps a benign quality compo-nent [41], and DICE sums interest and conformity scores [44]. Oth-ers keep the raw score beside a weighted second term. MACR subtracts a scaled learned bias [35], PPAC adds weighted popularity terms [28], and DecRS and CD2AN blend biased and debiased scores convexly [9, 34]. Collaborative filtering has long carried additive viewer and item baselines [17, 18]. In each, the kept term is a device of the particular model. None gives a general account of why a raw term should stay beside an incremental one, or explores the design space of §3 (Table 1). DecRS and PPAC compare the combined score with its parts offline [28, 34], CD2AN deployed its two endpoints separately online [9], and the four arms here are tested on live traf-fic. Closest are MBD [23] and, on YouTube Shorts, FLVM [7]. MBD learns cohort baselines in a ranking-model branch and can add a hinge on the standardised gap to the VM score. FLVM learns a base-line branch over duration, viewer propensity and session context; the two paths are complementary offline, but only the latent score is served. Both are model-based and adopted by training a ranking model. The scoring-time terms here are instead VM features that go to test as configuration changes, at essentially no capacity cost.

## 3.1 Why two terms and three baselines 

How a baseline acts. An item baseline varies across a request’s candidates and reorders them on the signal itself. A viewer or request baseline is constant within a request, so Δ keeps the order of 𝑝 among the candidates it applies to. It acts by reweighting the signal against the VM’s other signals, request by request, and by crediting candidates above 𝑏 more than those below. 

Why the raw term stays. The comparison inside Δ ignores the common level of 𝑝 and 𝑏 : 𝜎 is bounded and reads only where a can-didate sits relative to 𝑏 . The level of 𝑝 thus reaches Δ only through 

𝑚 , and not at all at 𝑚 = 𝑒 , which sees only the gap 𝑝 − 𝑏 . Ranking on Δ alone can therefore fail on each axis. A heavy liker’s genuine like, modestly above a high baseline, earns only part of its weight against the viewer’s other signals. A fluke watch on a short clip outranks a good long video that merely meets its duration base-line. The best candidates of a weak and a strong request are gated alike. The raw term is where the level enters unconditionally. With two independent weights, the tuner can sit anywhere between the purely raw and purely incremental endpoints rather than commit to one. This is the a-priori argument §6 tests. 

Why three baselines. The viewer and item baselines are historical per-viewer and per-item averages. The request baseline is read from the live candidate pool of one (viewer, session), so it tracks what that pool contains. A viewer’s gap between connected and unconnected content, for instance, is invisible to both averages. Yet in a connected-heavy request it raises the level of a batch that pools both kinds, against which an unconnected candidate is read. The request axis carries two effects. Batch-strength bias arises 

across requests: the same score is exceptional in a weak request and ordinary in a strong one. Composition bias arises within a request, from its mix of candidates, as in the connected example. A statistic of the request’s own predictions tracks both. Targeting different quantities, the three terms are additive and coexist on one signal. They overlap rather than conflict, and joint tuning resolves it. Chen et al.

## 4.1 Step 1 (viewer, item): is the split needed? 

The quantile Pearson 𝑟 correlates the decile-bucket indices of a signal’s prediction and its baseline feature. This rank statistic is needed because some baseline rate features carry non-physical values above 1 .0. Raw Pearson is not interchangeable, as our bands (Algorithm 1) are calibrated on the rank form. A high 𝑟 means the prediction tracks the baseline rather than anything candidate-specific. A variance ratio, Var% = Var (𝑏 )/ Var (𝑦 ) for 𝑦 the label or the prediction, measures the dependence’s magnitude , 𝑟 its shape .Their agreement makes a reading reliable. Both are computed on one week of candidate-level ranking logs, a 1% block-level sample. Quantile correlations move by about ±0.01 between runs. Var% needs proxy and target to share construct and units. This rules it out for implicit signals labelled on a different surface from the feature and for binary labels against a feature in seconds. We report it on the viewer axis only: on the item axis three signals exceed 100%, a precondition violation, not a property of the signals. 

The base rate shapes the reading. Frequent actions read highest: like and comment tap, the commonest, lead Table 2, and follow, the rarest, reads lowest. A frequent action becomes a reflex, which is what a viewer baseline measures; a rare one leaves too little history to habituate. Part of the pattern is also mechanical: a rare action leaves the viewer feature mostly at zero, which caps how high a rank-based 𝑟 can go. External share reads low although it is not the rarest action, consistent with its higher friction and a sparse viewer feature (89% zero). The base rate therefore also decides whether the baseline is resolvable. Every logit-scale term clamps its baseline away from 0, and on a sparse feature the clamp binds for most impressions, where the feature says little about the viewer. The share of impressions where it binds, the zero column of Table 2, exceeds 60% for three of nine viewer signals. A weak 𝑟 on such a signal is unresolved , not weak .

## 4.2 Step 2 (viewer, item): estimating the baseline 

On the viewer axis, implicit signals have no rate of their own and share the viewer’s feed time spent as a proxy. On the item axis, a popularity rate needs a minimum-impression guard and is partly a product of the ranker. It is thus a prior, not a causal quantity: one more reason the raw term stays. A media-type baseline comes from the item’s format-and-size bucket (video duration, carousel card count, photo OCR word count). Estimated across items, it is dense where a popularity rate is sparse, which helps §4.4 route it through the label. A historical rate needs an aggregation window, trading noise against staleness. A logit-scale term needs a clamp, setting its behaviour where the feature is empty. Base rate and zero share narrow both, and the final choice is made online.

## 4.4 Step 4 (item): VM term or debiased label? 

Instead of a VM term, a baseline can be removed from the label: a candidate is positive only if it out-performs its own bucket on the bias dimension. A VM term is a computationally cheap, tunable, reversible configuration change but sees only the score and one baseline feature. A debiased label adds a new predictor and its serving cost, but the model learns the correction and trains on the debiased target. The choice arises only on the item axis and follows the bias present (Table 3). Popularity bias gets a VM term: its baseline is one item-level statistic of the same signal, so a post-hoc gap captures it. The label is undistorted, and for a binary signal a debiased label is ill-defined per impression. Media-type bias gets a label: the distortion is in the label, and a high-cardinality non-linear correction in the VM would entangle with every other term. On a continuous signal, “out-performs its own size class” is well defined. The bucketised labels (§2) take this branch, the model-side exception to the scoring-time terms. ## 4.5 Step 5 (all axes): cost, and what to ship 

Viewer and item baselines are precomputed features with no scoring cost. A request term pays per request, 𝑂 (𝑁 ) in the pool size for a mean and 𝑂 (𝑁 log 𝑁 ) for a percentile, computationally cheap for one term but not for every signal. No scoring-time term adds model inference: each reads predictions and features the ranker already computes. Measured latency costs, app-start latency included, are a few milliseconds at most. The procedure ships one point after one test (Algorithm 1, line 8). One arm suffices because the raw term is not in question at ship time. Other points are swept only for signals worth the traffic. The four-arm design of §6 is a separate, retrospective study of whether the two-term form was right at all. No launch answers that question, because none runs the arm without the signal. 

## 5 Deployment 

The baseline–incremental decomposition has been shipping to Instagram Feed production for most of a year; the six studied terms launched over many months and are all live. Table 4 gives each term’s place in the design space of Eq. 3 and its approval metric. Each term serves all Feed traffic in its ranking stage. All three axes appear, with gates, hinges and debiased labels. The two feed time-spent gates read the same prediction against the same item baseline but differ in magnitude as well as in their VM; they are two related points. Table 5 shows that they behave differently. The launch bar is not uniform: a term is approved on the met-ric it was built to move, and the protocol of §6 relies on this. Three of the six (two within a bundle) cleared a significant, repli-cated foreground-session gain. The see-more term, which predicts whether the viewer taps “See more” to ask for more posts like a recommended one, shipped on +0.63% unconnected post inter-ested ratio at the cost of −0.118% feed time spent. The MainRank gate left sessions flat. It was approved on the rebalancing it pro-duced: +0.665% impressions and +2.21% impressions outside the video viewer, as photos, carousels and shorter content gained posi-tion. ## 6 The Ablation 

Each term is ablated in a four-arm online experiment on live traffic. The evaluation is online because a VM term’s effect depends on the other terms and on how users respond, which offline replay cannot measure. Users are randomised, each experiment in its own universe (two share one, with disjoint arms), and each arm gets 3% of traffic. Each delta is computed by one method on one seven-day period, relative to none, with 95% intervals. It was the only week all six experiments were live and ramped; each ran twelve to sev-enteen days. Each experiment crosses the two weights (𝑤 r, 𝑤 i) of Eq. 2, with prod the production weight: none (0, 0), raw (prod , 0),incremental (0, prod ) and both (prod , prod ). none, the common origin, is the control for every reported delta. both is production for every reported term except that raw was production for extShare 

· viewer during the measurement window. Its incremental term launched the day after the window closed. As comparators, raw is the status quo without debiasing, incremental the removal de-sign common in the literature (§2), and both ours. both − raw adds the incremental term at its production weight to a VM that already carries the raw term, and both − incremental the con-verse. Neither single-term arm was re-tuned. This favours both over incremental, whose weight was set beside the raw term. The design thus tests whether either term is redundant at the deployed point, not whether a re-tuned single-term VM could match it. Six experiments were run, one for each term live in production and designed through the procedure of §4. 

Evaluation protocol. Judging terms on a full metric panel invites false positives. The six experiments give 270 cells, and 95% intervals alone would flag about fourteen of them by chance. We therefore fixed, per term, the metrics it would be judged on: the criteria it would have been launched on, assigned as in Table 5. No significant cell outside that assignment is used as evidence. For five of the six terms the primary metric is foreground sessions. It is the best available proxy for daily actives and, because a session is a separate act of returning, the hardest to inflate by re-ordering. At Instagram’s scale, even a small relative gain in sessions is practically meaningful. Each term is also judged on its mechanism metric, the behaviour it is built to change. A term whose mechanism metric does not move has not done its job. The two feed time-spent gates are judged on feed time spent and impressions, since shifting attention across candidates of different length can cash out as time spent or as impressions. The sixth term, seeMore · req, is a content-quality signal whose launch criterion was the unconnected post interested ratio. It is judged on that alone, hence 13 pairs rather than fourteen. Both of its session contrasts are negative and not significant. 

Joint dominance. A (term, metric) pair is jointly dominant when the point estimates satisfy both > raw and both > incremental. Each is read from the direct arm-to-arm contrast, never from a difference of two deltas against none. We use point estimates be-cause sessions’ half-width is about ±0.020% against direct session contrasts of at most about 0 .05%, so requiring significance on both contrasts would set aside most session-judged pairs. We report sig-nificance throughout, counting significantly positive and negative contrasts separately, so a stricter reader can re-judge. Results. Of the 13 (term, metric) pairs, 11 are jointly dominant (Table 5). Each of the 6 terms is jointly dominant on at least one of its metrics, and 4 of 6 on all of them. Of the 26 direct contrasts, 17 are significantly positive and 1 is significantly negative. The positive side includes +21 .05% on external shares and +0.359% on feed time spent, against arms that already carry one of the two terms at production weight. Power differs by metric family. On the mechanism metrics, 12 of 16 contrasts are significantly positive. On sessions, 5 of 10 are, and 4 of the 5 session-judged terms are jointly dominant on sessions. Session verdicts therefore rest more often on non-significant contrasts. raw and incremental are restrictions of both, each with one weight set to zero, so both’s reachable set contains them by con-struction. The ablation tests whether the extra degree of freedom is used , that is, whether the interior point, with both weights non-zero, beats both boundary points, each with one weight zero. On 11 of 13 pairs it does: at the deployed operating point neither term is redundant. The 6 terms are a case series, not a sample, so we draw no cross-term rank correlations. Every load-bearing claim sits within one term. 

Why not simply raise the raw weight? The objection is that both puts more total weight on the signal than raw, so a re-tuned 𝑤 r

alone might reach the same place. The ablation has four points, not a weight sweep. Theorem 3.3 addresses the formal half. Whether the production point is good rests on how the VM is maintained. Its weights are the standing output of a continuous tuning loop. Each term in Table 4 passed launch review against the full metric panel and its guardrails, and has since been re-examined by boost and ablation experiments. Raising one weight is that loop’s simplest and most frequent experiment. raw at production weight is therefore close to the best the single-term form has reached under the same bar. A lift of 𝑤 r alone that bought what both buys would likely already have shipped. The argument is local : it says the produc-tion point is hard to improve by moving one weight, not that it is globally best. It holds more weakly for 𝑤 i, new on most of these signals and far less tuned, so if anything the ablation understates the two-term form. Before the four-arm design, earlier viewer-axis experiments ran the sweep this objection asks for: one signal at a time, each debias experiment paired with a matched weight-only one. The debiased arm consistently converted to sessions where the weight-only arm did not.

## 7 Lessons from Deployment 

L1: the procedure’s first choices held. Where Table 5 can check the procedure’s rules, their first choices held. The shape rule of §4.3 puts a p75 promotion hinge on p10s · req, a near-symmetric signal, and the term is jointly dominant on both its metrics. The label rule of §4.4 sends the media-type-biased time-spent family to a bucketised label. bucketTS-p75 · item is jointly dominant on both its metrics, with every feed time-spent arm and contrast significant. Offline, the video label retains almost no duration correlation ( 𝑟 ≈ − 0.017, against +0.216 for the raw log-time prediction; Table 3). The photo and carousel labels are bucketed on dimensions we did not audit, so this check covers one label of three. seeMore · req uses a batch mean 

where the shape rule prescribes ∼p75. It is jointly dominant on its mechanism metric and converts to nothing on sessions. On the request axis, the one recipe off the rule is the one mechanism-only result. 

L2: the VM saturates around a term. A term’s value depends on the VM around it, which keeps moving. logTS-TR · item launched on a replicated +0.066% session win, but in this window its both − raw on sessions is +0.009% (n.s.), its session effects are flat on every arm and its impression effects nearly so. On feed time spent the incremental term is still strong ( +0.200% ∗; both +0.216% ∗; both −

raw +0.218% ∗) and the raw term is at zero ( −0.002%, n.s.). The mechanism persists, but its conversion into sessions did not survive eight weeks of VM retuning since launch. A sessions-only read would have called the term useless. The viewer terms on like, re-share recipient and comment, all reading above 0 .45, have never produced a stable win, which we attribute, without direct evidence, to weights already tuned against the raw signal. None is in this abla-tion. Offline baseline is not scarce on Feed; unsaturated baseline is. 

L3: screen with the offline diagnostic, do not rank with it. As triage, the diagnostic did its job: it would not have screened out any tested viewer- or item-axis term in Table 5 (filled markers in Figure 3). One of them read ninth of ten on its axis. Offline queries are com-putationally cheap and experiment slots scarce, so the offline stage should be a high-recall filter , not a ranking. It must not discard a sig-nal that would have worked, and may pass more than will succeed. 

L4: a reading needs resolvability and headroom. The diagnostic’s two limits, which we anticipated before the ablation and which it con-firmed, should be checked in advance as preconditions alongside the correlation. They change a signal’s priority without discarding it. A sparse baseline feature gives an unresolved reading rather than a genuinely low one (§4.1). The one viewer-axis term in Table 5, significant on all four of its contrasts, is such a signal. Where its rate feature is zero the clamp binds, and the gate becomes a fixed function of 𝑝 : extra raw weight. Above the clamp it still compares each candidate with the viewer’s own baseline. The clamp level sets the share of impressions in each regime, a lever between raw weight and debiasing; both components remain. And a high reading says a baseline component exists, not that the VM still has room to exploit it, as saturation shows. Nor does the diagnostic see a term’s form or the surrounding weights: the two feed time-spent gates share one reading ( 𝑟 = 0.400) yet differ in magnitude and VM (Table 4) and behave differently in MainRank and TopRank. Coverage is thin (Fig-ure 3) and not concentrated where readings are strongest. Further terms on the untested high-reading viewer signals and the item axis are being tested. The strongest item-axis reading, profile tap, is producing promising early results in an experiment still being iterated on. Until these read out, the quantitative offline-to-online relationship is open. A study of 6 terms cannot pin it down. 

L5: judge time spent where the ranker acts. p10s · req moves overall time spent several times less than feed time spent. bucketTS-p75 ·

item’s incremental term is significant on feed time spent but not on overall time spent. Overall time spent aggregates surfaces the Feed ranker does not control, diluting real effects, so every time-spent judgement here uses feed time spent.

## 9 Conclusion 

The Instagram Feed deployments argue for separating, not remov-ing, at least at the deployed operating point: keep the raw prediction, weight the incremental part separately, and let the tuner choose. The three debiasing families are one operator with four degrees of freedom, with published methods along its axes. Its baselines coexist because two are historical averages and one is read from the live candidate pool. In a four-arm ablation of 6 deployed terms, at production weights, on point estimates, neither term is redundant in 11 of 13 judged pairs (7 of 13 with both contrasts significant). The transferable lesson: a computationally cheap offline diagnos-tic should screen signals, not rank them.
