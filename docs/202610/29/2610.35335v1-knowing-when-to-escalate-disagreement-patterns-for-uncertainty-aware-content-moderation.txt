## Abstract 

Agentic content-moderation systems increasingly rely on collective judgments from LLM agents, while aggregation methods commonly treat inter-agent disagreement as a reliability problem to be re-moved through consensus or voting. This framing is inappropriate for value-laden tasks, where disagreement may reflect irreducible value pluralism - a distinction standard uncertainty decompositions cannot recover, since they operate on output distributions and dis-card the reasoning behind each judgment. We propose Reasoning Trace Divergence Analysis (RTDA), which embeds model-generated justifications from perspective-differentiated agents and classifies each case by justification similarity and conclusion agreement. The resulting four-state taxonomy isolates convergent disagreement, where agents reasoning similarly but concluding differently, as a candidate signature of value-laden conflict. On 1,000 items from the Measuring Hate Speech corpus, this structural signal predicts human annotator disagreement better than magnitude-only and output-only baselines. We contribute a design principle: condition human escalation on the structure of disagreement, not its mag-nitude alone, with RTDA serving as a post-processing layer for systems that already generate reasoning traces.

## 2 Related Work 

Multi-agent debate can improve factuality and reasoning [4], but recent work reveals systematic limitations. When agents share training data, debate converges toward shared misconceptions [5] and heterogeneous debate can degrade performance through syco-phantic agreement that worsens with longer exchanges [35]. Recent domain-specific systems likewise use structured agent differenti-ation [25] and identity-grounded ethical tutoring [17] illustrate the broader use of role-conditioned panels in complex reasoning tasks. LLM groups reproduce human collective-reasoning failures, reaching premature consensus within two rounds [15]. These find-ings suggest the absence of disagreement may itself be a failure mode, motivating analysis of disagreement structure rather than its suppression. The data perspectivism movement challenges the assumption that annotation disagreement reflects error, distinguishing the mere collection of disaggregated labels from their preservation through-out the pipeline [2] and showing that disagreement carries different meaning under descriptive versus prescriptive annotation [20]. Sur-veys of learning-from-disagreement methods find that preserving disagreement generally matches or improves on gold-standard train-ing [14, 27] and annotator composition alone can alter a non-trivial fraction of outcomes [8]. We extend this tradition to multi-agent systems, treating agent disagreement as an analog of annotator disagreement. The standard information-theoretic decomposition splits predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic compo-nents [10], but this binary is increasingly contested: it violates intuitive axioms [34], the two estimates correlate strongly in prac-tice [18], and richer taxonomies have been proposed [23]. Content moderation involves genuine normative tensions rather than factual uncertainty alone [7, 9]. Safety judgments are socio-culturally situated with substantial disagreement between expert labels and diverse crowd judgments [1], standard RLHF narrows this distributional pluralism [24] while divergent community-model outputs can serve as a direct pluralism signal [6]. We operationalize these insights more narrowly: disagreement structure is evaluated as a predictor of when pluralistic human review may be useful, with-out assuming that agent disagreement and human value pluralism are the same construct. 

## 3 Theoretical Framework 

We frame multi-agent moderation as judgment under uncertainty and motivate a structural routing hypothesis. For the analysis that follows, we operationalize reasoning traces as the textual justifica-tions generated by agents alongside their moderation decisions.

We classify each content item into categories based on the joint distribution of A𝜏 𝑎 (𝑥 ) and S( 𝑥 ) relative to thresholds 𝜏 𝑎 and 𝜏 𝑠 :CA (𝑥 ) : S( 𝑥 ) ≥ 𝜏 𝑠 ∧ A 𝜏 𝑎 (𝑥 ) = 1 (Convergent Agreement) DA (𝑥 ) : S( 𝑥 ) < 𝜏 𝑠 ∧ A 𝜏 𝑎 (𝑥 ) = 1 (Divergent Agreement) CD (𝑥 ) : S( 𝑥 ) ≥ 𝜏 𝑠 ∧ A 𝜏 𝑎 (𝑥 ) = 0 (Convergent Disagreement) DD (𝑥 ) : S( 𝑥 ) < 𝜏 𝑠 ∧ A 𝜏 𝑎 (𝑥 ) = 0 (Divergent Disagreement) Each category maps to a distinct uncertainty interpretation: 

Convergent Agreement (high similarity, same conclusion): Agents reason about the content in compatible ways and reach the same judgment. Standard ensemble uncertainty is low. These cases are candidates for automated decisions. 

Divergent Agreement (low similarity, same conclusion): Agents reason differently but converge on the same judgment through inde-pendent paths. This pattern is consistent with conclusion stability across prompted perspectives, analogous to “diverse ensembles” in the wisdom-of-crowds literature, but does not by itself establish robustness to other models or policies [19, 22]. 

Convergent Disagreement (high similarity, different conclu-sions): Agents share a compatible understanding of the content but reach different judgments. This is the signature of irreducible value pluralism. The agents agree on what the content says but Knowing When to Escalate: Disagreement Patterns for Uncertainty-Aware Content Moderation CIKM ’26, November 07–11, 2026, Rome, Italy 

disagree on how it should be moderated. In the uncertainty decom-position framework, this corresponds to uncertainty that cannot be resolved by improving the model, only by specifying which values to prioritize. This is exactly where human judgment is needed. 

Divergent Disagreement (low similarity, different conclusions): Agents interpret the content differently and reach different judg-ments. This pattern may reflect epistemic uncertainty (addressable through better context or models), content ambiguity, or noise from inconsistent interpretation. 

## 3.3 Scope of Agent Diversity 

Our agents are homogeneous at the model-backend level and dif-ferentiated through perspective prompts. All agents within a panel share the same learned knowledge and architecture, while their sys-tem prompts emphasize different moderation considerations. This design provides controlled perspective variation but may retain shared blind spots and conformity tendencies [3, 33]. We therefore interpret disagreement operationally, as evidence that a decision is sensitive to the prompted perspective, rather than as proof of human-like value pluralism. Such sensitivity may nev-ertheless be useful for escalation because it identifies cases whose outcomes vary across relevant moderation viewpoints. The cross-model ablation tests whether this routing pattern recurs across separate models. 

## 4 Approach and Methods 

This section presents the taxonomy as a pipeline: perspective dif-ferentiated deliberation, reasoning-trace embedding and four-state classification. 

## 4.1 Perspective-Differentiated Deliberation 

We instantiate five agents from the same base model, differen-tiated by system prompts encoding distinct moderator perspec-tives: harm-focused (protection from psychological harm and harassment), context-sensitive (cultural and situational interpre-tation), community-norms (platform-specific behavioral stan-dards), free-expression (speech protection against restriction), and legal-framework (jurisdictional standards and precedent). This design creates controlled, prompt-level perspective vari-ation while holding the backend constant. We therefore do not call the agents model-heterogeneous. Agents independently gen-erate moderation decisions and structured justifications through chain-of-thought prompting [32], articulating: (1) content interpre-tation, (2) relevant considerations, (3) value trade-offs, and (4) final judgment with confidence.

## 5.4 Qualitative Examination 

To complement the aggregate metrics, we examine one CD item as an illustrative case: “These people have completely lost touch with reality and are destroying everything we built.” The agents identi-fied overlapping features: a group reference, degrading language, and political context - but weighted them differently. The harm-focused and community-norms agents recommended removal, whereas the free-expression and legal-framework agents recommended keeping the item; normalized human disagreement was ¯𝑑 ℎ = 0.82. Thus, the conclusion divergence did not arise from wholly unrelated generated justifications. The case nevertheless supports more than one interpretation. The different decisions are consistent with competing priorities between protection from group-directed harm and preservation of political expression. At the same time, the underspecified refer-ent and missing conversational context could have contributed to the disagreement, while generated justifications may rationalize decisions post hoc. RTDA cannot determine which explanation is correct. Its role is therefore to surface a complex case for human review while preserving information about how agents framed the decision, rather than diagnosing the disagreement as irreducible value conflict. 

## 6 Limitations 

Our evidence is intentionally limited to predictive validity. First, generated justifications may be post-hoc, prompt-sensitive rational-izations. Semantic similarity measures their surface-level content and not the models latent decision processes. Second, high anno-tator disagreement is an imperfect target. It can reflect compet-ing values, but also unclear items, missing conversational context, inconsistent labels, or annotator noise. RTDA therefore cannot determine why humans disagree. Third, the evaluation uses a stratified sample of 1,000 English items from one 2022 hate-speech corpus. The sample does not establish robustness across moderation policies, languages, cul-tures, or domains. Because the corpus is public and predates the evaluated models, pretraining exposure cannot be excluded. The results should not be interpreted as a clean test of uncontaminated zero-shot generalization. Fourth, agents within each panel share a backend and differ through prompts, so their diversity is controlled but not architectural or knowledge-base heterogeneity. Finally, rout-ing quality is evaluated against an annotator-disagreement proxy rather than through a study with professional moderators, and run-to-run stochastic uncertainty and end-to-end operational cost require further measurement. These limitations motivate cross-corpus replication, repeated-run analysis and an actual escalation study before deployment claims are warranted. 

## 7 Conclusion and Future Work 

The central contribution of this work is a four-state taxonomy over justification similarity and conclusion agreement, operationalized through RTDA as a post-processing method for multi-agent moder-ation. On the test set, this structural representation identifies high annotator-disagreement items more effectively than magnitude-only and output-only uncertainty baselines. The composition abla-tion indicates that this advantage depends on prompt-level perspec-tive differentiation rather than merely sampling multiple outputs, while the same qualitative ordering across separate LLMs suggests that the result is not specific to one backend. By distinguishing how agents disagree rather than only how much, RTDA turns disagree-ment into an actionable signal for allocating limited human review. This supports our practical design principle that escalation should be conditioned on the structure of inter-agent disagreement, not its magnitude alone. Future work should first test external validity across additional moderation corpora, languages, cultural settings and platform poli-cies, preferably including data unlikely to have appeared in model pretraining. A human-in-the-loop study should then compare RTDA-assisted escalation with confidence and vote-based routing, mea-suring decision quality, consistency, reviewer workload, and review time. Repeated stochastic runs and panels containing genuinely different model backends would help establish the stability of the taxonomy, while alternative rationale formats, embedding models, and similarity measures would test whether the signal depends on the current implementation. Finally, learning platform-specific thresholds or a complete state-to-action policy from observed esca-lation outcomes could adapt RTDA to different review budgets and risk tolerances.
