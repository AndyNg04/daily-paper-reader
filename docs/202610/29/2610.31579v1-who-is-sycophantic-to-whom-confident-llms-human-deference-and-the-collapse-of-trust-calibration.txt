# Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference, and the Collapse of Trust Calibration

Abstract 

Research on sycophancy asks whether large language models (LLMs) bend towards what their users want to hear. We ask the reverse question: when an LLM is confident, consistent, and wrong, do users bend towards it? In a within-subjects experiment, 32 participants learned up to five science topics by chatting with five assistants built on the same model (GPT-5-mini). Unknown to participants, each assistant’s briefing had 0, 25, 50, 75, or 100% of its facts replaced by plausible falsehoods, and every assistant spoke with full confidence. A surprise quiz after each topic offered the correct fact, the planted falsehood, and two distractors, with a confidence rating for every answer. Across 104 quizzes and 1,040 answers, scores fell from 7.4 to 2.9 out of 10, and the planted option was chosen on 60% of falsified items against 11% of correctly taught ones (odds ratio 12.5). Transcript coding showed that whenever the assistant stated a falsehood, it was adopted about 65% of the time at every falsification level. Absorbed falsehoods were held with high confidence (8.4/10), the modelled confidence advantage of right over wrong answers vanished, and the discrimination of confidence for correctness fell from an AUROC of 0.70 to chance, while post-task trust ratings barely moved. Participants tended to return to the chat more when the assistant was worse, but returning went with more absorption, not less, and 30% of all turns pasted quiz questions back to the source being checked. The assistant itself was almost never sycophantic and never conceded under pushback. We introduce a baseline-corrected Human Deference Index to quantify this human-side sycophancy and argue that trustworthiness is a property of the human–AI pair, that sycophancy evaluations should measure both directions, and that learning interfaces must make reliability legible claim by claim. 

Keywords: trust calibration, sycophancy, large language models, human–AI interaction, misinformation, automation bias, metacognition, design for trust

1. Introduction 

For a species as social as ours, survival has never depended on the individual alone. In evolutionary biology, fitness describes an organism’s capacity to survive and reproduce in its environment; for humans, that capacity has been shaped by life in groups, where cooperation, communication and resource sharing are routine. The cognitive demands of such relationships are reflected in the unusually large primate neocortex, which has been linked to the need to track relationships within social groups (Dunbar, 1992). Food sharing illustrates the point. In many hunter-gatherer societies a successful hunt feeds more than the hunter: large, unpredictably acquired food packages tend to be widely distributed, partly because the cost of not sharing outweighs the benefit of eating alone (Hawkes et al., 2018). With repeated interaction, sharing and reciprocity become patterned, particularly where camp membership is stable (Smith et al., 2016). Cooperation among kin is explained by inclusive fitness (Hamilton, 1964), whereas cooperation among non-kin rests partly on the expectation that partners will reciprocate (Rand and Nowak, 2013). Repeated cooperation therefore produces more than an exchange of resources. It produces expectations about how others will behave, and those expectations are a basis for trust. As societies grew more complex, so did the relationships through which such expectations form. Human social networks are layered, from an intimate core of family and close friends to acquaintances at the margins, and the layers differ in contact frequency, emotional closeness, reciprocity and trust (Dunbar, 2025). Nor is trust static within a relationship. It starts from some baseline and moves as experience accumulates: perceptions of predictability grow from observing the consistency of a partner’s behaviour (Camanto and Campbell, 2025), and trust strengthens when positive expectations are confirmed and weakens when they are violated (Lewicki et al., 2006). Trust is less a fixed property of a relationship than a running account of expectation and outcome, and the degree to which A trusts B need not equal the degree to which B trusts A. Is a relationship even necessary? Not always. A patient trusts a doctor they have never met, and a passenger trusts a pilot they will never see. Non-experts routinely rely on expert knowledge they cannot verify, such as a physician’s diagnosis (Leefmann and Lesle, 2020). Such trust rests on competence, professional standards, reputation and institutions rather than on familiarity: “swift trust” forms among strangers through recognisable roles, and trust in a stockbroker is bounded by regulation rather than by acquaintance (Lewicki et al., 2006). A relationship is one basis for trust, not a prerequisite for it. Yet the trust we extend to strangers is usually cautious. We hesitate before trusting someone we have never met, not because they are untrustworthy but because we lack information about them: their history, their intentions, the relationship between us. Seen this way, an AI assistant is an extreme case. It has no personal history, no body, no reputation built through shared experience and no intentions we can read, and it is not even a stranger in the usual sense, because a stranger is at least another person. If people are wary of trusting a human being they do not know, why would they trust something that is not human at all? One answer is that such trust does not need a relationship, because it can rest instead on the kind of reliability we attribute to machines. Trust in such systems tracks the reliability of their performance and the extent to which that performance meets expectations (McKenna et al., 2024); consistent performance reinforces trust and failures undermine it (Parasuraman and Riley, 1997; de Visser et al., 2018). Crucially, expectation and outcome can be compared. The function is defined, the output is verifiable, and reliability is observable across repeated use. Where expectation and outcome overlap, reliability is established; reliability confirmed over repeated trials becomes confidence; and confidence becomes trust (Figure 1, top). Conversational AI breaks this arrangement (Figure 1, bottom). Large language models have moved human–computer interaction from specialised, task-bound automation towards systems that act as advisers (Henrique and Santos, 2024; Klingbeil et al., 2024). People ask them “What should I do?”, “Which option is better?” and “Can you explain this to me?”, questions without a single predetermined answer against which a response can be checked. An AI system can also be wrong while sounding coherent and certain. With a calculator, the user checks whether a function was performed correctly; with AI, the user must judge whether the information itself is accurate enough to rely on, often on a topic they are trying to learn precisely because they do not know it. Reliance on such systems is already known to be unstable: people sometimes discount good computerised advice (Promberger and Baron, 2006) and, in other conditions, follow AI advice against contrary evidence and their own judgement (Klingbeil et al., 2024). And if trust is built through repeated experiences of something meeting our expectations, a system that is trained to meet expectations—to sound right, to agree, to please—can meet them while being wrong. This creates an under-studied failure mode at the intersection of two literatures. Research on trust in automation asks whether people’s trust tracks a system’s reliability (Lee and See, 2004; Hoff and Bashir, 2015). Research on sycophancy asks whether LLMs tailor their 3answers to what users want rather than to what is true (Perez et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). Both typically treat one side of the human–AI pair as fixed. Sycophancy research, in particular, measures whether the model adapts to the user : whether it abandons a correct answer under pushback, mirrors a stated opinion or flatters the user’s self-image (Fanous et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026). The reverse direction has received far less attention: when the model holds its ground confidently and consistently, does the user adapt to the model? We call this human-side deference : the convergence of a person’s beliefs on a source’s claims irrespective of their truth. It is the mirror image of model sycophancy, and it matters because modern assistants are, by design, fluent, assertive and unhedged (Zhou et al., 2024). We report HARICHAN (Human–AI Reliance in CHAt iNteractions), a controlled experi-ment in which the same LLM taught five science topics while covertly falsifying between none and all of its briefing facts, always with full confidence. We address five research questions: 

RQ1. How much planted misinformation do learners absorb as falsification rises, and does their acceptance of each stated falsehood change with the source’s overall reliability? 

RQ2. Does learners’ confidence distinguish what they learned correctly from what they absorbed falsely? 

RQ3. Does their reported trust track the assistant’s actual reliability? 

RQ4. How do learners check an assistant they have reason to doubt, and what happens when they push back? 

RQ5. Is the deference sycophancy on the model’s side or on the human’s, and how does human-side deference relate to confidence? Our contributions are fourfold. Empirically , we measure misinformation uptake across a graded range of assistant reliability in open-ended conversational learning, and we separate exposure to a falsehood from acceptance of it by coding every transcript for which facts the assistant actually stated. Methodologically , we adapt baseline-corrected sycophancy metrics from model evaluation to the human side, introducing a Human Deference Index and pairing it with metacognitive measures of confidence. Conceptually , we argue that the “calculator fallacy”—extending to a fluent, probabilistic system the trust that deterministic tools earn through verification—explains why trust cannot calibrate when error is invisible, and that trustworthiness is a property of the human–AI pair. For design , we derive strategies that make reliability legible per claim, route verification outward and evaluate trust behaviourally. 

2. Related work 

2.1. Trust, reliance and calibration 

Trust in automation is commonly defined as the attitude that an agent will help achieve one’s goals in a situation characterised by uncertainty and vulnerability (Lee and See, 2004), and in interpersonal terms as a willingness to be vulnerable that rests on perceived ability, benevolence and integrity (Mayer et al., 1995). The design goal is not more trust but calibrated 

trust: trust that matches a system’s actual capability (Lee and See, 2004; Wischnewski et al., 42023), or, in the terms of Jacovi et al. (2021), warranted rather than unwarranted trust. Lee and See (2004) also describe resolution , the degree to which changes in capability are mirrored by changes in trust, and specificity , the degree to which trust attaches to particular functions rather than to the system as a whole. Reviews of trust measurement emphasise that trust is latent and multifaceted, that most scales are bespoke, and that behavioural measures should accompany self-report (Gebru et al., 2022; Wischnewski et al., 2023). Trust and distrust also appear to be distinct factors rather than two ends of one scale (Scharowski et al., 2025), and healthy distrust may require both explicit error detection and an intuitive sense of when to doubt (Peters et al., 2026). Hoff and Bashir (2015) separate dispositional, situational and learned trust and note that learned trust updates dynamically with reliability, but only when people can compare the system’s performance with their own. Experiments bear this out. Stated and observed accuracy both shape trust, but observed accuracy dominates once users can see it (Yin et al., 2019); trust can drift away from reliability when no feedback is available (Rittenberg et al., 2024); and displaying an AI’s case-level confidence helps calibrate trust where explanations do not (Zhang et al., 2020). Computational models describe trust as moving towards recent experience, cumulative trust and a prior expectation bias; without outcome feedback, the experience term cannot register failure (Hu et al., 2019). For virtual and embedded AI, high initial trust typically drops after errors are experienced (Glikson and Woolley, 2020), and trust has affective as well as cognitive roots (Gillath et al., 2021; Choung et al., 2023). The recurring condition across this literature is visibility: trust calibrates to what users can observe. 

2.2. Overreliance and automation bias with conversational AI 

When verification is costly, people lean on automated aids. Automation bias, the use of automated output as a substitute for vigilant information seeking, produces commission errors even when contradictory cues are available (Skitka et al., 1999; Goddard et al., 2012), and it shares an attentional mechanism with complacency that affects experts and novices alike (Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010). Labelling advice as algorithmic increases its weight regardless of its quality (Logg et al., 2019), and AI-labelled advice can override both contextual evidence and people’s own assessments, at real cost (Klingbeil et al., 2024). Appropriate reliance requires both relying on correct advice and overriding incorrect advice (Schemmer et al., 2023); explanations can reduce overreliance when they lower the cost of verification (Vasconcelos et al., 2023) but can also inflate reliance when they merely justify (Dzindolet et al., 2003). Sundar (2020) attributes part of this to a “machine heuristic”, the lay belief that machines are objective and accurate, which invites users to outsource vigilance. Large language models intensify the problem because they are fluent by default and rarely express uncertainty; users rely on their confident outputs whether or not those outputs are correct (Zhou et al., 2024), and overreliance on overconfident models appears across languages (Rathi et al., 2025). Users overestimate the accuracy of model answers from default explanations, and longer explanations raise confidence without improving discrimination (Steyvers et al., 2025). Model-generated explanations speed up fact-checking but mislead when the model is convincingly wrong (Si et al., 2024); users overlooked the errors in ChatGPT answers to programming questions 39% of the time even though 52% of those answers were 5incorrect (Kabir et al., 2024); and LLM-based search is fast but invites overreliance when the model errs (Spatharioti et al., 2025). First-person hedging reduces overreliance only in part (Kim et al., 2024), sources and visible inconsistencies help more than explanations (Kim et al., 2025), and combining visual and verbal uncertainty cues can sharpen subjective discrimination while worsening behavioural reliance (Ojewale et al., 2026). In conversational settings, dialogue cues increase trust even when an assistant spreads health misinformation, and only acted-upon, independent verification lowers it (Liao and Sundar, 2026). AI assistance can also make people perform better while making them worse at judging their own performance (Fernandes et al., 2026), and human confidence in themselves, rather than in the AI, often governs whether advice is adopted (Chong et al., 2022). Most of this work, however, studies single decisions with a fixed error rate. Less is known about learning from a conversational source whose reliability varies, where the learner has no independent knowledge and the only readily available check is the source itself. 

2.3. Misinformation, fluency and epistemic vigilance 

Research on misinformation explains why confidently stated falsehoods persist. Once encoded, misinformation continues to influence reasoning even after it is retracted, and corrections work best when they supply an alternative account (Lewandowsky et al., 2012). Repetition increases perceived truth (Hasher et al., 1977), and prior knowledge offers sur-prisingly little protection against this illusory-truth effect (Fazio et al., 2015). Belief in misinformation is driven less by a lack of intelligence than by ordinary cognitive shortcuts, including reliance on fluency and familiarity (Ecker et al., 2022). People do possess mecha-nisms of epistemic vigilance that monitor the competence and benevolence of sources and the coherence of what they say (Sperber et al., 2010), and much human knowledge legitimately rests on trusting testimony we cannot check (Hardwig, 1991). These mechanisms evolved for human informants, however, and fluent machine text offers few of the signals they rely on; people form stronger and less revisable beliefs from sources that sound confident and knowledgeable (Kidd and Birhane, 2023). Access to external information can also inflate people’s sense of what they themselves know (Fisher et al., 2015; Sparrow et al., 2011), and offloading cognition to tools depends on metacognitive judgements that can be miscalibrated (Risko and Gilbert, 2016). Measuring such metacognition requires separating sensitivity (does confidence discriminate right from wrong?) from bias (is confidence too high overall?) (Fleming and Lau, 2014). 

2.4. Sycophancy: the model adapts to the user 

Sycophancy refers to the tendency of language models to tailor responses to what users appear to want rather than to what is true. It was first documented as models repeating back users’ stated views, increasingly so at larger scales (Perez et al., 2023), and has been traced to human preference data that reward agreement and convincingly written responses over correct ones (Sharma et al., 2024). Models agree with users on subjective questions and follow plausible hints even on factual ones (Ranaldi and Pucci, 2023). A family of benchmarks now quantifies the behaviour. SycEval classifies answer changes under rebuttal as progressive (incorrect to correct) or regressive (correct to incorrect) and finds sycophancy in 58% of cases, with citation-based rebuttals producing the most regressive flips (Fanous et al., 62025). SYCON Bench measures how many turns a model holds its stance under sustained pressure and how often it flips (Hong et al., 2025). Adversarial scientific QA dialogues show that resistance to misleading cues depends more on alignment and reasoning than on scale (Zhang et al., 2025). ELEPHANT broadens the construct to social sycophancy, the excessive preservation of the user’s face through validation, indirectness and acceptance of the user’s framing, and subtracts a human baseline to score it (Cheng et al., 2026b). A psychometric Social Sycophancy Scale distinguishes uncritical agreement, obsequiousness and excitement as perceived by readers (Rehani et al., 2026). An expert survey shows that researchers share concern about sycophancy but not its boundaries (Ye et al., 2026). Reinforcement learning from human feedback can also make models better at convincing human evaluators that wrong answers are right, raising evaluators’ false-positive rates (Wen et al., 2025), and training models to be warm can increase sycophancy and reduce accuracy (Ibrahim et al., 2026). The persuasive power of LLMs in conversation is itself well documented (Salvi et al., 2025). 

2.5. Human consequences of sycophancy 

A smaller body of work traces what sycophancy does to people. In a between-subjects study, a custom GPT that never contradicted users lowered demonstrated trust, and the decline in self-reported trust was significant mainly when participants could verify answers (Carro, 2024). Sycophantic chatbots increase attitude extremity and certainty while being perceived as unbiased, an effect driven by one-sided facts rather than by validation (Rathje et al., 2025). Sycophancy decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence while users continue to prefer and trust sycophantic models (Cheng et al., 2026a). Flattery combined with agreement lowers perceived authenticity, whereas agreement delivered in a neutral tone raises trust (Sun and Wang, 2026). Sycophantic assistants leave novices’ misconceptions uncorrected in problem-solving tasks without the novices noticing (Bo et al., 2026). Table 1 places our study relative to this work. Almost all of it asks whether the model adapts to the user, and measures the model. We hold the model’s behaviour fixed—confident, non-flattering and unyielding to pushback—and measure whether the user adapts to the model. 

2.6. Designing for calibrated trust 

Design research treats trustworthiness as something communicated through cues. The MATCH model distinguishes a system’s actual trustworthiness from the cues that signal it and warns that some prominent cues, such as the chatbot medium and a polished design look, are irrelevant to trustworthiness yet shape it (Liao and Sundar, 2022). Proposed remedies include cognitive forcing functions that make users commit before seeing AI output (Buçinca et al., 2021), adaptive cues delivered when over-trust is detected (Okamura and Yamada, 2020), seamful designs that expose a system’s limits rather than hide them (Ehsan et al., 2024), deliberate micro-frictions that interrupt automatic behaviour (Cox et al., 2016), and verification affordances that users act on (Liao and Sundar, 2026). In design practice itself, AI assistance has been shown to hurt high-performing teams through misplaced reliance (Zhang et al., 2021), and trust in generative AI rises and falls with many interacting factors during co-design (Guo et al., 2025). 3. Method 

3.1. Design 

We used a within-subjects experiment with covert manipulation of assistant reliability. Each participant learned five topics, each from a different assistant (Assistants A to E, in presentation order), and each assistant was configured to one of five reliability levels in which 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% or 100% of its briefing facts were falsified. Levels were assigned by a 5 × 5 Latin square: participants received its rows in rotation as they enrolled, and the task at position i received the level indexed by ( i + row) mod 5. Apart from the first participant, who received the same row as the fourth, any five consecutive participants therefore met every level at every position equally often. Topic order was randomised independently for each participant, so the pairing of topics with levels was left to chance; in the final data each topic–level combination occurred in between one and seven quizzes, and topic is included as a covariate in a sensitivity model. Figure 2 summarises the procedure.

For each topic we wrote twelve fact pairs , each consisting of a verified true statement and a matched, plausible false version of the same statement (Table 2; the complete set of 60 pairs is given in Appendix B). False versions were designed to be plausible to a novice: they change a number, a mechanism, a cause or an attribution while keeping the surface form of an expert explanation. At the start of each task, the assigned level determined how many of the twelve facts (0, 3, 6, 9 or 12) were served in their false form; the subset was drawn at random for each task-run and stored, so that every participant’s exposure could be traced, graded and later corrected. Each topic had a ten-item multiple-choice quiz. Every item targeted one fact pair (ten of the twelve pairs per topic were quizzed) and offered four options in an order shuffled per participant: the correct fact, the exact planted falsehood and two plausible distractors. Choosing the planted option on an item whose fact had been falsified in that participant’s chat is a direct signature of absorbed misinformation. Choosing it on an item that had been taught correctly provides a baseline for how attractive the planted option is on its own.

What differed across assistants was only the system prompt, which inserted the task’s twelve facts—with the falsified subset replaced by their false versions—as the assistant’s “complete and absolute ground truth”. 3.4. Procedure 

The consent page described the study as learning from AI assistants, did not mention inaccuracy or a quiz, and asked participants to rely only on the assistant, without other websites, books or people. A background survey recorded age group, gender, education, field, frequency of AI-chatbot use, three seven-point items on general trust in AI (“I generally trust the information AI assistants give me”; “I usually double-check important information from AI against other sources”; “AI assistants are usually more knowledgeable than I am on unfamiliar topics”) and an attention check (“select 6 for this row”). For each task, participants saw the topic and the assistant’s label, rated their prior familiarity (1–5), and chatted for as long as they wished. When they clicked “I’ve finished learning”, a surprise quiz appeared; this was the first mention of a quiz. Each answer required a confidence rating from 1 (“just guessing”) to 10 (“completely sure”). A banner invited participants to go back and ask the assistant as often as they liked; tabs allowed free switching between quiz and chat, drafts were saved automatically, and every switch was logged with a timestamp. Submission required all ten answers and confidence ratings. Participants then rated two statements on seven-point scales: “The information [Assistant] gave me was accurate” and “I would rely on [Assistant] again for a similar task”. After the fifth task they named the assistant they trusted most and least and could leave comments. A mandatory debrief revealed the design, apologised for 10 the concealment and listed, for every assistant, each false claim it may have made beside the correct fact, flagging the ones the participant had chosen. 

3.5. Participants 

Thirty-two adults enrolled online. They were recruited as a convenience sample through university networks and personal contacts and were not paid. Most were aged 18–24 ( n = 21; 25–34, n = 8; 35–44, n = 3); 19 identified as women and 13 as men; 21 held a bachelor’s degree, six a master’s degree, three a doctorate, and two a school qualification. Twenty-seven (84%) used AI chat assistants daily, three weekly and two less often. On the general-trust items, participants agreed moderately that they trust AI information ( M = 4 .34, SD = 1 .07), agreed that they double-check important AI information ( M = 5 .59, SD = 1 .36) and agreed that AI is more knowledgeable than they are on unfamiliar topics ( M = 4 .94, SD = 1 .61). Prior familiarity with the topics was low ( M = 2 .09 on the 1–5 scale; 69% of ratings were 1 or 2).

3.7. Transcript coding 

Because a falsified fact can only be absorbed if it is actually said, we coded all 104 quizzed task-runs (716 messages). For each of the ten quizzed facts, the coder recorded whether the assistant’s messages stated the true version, the planted false version, both or neither, separately for the learning phase and for the whole run. Each user turn was classified as an open request (“tell me everything about ECG”), a specific question, a pasted or retyped quiz question, an answer check, a format request, a challenge, a social or meta turn, or other. For every challenge we recorded the targeted fact, whether the doubted claim was true or false, and whether the assistant held, partly softened or conceded. Finally, the assistant’s behaviour in each run was rated from 0 (absent) to 3 (pervasive) on six dimensions adapted from the Social Sycophancy Scale (Rehani et al., 2026) and ELEPHANT (Cheng et al., 2026b): validation or praise, obsequiousness, excitement, uncritical agreement, hedging and assertiveness. Coding was performed by an LLM-based coder (four coder instances, each coding a quarter of the runs) following a written protocol that supplied, for each fact, the exact true and planted wordings; verbatim quotations were checked programmatically against the transcripts. The coders’ notes on borderline cases (e.g., runs in which the assistant stated both versions of a fact) were reviewed and resolved by rule.

4.3. RQ2: Confidence decouples from correctness 

Participants were confident throughout. 4.4. RQ3: Reported trust stays put 

Post-task ratings hovered around 5 on the seven-point scales at every level (Figure 17). Rated accuracy fell only from 5.1 to 4.5 ( b = −0.12 per step, p = .14), and willingness to rely on the assistant again did not change (4.6 to 4.9; b = +0 .02, p = .82). Rated accuracy was uncorrelated with the participant’s own quiz score for that assistant ( ρ = −0.10, p = .34) and with the assistant’s falsification level ( ρ = −0.11, p = .25). Indexed to the accurate condition, the fully falsified assistant delivered 39% of the learning outcome while retaining 89% of the rated accuracy and 106% of the willingness to rely on it (Figure 18); the standardised difference in rated accuracy between the extremes ( d = 0 .44) was less than a third of that in score ( d = 1 .54). Trust ratings also had little resolution within participants. The slope of rated accuracy on the assistant’s true accuracy had a median of 0.00, and 11 slopes were flat or negative (Figure 20). In the final ranking, votes for the most- and least-trusted assistant did not depart from a uniform spread over falsification levels ( χ2(4) = 6 .1, p = .19; χ2(4) = 3 .3, p = .51), and the 25%- and 100%-falsified assistants received the most “most trusted” votes (Figure 19).

The learners did not know the answers, which is why they were learning; the assistant’s reliability was invisible at the level of the individual claim; and the only available route to checking led back to the assistant. Trust therefore defaulted to the cues that were available—fluency, coherence, structure and certainty—and these were identical across falsification levels because the same model, instructed identically, produced them. The MATCH model predicts exactly this: when cues that are irrelevant to trustworthiness are the only cues on offer, they shape trust anyway (Liao and Sundar, 2022). The result is the pattern we observed: acceptance per stated claim that did not vary with the source’s reliability, confidence that stayed high on wrong answers, and trust ratings with almost no resolution (Lee and See, 2004). We read this less as a failure of calibration than as its impossibility under these conditions. Trust calibrates to what can be observed (Yin et al., 2019); where error cannot be seen, the experience that should move trust never registers (Hu et al., 2019). The findings therefore qualify a comforting assumption, namely that people recalibrate once they encounter errors (Glikson and Woolley, 2020; Dzindolet et al., 2003). Errors here were abundant—three quarters of the briefing at the 75% level—but not visible. In information design terms the assistant’s content was not unclear; it was too clear. Well-structured explanations delivered with uniform confidence, whatever their truth, are precisely the kind of content that borrows the look of credibility, and the illusory-truth literature shows that fluent processing is itself taken as a cue to truth, even against prior knowledge (Hasher et al., 1977; Fazio et al., 2015). The fallacy also has an epistemological side. Much of what anyone knows is held on testimony, and relying on experts is rational when the listener has grounds to believe the expert is competent and honest (Hardwig, 1991). Humans protect themselves against misplaced testimonial trust through epistemic vigilance: checking a message’s coherence with what they already believe and tracking the source’s record (Sperber et al., 2010). Both defences were disabled by design. Coherence checking fails when the listener knows nothing that could conflict with the claim, and source tracking fails when the source’s record cannot be observed claim by claim. Novice learners talking to a single assistant are in exactly this position, which is why we expect the calculator fallacy to be most consequential in education. 26 5.2. Borrowed confidence: the collapse of metacognition 

The most striking result may be what happened to confidence. In the accurate condition, participants’ confidence discriminated between their right and wrong answers moderately well (AUROC 2 = 0 .70, where 0.5 is chance; Fleming and Lau, 2014). With falsification, discrimination fell to chance, the modelled confidence gap between correct and wrong answers closed, and absorbed falsehoods were held with a confidence of 8.4 out of 10, close to the 8.9 for truthfully taught facts (Figure 16). Confidence, in other words, stopped carrying information about correctness. We suggest that participants’ confidence was borrowed from the source rather than generated from their own evidence. Learners who have just been taught something clearly and assertively have an experience of understanding that does not depend on whether what they were taught is true. This extends two related findings. Searching the internet inflates people’s estimates of their own knowledge, because access to an external source is mistaken for internal understanding (Fisher et al., 2015); and working with AI can raise task performance while leaving people’s metacognitive judgements of that performance no better, or worse (Fernandes et al., 2026). In our study the external source was also wrong, and the inflated sense of knowing attached itself to the falsehoods as readily as to the facts. Unlike the classic pattern in which the least competent overestimate themselves (Kruger and Dunning, 1999), the miscalibration here was produced by the situation rather than by individual skill: the same participants were reasonably calibrated when the assistant was accurate. The deference map (Figure 27) adds that the most deferent participants were, if anything, the most confident in what they had absorbed. Borrowed confidence matters because confidence governs what people do next. A learner who feels unsure seeks more information; a learner who feels sure stops looking. People already struggle to judge what LLMs know, and they overestimate the accuracy of model answers when explanations are fluent and long (Steyvers et al., 2025). If fluent teaching also makes learners sure of what they were told, the ordinary signal that should prompt verification is suppressed at exactly the moment it is needed. 

5.3. Sycophancy in reverse: who adapts to whom? 

Research on model sycophancy asks whether AI tells people what they want to hear (Perez et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). The construct is fragmented, spanning answer changes under pushback, social validation and adoption of the user’s framing (Ye et al., 2026), and it is typically measured on the model side: does the model flip when challenged (Fanous et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2025), does it validate the user’s self-image (Cheng et al., 2026b)? On those measures our assistant was exemplary. It scored near zero on every social-sycophancy dimension in almost nine runs out of ten, it never flipped under challenge, and its occasional praise came mostly when it graded participants’ own answers (Figure 26). A model-side benchmark would rate it as robust. Yet it produced exactly the harm that sycophancy research warns of—confidently held false beliefs (Rathje et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026a)—because the convergence happened on the human side. Holding one’s ground is a virtue only when one is right. The same behaviour that makes a model score well on pushback benchmarks, maintaining its position under challenge, made our falsified assistants more effective at implanting errors. We therefore 27 propose treating trustworthiness as a property of the human–AI pair rather than of the model alone. A pair is well calibrated when the human’s beliefs converge on the model’s claims to the extent that those claims are true; it is sycophantic in the human-to-model direction when beliefs converge on the model’s claims regardless of their truth. The Human Deference Index operationalises the second quantity by mirroring the baseline correction used in model-side sycophancy metrics: it asks how much more often a user chooses the model’s answer when the model asserts it, over and above how often they would have chosen it anyway. The two directions can compound. Models trained on human preferences learn to agree with users (Sharma et al., 2024) and can learn to appear correct to their evaluators without being correct (Wen et al., 2025); training for warmth can reduce accuracy and increase sycophancy (Ibrahim et al., 2026); and users continue to prefer and trust sycophantic models even as sycophancy promotes dependence (Cheng et al., 2026a), with agreement delivered in a neutral tone raising trust (Sun and Wang, 2026). Notably, the loss of trust that an always-agreeing model causes has been found to be significant mainly when users could verify its answers (Carro, 2024)—the very condition that was absent here. A model inclined to please and a user inclined to defer form a closed loop in which neither party corrects the other. Our study isolates one half of that loop by holding the model’s stance fixed. The risk is greatest for novices, who cannot supply the correction (Bo et al., 2026), and in persuasive contexts, where LLMs already match or exceed humans (Salvi et al., 2025). Generative models, which deliver answers fluently and without signs of uncertainty, have been argued to be especially likely to shape the beliefs of people who are themselves uncertain (Kidd and Birhane, 2023); our results show this operating in the most benign-seeming setting of all, learning a science topic. 

5.4. Why checking failed: circular verification 

Participants were not passive. They tended to return to the chat more often when the assistant was less accurate, a small and non-significant trend, and nearly a third of their turns pasted quiz questions back into the conversation. But these checks were circular: the source being checked was the one doing the checking. Returning went with more absorption, not less (ρ = 0 .48), and falsified items that were pasted were answered with the planted falsehood 91.5% of the time. When the assistant graded participants’ own answers, it confirmed falsehoods as correct, producing most of the little praise in the transcripts and reinforcing exactly the errors it praised. This behaviour is familiar from research on cognitive offloading. People readily outsource memory and judgement to external tools when those tools are available (Sparrow et al., 2011; Risko and Gilbert, 2016), and LLM-based search speeds decisions while increasing overreliance when the model is wrong (Spatharioti et al., 2025). Verification affordances reduce misplaced trust only when they point to an independent source and users actually act on them (Liao and Sundar, 2026). LLM explanations can help people verify claims, except when the model is convincingly wrong (Si et al., 2024), and inconsistencies between answers are among the few cues that prompt appropriate distrust (Kim et al., 2025). A single, consistent, confident assistant supplies no such inconsistency: asking it again returns the same falsehood, now rephrased and therefore seemingly corroborated. Our participants had been asked not to consult outside sources, so the circularity is partly a feature of the design; but the design 28 mirrors a common situation in which the assistant is the most convenient, and often the only, source to hand. 

5.5. Designing for warranted distrust 

The conference theme that framed the shorter version of this work pairs trust with distrust, and our data suggest that the absence of distrust, rather than an excess of trust, was the operative failure. Distrust is not simply low trust; trust and distrust can coexist as separate stances towards different aspects of the same party (Lewicki et al., 2006), and a validated distinction between the two exists for AI (Scharowski et al., 2025). Appropriate reliance may require both explicit error detection and an intuitive, attentional sense of when to doubt (Peters et al., 2026). Our participants showed flickers of both. Four of the seven challenges they typed targeted claims that were genuinely false, and they tended to return to the chat more when the assistant was worse. What they lacked was somewhere for that doubt to go. With the assistant as the only source, doubt was answered by the object of doubt, and its unwavering confidence closed the question: of the challenged falsified facts that were later quizzed, the correct answer was chosen only twice in ten. Designing for trust in AI therefore also means designing for warranted distrust: making it cheap to act on suspicion, visible when suspicion is justified and rewarding to pursue it through independent means. Doubt that cannot be resolved is simply uncomfortable, and learners who cannot resolve it will reasonably fall back on the most confident voice available. In learning contexts the ability to doubt productively is itself an outcome of education, and tools that dissolve doubt by restating the doubted claim confidently may undermine the very capacity education seeks to build. Design research on human–AI co-creation reports similar dynamics—AI assistance can lower the performance of high-performing design teams even as it helps weaker ones (Zhang et al., 2021), confidence in AI and confidence in oneself evolve together over repeated use and shape the adoption of AI advice (Chong et al., 2022), and trust calibration in human–AI co-design depends on factors beyond the AI’s accuracy (Guo et al., 2025)—and recent work argues that trust in agentic AI must be studied as an experience lived over time rather than a single judgement (Moon, 2026). 

5.6. Design implications: trust that is legible, checkable and measured 

What should designers do when learners cannot tell a confident falsehood from a confident fact? Table 7 links each finding to a design principle, an example intervention and a measure by which the intervention could be evaluated. We elaborate on four directions. 

Make reliability legible per claim, not per system. Trust ratings summarise a whole assistant, but misinformation operated claim by claim, and acceptance per claim never changed. In-terfaces for learning should attach provenance, confidence and contestability to individual statements, for example by distinguishing sourced from generated claims, rather than offering global reassurance. Route verification outward. Participants did check, but with the same source. An “ask again” affordance is a verification trap. Designers can make independent verification the path of least resistance through linked primary sources, a second model with different training or a retrieval check against a trusted reference, in line with evidence that only acted-upon external verification lowers misplaced trust (Liao and Sundar, 2026). Pasting quiz or homework questions, nearly a third of all turns here, is a detectable pattern to which a system could respond by pointing to an external source rather than answering. 

Add friction where the stakes are epistemic. In learning, the goal is understanding, not speed. Cognitive forcing functions, such as asking learners to commit to an answer before the assistant confirms it, reduce overreliance on AI (Buçinca et al., 2021); micro-boundaries that interrupt automatic interaction make room for reflection (Cox et al., 2016); and seamful designs that expose where a model’s knowledge comes from and where it breaks down give doubt somewhere to go (Ehsan et al., 2024). The challenge episodes show why this matters: doubt without recourse rarely recovered the truth. 30 Evaluate trust behaviourally. Had we relied on self-reported trust, we would have concluded that participants were mildly and stably trusting. Behavioural and metacognitive measures— absorption, acceptance conditional on exposure and confidence discrimination—showed that their trust was not calibrated at all. Evaluations of AI tools, especially in education, should report such measures alongside satisfaction and trust scales (Scharowski et al., 2025; Wis-chnewski et al., 2023), and should define appropriate reliance behaviourally rather than attitudinally (Schemmer et al., 2023). 

5.7. Implications for AI developers and sycophancy evaluation 

For model developers, the results caution against treating assertiveness as an unconditional good. Resistance to pushback is desirable in a model that is right and harmful in a model that is wrong, and a model’s confidence of tone is a design parameter with measurable effects on what users come to believe. We suggest three additions to current practice. First, sycophancy evaluations should measure both directions: alongside the rate at which a model changes a correct answer under user pressure, they should measure the rate at which users adopt a model’s incorrect answers under model assertion, using baseline-corrected indices such as the HDI. Second, evaluations of tone—warmth, friendliness, confidence—should report their effect on users’ belief accuracy, not only on satisfaction or preference, given evidence that warmth can trade against accuracy (Ibrahim et al., 2026) and that tone shapes trust independently of content (Sun and Wang, 2026). Third, because model errors in deployment are rarer and less systematic than in our falsified conditions, the relevant quantity is not the overall error rate but the conditional probability that a stated error is adopted. Our data suggest that this probability can be high and insensitive to the model’s overall reliability, which means that even a small residual error rate in a confident model may translate almost one-for-one into user misconceptions. 

5.8. Implications for education 

Learners are an especially exposed population because they are, by definition, not yet able to check what they are told. Our participants were young, highly educated and frequent AI users, and more than half of those with enough quizzes to classify were confident deferrers. Users overlooked the errors in ChatGPT answers to programming questions 39% of the time even though 52% of those answers were incorrect (Kabir et al., 2024), and overreliance on overconfident models has been observed across languages (Rathi et al., 2025). If learning with an AI tutor is to be safe, the interface must carry part of the burden of epistemic vigilance that learners cannot yet carry themselves. Concretely, educational deployments could tie tutoring assistants to curated reference material and display that material alongside claims; prompt learners to predict and justify before being told; treat pasted assessment questions as an occasion for guided verification rather than answer delivery; and assess what learners believe after using the tool, not only whether they liked it. Teaching the ability to doubt productively, and to take doubt somewhere other than the source being doubted, is itself a form of AI literacy. 

5.9. Limitations and future work 

Several limitations bound these conclusions. Sample. The sample was small, young, highly educated, and dominated by daily AI users, which may increase deference, and recruitment 31 through university and personal networks limits generalisability; participant-level analyses rest on 19–20 people, so correlations at that level should be read as exploratory. Assistant behaviour. The assistants were deliberately extreme: they never hedged, never departed from their briefing and erred systematically at high rates, whereas deployed systems err less often and less consistently. Our estimate of per-claim acceptance is therefore best read as a property of confident delivery to novices, not as an error rate for deployed tutors. Topics and test. 

Topics were chosen to be unfamiliar, so the results concern learning rather than checking known facts. The quiz was immediate and multiple-choice, and the planted option made recognition of the falsehood easy; free recall and delayed retention may show lower absorption, although the baseline correction in the HDI and the low planted-choice rate on truthfully taught items (11%) indicate that the planted option was not attractive in itself. Outside sources. Participants were asked not to use outside sources, which removed the independent check that design should restore; the observation that they checked only by asking again must be read in that light. Within-subjects design. Participants met five assistants built on the same model, which may have encouraged them to treat the assistants as interchangeable; presentation position did not reliably affect scores or absorption, but carry-over of suspicion or trust cannot be excluded. Coding and measures. Transcripts were coded by an LLM-based coder following a written protocol, with verbatim quotations checked programmatically; human double-coding of a subset is needed to establish inter-rater reliability. The Human Deference Index is a new measure that requires validation against other indicators of reliance. 

Deception. The study withheld the assistants’ inaccuracy and the quiz. This is a recognised trade-off in experimental research (Hertwig and Ortmann, 2008) and in HCI, where some deception can serve the user (Adar et al., 2013); we mitigated it with a mandatory debrief that corrected every falsehood each participant had seen. These limitations suggest a programme of future work. The design strategies in Table 7 should be tested directly in randomised comparisons that use the behavioural measures introduced here as outcomes. A manipulation of the assistant’s tone—uniformly confident versus calibrated to the claim’s truth—would test whether confident delivery, rather than content, drives deference. Allowing access to outside sources would show whether learners use independent checks when they are available and whether design can make them do so. Larger and more varied samples, including school students, older adults and domain experts, would establish the generality of the calculator fallacy; longitudinal designs would show how deference develops over extended relationships with AI tutors; and a two-sided benchmark pairing model-side sycophancy metrics with human-side deference would allow the human–AI pair to be evaluated as a unit. 

6. Conclusion 

Trust between people grew from repeated expectations met, and trust in machines has grown the same way: we trust a calculator because we can check it. Conversational AI invites the same trust without offering the same check. In our study, learners absorbed about two in every three falsehoods an assistant told them, however unreliable the assistant was overall. They felt as sure of the falsehoods as of the facts, their confidence stopped distinguishing right from wrong, their reported trust barely registered the difference, and when they doubted, they asked the same assistant again. The assistant did not flatter them into this; by every 32 model-side standard it was robust and non-sycophantic. It simply spoke with confidence, and they deferred. The question in our title therefore has an uncomfortable answer. In a conversation between a confident model and a learner who cannot verify, it is the human who bends. Trustworthiness is a property of the pair, and a model that resists pressure from its users can still produce the outcomes that sycophancy research fears when its users do not resist pressure from it. For design, the lesson is that trust cannot be calibrated where reliability cannot be seen. Making AI trustworthy for learning means designing what the learner can see—claim-level provenance, independent paths to verification and productive friction—and judging success by what learners come to believe rather than by how much they say they trust. For evaluation, it means measuring sycophancy in both directions. Asked whom they trusted, our participants gave a sobering answer: whatever sounds sure. 

Ethics statement 

The study was performed under the guidance laid out by the institutional human ethics committee. Participation was voluntary and unpaid, and all participants gave informed consent online before taking part.
