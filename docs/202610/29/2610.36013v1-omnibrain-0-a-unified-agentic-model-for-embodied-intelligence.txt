Abstract
General-purpose embodied intelligence requires robots to coordinate diverse skills over long hori-
zons, track task progress, and recover from failures. Specialist policies alone do not ensure task-
level reliability, while powerful multimodal agents rely on costly reasoning and external experience
that compact models struggle to reproduce. We introduce OmniBrain-0, a lightweight unified em-
bodied agent that coordinates manipulation and navigation through a shared high-level decision
model, integrating task understanding, planning, progress tracking, and feedback-driven recovery.
Our progressive training framework combines embodied pretraining, domain-specialized agent dis-
tillation, and multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). It distills powerful teachers’ interaction
experience into manipulation and navigation experts, then consolidates their decision capabilities
into OmniBrain-0. Unification therefore occurs within the learned decision model rather than only
at the tool interface. We further introduce Rubric-Guided Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI), a
data flywheel in which a Meta-Agent turns execution evidence into refined rubrics, step-level
reminders, and verified trajectories for domain-expert updates before their reconsolidation into
OmniBrain-0 through MOPD. We evaluate OmniBrain-0 on LIBERO-Pro, LIBERO-Plus, Robo-
Casa365, RoboTwin 2.0, and R2R-CE Val-Unseen, as well as multiple real-world manipulation
tasks. OmniBrain-0 combines strong cross-domain task performance with substantially lower in-
ference and execution overhead than its closed-source teacher, demonstrating eﬀicient transfer
and consolidation of embodied capabilities into a compact unified agent.

General-purpose embodied intelligence requires diverse physical skills, long-horizon planning, skill compo-
sition, and closed-loop correction to fulfill task intent.
Vision-language-action (VLA) models and vision-
and-language navigation (VLN) policies advance manipulation and navigation [4, 22, 43, 47], but reliable
individual skills do not guarantee task-level success. Under complex constraints, multi-stage dependencies,
or distribution shifts, local action prediction may fail to maintain goal alignment, requiring feedback-driven
reassessment and replanning [51, 61]. Large-model-based embodied agents offer a promising approach by
exposing perception, specialist policies, and motion controllers as tools. A high-level model interprets in-
structions, decomposes tasks, orchestrates skills, and uses execution feedback to track progress and recover
from failures, while low-level policies retain their physical interaction expertise [16, 28, 61]. This division of
labor enables goal- and feedback-driven execution, but reliable coordination also requires the high-level model
to reason about skill applicability, capability boundaries, and handoff states [15]. These shared task-level
requirements motivate a common high-level decision mechanism for manipulation and navigation, spanning
task understanding, skill orchestration, and feedback-driven adaptation.
3


However, successful task execution by powerful models does not by itself establish how their planning and
recovery capabilities can be learned by a compact, unified agent. These models combine contextual reason-
ing, experience, and environmental feedback [15, 61], and compact agents must learn from this interaction
experience how to decompose tasks, coordinate skills, track progress, and adapt to execution outcomes across
domains. Repeated reasoning by powerful models during long-horizon interaction incurs computational over-
head and decision latency, constraining deployment eﬀiciency [9, 27, 29, 30, 33]. Distilling these capabilities
into compact agents offers a way to improve deployment eﬀiciency, but effective interaction data remain
limited, particularly for states that require feedback-driven correction. Useful supervision must therefore
preserve how tasks progress, how skills are coordinated, and how decisions change in response to execution
feedback, including during recovery from failure.
Alongside effective learning and consolidation methods, we therefore need a scalable data construction mech-
anism that preserves informative task progression and recovery while continually expanding coverage of
execution challenges encountered during interaction [7, 32]. These requirements motivate a unified learning
framework that combines scalable interaction data construction with capability transfer and consolidation,
enabling a compact agent to coordinate planning and recovery across manipulation and navigation.
First, capability transfer aims to learn effective decision behaviors from teacher interactions rather than
reproduce teachers’ language outputs or reasoning processes [19].
Because embodied decisions alter the
environment and subsequent observations, students must learn to interpret execution feedback, track progress,
and adapt when plans become invalid. Fixed demonstrations alone may not provide suﬀicient supervision
for appropriate decisions in states encountered during autonomous execution [1, 16].
Second, acquiring
specialized capabilities and integrating them into a unified agent are distinct problems. Manipulation and
navigation differ in scene distributions, skill interfaces, and task structures, so expertise learned by specialized
planners must be further integrated for joint deployment [35].
Therefore, we introduce OmniBrain-0, a lightweight, unified embodied agent for manipulation and navigation
(Figure 2). OmniBrain-0 integrates specialist policies and tools through a shared skill-invocation framework.
A compact high-level planner performs task understanding, long-horizon planning, skill orchestration, state
tracking, and failure recovery. Conditioned on observations, instructions, and interaction history, it selects
skills and adapts to execution feedback, preserving low-level specialization while learning task decomposition
and adaptive correction.
To realize these capability transformations, we develop a progressive training framework comprising shared
embodied initialization, specialized capability acquisition, and cross-domain capability integration. First, we
combine embodied, question-answering, and general language data with limited long-horizon interaction data
to establish a shared initialization for a compact model. We then train manipulation and navigation planners
from this initialization using quality-filtered interaction trajectories generated by multiple powerful models.
By learning observation–decision–feedback relationships from canonical execution and successful recovery,
these planners acquire the teachers’ planning and correction behaviors. Finally, we adopt multi-teacher on-
policy distillation (MOPD) [35], using the domain experts to supervise student-generated decision sequences
and integrate specialized capabilities into a unified student initialized from the shared pretrained model. The
resulting OmniBrain-0 thus unifies manipulation and navigation at the level of learned high-level decisions
as well as the skill-invocation interface.
To support the scalable construction of effective interaction data, we introduce Rubric-Guided Recursive
Self-Improvement (RSI), a closed-loop mechanism connecting data collection, execution-guidance refinement,
and model training (Section 5). OmniBrain-0’s autonomous interactions reveal challenges in task progression,
skill coordination, and failure recovery. A Meta-Agent analyzes execution evidence from successful and failed
trajectories, derives execution rubrics, and refines step-level reminders to guide subsequent interaction and
collection. Candidate reminders are validated through interaction with model parameters fixed, while suc-
cessful, failed, and ineﬀicient executions inform further refinement. Environment-verified canonical successes
and successful recoveries are curated and structured to augment manipulation and navigation training pools,
which are then used to update domain experts. MOPD consolidates the updated experts’ capabilities into the
unified model, whose subsequent interactions provide evidence for the next round of guidance refinement and
data collection. This process connects challenge discovery, execution guidance, trajectory verification, and
capability learning, supporting the continued expansion of effective embodied interaction data. We evaluate
4


OmniBrain-0 on five manipulation and navigation benchmarks—LIBERO-Pro, LIBERO-Plus, RoboCasa365,
RoboTwin 2.0, and R2R-CE Val-Unseen—with additional assessments of spatial understanding, spatial re-
ferring, and real-world manipulation.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• A unified agentic model for manipulation and navigation.
We introduce OmniBrain-0, a compact
high-level agent that coordinates heterogeneous perception, manipulation, navigation, and motion skills
through a shared decision model for planning, orchestration, and recovery.
• Scalable interaction data construction for embodied capability transfer. We construct structured trajec-
tories that preserve observation–decision–feedback dependencies and recovery, and develop a closed-loop
mechanism that combines challenge-driven collection, execution-guidance refinement, and environment
verification to expand training data for compact domain-specialized agents.
• Progressive specialization and multi-expert consolidation. Embodied pretraining, domain-specialized dis-
tillation, and MOPD unify manipulation and navigation expertise while preserving low-level specialization.

Related Work
2.1
Specialist policies for manipulation and navigation
VLA models such as π0 [5], π0.5 [4], and LingBot-VLA [49, 50] learn multitask control from large-scale
robot data and vision-language pretraining. These policies provide a foundation for reusable manipulation
skills [43], while task-level coordination must also account for sub-goal dependencies, execution progress, and
the conditions required by subsequent skills. Yet even π0.5 and LingBot-VLA 2.0 degrade substantially on
long-horizon tasks under object and initial-position shifts [50]. For navigation, NavGPT-2 [66] couples lan-
guage reasoning with a navigation policy, and DualVLN (InternVLA-N1) [47] connects high-level planning
to low-level execution in a dual-system design. Manipulation and navigation nevertheless involve different
observations, action interfaces, and temporal structures, motivating a shared decision layer that can coor-
dinate domain-specific execution. Rather than replacing these policies, OmniBrain-0 invokes them as skills
and learns a shared high-level decision model across both domains.
2.2
Tool-using embodied agents
SayCan [2] and Code as Policies [28] ground task decomposition in executable skills, and Inner Monologue [16]
feeds environmental feedback back into planning. This division between task reasoning and skill execution
provides a basis for composing heterogeneous capabilities, with feedback enabling the planner to reassess
whether intermediate outcomes satisfy the current goal [34]. Agent harnesses, including Harness VLA [61],
RoboHarness [15], and Pigey [14], add state tracking, outcome verification, and recovery through agent loops.
However, their orchestration relies on repeated reasoning by large models at deployment time. OmniBrain-0
instead internalizes planning, progress tracking, and recovery into a compact decision model learned from
interaction experience. Building on this execution paradigm, we retain the tool interfaces and feedback loop
while using curated interaction trajectories to supervise the high-level decisions made within it.
2.3
Agent distillation and expert consolidation
AgentTuning [56] and STEVE-2 [63] transfer agent capabilities via trajectory tuning and hierarchical distil-
lation, but fixed teacher trajectories may not cover states encountered by students. For embodied capability
transfer, useful supervision must preserve the relationship between observations, tool choices, and execution
outcomes, including corrective decisions that lead to successful recovery. On-policy distillation mitigates
this mismatch by supervising student-generated sequences [1, 31]; TurnOPD [68] adds turn-aware super-
vision for agents, and MOPD [35] merges domain-specialized teachers.
Such consolidation complements
trajectory-based specialization: the latter acquires domain-specific decision behaviors, while the former in-
tegrates complementary expert distributions within shared model parameters. In embodied settings, each
decision alters subsequent observations and skill applicability; OmniBrain-0 applies multi-teacher distillation
to consolidate manipulation and navigation expertise, including planning and recovery, into a single model.
5


2.4
Recursive self-improvement of embodied agents
Recursive self-improvement (RSI) uses successive interactions to refine an agent’s guidance or learned policy.
Reflexion [41] stores verbal reflections in episodic memory, while ACE [60] evolves contextual playbooks
through reflection and curation. ReST meets ReAct [3] iteratively trains agents on feedback-filtered trajec-
tories. In robotics, EmbodiRSI [24] targets corrective data collection at policy weaknesses, and AdaRSI [44]
combines harness refinement with selective executor fine-tuning. RE-0 [57] verifies local teacher corrections
before distilling them into embodied code-generating agents. OmniBrain-0 connects rubric-guided reminder
refinement with verified trajectory curation, domain-expert SFT, and MOPD for a decision model shared
across manipulation and navigation. Reminders are validated without changing model parameters; subse-
quent training updates the agent, whose new interactions guide the next improvement round. This cycle
retains successful recoveries while using failed episodes for diagnosis.

3.1

We consider language-conditioned manipulation and navigation tasks, with tool invocation serving as a unified
high-level action representation. Given a task instruction g, OmniBrain-0 selects tools based on current
observations and interaction history, continually updating its decisions through environmental feedback. At
decision step t, the observation and interaction context are defined as

3.2
Skill Interfaces and Context Organization
The agentic execution framework of OmniBrain-0 follows the system design of Harness VLA [61], with its
tools and experience library summarized in Figure 3 (left). OmniBrain-0 uses the general-purpose multimodal
model Qwen3.5-9B [38] as a shared agent, decoupling task-level decision-making from specialized tool exe-
cution. The agent performs instruction understanding, long-horizon task decomposition, tool orchestration,
and failure recovery, while execution modules provide perception, manipulation, navigation, and motion-
control capabilities. A unified invocation-and-feedback interface connects the agent and execution modules
in a closed loop, enabling the same decision model to coordinate heterogeneous tools with different inputs
and control mechanisms.
Unified tool abstraction.
We encapsulate heterogeneous execution capabilities as parameterized semantic
tools. Perception tools provide scene understanding and target localization; VLA tools handle manipula-
tion and contact interactions; navigation tools support goal reaching through VLN policies or geometric
navigators; and motion-control tools provide explicit pose and actuator adjustments. Each tool retains its
native conditioning and low-level implementation while exposing a unified invocation-and-feedback interface.
Thus, the agent shares a common high-level decision mechanism and interaction protocol rather than a single
low-level control policy.
Context-driven tool orchestration.
The agent jointly processes task instructions, current observations, and
historical feedback to select subsequent tools and determine their invocation parameters. Interaction history
supports task progress tracking, while an optional experience library provides priors on task organization, tool
composition, and recovery strategies. Experience guides task-level organization, whereas online perception
grounds tool invocation in the current execution state.
3.3
Closed-Loop Planning and Failure Recovery
At each turn, the agent selects one tool and makes the next decision only after receiving its execution
feedback. High-level tool invocations are executed sequentially and connected through execution outcomes.
The agent continuously updates its decisions based on observations and feedback, adapting target localization,
invocation parameters, or tool selection when execution deviates from expectations. Detailed tool definitions
and implementation specifications are provided in the Appendix A.
4
Embodied Capability Learning and Consolidation
Figure 5 summarizes our three-stage capability transfer framework: embodied pretraining establishes a shared
initialization (Section 4.2), domain-specialized distillation transfers expert interaction experience into spe-
cialized decision-making capabilities (Section 4.3), and multi-teacher on-policy distillation consolidates these
capabilities into a unified shared model (Section 4.4). This process separates capability acquisition from
cross-domain integration, allowing the shared agent to inherit complementary planning, tool orchestration,
and recovery capabilities while preserving the specialization of low-level execution tools.

The pipeline aligns raw logs, images, and environment states into supervision compatible with the student’s

Interaction Normalization.
We parse raw interaction logs and reconstruct each episode as a temporally
ordered sequence of reasoning–tool call–tool feedback units, retaining synchronized images and robot states
at each decision step. Tool calls and feedback are normalized into a unified format, with visual observa-
tions aligned to the corresponding interaction steps. Context unavailable to the student is removed, while
infrastructure errors and incomplete logs are filtered out to obtain executable interaction sequences.
Trajectory Quality Control.
We use rule-based failure detection and visual verification to assess task out-
comes and execution quality, discarding unsuccessful task trajectories. Successful trajectories do not necessar-
ily provide equal training value. Canonical successful trajectories provide reliable demonstrations of standard
task execution, while failure-recovery trajectories offer valuable state-dependent supervision by capturing how
the agent diagnoses and corrects execution failures. In contrast, blind repetitions without new observations
or state information may reinforce ineﬀicient action patterns. We therefore categorize successful trajectories
into three types:
• Canonical trajectories: The task is completed without tool failures, with perception, manipulation, and
termination proceeding as expected.
• Recovery trajectories: One or more grasping, contact, or other execution failures occur, but the agent
subsequently recovers through re-localization, gripper adjustment, re-grasping, or other corrective actions
and eventually receives an environment success signal.
• Noisy trajectories: The agent repeatedly executes the same action without acquiring new observations or
state information, reflecting blind retries rather than feedback-driven decision making.
Both canonical and recovery trajectories are retained, while noisy trajectories are discarded. Canonical trajec-
tories provide standard execution demonstrations, while recovery trajectories capture the failure–diagnosis–
correction–continuation process.
Reasoning Reconstruction.
The raw reasoning text generated by the expert model may introduce unneces-
sary linguistic variation. We therefore rewrite each reasoning step into a structured representation using only
information available at the corresponding decision step, following two principles: state grounding, which
derives the state from tool outputs, robot states, and images, and evidence traceability, which excludes
future information. Specifically, each reasoning step is represented by four fields:
8


• Phase: the current stage of task execution, such as localization, grasping, transport, placement, release,
success verification, or recovery.
• State: the current interaction state, including the held object, gripper and grasp status, target and
destination locations, and task completion status.
• Evidence: relevant visual observations and tool feedback supporting the current state assessment.
• Decision: the next tool to be invoked and its action description determined by the actual tool parameters.
We generate these fields by replaying each trajectory chronologically: tool feedback first updates the interac-
tion state, while visual auditing verifies information not reliably available from tool outputs, such as object
identity, grasp status, and placement.
The Phase, relevant Evidence, and Decision are then determined
from the resulting state and the actual next tool call. Failed actions are marked as recovery operations, while
environment-confirmed completion produces the terminal state. For long-horizon tasks, the State additionally
tracks the current sub-goal and its completion progress. The resulting structured reasoning is independent
of the teacher’s wording and remains one-to-one aligned with each tool call.
Consistency Validation.
We finally check temporal consistency, state–action consistency, and agreement
between visual observations and textual evidence. These checks verify consistency among the state, evidence,
phase, and tool call, including target/destination localization, action–decision alignment, and environment-
verified termination. Evidence-deficient trajectories and those failing consistency validation are discarded,
while canonical successes and successful recovery trajectories are retained as high-quality training data.

4.2
Embodied Pretraining
As shown in Figure 5 (left), we pretrain on a mixture of embodied question-answering data and long-horizon
agent interaction data to establish a shared basis for embodied capabilities in manipulation and navigation.
Embodied question answering provides supervision for scene understanding and task-related knowledge, while
long-horizon interactions guide the model to organize decisions and use feedback in multi-turn tasks through
temporal relationships among instructions, observations, tool invocations, and execution feedback. These
complementary sources connect static embodied understanding with dynamic interactive decision-making,
providing shared knowledge and interaction priors for subsequent specialization. The resulting model, denoted
as OmniBrain-Base with parameters θbase, initializes both the domain teachers and the unified student.

Rubric-Guided Recursive Self-Improvement
We introduce Rubric-Guided Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) to support the scalable construction of effec-
tive embodied interaction data. A Meta-Agent derives step-level guidance from interaction evidence, while
verified trajectories augment domain-expert SFT. MOPD consolidates the updated experts into OmniBrain-0,
whose subsequent interactions support the next round of improvement.
5.1
Rubric-Guided Reminder Policy
Rubric-based policy update.
Let Dk denote the self-collected trajectories at refinement round k, follow-
ing the interaction representation in Equation 3. The initial collection includes both successful and failed
manipulation and navigation episodes. A verifier assesses goal attainment and execution eﬀiciency, provid-
ing outcome evidence for the Meta-Agent to analyze, diagnose, and revise execution guidance. Successful
trajectories reveal effective task progression, while failed and ineﬀicient trajectories expose unresolved execu-
tion challenges. The Meta-Agent relates these outcomes to the corresponding observations, decisions, and
feedback, organizing relevant trajectory patches and rubric candidates into a shared set of robotics rubrics
Uk. New evidence supports adding or revising rubrics, while recurring supporting evidence reinforces applica-
ble criteria. These rubrics capture principles for task prerequisites, skill ordering, progress verification, and
recovery. Figure 6 summarizes rubric-based policy update and reminder-policy validation.

Ablation Study on the Reminder Policy.
Figure 9 isolates the inference-time contribution of the reminder

Ablation Study on the Data Processing Pipeline.
The proposed data processing pipeline is designed to
transform expert interaction data into a format suitable for training compact models. The ablation results
on LIBERO-Pro in Figure 10 demonstrate the impact of key components of the pipeline. When the final
training data excludes expert failure-recovery trajectories and retains only a single successful trajectory, the
model trained on this ablated dataset suffers an 9% drop in success rate. This degradation highlights the value
of failure-recovery trajectories, which provide supervision for actively selecting corrective actions in response
to concrete failure evidence, such as failed grasps or object drops during transportation. Furthermore, when
the expert-generated reasoning is used directly as free-form text rather than being structurally rewritten, the
model trained on the resulting data also exhibits a substantial performance drop. This is because expert
models typically produce lengthy and unconstrained reasoning, and directly using such reasoning to construct
long-horizon training trajectories for compact models can lead to overfitting to the linguistic style of the expert
model, while weakening the learning of visual grounding and decision-making logic.
Figure 10 Data-pipeline ablation on LIBERO-Pro. Success rates for removing reasoning reconstruction, removing
recovery trajectories, and using the full data pipeline across task and swap conditions.
Ablation Study on Experience Library.
Figure 11 evaluates the effect of the Experience Library on agent
execution, using GPT-5.6-Sol with and without access to the library. The library consistently improves the
success rate across all three benchmarks, with gains of 27.7, 6.8, and 26.4 points on LIBERO-Pro, RoboTwin
2.0, and RoboCasa365, respectively. By providing reusable priors from previously collected cross-episode
experiences, the Experience Library helps the agent avoid repeated exploration and make more informed
execution decisions. Since only successful trajectories are retained for training, the Experience Library also
increases the yield of canonical and recovery trajectories available for distillation into OmniBrain-0.
Figure 11 Experience-library ablation. Success rates with and without access to the Experience Library on LIBERO-
Pro, RoboTwin 2.0, and RoboCasa365.

Figures 17 and 18 illustrate the inference-time reminder mechanism introduced in Section 5.1. The reminder
policy is checked against the current interaction context before the agent selects its next tool invocation.
In the manipulation example (Figure 17), the initial move_arm and vla_skill decisions proceed without
reminders. Guidance is triggered at step 2 before grasp and again at step 3 before move_arm, concentrating

the intervention around grasping and the transition to subsequent motion. The later transport motions,

supplies guidance at selected states within an ongoing task, while OmniBrain-0 continues to generate the
tool calls. Aligning the observations, trigger states, and tool sequence makes the timing of reminder guidance
explicit within closed-loop execution.

Conclusion and Limitations
We introduced OmniBrain-0, a compact unified embodied agent that coordinates manipulation and naviga-
tion through a shared high-level decision model. Our progressive training framework combines embodied
pretraining, domain-specialized agent distillation, and multi-teacher on-policy distillation to transfer and
consolidate planning, skill orchestration, and recovery capabilities. A structured data pipeline converts ex-
pert interactions into state-grounded supervision, retaining both canonical successes and successful recoveries.
Rubric-Guided Recursive Self-Improvement further connects execution evidence, reminder-policy refinement,
verified trajectory collection, and expert consolidation, supporting the continued construction of effective
interaction data. Simulated manipulation and navigation benchmarks show strong task performance with
substantially lower token consumption and shorter task completion times than the closed-source teacher, while
spatial and real-world evaluations further assess grounding and physical applicability. These results support
learning transferable embodied decision-making capabilities in a compact model while retaining specialized
low-level skills.
OmniBrain-0 remains dependent on the coverage and reliability of its low-level skills, and gaps persist on
unseen task compositions and spatial localization. Real-world evaluation also covers a limited set of tasks. Fu-
ture work will broaden task and embodiment coverage, strengthen grounding and recovery under distribution
shifts, and study sustained improvement through larger and more diverse verified interaction corpora.

Overview. This appendix provides additional details on the agentic system, evaluation, training data, and task
execution. Section A presents the manipulation and navigation tools and the organization of the Experience
Library. Section B specifies the benchmark settings, evaluation episodes, and metrics for simulated tasks,
spatial understanding and referring, and real-world manipulation. Section C describes the VQA and agentic

A
Details of the Agentic System Design
This section provides additional details of the agentic system design, including the tools developed for the
VLA and VLN tasks, as well as the construction and utilization of the Experience Library.
The tools
provide standardized interfaces for interacting with heterogeneous model-based and scripted skills, while
the Experience Library organizes reusable knowledge and interaction experiences accumulated from expert
models.
Tools.
We design a unified set of tools for VLA and VLN tasks, serving as the interface through which the
agent interacts with and acts upon the physical environment. The detailed definitions of the tools are as
follows.
Figure A.1 General Tools: Shared tools for environment observation, pixel-to-metric localization, text-guided object
segmentation, execution termination, and mobile-base control.
Figure A.2
VLA-Specific Tools: Tools for end-effector motion, wrist orientation, gripper control, and multi-step
manipulation or interaction through a learned VLA policy.
30


Figure A.3 VLN-Specific Tools: Tools for panoramic observation, instruction following, navigation-goal grounding and
execution, point-directed navigation, and trajectory backtracking.
Experience Library.
To collect diverse and successful expert interaction trajectories, we follow the data
collection paradigm of Harness VLA [61], allowing expert models to explore the same task environment
across multiple episodes and autonomously reflect on the causes of failures and the factors contributing to
successful executions. These cross-episode experiences are then manually reviewed and consolidated into an
Experience Library, which provides reusable priors for subsequent model execution. The library is organized
into four complementary categories, covering general principles, task execution patterns, failure recovery, and
the capability boundaries of VLA/VLN skills.
1. General Principles. Reusable perception, coordinate, gripper, controller, navigation, and safety knowledge
that applies across tasks.
Examples include image-first decision making, object-to-end-effector offsets,
appropriate camera/view selection, spatial landmark awareness, and short, capped contact or navigation
motions.
2. Task Execution Patterns.
Reusable recipes for pick–transport–place, containment, articulated fixtures,
stacking, handover, ranking, multi-object ordering, and navigation. These experiences describe task phases,
skill composition, and verification gates rather than fixed scene coordinates, and cover both VLA-based
manipulation and VLN-based navigation behaviors.
3. Failure Cases and Recovery Strategies. Negative evidence and recovery strategies accumulated from un-
successful executions, including common failure modes, their observable symptoms, recovery actions, and
appropriate execution budgets. For VLA tasks, these may involve grasp failures, collisions, or inaccu-
rate object localization; for VLN tasks, they include navigation deviations, incorrect waypoint selection,
ambiguous landmark grounding, and ineffective recovery behaviors.
4. VLA/VLN Capability Boundaries. Knowledge about the respective capabilities and limitations of VLA and
VLN skills, including the conditions under which to invoke learned VLA/VLN policies, switch between
them, or fall back to scripted tools. These experiences capture when learned skills are more reliable and
when explicit geometric or rule-based actions are preferable.
B
Benchmarks and Evaluation Protocol
We use a common episode-level protocol across the closed-loop benchmarks. Each episode is associated with
its benchmark, task suite, task, perturbation, random seed, and model version. Duplicate records of the
same evaluation case are removed before aggregation. The planner receives the task instruction, camera
observations, robot state or proprioception, and tool feedback.
Hidden task specifications, ground-truth
object poses, and evaluation labels are used for scene construction or scoring and are not supplied to the
planner. The planner also has no access to the environment state of other evaluation episodes.
An episode ends when the environment confirms success, the planner requests termination, an execution
limit is reached, or an infrastructure failure prevents further interaction. Completion claimed by the planner
is accepted only when supported by the environment’s success criterion. Unless a benchmark specifies a
31


macro-average, Overall is computed as the total number of successful episodes divided by the total number of
evaluated episodes. Task failures, timeouts, simulator initialization failures, infrastructure errors, and invalid
or missing results are recorded separately. When infrastructure failures are retried, both the original attempt
and the retry are retained in the evaluation records.
LIBERO-Pro.
LIBERO-Pro [67] is an extension of the LIBERO benchmark designed to evaluate the gener-
alization capabilities of vision-language-action (VLA) models. It introduces controlled perturbations to the
original LIBERO tasks, including changes to manipulated objects, object positions, task instructions, task
configurations, and environments. Following Harness VLA [61], we evaluate eight LIBERO-Pro conditions:
Spatial-T, Spatial-S, Object-T, Object-S, Goal-T, Goal-S, L10-T, and L10-S, where T denotes task
or instruction redirection and S denotes spatial swapping of object configurations. Each condition contains
ten tasks evaluated with seeds s1–s10, yielding 100 episodes per condition and 800 episodes overall.
LIBERO-Plus.
LIBERO-Plus [13] extends LIBERO with 10,030 task variants spanning camera viewpoints,
robot initial states, language instructions, lighting, background textures, sensor noise, and object layout.
These variations assess robustness to visual, linguistic, and physical changes in manipulation settings. For
LIBERO-Plus, we evaluate four suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10. From each suite, we
sample 210 task variants, evenly distributed across seven perturbation types: Camera, Robot, Language,
Light, Background, Noise, and Layout. Each perturbation type contains 30 tasks per suite, all evaluated
with seed s0, yielding 840 episodes overall.
RoboTwin 2.0.
RoboTwin 2.0 [8] is a simulation benchmark for evaluating generalist policies in bimanual
robotic manipulation, featuring 50 diverse manipulation tasks. It provides two evaluation settings: a Clean
setting (Easy), which largely preserves the standard environment, and a Randomized setting (Hard), which
introduces substantial environmental variations. This design enables evaluation of both task execution capa-
bility and robustness to distribution shifts. Following Harness VLA [61], we evaluate all 50 tasks under both
the Clean and Randomized settings, with five seeds per task in each setting, resulting in 2 × 50 × 5 = 500
episodes. The planner receives task instructions, camera observations, robot state, and access to the available
bimanual tools and skills. Scene randomization and ground-truth simulator state are used to construct and
evaluate episodes; hidden evaluation labels are not provided to the planner. Success is determined by the
simulator’s built-in task completion criterion. We report separate success rates for Clean and Randomized
environments and an Overall rate across all 500 episodes.
RoboCasa365.
RoboCasa365 [36] is a large-scale simulation benchmark for evaluating generalist robot
policies on everyday household tasks. The benchmark includes both atomic tasks targeting individual ma-
nipulation skills and composite tasks that require sequencing multiple skills for longer-horizon activities,
enabling evaluation of general task-solving capabilities and generalization across diverse environments and
task compositions. Following Harness VLA [61], we evaluate on the RoboCasa365 Target-50 split, comprising
18 Atomic-Seen, 16 Composite-Seen, and 16 Composite-Unseen tasks. The Seen groups correspond
to task templates present in pretraining, while Unseen contains templates held out from pretraining. We
use held-out seeds s1–s10 for Atomic-Seen and s1–s5 for both composite groups, yielding 18 × 10 = 180,
16 × 5 = 80, and 16 × 5 = 80 episodes, respectively, for 340 episodes overall. RoboCasa365 uses a task-macro
Overall: each task first receives a success rate computed over its evaluation episodes, and the 50 task rates
are then averaged with equal weight.
Thus, the three split groups contribute 18, 16, and 16 task rates,
respectively, and this Overall differs from the episode-micro average used by the general rule above.
R2R-CE.
R2R-CE [23] is the continuous-environment adaptation of the Room-to-Room (R2R) vision-and-
language navigation benchmark. It ports language-annotated navigation episodes to the Habitat simulator,
where an agent follows a natural-language route instruction through reconstructed indoor environments. Un-
like the original navigation-graph setting, the agent moves through continuous space rather than jumping
between predefined viewpoints. We evaluate a fixed subset of 100 episodes from the Val-Unseen split. The
planner receives the navigation instruction, camera observations, and execution feedback, and uses Smart-
Way [40] for navigation. Ground-truth shortest paths and goal positions are used for scoring only. We report
Navigation Error (NE), the final distance to the goal; Success Rate (SR), the percentage of episodes ending
within 3 m of the goal; Oracle Success Rate (OSR), the percentage whose trajectory reaches within 3 m of
the goal at any point; and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), which accounts for both success and
32


path eﬀiciency. All four metrics are computed on the same 100 episodes, with service failures, invalid actions,
timeouts, and navigation failures recorded separately.
EmbSpatial-Bench.
EmbSpatial-Bench [12] contains 3640 multiple-choice visual questions derived from
MP3D, AI2-THOR, and ScanNet scenes. It tests six egocentric relations: above, below, left, right, close, and
far. MP3D contributes 1201 questions, AI2-THOR contributes 1239, and ScanNet contributes 1200. Each
input consists of an image, a spatial question, and candidate answers. Accuracy is the percentage of questions
answered correctly. Table 6 reports accuracy for each source environment and across all questions; Overall
therefore weights each environment by its question count.
RefSpatial-Bench.
RefSpatial-Bench [65] evaluates spatial referring from an image and a spatially con-
strained instruction. Its Location and Placement sets contain 100 examples each, requiring target-object
localization and free-space selection, respectively. Unseen comprises 77 examples drawn from these sets with
spatial-relation combinations absent from the benchmark authors’ RefSpatial training data. A prediction
is correct when the output point falls within the annotated target mask. Table 7 reports the percentage
of correct predictions for Location, Placement, and Unseen. Overall weights each category by its size: 100
examples for Location, 100 for Placement, and 77 for Unseen. Because Unseen overlaps the two task sets,
Overall summarizes the evaluation categories rather than accuracy on a disjoint set of 277 examples.
Real-world manipulation.
The real-world manipulation suite comprises three language-conditioned tasks:
block stacking, where the robot places the yellow cube on the red cube; apple placement, where the robot
picks up the apple and places it in the purple bowl; and drawer opening, where the robot grasps the handle
and pulls the drawer open. These tasks cover precise object placement, pick-and-place manipulation, and
contact-rich articulated-object manipulation. Each method is evaluated over 20 independent trials per task.
A trial is successful if the robot completes the task within the allotted execution budget: placing the red
cube stably on the blue cube, placing and releasing the apple inside the blue bowl, or opening the drawer to
the specified target position. We report per-task Success Rate (SR), defined as the percentage of successful
trials, and the macro-average SR across the three tasks.

Datasets and Experimental Details
VQA Data.
We construct our embodied pretraining corpus from EmbSpatial-SFT [12], RefSpatial [65],
ShareRobot [18], and RoboInter-VQA [26]. EmbSpatial-SFT provides instruction-tuning data for egocentric
spatial relationship understanding and object localization, covering six fundamental spatial relations. RefS-
patial further enriches spatial understanding and referring with 2.5M samples and 20M question-answer pairs
from 2D images, 3D embodied videos, and simulated scenes, spanning 31 spatial relations and multi-step
spatial reasoning. ShareRobot provides heterogeneous supervision for task planning, object affordance, and
end-effector trajectories, spanning 23 source datasets, 12 embodiments, and 107 atomic task types, with over
1.0M planning-oriented VQA pairs. RoboInter-VQA complements these datasets with approximately 2.3M
embodied VQA samples covering spatial and temporal reasoning, intermediate-representation understanding,
and task planning. From these sources, we select samples focusing on spatial grounding, spatial reasoning,
affordance understanding, and task planning, and incorporate agentic interaction data at a predefined ratio,
ultimately obtaining approximately 820K embodied pretraining samples.
Agentic Data.
The interaction records preserve task instructions, visual observations, robot states, decision
text, tool calls, and environment feedback in temporal order. We apply the data pipeline in Section 4.1
to verify task completion, remove incomplete, invalid, or noisy records, and normalize retained trajectories
into multi-turn multimodal conversations. Each decision and tool invocation is aligned with the observations
and feedback available at that step. The resulting corpus combines successful trajectories from the initial
expert collection with additional verified successful trajectories collected through the RSI Data Flywheel.
LIBERO/LIBERO-Pro, RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365, and R2R-CE contribute 2,349, 513, 880, and 1,246 us-
able expert demonstrations, respectively. Supervision targets the planner’s decision text and tool invocations,
with observations and environment feedback as conditioning context.

Expert Collection and Execution Configuration.
We use GPT-5.6-Sol and GLM-5.3-Flash to collect closed-
loop interaction trajectories on LIBERO/LIBERO-Pro, RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365, and R2R-CE. At each
decision step, an expert receives visual observations and available state feedback, together with an experience
library that provides reusable guidance on task organization, tool composition, and failure recovery. It selects
a tool and its parameters, then conditions its next decision on the resulting observations and execution
status. Following the tool-based execution paradigm of Harness VLA [61], a shared invocation-and-feedback
interface separates high-level decisions from benchmark-specific low-level execution. Available manipulation
skills include π0.5 [4], LingBot-VLA [49], and RLDX-1 [20]; navigation uses SmartWay [40]. Detailed tool
definitions are given in Appendix A.
Low-Level Model Sizes.
The manipulation policies used in our experiments contain approximately 3.617B
parameters for π0.5, 4.197B for LingBot-VLA, and 6.913B for RLDX-1. These counts refer to the complete
deployed policy weights, including vision and action components, rather than only the language backbone.
For navigation, SmartWay’s waypoint predictor contains 54M parameters; this count applies to the way-
point prediction component alone. InternVLA-N1, used as a navigation comparison model in the qualitative
experiments, has 7B parameters. Here, B and M denote billions and millions of parameters, respectively.
RSI Model Configuration.
GPT-6.1-Sol serves as the Meta-Agent for rubric analysis and reminder-policy
refinement in Section 5.1. GPT-5.6-Sol serves as the judge model for reminder-policy evaluation.
Hardware Configuration and Training Parameters.
We train Qwen3.5-4B/9B on 16 NVIDIA H20 GPUs
using bfloat16 precision. Embodied Pretraining runs for 2 epochs with a batch size of 1, a learning rate of
5 × 10−6, 4 gradient accumulation steps, and utilizes the Adam optimizer with a weight decay of 0.05 and a
warmup ratio of 0.1. We then perform Domain-Specialized Distillation for 3 epochs using a batch size of 2, a
learning rate of 1 × 10−5, 2 gradient accumulation steps, and the same Adam optimizer setup (weight decay
0.05, warmup ratio 0.1). Finally, MOPD is applied for 2 epochs with a batch size of 2, a learning rate of
2 × 10−6, and 2 gradient accumulation steps. Multiple frozen teacher models are independently deployed on
dedicated GPUs. MOPD-specific configurations include a rollout temperature of 1.0, a distillation support
top-k of 64, a Reverse KL distillation objective, and ρV LA = ρV LN = 0.5.
