## Abstract 

Self-disclosures on the Web support diverse psychological analyses, from recognizing expressed experiences to identifying symptom-relevant information. We study a shared, question-conditioned decision interface for this heterogeneous problem in content analy-sis. We organize PsyJudgeBench, comprising 13 annotated datasets and 82 questions across six psychological areas, while preserv-ing source-specific criteria and masks for unobserved labels. We introduce PsyJev, a System-1 model that directly produces distri-butions over answers specified in each request. Joint supervised training establishes the shared judgment function, followed by Judgment-Anchored RLCD (JA-RLCD), which adapts logit-space reward optimization to heterogeneous psychological judgments through balanced supervision, selective retention of correct SFT predictions, and question-group reward optimization. The Qwen3.5-based 2B, 4B, and 9B models achieve 64.33, 66.21, and 67.56 average test F1, respectively. The 9B model exceeds the strongest external comparison by 2.54 points. Controlled 2B comparisons examine the training stages and anchored refinement. Further analyses exam-ine question-conditioned judgment, inference cost, and probability quality, complemented by qualitative comparisons across models.

## 1 Introduction 

Social media, online forums, and Web communities continuously produce spontaneous accounts of personal experiences, emotional difficulties, relationships, and everyday stress. These self-disclosures provide a rich source of Web content for psychological analysis, supporting tasks ranging from recognizing expressed experiences to identifying symptom-relevant information and questionnaire answerability. However, analyzing the same content for different purposes requires distinct judgments. A post may mention a symp-tom without expressing it, or contain symptom-relevant informa-tion without providing enough detail to answer a questionnaire item. Such distinctions matter when organizing and analyzing Web content across different psychological tasks, where similar textual expressions may need to be interpreted under different decision cri-teria. This motivates a shared framework for addressing a challenge in psychological analysis of Web content: distinguishing question-specific judgments across heterogeneous datasets drawn from the social Web while preserving their source-specific decision criteria. Research on web-based psychological analysis has produced specialized datasets and models for diverse targets, from symptom relevance and questionnaire answerability to stressors and interper-sonal risk factors [10, 14, 15, 18, 22]. Recent work uses multi-task learning and instruction tuning to bring these tasks into shared frameworks. For instance, MentaLLaMA and the IMHI benchmark unify mental health analysis by generating joint predictions and text explanations [33]. The constituent tasks retain distinct label semantics, answer spaces, and decision criteria. A shared predic-tion interface therefore needs to preserve these distinctions while supporting categorical evaluation. Building such a framework poses three main challenges. First, 

heterogeneous judgment semantics : different analytical questions may require distinct criteria and answer sets, even when applied to the same text. This calls for predictions conditioned on the ques-tion rather than the text alone. Second, fragmented supervision :existing datasets differ in annotation targets, label schemes, and coverage, complicating their integration into a shared judgment format while retaining source-specific criteria. Third, uneven super-vision : differences in dataset sizes and label frequencies can favor well-represented tasks and frequent classes during joint training. Addressing these challenges calls for a shared question-conditioned judgment framework and a training strategy that accounts for het-erogeneous, imbalanced supervision. To address these challenges, we introduce PsyJudgeBench, a benchmark for question-conditioned psychological analysis. It or-ganizes 13 existing datasets into 82 registered questions across six psychological content areas. Rather than merging heterogeneous tasks into a single label space, PsyJudgeBench represents each judgment through an analytical question, its legal answer set, and source-specific decision criteria retained through documented label mappings. Missing-label masking and coordinated dataset splits support joint training and task-specific evaluation under partial supervision. The resulting benchmark also enables evaluating how models distinguish different judgments about the same text. Building on this task formulation, we introduce PsyJev, a System-1 model that directly predicts distributions over question-defined answers. Given an input text, an analytical question, and candidate options, PsyJev produces a categorical answer distribution through a shared readout, without intermediate response generation or post-hoc category extraction [1, 5]. To account for uneven supervision across sources, we further propose Judgment-Anchored RLCD (JA-RLCD), adapting logit-space RLCD [17] to heterogeneous psycho-logical judgments. Its judgment anchor combines source/question weighting and class-balanced supervision with selective retention of correct SFT predictions. A Brier-based question-group reward further refines predictions through logit-space perturbations and pathwise gradients. Across 2B, 4B, and 9B model scales, PsyJev out-performs the corresponding same-backbone answer-SFT baselines.

Further analyses examine question-conditioned discrimination on co-annotated texts, inference latency, and proba-bility quality. Our main contributions are as follows: 

• A unified psychological judgment benchmark. We con-struct PsyJudgeBench, organizing 13 heterogeneous data-sets into a shared question-defined judgment framework that preserves task-specific criteria and supports unified training and evaluation across questions. 

• System-1 modeling with judgment-anchored reward refinement. We propose PsyJev for direct probabilistic psychological judgment and JA-RLCD, a joint refinement formulation that adapts logit-space RLCD through a class-balanced judgment anchor, selective SFT-reference reten-tion, and question-group reward optimization under un-even supervision. 

• Systematic empirical evaluation. We evaluate PsyJev across model scales and heterogeneous psychological tasks, with comparisons against benchmark-adapted baselines and matched training continuations. Ablations and fur-ther analyses assess the training components, question-conditioned discrimination, inference efficiency, and proba-bility quality. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Psychological Analysis 

Psychological analysis of social Web content spans diverse tasks, including identifying depression and anxiety indications, inter-personal difficulties, self-perceptions, and emotional experiences [6, 9, 10, 15, 25]. Other resources address questionnaire answerabil-ity, symptom relevance, stressors, cognitive distortions, wellness dimensions, and crisis-related expressions [2, 7, 8, 14, 18, 22, 26, 27]. These tasks involve related psychological content but differ in their analytical targets and annotation criteria. Existing mod-els range from domain-adapted encoders such as MentalBERT to instruction-tuned language models for psychological prediction and explanation [16, 31, 35]. Flexible classification approaches, in-cluding GLiClass and GLiNER2, further support label- or schema-conditioned predictions [28, 34]. MentaLLaMA and its IMHI bench-mark extend psychological analysis toward multi-task instruction learning over social-media content [33]. Building on this direction, PsyJudgeBench organizes heterogeneous psychological tasks as question-defined judgments, while PsyJev learns a shared direct-prediction function and refines it through JA-RLCD.

## 3 PsyJudgeBench 

PsyJudgeBench organizes 13 annotated datasets into 53,775 source records and 222,191 observed judgments. Figure 1 summarizes their coverage across six thematic groups: depression/anxiety-related information, self and relationships, stress and distress, cognition, wellness, and crisis experiences. Construction involves a question registry, source-specific annotation conversion, and coordinated data partitions. 

Question-defined tasks. PsyJudgeBench represents psychological analysis tasks as question-defined judgments, each specifying a judgment target, a decision criterion, and a set of valid answers. This formulation accommodates different analytical requirements while preserving their original annotation criteria. For example, PRIMATE assesses whether a text provides enough information to answer a PHQ-9 item, BDI-Sen evaluates sentence relevance to a BDI-II symptom [14, 22], and MultiWD identifies mentions of specific wellness dimensions. Each judgment is represented as 

(𝑥, 𝑞, A𝑞 , 𝑦 ), comprising the source text, question, valid answer set, and observed answer. The resulting registry contains 82 questions, including 79 binary and three multiclass questions. 

> 2PsyJev: Psychological Analysis via System-1 Judgment

Annotation harmonization. Following these question definitions, we convert heterogeneous source annotations into observed judg-ments while retaining their original labeling criteria. Binary and categorical labels are mapped to their corresponding answer sets, while multi-label and category–time annotations are expanded into separate questions. For Thinking Traps, we normalize category names and aggregate repeated completions to recover 15-category label sets. For CRADLE, seven crisis categories combined with ongoing/past status yield 14 questions. For TherapistQA, we re-tain the dominant distortion category and use only the patient’s question, excluding therapist responses and annotated spans. After conversion, we check for conflicting annotations associated with the same text and judgment target. We remove two TherapistQA records with identical questions but conflicting dominant labels and mask 22 conflicting judgments across 11 same-text/same-target keys. Unaffected judgments remain available for supervision, while source identifiers and annotation provenance, including gold/silver distinctions, are preserved. 

Partial supervision and coordinated splits. The harmonized records provide supervision only for questions with observed annotations; unannotated questions are excluded rather than treated as nega-tive examples. To construct consistent evaluation partitions, we coordinate records sharing identical texts or available source-group identifiers and group identified near-duplicates to reduce overlap across splits. When grouping conflicts with the original partitions, held-out assignments take precedence. The resulting benchmark contains 33,511 training, 5,852 validation, and 14,412 test source records. These counts refer to source records rather than unique texts. Further details on data provenance, partitioning, and dupli-cate checks are provided in Appendix A. 

## 4 PsyJev 

PsyJev is a System-1-style model for unified psychological judg-ment. Given a text, an analytical question, and its legal answers, PsyJev directly produces a categorical distribution through a shared, question-conditioned readout, without autoregressive answer gen-eration. Its training proceeds in two stages: joint supervised fine-tuning (SFT) learns the shared decision function across heteroge-neous tasks, followed by Judgment-Anchored RLCD (JA-RLCD), which combines balanced supervision, selective SFT-reference re-tention, and question-group reward optimization through logit-space exploration. Figure 2 illustrates the overall framework. 

## 4.1 System-1-Style Direct Judgment 

PsyJev implements direct psychological judgment through a question-conditioned, constrained prediction interface. Rather than gener-ating and parsing free-form responses, it evaluates the admissible answers using their corresponding language-model logits and di-rectly produces a normalized probability distribution. 

Question-conditioned representation. Each candidate is as-signed a distinct answer identifier, allowing different psychological judgments to be expressed through the same input format. A shared Qwen3.5 backbone processes the complete judgment request and produces a contextual representation at the answer position.

## 4.3 Judgment-Anchored RLCD 

RLCD explores probabilistic decisions through logit-space pertur-bations and reward evaluation [17]. Building on this formulation, JA-RLCD addresses uneven source, question, and answer frequen-cies in psychological judgments through a joint refinement ob-jective: a judgment anchor combines class-balanced supervision and selective retention of correct SFT predictions, while a Brier-based reward emphasizes higher-error question groups within each microbatch. Starting from the SFT checkpoint, we treat the legal-answer distribution as a judgment policy and refine it through paired Gaussian perturbations and differentiable reward optimiza-tion. For each source-specific microbatch of observed judgments, reward gradients are computed through pathwise differentiation and combined with the anchor gradients before accumulation and AdamW updates. ## 5 Experiments 5.1 Experimental Setup 

5.1.1 Benchmark and Metrics. We evaluate PsyJev on PsyJudgeBench using the coordinated splits described in Section 3. We report positive-class F1 for binary questions and macro-F1 for multiclass questions, averaging across observed questions within each dataset. The overall score equally weights the 13 datasets. We assess proba-bility quality using normalized negative log-likelihood (NLL), Brier score, and expected calibration error (ECE) with 15 equal-width bins. NLL is normalized by log |A 𝑞 | to account for varying answer-set sizes. Efficiency is measured by model computation time per judgment. 

5.1.2 Baselines. We compare with 18 benchmark-adapted base-lines and the zero-shot Jev API. The adapted baselines fall into three groups. Compact classification and decision models include Men-talBERT, MentalRoBERTa, ModernBERT-large, GLiClass, GLiNER2, and Laya-multilingual [16, 17, 28, 30, 34]. Laya uses its released architecture and checkpoint; its reported result follows benchmark SFT and continuation with its native RLCD objective. The 2–4B language models are FLAN-T5-XL, Qwen3.5-2B/4B, Llama-3.2-3B, Phi-4-mini, and Gemma-3-4B [3, 12, 19, 20, 24]. The 7–9B models include MentaLLaMA-7B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B, Gemma-2-9B, and Qwen3.5-9B [4, 11, 23, 24, 32, 33]. All 18 models are adapted on the benchmark training partition. Classification and decision models predict the observed task labels directly. Language models learn to produce the corresponding answers and are eval-uated by scoring the allowed answer sequences. We additionally evaluate Typesafe’s Jev ( jev-1.14.0 ) through its official API with-out benchmark fine-tuning or demonstrations [1]. Appendix B.3 specifies both adaptation and API protocols. 

5.1.3 Implementation Details. We initialize PsyJev-2B/4B/9B from the corresponding Qwen3.5 text backbones and train each model with joint SFT followed by JA-RLCD, using one epoch per stage. Source texts are truncated to 512 tokens, with batches of up to 64 texts. The 2B model updates all parameters at a learning rate of 2 × 10 −6 in both stages. The 4B/9B models use LoRA (rank 16, scaling 32, zero dropout), with learning rates of 2 × 10 −4 for SFT and 2 × 10 −6 for JA-RLCD. All models use AdamW with weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping at 1.0, and 3% warmup followed by cosine decay. Backbone computation uses BF16, and the final checkpoint of each stage is retained. We measure model computation time using a single H100 80GB GPU, one text per request, and a fixed 13-text training probe shared by all timed models; classification metrics use the complete test partition. Further details appear in Appendices B and B.4. 

## 5.2 Performance Evaluation 

Table 1 shows that PsyJev consistently achieves the highest average F1 within each model-size group, reaching 64.33, 66.21, and 67.56 at 2B, 4B, and 9B, respectively. Compared with the corresponding Qwen3.5 answer models, these results represent gains of 4.01, 1.71, and 3.88 points. Notably, the 4B and 9B variants also outperform the strongest external baseline, despite the smaller size of the former. Across datasets, PsyJev shows notable gains on tasks involving cog-nitive distortions and wellness-related judgments, although gains vary across question criteria and are not uniform across all data-sets. The general-purpose Jev API achieves 52.91 average F1 in zero-shot evaluation, while the benchmark-adapted PsyJev vari-ants perform substantially better, providing a zero-shot reference alongside the benchmark-adapted comparisons. Overall, these re-sults demonstrate the effectiveness of PsyJev across heterogeneous psychological judgment tasks and model scales.

5.3.1 Training Stages. JA-RLCD achieves 64.33 average F1, im-proving upon SFT by 3.65 points. It also outperforms continued cross-entropy (CE) training and adapted RLCD by 2.61 and 5.46 points, respectively. Continued CE controls for additional training, while adapted RLCD combines CE supervision with the original RLCD’s noisy-logit score-function objective. These comparisons show that additional training alone does not explain the improve-ment and demonstrate the effectiveness of the overall JA-RLCD formulation. Since adapted RLCD differs in both its reward formu-lation and gradient estimator, the comparison does not isolate the contribution of either component. 

5.3.2 Component Ablations. Class-balanced CE alone achieves 63.29 F1, compared with 63.70 for anchor-only training, which also retains selective reference KL. Replacing the question-group/tail reward with a per-judgment Brier reward gives 63.36 F1 while retaining the anchor, perturba-tions, and pathwise estimator. The 0.97-point advantage supports the combined group/tail reward formulation. Removing class costs reduces F1 to 62.15; removing reference KL or replacing pathwise differentiation with a paired score-function estimator gives 64.12 and 63.91, respectively. ## 5.4 Analysis 

Beyond overall performance, we analyze PsyJev’s ability to dis-tinguish question-specific judgments, its inference efficiency, the reliability of its predicted probabilities, and its transfer to an unseen dataset, complemented by qualitative examples. 

5.4.1 Question-Conditioned Judgment. We examine whether Psy-Jev can distinguish different psychological judgments about the same text. Specifically, we evaluate co-annotated binary question pairs with opposite ground-truth labels, where successful predic-tion requires both questions to be answered correctly. We compare PsyJev-4B with GLiClass, Gemma-2-9B, and the corresponding Qwen3.5-4B answer model using identical texts, questions, and semantic answer sets. As shown in Table 4, PsyJev correctly answers both questions in 70.06% of the 35,992 pairs from eight sources, outperforming the same-size answer model (66.24%), Gemma-2-9B (67.85%), and GLiClass (55.84%). Compared with the answer model, same-answer errors decrease from 31.95% to 28.09%, with a paired text-group 95% confidence interval of 1.73–5.96 percentage points for the im-provement in paired correctness. These results indicate stronger question-conditioned discrimination on this matched-pair subset, where the same text requires different answers under different crite-ria. ## 6 Conclusion 

We presented PsyJudgeBench and PsyJev for unified psycholog-ical analysis of Web content through question-conditioned judg-ments. PsyJudgeBench organizes heterogeneous tasks while re-taining their distinct decision criteria, and PsyJev enables direct probabilistic prediction with JA-RLCD for judgment-anchored re-finement. Across 2B, 4B, and 9B scales, PsyJev achieves average F1 scores of 64.33, 66.21, and 67.56, respectively, outperforming the corresponding answer-SFT baselines. Additional analyses exam-ine question-conditioned discrimination, inference efficiency, and probability quality. These results demonstrate the value of a shared, question-conditioned approach to heterogeneous psychological content analysis.

## B.3 Baseline Protocols 

All benchmark-adapted baselines use the same cleaned records, question registry, and observed-label masks. Fixed-head classifiers use question-specific output heads, while description-driven mod-els preserve the original decision criteria in their input schemas. Answer-SFT models score complete legal-answer sequences, includ-ing EOS. All models use their final training checkpoints, except ModernBERT, which selects among three epochs based on valida-tion F1, followed by NLL and earlier-epoch tie-breaking. MentaL-LaMA undergoes benchmark-specific answer SFT from its existing mental-health instruction-tuned checkpoint [33]. 

Open-source Laya. We reproduce Laya’s SFT and RLCD training pipeline on PsyJudgeBench using the released Laya-multilingual checkpoint (revision 1720e3e3 ), preserving its native architecture and RLCD objective. We first perform one epoch of full-parameter SFT (524 updates, learning rate 10 −5), followed by one epoch of RLCD (524 updates, learning rate 2 × 10 −6). RLCD combines cross-entropy with score-function optimization over Gaussian-perturbed answer logits, using four samples, a noise schedule from 0 .4 to 0 .1, and spherical/ranked-probability reward weights of 0 .75 /1.0. Both stages use the benchmark training partition and are evaluated using their final checkpoints. 

Jev API evaluation. We evaluate jev-1.14.0 through Typesafe’s official API on all 44,999 test judgments. Each request includes the source text, question criterion, and legal answers, with the source text capped at 512 Qwen tokens. Binary and multiclass questions use Noul and Choice, respectively. The API is evaluated zero-shot without benchmark fine-tuning or demonstrations, and its returned probabilities are aligned with the benchmark’s semantic labels. All 84 invalid responses remain in the evaluation as incorrect predictions; the invalid label is excluded from the legal-class macro average when computing F1. Results follow the same question- and source-level aggregation as the main evaluation. 

Matched refinement controls. All direct-scoring 2B continuations start independently from the same final SFT checkpoint and retain the same records, registry, observed-label masks, record sched-ule, trainable parameters, optimizer, and 524 additional updates. Adapted RLCD combines ordinary CE with Laya’s categorical score-function objective [17]: four independent centered Gaussian logit actions, advantages centered and normalized by their standard de-viation, and reward weights of 0.75 for the spherical term and 1.0 for ranked probability. ## B.4 Unified Timing Protocol 

Model computation time is measured on a shared probe comprising 13 training texts (the second record in each source’s stored training order) and 64 observed judgments. Each request contains one text and its observed questions and is repeated five times after warmup. All models run on a single NVIDIA H100 80GB using BF16 and CUDA Graphs. CUDA events measure GPU execution, including text prefill and answer scoring, while excluding loading, tokeniza-tion, data transfer, and graph capture. Inputs reside on the GPU, and latency is reported as total measured time divided by the number of judgments. Each model retains its native scoring mechanism under the same protocol. For the matched 4B comparison, full-answer and first-token scoring share the Qwen3.5-4B answer-SFT checkpoint, while PsyJev uses its final JA-RLCD checkpoint. Their respective latencies are 25.25, 11.87, and 12.01 ms per judgment.
