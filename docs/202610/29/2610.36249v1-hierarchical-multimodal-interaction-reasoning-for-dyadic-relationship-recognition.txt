Hierarchical Multimodal Interaction Reasoning for
Dyadic Relationship Recognition
Wang Tang, Fethiye Irmak Dogan, Linbo Qing, Ce Zhu, Fellow, IEEE, Hatice Gunes, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—Dyadic relationship recognition aims to identify the
latent social relationship between two individuals from their
multimodal interactions. Existing methods typically project het-
erogeneous cues into a shared representation and optimise a flat
classification objective, overlooking that relationships may rely on
different interaction patterns. For instance, distant relationships
can often be inferred from direct behavioural correspondence,
whereas close relationships are better reflected by coordinated
affective, behavioural, and multimodal responses. To address this
limitation, we propose HiRe-DRR, a Hierarchical Multimodal
Interaction Reasoning framework that organises relationship
evidence from three complementary perspectives: feature level,
interaction type, and relationship hierarchy. HiRe-DRR decom-
poses facial, bodily, acoustic, and textual cues into low- and
high-level representations and constructs homogeneous and het-
erogeneous cross-participant interactions. We further introduce
the Mixture-of-Interaction Transformer (MoI-Former), which
treats diverse interactions as specialised experts and employs
relation-aware sparse routing to identify relationship-relevant
evidence while preserving contextual interaction information.
The proposed Hierarchical Conditional Loss (HC-Loss) exploits
the hierarchical nature of social relationships through coarse-
to-fine supervision, enabling structured relationship reasoning.
Extensive experiments on three dyadic relationship benchmarks
demonstrate that HiRe-DRR outperforms recent representative
image-, video-, and multimodal methods. Our routing analysis
further reveals that distant relationships tend to rely on low-level
homogeneous interactions, whereas closer relationships focus
on high-level heterogeneous interactions. Our code is publicly
available at: https://github.com/tw-repository/HiRe-DRR.

H
UMAN dyadic relationship recognition (DRR) aims to
infer the latent social relationship between two indi-
viduals from their multimodal interactions. Unlike transient
actions or emotions, relationships are higher-order social con-
structs reflected in recurring behavioural patterns, affective
exchanges, verbal communication, and social intentions [1].
For example, interactions between unfamiliar individuals are
often characterised by observable behavioural coordination,
such as gaze alignment, speaking turns, and physical distance,
whereas close relationships may involve richer emotional
responses and implicit behavioural adaptation. Understanding
such interaction dynamics is fundamental to socially intel-
ligent systems [2]. In human-robot interaction, relationship-
aware agents can adapt their behaviours to their partners’
social contexts [3, 4], while pairwise relationships provide
latent structures for interpreting group activities [5]. DRR
also supports affective computing [6] and social interaction
analysis [7].
Early social relationship recognition (SRR) methods fo-
cused mainly on static images, using visual appearance [8, 9],
scene context and person-object interactions [10, 11]. Subse-
quent video and multimodal approaches incorporated temporal
behaviours, vocal cues, and linguistic information [12, 13],
typically projecting them into a shared space for attention-
or graph-based fusion [14–19], as illustrated in Fig. 1(a).
Although these methods improve recognition performance,
their holistic fusion generally encodes multimodal associations
implicitly, making it difficult to determine which interaction
structures characterise a particular relationship.
Recent multimodal foundation models provide transferable
representations, broad semantic knowledge, and the ability
to produce natural-language explanations [20, 21]. However,
improved semantic reasoning does not necessarily reveal how
a relationship decision is grounded in participant-specific
interaction evidence. Foundation models often rely on glob-
ally aggregated multimodal representations, making it difficult
to identify which interaction structures contribute to differ-
ent relationships. For example, distant relationships may be
characterised by observable behavioural consistencies, such
as facial alignment, speaking patterns, or behavioural syn-
chrony, whereas close relationships may involve subtle emo-
tional responses and richer cross-modal behavioural exchanges
between individuals. Therefore, how multimodal interaction
evidence at different semantic levels characterises distinct
relationships, and how such evidence varies across the relation-
ship hierarchy, remains an open question. We formulate this
problem through three aspects: feature level, cross-participant
interaction type, and relationship hierarchy.

First, relationship evidence spans multiple semantic levels
(Fig. 1(b)) [22, 23]. Low-level cues, including facial land-
marks, body pose, acoustic rhythm, and spectral descrip-
tors, capture observable behavioural consistency, such as syn-
chronized expressions, posture alignment, and conversational
rhythms [23]. High-level cues, such as facial action units,
gaze, body openness, affective states, and linguistic sentiment,
provide richer social semantics by describing emotional re-
sponses, engagement, and interpersonal coordination [23, 24].
Directly projecting these cues into a single space may ob-
scure their semantic distinctions and prevent the model from
revealing how evidence at different levels contributes to each
relationship. (Challenge I)
Second,
relationships
emerge
through
different
cross-
participant interaction patterns (Fig. 1(c)). Homogeneous in-
teractions capture dependencies between the same modalities,
such as face-face correspondence [25], whereas heteroge-
neous interactions represent cross-modal responses, such as
associations between one participant’s speech and the other’s
facial or bodily reactions [26]. For example, a stranger may
mainly exhibit consistent facial or speech patterns, while close
partners may respond through coordinated facial, bodily, and
acoustic behaviours. Uniformly aggregating all interactions
conceals such relationship-specific patterns. The model must
therefore represent these structures separately and adaptively
identify those relevant to each relationship. (Challenge II)
Third, relationship classes exhibit an inherent hierarchy,
rather than independent class boundaries. As shown in
Fig. 1(d), relationship recognition can naturally be decom-
posed into a sequence of conditional decisions. The model
can first determines whether two individuals are acquainted;
if they are, it further distinguishes an ordinary Acquaintance
from Friend; once a friendship is determined, it finally de-
termines whether the pair are Very Good Friends. However,
conventional cross-entropy treats all classes as independent
targets and assigns identical penalties to different types of
errors, ignoring the semantic proximity among relationship
classes. This motivates the third challenge of incorporating
relationship hierarchy into the learning objective to supervise
hierarchical relationship reasoning. (Challenge III)
To address these challenges, we propose HiRe-DRR, a
Hierarchical Multimodal Interaction Reasoning framework for
Dyadic Relationship Recognition. HiRe-DRR organises mul-
timodal representations according to their semantic levels and
constructs homogeneous and heterogeneous cross-participant
interactions at each level. We then propose the Mixture-
of-Interaction Transformer (MoI-Former), which treats these
interaction features as specialised experts and uses top-K
sparse routing to select relationship-relevant evidence. Its
routing preferences provide an explicit view of how different
relationships depend on different feature levels and interaction
types. We further introduce a Hierarchical Conditional Loss
(HC-Loss), which decomposes supervision into conditional de-
cisions along the relationship hierarchy, connecting interaction
representations with coarse-to-fine relationship semantics. Our
main contributions are as follows:
• We propose HiRe-DRR, a hierarchical multimodal inter-
action reasoning framework that jointly models feature
level, interaction type, and relationship hierarchy for
dyadic relationship recognition. (Addressing Challenge I,
II, III)
• We introduce MoI-Former, which treats different interac-
tion cues as specialised relationship experts and employs
Top-K sparse routing to adaptively select and aggre-
gate relationship-relevant interaction evidence. Address-
ing Challenge I, II)
• We propose HC-Loss, which incorporates relationship hi-
erarchy into the supervisory process via a series of binary
conditional decisions, enabling the model to learn hier-
archical relationship representations. (Addressing Chal-
lenge III)
• Experiments on NoXi, UDIVA, and Seamless Interaction
demonstrate competitive relationship recognition, while
ablation and routing analyses reveal distinct feature-level
and interaction patterns across relationship classes.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Static Image-based SRR
Early image-based SRR inferred interpersonal relationships
from visual appearance, facial attributes, pairwise geometry,
and scene context [8, 9]. Li et al. [29] combined appearance,
facial cues, and interpersonal geometry to represent person
pairs, while subsequent methods introduced contextual in-
formation for improved relationship discrimination
[8, 9].
More recently, Wu et al. [30] adapted vision-language repre-
sentations to identify socially informative objects, activities,
and contextual cues. These studies extended SRR beyond
individual appearance towards contextual semantic reasoning.


3
Graph-based methods further represent people, objects, and
context as structured relational entities [14, 31, 32]. Tang
et al. constructed progressive graphs across individual, pair,
and group levels [33]. Other studies introduced triangular
constraints for third-party influence [10], multi-level attention
across representational scales [16], and graph-based knowl-
edge distillation for incremental learning [34]. Hypergraph
Mamba Reasoning [11] further captured higher-order and
long-range dependencies through person-person and person-
object hypergraphs.
Large multimodal models have recently connected visual
observations with abstract social semantics. SocialGPT [35]
transforms visual scenes into socially grounded descriptions
for zero-shot inference, while Wang et al. [36] align visual
and linguistic representations for complementary reasoning.
GMLLM-SRR [37] further combines graph-structured visual
evidence with multimodal language models through cross-
attention. Although these methods provide strong semantic
priors and explanatory outputs, their image-based evidence
cannot capture recurring behaviours or temporally evolving
responses between participants.
B. Spatio-temporal Multimodal SRR
Video-based SRR extends static inference by modelling
evolving social behaviours and their temporal dependencies.
Earlier structured approaches represented people, objects, and
narrative context over time using pyramid graph convolu-
tions [12], dynamic character-relationship graphs [38], spatio-
temporal message passing [18], and multi granularity graph
aggregation [39]. Subsequent multimodal methods incorpo-
rated speech, acoustic cues, and linguistic semantics [13, 27]
through action-scene attention [40], progressive aggregation of
frame-level multimodal graphs [41], cross-modal pre-training
of visual, speech, and textual signals [42], and cumula-
tive graph–Transformer reasoning for long-range dependen-
cies [19]. Recent work has further introduced causal mod-
elling to mitigate culture-specific correlations [43] and capture
dependencies among temporal observations [44]. Together,
these advances have shifted video SRR from conventional
sequence encoding towards structured multimodal temporal
reasoning; however, most remain character-centric and tailored
to movie narratives, with limited modelling of real-world
dyadic interactions.
For real-world DRR, recent studies have extended social
understanding beyond movie-based scenarios by modelling in-
terpersonal dynamics in natural interactions. AsyReC [45] cap-
tures asymmetric dependencies between participants through
multimodal graph reasoning and temporal encoding, highlight-
ing the importance of modelling evolving interaction patterns
in dyadic conversations. Meanwhile, multimodal foundation
models have introduced new directions for social reasoning
by integrating visual, auditory, and linguistic information with
large-scale semantic knowledge. Ouyang et al. [21] investi-
gate multimodal social interaction in multi-speaker scenarios
and propose attention alignment strategies to strengthen the
correspondence between speakers’ visual behaviours and ut-
terances. MimeQA [46] further establishes a benchmark for
evaluating nonverbal social intelligence in foundation models,
emphasizing the role of embodied gestures and contextual
cues in social understanding. FriendBench [20] directly studies
dyadic familiarity inference with multimodal large language
models and human comparisons, providing insights into how
different modalities contribute to relationship perception.
Collectively, these advances demonstrate that understanding
dyadic relationships requires reasoning over rich multimodal
social behaviours rather than relying solely on linguistic
semantics. However, existing approaches generally focus on
improving recognition ability or semantic reasoning, while the
organisation of relationship evidence remains largely implicit.
It remains unclear which interaction patterns distinguish dif-
ferent relationships and how such evidence is structured across
relationship categories. Addressing this issue requires an ex-
plicit and interpretable framework that organises multimodal
evidence according to its semantic characteristics, interaction
structures, and relational hierarchy. In this work, HiRe-DRR
addresses this gap by performing hierarchical multimodal
interaction reasoning.
III. METHOD
The HiRe-DRR pipeline is shown in Fig. 2. Each video is
divided into T temporally ordered clips following [45], yield-
ing synchronised face, body, audio, and text streams for par-
ticipants A and B (Fig. 2(a)). Modality-specific encoders then
extract low- and high-level features [22–24] (Fig. 2(b)). Homo-
geneous and heterogeneous cross-participant interactions are
constructed separately at each semantic level (Fig. 2(c)). MoI-
Former subsequently performs relation-aware sparse reasoning
over these interactions and aggregates the selected evidence
into a relationship representation (Fig. 2(d)). Finally, HC-Loss
decomposes recognition into hierarchy-aware conditional de-
cisions and is jointly optimised with the standard classification
objective (Fig. 2(e)).

IV. EVALUATION SETUP
A. Datasets
We evaluate our method on NoXi [48], UDIVA [49], and
Seamless Interaction (SeamInt) [47]. NoXi comprises 168
videos spanning four relationship classes: Stranger (Str.), Ac-
quaintance (Acq.), Friend (Fri.), and Very Good Friend (Vgf.).
The dataset provides annotations from two participant per-
spectives, referred to as NoXi-I and NoXi-J. UDIVA contains
1,160 videos and defines a binary relationship-recognition task
with the classes Known (Kno.) and Unknown (Unk.). SeamInt
is substantially larger, including more than 4,000 hours of
face-to-face interactions involving over 4,000 participants,
with a total data volume of approximately 27 TB. Owing to
computational and storage constraints, we randomly sampled
2,738 videos from SeamInt for our experiments, covering five
relationship classes: Str., Acq., Fri., Fam., and Cou. Following
the clip-construction protocol of [45], each video is partitioned
into temporally ordered clips, which constitute the clip-level
datasets used for training and evaluation.
B. Implementation Details
1) Feature Extraction: As summarised in Table I, modality-
specific encoders extract complementary low- and high-level
features from facial, bodily, acoustic, and textual signals.
Low-level features describe fundamental geometric, acoustic,
and semantic cues, whereas high-level features capture be-
havioural, affective, and appearance-related information. These
features are temporally aggregated at the clip level and pro-
jected into a unified representation space for dyadic interaction
modelling. The pre-trained encoders remain frozen, while the
projection and interaction reasoning modules are optimised
end-to-end.
2) Experimental Setup: All experiments are implemented
in PyTorch and conducted on a server equipped with an
Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6330 CPU @ 2.00 GHz, 256 GB RAM,
and an NVIDIA A100 80 GB PCIe GPU. Following [45],
we adopt 3-fold cross-validation with non-overlapping dyadic
video partitions, ensuring that clips from the same interaction
are not shared between training and testing sets. We train the
model for 100 epochs using AdamW with a batch size of 64.
The initial learning rate is set to 1×10−4 with a weight decay
of 5×10−4, and is decayed by a factor of 10 every 15 epochs.
For MoI-Former, interaction tokens are projected into a
2048-dimensional latent space and processed by two Trans-
former encoder layers with 8 attention heads, followed by the
interaction decoder. The sparse router selects the top-K = 5
interaction tokens at each routing stage, and the dropout rate
is set to 0.3.
3) Evaluation Metrics: Considering the class imbalance in
social relationship datasets, we follow the evaluation protocol
commonly adopted in [39, 45] and report class-wise Recall,
Unweighted Average Recall (UAR), and mean Average Preci-
sion (mAP) for fair comparison. Class-wise Recall measures
recognition performance for individual relationship categories,
while UAR gives equal weight to all classes and is there-
fore robust to imbalanced distributions. Additionally, mAP
evaluates the quality of predicted confidence scores across
relationship categories.
C. Baseline Models
We compare HiRe-DRR with representative image-based,
video-based, and multimodal relationship recognition meth-
ods. For image-based baselines, we adapt them to the video
setting by temporally aggregating frame-level visual features
within each clip to obtain person-level representations, which
are then processed by their original relation reasoning mod-
ules. Audio and textual modalities are excluded to maintain
their original modality assumptions.
• GR2N [14] performs image-based social relationship rea-
soning by constructing pairwise graph structures. (Image-
bsed method)
• HGM [11] extends pairwise graph reasoning to higher-
order interaction modelling by employing hypergraphs.
(Image-bsed method)
• PGCN [12] models video-based social relationships
through pyramid graph convolution. (Video-bsed method)
• LIReC [13] is a multimodal framework that jointly
models visual and textual cues to infer character social
relationships. (Multimodal-bsed method)
• Cumulative Transformer (CT) [19] is Transformer ar-
chitecture that captures long-term relationship dynamics
by progressively accumulating historical interaction rep-
resentations. (Multimodal-bsed method)
• AsyReC [45] is multimodal framework that integrates
facial, bodily, acoustic, and textual cues, while modelling
directional interpersonal dependencies and temporal inter-
action patterns. (Multimodal-bsed method)

VI. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Discussion. HiRe-DRR reveals that dyadic relationships
can be understood through relationship-dependent interaction
evidence rather than uniformly aggregated multimodal cues.
The routing analysis indicates that different relationships rely
on distinct interaction patterns, suggesting that future socially
intelligent systems may benefit from explicitly modelling
the behavioural and semantic cues underlying interpersonal
relationships. In human-robot interaction, such relationship-
aware understanding can provide additional context for adapt-
ing communication strategies and interaction behaviours to
different social partners [1, 3]. In social assistance and in-
teractive agents, recognising relationship-specific interaction
patterns can support more personalised and context-aware re-
sponses [4]. Beyond these scenarios, the discovered interaction
patterns may also provide useful relational context for broader
applications, including human activity understanding [60] and
social interaction analysis [7].
Conclusion. We introduced HiRe-DRR, a hierarchical mul-
timodal interaction reasoning framework for dyadic relation-
ship recognition. By organising relational evidence across fea-
ture level, cross-participant interaction type, and relationship
hierarchy, HiRe-DRR preserves relationship-specific cues that
may be obscured by conventional multimodal fusion. MoI-
Former adaptively identifies relationship-relevant interactions,
while HC-Loss exploits the hierarchical nature of social re-
lationships through coarse-to-fine supervision. Experiments
on three benchmarks demonstrate consistent improvements
over representative image-, video-, and multimodal methods.
Routing analyses further reveal that distant relationships rely
more on observable behavioural correspondences, whereas
closer relationships increasingly involve high-level semantic
and heterogeneous cross-modal interactions.
Despite these advances, recognising dyadic relationships
in real-world scenarios remains challenging. Existing bench-
marks mainly capture short-term interactions, while human
relationships evolve through long-term behavioural patterns,
changing contexts, and broader social structures. Moreover,
socially adjacent relationships may share similar interaction
evidence due to individual communication styles and situa-
tional variations. Future work will explore long-range temporal
relationship modelling, multi-person relational reasoning, and
more adaptive evidence discovery mechanisms to support
robust social understanding in complex environments.
