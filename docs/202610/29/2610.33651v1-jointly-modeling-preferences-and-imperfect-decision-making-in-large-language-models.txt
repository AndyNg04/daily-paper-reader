Abstract 

Do preferences Large Language Models (LLMs) express in standard elicitation tasks predict their strategic behaviour? LLM agents interacting with humans and other AI systems face potentially conflicting objectives, such as individual welfare, group welfare, and environmental concerns. Prior work characterizes LLM preferences through behavioral-economic and psychological elicitation or strategic games, but rarely tests consistency across settings and prediction in other strategic environments. This requires modeling how competing objectives jointly shape behavior, whereas existing approaches typically measure preference dimensions in isolation. Drawing on utility theory, we introduce the Preference-Behavior Model (PBM), which combines a preference representation with mechanisms capturing imperfect utility maximization. We formulate estimation as an inverse-game problem, jointly inferring preferences and behavioral parameters from observed choices. We evaluate PBM in three social dilemmas that expose conflicting motives: a Public Goods Game, a Collective-Risk Dilemma, and a Common-Pool Resource dilemma. We find that while preferences estimated from established behavioral economic elicitation instruments do not consistently predict behavior in these dilemmas, models fitted in one dilemma retain predictive value in others, suggesting that inferred preferences capture meaningful aspects of LLM decision-making that elicitation instruments may miss. Finally, we assess alignment with persona instructions, finding that inferred preferences broadly reflect intended priorities with specific divergences across dimensions, LLMs, and games being identified by our approach. Our methodology provides a framework for studying how consistently LLM preferences explain behavior, assessing alignment with intended priorities, and revealing trade-offs and behavioral patterns that standard elicitation may miss. 

Keywords: Large language models; behavioral modeling; social preferences; alignment; inverse game theory; per-sonas; social dilemmas 

# 1 Introduction 

As large language models (LLMs) move from pure text generation toward autonomous and semi-autonomous agents, they are increasingly placed in diverse contexts, where their decisions depend on and affect others, with consequences that may extend to human populations (Akata et al., 2025; Piatti et al., 2024; Tang and Yeh, 2026). These agentic systems often face trade-offs when encountering situations that can be described as so-cial dilemmas, e.g., managing shared resources (Kollock, 1998; Ostrom, 1990). Thus, to ensure that large multi-agent systems are safe (Hammond et al., 2025; Tomašev et al., 2025), it is crucial to understand what LLMs value in such mixed-motive scenarios, how they make decisions, and how to steer their preferences toward the objectives of the humans they represent (Bakker et al., 2022; Chakraborty et al., 2024; Sorensen et al., 2024; Köbis et al., 2025; Fernández Domingos et al., 2022). Existing work has approached this problem from two complementary directions. Studies of LLMs in social dilemmas and behavioral games have characterized co-operation, coordination, strategic behavior, and more recently, the values expressed by LLMs across different forms of multi-agent cooperation (Akata et al., 2025; Pi-atti et al., 2024; Tang and Yeh, 2026). This literature provides important evidence about what LLM agents do in social settings, but does not necessarily identify a quantitative representation of the preferences under-lying their behavior. A complementary line of work has used economic choice experiments to recover inter-pretable preferences over risk, time, and social outcomes from LLM choices (Chen et al., 2023; Cook et al., 2026; Buchanan and Foster, 2026). In the strategic settings we study these comprise own payoff, others’ payoffs, payoff inequality, and environmental outcomes. (2) Observe the behavior of the LLM–persona in both strategic games (here, public goods game, collective-risk dilemma, and common-pool resource dilemma) and elicitation tests (15 preference-elicitation instruments). (3) Infer the joint preference and behavior models for games (ˆθ, ˆψ) and elicitation battery (ˆθ′, ˆψ′) separately. It remains unclear whether such preferences elicited in isolation character-ize how LLMs behave when multiple objectives conflict within the same decision. An agent may, for example, exhibit concern for others or the environment when these preferences are elicited in isolation, yet prioritize its own payoff when these objectives compete in a strategic inter-action. We argue here that neither observing behavior (e.g., cooperation) nor eliciting preferences (e.g. altru-ism) in isolation necessarily tells us how multiple, poten-tially conflicting, preferences jointly shape LLM behav-ior. Recovering such preferences from strategic behavior is itself challenging because actions may not be direct expressions of preferences. Even in non-strategic set-tings, behavior may reflect a combination of preferences, bounded rationality, stochasticity, and task representa-tion. In strategic interactions, the identification prob-lem becomes substantially harder, because actions also depend on beliefs about other agents, planning depth, anticipated responses, and the dynamics of the environ-ment. Cooperation, for example, may reflect concern for another player, instrumental reciprocity, or an inac-curate model of the opponent, while apparent selfish-ness may arise from limited planning (Xu et al., 2026). Assuming that LLMs perfectly maximize a utility func-tion therefore risks attributing systematic reasoning or decision-making errors to the preferences themselves. We therefore need to model not only what an LLM val-ues, but also how those values are translated into deci-sions. We address this problem with the Preference-Behavior Model (PBM), a behavioral model combining a util-ity function that represents preferences over outcomes with mechanisms allowing decisions to depart from util-ity maximization. The utility function describes what an agent values, while the behavioral mechanisms cap-ture how those values translate into choices, allowing for imperfect reasoning and stochastic choice. We formu-late parameter estimation as an inverse-game problem ,jointly inferring preference and behavioral parameters from observed behavior. This joint estimation allows both preferences and decision-making tendencies to con-tribute to the explanation of observed choices. As illustrated in Figure 1, we apply this method-ology to infer preference estimates from strategic set-tings. Here, we consider three social dilemmas with complementary strategic structures: a Public Goods Game (PGG), a Collective-Risk Dilemma (CRD) (Milin-ski et al., 2008), and a Common-Pool Resource dilemma (CPR) (Ostrom, 1990). Across all three games, out-comes can be represented along the same preference di-mensions: the focal agent’s payoff, others’ payoff, in-equality, and environmental outcomes. These shared preference dimensions make it possible to transfer pref-erence estimates while allowing the decision process to depend on the strategic structure of each game. We can therefore compare preference estimates across settings and test whether a model fitted in one game predicts choices in the others. We also compare preferences inferred from strategic behavior with those inferred from a separate battery of established preference-elicitation instruments (Murphy et al., 2011; Fisman et al., 2007; Kerschbamer, 2015; Bruhin et al., 2019; Fleiß et al., 2020; Andre et al., 2026; Goeschl et al., 2020; Mizuno and Shimizu, 2024), which target the same dimensions. Rather than treat-ing elicited preferences as ground truth, we ask whether the preferences they reveal are consistent with those ex-pressed through behavior in strategically richer environ-ments. This provides a direct test of whether preferences measured in targeted elicitation tasks transfer to settings in which multiple objectives compete and decisions de-2pend on the behavior of others. Throughout, we use persona prompting to introduce controlled variation in agents’ intended preferences. Per-sona prompts are increasingly used to make LLMs rep-resent specified preference profiles, either to reproduce the diversity of human populations (Argyle et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024; Abels et al., 2026; Saponara et al., 2026), or to represent the preferences of users on whose behalf an agent acts. However, the literature often im-plicitly assumes that the instructed profile is reflected in the model’s decisions, while evidence that simulated populations reproduce human behavior remains mixed (Bisbee et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). An “environmen-tally concerned” persona, for example, may express en-vironmental concern when asked directly without acting accordingly when environmental outcomes conflict with its own payoff. We systematically vary persona descrip-tions across 24 preference profiles and assess whether estimates from strategic behavior and elicitation reflect these intended differences. This provides a controlled test of whether differences in what agents are instructed to value produce corresponding differences in what their decisions reveal they value, a central requirement when agents are intended to represent the preferences of hu-mans (Sorensen et al., 2024). Our results show that preferences inferred from elicita-tion tasks do not consistently account for LLM strategic behavior. Jointly estimating preference and behavioral parameters improves held-out prediction relative to con-strained and optimal-planning benchmarks, and models fitted in one game retain predictive value in other games. Persona instructions produce distinguishable broad pri-orities, although the inferred weights vary across prefer-ence dimensions, LLMs, and games. In summary, our contributions are: • We introduce PBM, an interpretable behavioral model combining a utility function capturing preferences over outcomes with mechanisms allowing departures from utility-maximizing decisions. • We formulate parameter estimation as an inverse-game problem and develop a structural inference methodology that jointly estimates preference and be-havioral parameters, distinguishing what an agent val-ues from how those values translate into choices. • We test whether preferences recovered from strategic trajectories generalize beyond the observations used to estimate them, across held-out game contexts, across games, and against a separate LLM-tailored battery of established preference-elicitation instruments. We assess alignment by testing whether inferred prefer-ences reflect the relative priorities specified in persona instructions. 

# 2 Related Work 

A growing literature applies tools from behavioral eco-nomics and decision theory to characterize LLM prefer-ences in non-strategic settings, where an agent’s pay-off does not depend on another decision maker’s re-sponse. Revealed-preference studies find substantial eco-nomic consistency alongside context effects (Chen et al., 2023; Ross et al., 2024), while more recent work directly estimates economically interpretable latent parameters from LLM choices. Cook et al. (2026) recover inequity-aversion parameters from dictator-style allocations and effective discounting from sequential job search, and Buchanan and Foster (2026) use a random-utility in-terpretation of language-model choice probabilities to estimate risk preferences. Related studies estimate risk attitudes, reference dependence, and willingness-to-pay measures from lottery, discrete-choice, and stated-preference tasks (de Castro Soares et al., 2026; Iwamoto et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026). This literature establishes that LLM decisions can support structural estimation of economically meaningful latent quantities rather than only descriptive comparisons of observed choices. Recent studies also examine LLMs in strategic and multi-agent environments. In these settings, behavior depends not only on individual preferences but also on beliefs about other agents. Experiments document sys-tematic differences across models in trust, reciprocity, bargaining, cooperation, and coordination (Mei et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Ou et al., 2025; Araujo and Uhlig, 2026). Repeated-game studies further show sensitivity to opponent information, game structure, and interac-tion history (Akata et al., 2025; Kirshner et al., 2025), while larger multi-agent environments reveal heteroge-neous patterns of cooperation, sustainability, value ex-pression, and market behavior (Piatti et al., 2024; Tang and Yeh, 2026; Struski et al., 2026). These results show that in strategic settings the mapping from latent pref-erences to observed actions is mediated by beliefs and strategic reasoning. Across both non-strategic and strategic settings, aseparate literature raises the question of whether mea-sured LLM preferences constitute stable characteristics at all. Choices can vary with option order, elicitation protocol, personas, reasoning configuration, and task presentation (Yin et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2025; Maha-jan et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2025; Zhou and Ackerman, 2026), while stated or elicited preferences do not al-ways reliably predict subsequent behavior (Yamin et al., 2026). Population-level work likewise emphasizes het-erogeneity, highlighting that insufficient diversity among LLM agents can preserve or amplify systematic biases (Abels and Lenaerts, 2025), whereas conditioning agents on value profiles sampled from empirical human pop-ulations can substantially alter and diversify their re-sponse distributions (Abels et al., 2026). These findings motivate treating preference as an inferred latent quan-3tity whose stability and external validity must be tested, rather than equating it with either a single observed ac-tion or a direct questionnaire response. Our methodological starting point is therefore the broader literature on inverse decision problems. For static non-strategic choices, random-utility and struc-tural discrete-choice models provide a natural mapping from latent utilities to stochastic decisions. For sequen-tial single-agent behavior, inverse reinforcement learning infers latent rewards from observed policies or trajecto-ries (Ng and Russell, 2000; Ramachandran and Amir, 2007; Ziebart et al., 2008), with related inverse-reward approaches recently explored for language-model objec-tives and alignment (Sun and van der Schaar, 2024; Joselowitz et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025). Strate-gic environments, however, require a different forward model. For instance, inverse game theory and inverse-equilibrium methods explicitly account for the depen-dence of one agent’s actions on the anticipated behav-ior of others (Waugh et al., 2011; Kuleshov and Schri-jvers, 2015; Wu et al., 2022; Liao et al., 2025). Quantal-response models provide a complementary account of stochastic boundedly rational strategic behavior (McK-elvey and Palfrey, 1995; Bland and Turocy, 2026), and Xu et al. (2026) recently demonstrated structural recov-ery of latent assumptions about opponents’ reasoning from LLM choices in normal-form games. Nevertheless, in our opinion, several gaps remain. Ex-isting studies typically estimate preference parameters within a particular task, characterize strategic behav-ior without estimating the preferences that might gen-erate it, or elicit preferences without testing whether they explain behavior in strategically different environ-ments. We instead treat cross-game preference recovery and elicitation as parts of the same identification prob-lem, that is, a useful latent preference representation should explain behavior across distinct strategic envi-ronments and correspond to independent measurements targeting the same dimensions. Our elicitation battery therefore draws on established instruments for social, in-equality, and related preferences (Murphy et al., 2011; Kerschbamer, 2015; Andreoni and Miller, 2002; Bruhin et al., 2019; Fehr et al., 2023), but uses them as exter-nal validation rather than as the definition of the latent preference itself. 

# 3 Methods 

Our approach is based on the hypothesis that LLM be-havior in strategic games reflects two distinct compo-nents: (i) preferences over outcomes and (ii) behavioral mechanisms. Preferences determine which outcomes an agent values (e.g., environmental benefits), whereas be-havioral parameters determine how those preferences translate into decisions, potentially departing from op-timal behavior. We jointly estimate preference and be-havioral parameters within each game and compare the inferred preferences across games to examine their con-sistency under different strategic incentives and decision processes. Specifically, we apply our methodology to three so-cial dilemmas using a utility function that captures ma-terial, inequality, and environmental preferences along-side a behavioral model that accommodates departures from perfect utility-maximizing behavior. Persona in-structions specify intended profiles against which we as-sess alignment, while a separate elicitation battery pro-vides a comparison with preferences measured outside the games. Figure 1 summarizes the experimental pipeline: (1) in-duce preferences in the LLM through persona prompts that specify intended priorities over own payoff, oth-ers’ payoffs, payoff inequality, and environmental out-comes; (2) collect game trajectories and elicitation re-sponses; and (3) infer preference and behavioral param-eters, which are then cross-validated throughout the dif-ferent dilemmas, and compared with those inferred from elicitation. Finally, (4) we evaluate the models in terms of alignment with the intended preferences, and how well they retain predictive power for predicting behavior in previously unseen scenarios (held-out prediction) and across games (cross-game transfer). 

## 3.1 Preference inference framework 

3.1.1 Preferences and utility 

We assume that an agent evaluates possible outcomes according to some utility function Uθ , parameterized by latent preference parameters θ. The framework does not require a particular set of preference dimensions or func-tional form. In this work, we use the utility function defined in Equation 19. To infer these preference parameters from observed choices, we need to relate the utility of outcomes to the actions available to the agent. In the games considered here, an action’s consequences may depend on the part-ner’s response, uncertain events, and decisions made in subsequent rounds. Evaluating an action therefore re-quires a model of how the interaction may unfold. To capture this planning process, we formulate the in-teraction as a sequential decision process in which the agent observes state s and chooses an available action 

a ∈ A (s). We include in s the number of remaining rounds and the observed history needed to evaluate the choice, including the previous action when relevant. Un-der the assumed model of state transitions and partner behavior, the action-value function Qθ (s, a ) gives the ex-pected utility from choosing a and subsequently acting optimally over the remaining rounds. The utility function thus describes how the agent values outcomes, whereas the action-value function de-scribes how it evaluates actions given their possible con-sequences. An action can therefore receive different val-4ues under different planning or partner assumptions even when preferences remain fixed. 

3.1.2 Behavioral mechanisms 

While the utility function defines what an agent values, predicting its choices also requires assumptions about how it evaluates and selects actions. Agents may have limited planning capacity, assign less weight to distant consequences, or select inconsistently among the actions they judge to be best. Their choices may also reflect a tendency to repeat previous actions or occasional choices that are independent of action values. We therefore in-troduce behavioral parameters that allow for these mech-anisms and estimate them jointly with the preference pa-rameters. These mechanisms provide alternative expla-nations for departures from optimal behavior that might otherwise be absorbed into preference estimates. We allow these mechanisms to operate together within a single behavioral model. Discounting and limited plan-ning affect the consequences an agent considers and the weight it assigns to them, while decision precision, rep-etition bias, and lapses affect how it selects among the evaluated actions. Below, we introduce these mecha-nisms and show how they combine to determine choice probabilities.

## 3.3 Games 

We employ three well-known social dilemmas as the strategic settings for our experiments: a Public Goods Game (PGG), a Collective-Risk Dilemma (CRD), and a Common-Pool Resource Dilemma (CPR). As shown in Figure 1, the three games offer complementary charac-teristics that allow us to study how LLMs balance ma-terial payoffs, inequality, and environmental outcomes under different strategic and dynamic trade-offs. Each game episode involves a focal LLM i and a scripted partner j and lasts H0 ∈ { 1, . . . , 5} rounds. The players begin with initial monetary holdings X0 and Y0,6respectively, which may differ to introduce initial payoff inequality. In addition to these holdings, both players receive a fresh endowment of 10 tokens in every round t

and choose simultaneously from A = {0, 1, . . . , 10 }. The interpretation of these actions and their consequences depends on the game, as described below. ## 3.5 Elicitation battery 

Following the use of economic choice tasks to evaluate LLM preferences (Chen et al., 2023; Cook et al., 2026), we administer a comprehensive battery of ten established choice instruments targeting the preference dimensions represented in our utility function. We also design five choice instruments for this work, three environmental lists using the Multiple Price List (MPL) protocol (Holt and Laury, 2002; Andersen et al., 2006) and two budget-line tasks (see SI Table S3). We use this battery to ex-amine whether preferences elicited through these instru-ments are consistent with those inferred from strategic interactions. Thus, in total, the battery contains fifteen choice in-struments adapted so that they can be administered to LLMs (see SI Sec. S1.5 for more information). Each choice instrument (test) consists of one or more deci-sions. In each decision the model is shown a menu – the finite set of options available in that decision – and is asked to make a choice. For example, one row of a price list is a two-option menu. 

Eliciting social preferences. To elicit social prefer-ences, we use allocation budgets (Andreoni and Miller, 2002; Fisman et al., 2007, 2017), Bruhin–Fehr–Schunk choices (Bruhin et al., 2019), the Equality Equivalence Test (Kerschbamer, 2015), SVO-type allocations (Mur-phy et al., 2011; Murphy and Ackermann, 2014; Mizuno and Shimizu, 2024), and newly designed social budget lines. 

Eliciting environmental preferences. To elicit environmental preferences we use matched allocation/value-orientation tasks for three environ-mental causes (Fleiß et al., 2020), newly designed environmental price and impact lists motivated by Rodemeier (2026), newly designed environmental bud-get lines, a climate allocation (Andre et al., 2026), and a contribution task in which tokens retire EU emission permits instead of going to an organization, together with a price list built on the same permit retirement (Goeschl et al., 2020). 

Preference inference from elicitation choices. 

While no single elicitation test allows us to jointly in-fer all preference parameters, we can combine choices across tests to estimate them within the shared utility framework. To do so, we map every option in the choice 8instruments to an outcome (X, Y, E ) and evaluate it us-ing the utility function in Equation 19. All monetary choice tasks are mapped to the common token scale by positive within-instrument rescaling, preserving relative prices. This gives preference estimates expressed in the same terms as those inferred from game trajectories. As in the inference from game trajectories, we jointly estimate preference and behavioral parameters. How-ever, because the elicitation tasks describe one-shot de-cisions with no subsequent rounds to anticipate, the rep-etition bias, planning-depth and temporal-discounting mechanisms do not apply. We therefore use a re-duced behavioral specification consisting of the Boltz-mann choice rule with uniform lapses. We weight choices to balance outcome domains, instrument families, and variants, so that instruments with more decisions or vari-ants do not dominate the fit (SI Sec. S1.5.1). 

# 4 Experimental Setting 

Compared with experiments involving human partici-pants, LLMs can be queried repeatedly across a broad range of controlled scenarios. We use this opportunity to vary the trade-offs agents face, providing information about their preferences across different decision settings. For each game, we select 500 random scenarios specified by an initial state, the partner’s policy, and the parame-ters governing the game dynamics. Across scenarios, we vary the horizon, initial payoff inequality, relative mon-etary returns, environmental stakes, partner policy, and game-specific parameters. This variation creates choices in which the preference dimensions favor different ac-tions, providing information about how the agent trades them off. Partner policies include fixed actions, copying the focal player’s previous action, forgiving trigger re-sponses, threshold compensation in the CRD, and stock-conserving responses in the CPR. These policies are de-terministic, with reactive choices depending on public history or the current stock, rather than on the focal player’s simultaneous action. Using disclosed rules gives the LLM and the inference model the same description of partner behavior, while varying the rules tests re-sponses to different co-player behaviors. Full definitions of the policies and scenario parameters are provided in SI Sec. S1.4.

## 4.4 Evaluation 

We evaluate alignment with intended persona profiles, prediction of held-out choices, and generalization across decision settings. 

4.4.1 Held-out prediction 

Within each game, we use four-fold cross-validation to assess prediction in scenarios not used for estimation. We keep all repetitions and decisions from a scenario in the same fold so that training data exclude repeated observations of held-out scenarios. We jointly estimate preference and behavioral parameters on the training scenarios and evaluate choice probabilities at the ob-served states in the held-out scenarios. Predicted proba-bilities are averaged over joint posterior draws from the training fit, retaining uncertainty and dependence be-tween preference and behavioral parameters. For the elicitation battery, we hold out each instru-ment in turn and predict its choices from fits to the re-maining instruments. We compare a test-only protocol with a menu-disjoint protocol that additionally removes training menus with identical payoffs (SI Sec. S1.5.1). 

4.4.2 Predictive metrics 

We evaluate predictions at three levels: the selected ac-tion, the distribution of choices, and the resulting out-comes. The following six metrics capture these comple-mentary aspects of prediction quality: 

Negative log-likelihood (NLL) evaluates the prob-ability assigned to observed choices, heavily penalizing predictions that assign low probability to choices that actually occur. 10 Choice accuracy measures the proportion of in-stances in which the model’s most probable action matches the observed choice. 

Ranked probability score (RPS) evaluates cumu-lative choice probabilities across the ordered action lev-els. It complements NLL by assessing the distribution across action thresholds, while accounting for how far predicted actions lie from the observed choice. 

Calibration error assesses whether predicted proba-bilities correspond to observed frequencies, allowing us to examine whether the model systematically overstates or understates the likelihood of particular actions. Within each experimental condition, we divide predicted prob-abilities into ten equal-width bins separately for each of the eleven actions. We average the absolute difference between predicted probability and observed action fre-quency, weighting bins by their frequency, then averag-ing equally across actions. Condition scores are averaged within personas, and personas receive equal weight in the reported summaries. 

Outcome Euclidean distance assesses whether choice predictions reproduce the material, distributional, and environmental consequences of observed behavior. We compare aggregate profiles across self-payoff, other-payoff, absolute payoff inequality, and environmental outcomes. For each observed decision, we compute the immediate expected outcome of every available action under the game rules and scripted partner policy, in-tegrating over the CRD lottery. The observed profile uses the chosen action; the predicted profile averages over the predicted action probabilities. Each compo-nent is normalized by its aggregate attainable range over the evaluated decisions before the Euclidean distance is computed. Components with zero aggregate range are omitted. # 5 Results 

## 5.1 Elicitation and games yield different preference estimates 

Dedicated elicitation instruments provide an established and validated way of characterizing preferences in both behavioral economics and social psychology. However, the correlation between the one-shot decisions that com-pose these instruments and behavior in strategic set-tings has only been validated through human experi-ments. Therefore, here, contrarily to existing literature, we do not assume that LLM preferences elicited through them correlate well with LLM behavior. Instead, we ask the question of whether the LLM preference esti-mates inferred from elicitation instruments also account for choices in strategic interactions, where agents must weigh several competing objectives simultaneously. In fact, we hypothesize that elicitation is indeed not enough for LLMs, and their behavior in strategic and multi-objective tasks may not align with preferences inferred in this way. Thus, we first examine this question in the Control condition, where agents receive no persona instructions specifying which outcomes to prioritize. This allows us to assess whether elicited preferences carry over to strategic interactions without explicit preference instruc-tions that might encourage consistency across settings. We find that preferences estimated from elicitation place greater emphasis on the partner’s payoff and envi-ronmental outcomes than those inferred from strategic choices (Figure 3). In the elicitation battery, all three LLMs allocate the material component approximately equally between their own and their partner’s payoff. This suggests that, holding the other outcome dimen-sions fixed, the LLMs we test favor increases in com-bined payoff regardless of who benefits. In every game, by contrast, inferred altruism is close to zero, assigning almost all of the material component to the focal agent’s own payoff. These estimates imply a willingness to forgo larger gains for the partner in favor of smaller gains for themselves. Environmental weights show a similar pattern. Elici-tation yields high values of θenv for every LLM, whereas game-based estimates are substantially lower. Environ-mental weight is close to zero in CPR for all three LLMs and in PGG for Muse and Qwen. Gemma retains a some-what larger environmental weight in PGG, and all three LLMs assign more weight to environmental outcomes in CRD than in CPR. Even in these cases, environmental weights remain below their elicitation counterparts. Inferred inequality weights, meanwhile, are close to zero in both elicitation and games, suggesting that con-cern for the payoff gap contributes little to explaining behavior in either setting. The lower environmental weights are therefore accompanied primarily by higher material weights θm. Combined with the lower altru-ism estimates, this indicates a shift toward the focal agent’s own monetary payoff: monetary outcomes re-ceive greater relative weight, and a larger share of that weight is assigned to the agent itself. These findings suggest that preferences estimated from dedicated elicitation tasks do not directly carry over to strategic interactions. However, distinct preference pro-files can favor the same actions when outcomes align or when the available choices do not distinguish between them. To account for this, we next examine the behav-ioral consequences of these differences.

Are game-inferred preferences consistent with observed behavior? Before testing their predictive performance, we examine whether the game-inferred preferences offer a plausible account of these observed outcome patterns. 13 The high own payoffs and low partner payoffs observed for Muse and Qwen in PGG are consistent with their high material weights and near-zero altruism. Although partner payoffs are higher in CRD and CPR, this does not contradict the near-zero altruism inferred from game choices, as pursuing one’s own payoff can also preserve or improve the partner’s earnings. In CRD, contributing enough to prevent a collective loss can pro-tect both players. In CPR, excessive appropriation effort reduces returns through crowding, so maximizing one’s own payoff does not imply maximizing extraction and can leave the partner with substantial earnings. These incentives allow high own payoffs to coexist with sub-stantial partner payoffs even when altruism is close to zero. Similarly, favorable environmental outcomes can arise from pursuing one’s own monetary payoff. In CPR, for example, reducing excessive appropriation effort can in-crease one’s own earnings by limiting crowding while also reducing resource depletion. Favorable environmen-tal outcomes are therefore compatible with low inferred environmental weights. In PGG, Gemma’s higher envi-ronmental outcomes and lower own payoffs relative to Muse and Qwen are consistent with its higher inferred environmental weight.

## 5.3 Inferred preferences and behavioral mechanisms improve held-out pre-diction 

Differences between inferred and intended preferences raise the question of whether these departures reflect estimation error or systematic behavior consistent with different preferences. If the inferred preferences cap-ture such behavior, allowing them to depart from the intended targets should improve prediction of held-out choices. We therefore start with a benchmark that acts optimally on the intended preferences. We then allow behavioral parameters, preference parameters, or both to be estimated, to examine which flexibility improves prediction. All model comparisons use matched held-out decisions. As a first comparison, we evaluate whether the jointly fitted model (PBM) better predicts behavior than an agent acting optimally on the intended preferences. This tests whether the intended preferences and optimal decision-making adequately explain observed choices. We show this comparison in Figure 7, row A of each sub-plot, where the jointly fitted model generally improves prediction for all metrics. The gains therefore extend beyond identifying the selected action: the model better predicts response probabilities and aggregate outcomes. Because these improvements could be due to behav-ioral flexibility, we next compare the jointly fitted model to one where preference parameters are again fixed to their intended values, but behavioral parameters are fit-ted to observed behavior (row B). We still find consis-tently improved performance, although the advantage is less marked, suggesting that departures from intended preferences contribute to predicting held-out behavior even after accounting for behavioral imperfections. Amodel where we only enable the precision mechanism while retaining intended preferences also results in worse predictive performance than the jointly fitted model (row C). For both comparisons, the improvements are signifi-cant across all six metrics in every game. Predictive transfer across games. We next test whether the cross-game alignment in preference esti-mates extends to predicting choices in another game. We transfer both preference and behavioral parameters from each source game to each destination game with-out further adaptation, recomputing action values under the destination game’s rules. We compare these predic-tions with intended optimal behavior: an optimal plan-ner using the persona’s intended preferences and fixed near-greedy decision precision. This tests whether ob-serving behavior in another game provides as useful a basis for prediction as knowing the intended preferences and assuming optimal behavior. Transferred PBM generally matches or exceeds the choice accuracy of intended optimal behavior (Figure 9). Accuracy improves in most comparisons, including every transfer to CRD. The exceptions are transfers to CPR for Qwen and from CPR to PGG for Muse, where reduc-tions are modest. A model fitted to behavior in another game can therefore predict the selected action as accu-rately as, and often more accurately than, the intended optimal benchmark. The probability-based metrics provide further sup-port for transfer. Transferred PBM consistently achieves lower NLL, assigning greater probability to observed choices. The size of this advantage mainly reflects the near-determinism of the intended optimal baseline, which assigns very little probability to choices other than its preferred action. When an observed action differs from that choice, the intended optimal policy incurs a large NLL penalty. RPS also improves in every compar-ison, showing that the advantage extends to predictions of the ordered action distribution. Calibration error is lower in all transfers except CPR to PGG, indicating that predicted action probabilities generally correspond more closely to observed choice frequencies. Outcome Euclidean distance asks whether the pre-dicted choices also reproduce the balance and levels of aggregate outcomes. The results are mixed, with im-provements in most comparisons but variation across LLMs and transfer directions. Every transfer from CRD yields a closer outcome profile than intended optimal be-havior, whereas every transfer from CPR to PGG yields a less accurate profile. Better prediction of the action distribution therefore does not uniformly imply better prediction of aggregate material and environmental out-comes. Transfer performance also depends on the source game. CRD provides better predictions of PGG and CPR than the alternative source across all three LLMs and all six metrics. Behavior observed in CRD therefore provides a particularly informative basis for predicting choices in the other settings. One possible explanation is that CRD shares features with both games: contri-butions involve an immediate personal cost, as in PGG, while crossing a threshold can prevent an adverse collec-tive outcome, as in CPR. Finally, across transfer sources, we find that predictive performance is generally better for Gemma, and worse for Muse, with Qwen in between. These results show that observing behavior in one game can provide as useful a basis for predicting choices in another as knowing the intended preferences and as-suming optimal behavior. Without access to the persona instructions or adaptation to destination-game choices, the transferred model generally matches or improves choice accuracy and better captures the response dis-tribution. The advantage is less consistent for action distance and aggregate outcomes, but the overall results support the predictive value of transferring the jointly fitted model across games. 

# 6 Discussion & Conclusion 

Understanding how LLM agents balance competing ob-jectives is essential for assessing whether their behav-ior aligns with the priorities they are intended to repre-sent. However, preferences that LLMs express in ded-icated elicitation tasks may not carry over to strate-gic interactions, and observed choices can reflect both preferences and imperfections in decision-making. We addressed this problem with the Preference-Behavior Model (PBM), formulating preference inference as an inverse-game problem, jointly estimating how agents value outcomes and how those values translate into choices. Our results show that preferences estimated from ded-icated elicitation tasks do not consistently account for strategic behavior. In the control condition, elicitation suggests greater concern for others and the environment, while observed game choices favor the agent’s own pay-off more strongly than elicitation-fitted models predict. This gap persists in final-round decisions, indicating that difficulties with multi-round planning alone cannot ex-plain it. We further found that persona instructions produce distinguishable broad priorities, but their precise ex-pression varies across preference dimensions, LLMs and games. Allowing preference parameters to depart from their intended targets improves held-out prediction even when behavioral parameters are re-estimated. Estimat-ing preferences without a behavioral model (i.e., under full-horizon optimal planning with near-deterministic choice) also performs worse on most metrics than joint estimation. The predictive gains suggest that differ-ences in behavior are more informative when described through both preferences and decision-making tenden-cies. Agents with similar priorities can act differently because they differ in planning, choice consistency or sensitivity to previous actions. The framework allows these differences to be characterized alongside differences in preferences. By contrast, preference estimates from one game gen-erally correlate with estimates from other games nearly as strongly as, or more strongly than, the intended tar-gets do, and more strongly than elicitation estimates, particularly for environmental and material weights. Transferring preference and behavioral parameters with-out adaptation also generally matches or improves choice accuracy relative to intended optimal behavior. Ob-served choices therefore provide a useful basis for pre-dicting behavior in another game without access to the persona instructions. However, the extent to which these findings gener-alize beyond the evaluated games depends on several features of our experimental design. In particular, our experiments are limited to short interactions with dis-closed, scripted partners and a common representation of monetary, inequality and environmental outcomes. Each game is also presented with a single description and each persona with a single wording, so the sensitivity of the estimates to how scenarios and instructions are phrased remains untested. This design supports controlled com-parisons, but transfer to unknown or adaptive partners remains untested. Such settings would require model-ing agents’ expectations about others, since inaccurate expectations could otherwise be absorbed into inferred preferences. The assumed utility function also restricts the motives represented, and joint estimation does not establish a unique separation between preferences and decision-making. Longer interactions, richer utility func-tions and a broader range of LLMs would help establish the scope of the observed transfer. Experiments vary-ing the number of competing objectives independently of how outcomes are presented would also help explain the gap between elicitation and strategic behavior. Despite these limitations, our findings call for a more systematic study of how persona prompts and persona vectors (Chen et al., 2025) translate into preferences 19 and behavior. Work that uses personas to represent hu-man populations or users often takes this translation for granted (Phelps and Russell, 2025; Cook et al., 2026), and studies that characterize LLM preferences typically apply elicitation instruments that were validated only for human participants (Chen et al., 2023; Cook et al., 2026). For humans, these instruments are trusted be-cause their scores are reliable and predict incentivized behavior (Murphy et al., 2011; Dohmen et al., 2011), whereas the choices of LLMs depend strongly on the context of the prompt, shift with the elicitation proto-col and often fail to transfer across tasks (Kovač et al., 2024; Röttger et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2025; Yamin et al., 2026). Since elicitation tasks present isolated, one-shot decisions, the preferences they reveal may differ from those that guide an LLM in strategic interactions. Our results suggest that neither assump-tion should go untested, since intended and elicited pref-erences only partly predict how LLMs trade off com-peting objectives once the outcomes also depend on the choices of others. By jointly modeling preferences and decision-making, PBM provides a systematic and formal way to define the alignment between intended and in-ferred preferences and to test how well these preferences carry over from one game to another. As LLM agents increasingly interact with each other and with humans on behalf of others, being able to evaluate what agents value through their decisions, rather than what they re-port or are instructed to value, will be central to assess-ing whether their behavior is aligned with the priorities they are meant to represent.

# S1 Supporting methodology 

## S1.1 Preference model 

Here we further detail the properties of the utility in Eq. (19), which values the focal agent’s cumulative monetary payoff X, the partner’s cumulative monetary payoff Y and the cumulative environmental outcome E, with θa ∈ [0 , 1] ,

θenv ≥ 0, θeq ∈ [−1, 1] and |θeq | + θenv ≤ 1.

Geometry. The three magnitudes (θm, |θeq |, θ env ) are nonnegative and sum to one, so they are a point of a triangle (2D-Simplex) whose vertices are pure concern for material payoffs, for the payoff gap and for the environment. The sign of θeq doubles this triangle into the two triangles of Figure 2 of the main text, which share the edge θeq = 0 ,with inequality aversion on one side and inequality seeking on the other. A preference parameter vector consists of a point in this region together with the altruism share θa. On the edge θm = 0 the material component has no weight and θa has no effect on utility, although monetary payoffs still matter through the gap whenever θeq ̸ = 0 .

Exchange rates. An exchange rate states how much of one outcome an agent is willing to give up for another. Here we express every outcome in tokens of material payoff, the combination (1 − θa)X + θaY that the material weight values, which equals the agent’s own payoff when no other person is affected. The environmental exchange rate is then the number of material tokens that one environmental unit is worth to the agent, and the inequality exchange rate is the number of material tokens that a one-token change in the payoff gap is worth. An agent with an environmental rate of two, for example, is indifferent between keeping two tokens and delivering one environmental unit. In (19), one material token adds θm/B to utility, one environmental unit adds θenv /B and a one-token increase of the gap subtracts θeq /B , so for θm > 0 the two rates are

Scale and precision. All outcomes are expressed in the fixed unit B = 10 , the per-round endowment of the games. We do not normalize by each scenario’s attainable utility range, because doing so would alter the relative weight of scenarios and erase information about the magnitude of preferences under a common precision. Since payoff differences are multiplied by θm, an agent with a small material weight needs a larger precision to choose between payoffs as sharply as one with a large material weight. 

Relation to established social-preference models. For θenv = 0 the linear form (S1) is the altruism–equality utility of Mizuno and Shimizu (2024) with altruism θa and inequality aversion ρeq , and, splitting the gap by its sign, it is the piecewise-linear utility of Bruhin et al. ## S1.3 Preference inference 

Here we further detail how the joint posterior in Eq. (13) is computed and evaluated. 

Data. A fit uses all valid decisions of one game, LLM and persona, pooled over repetitions and scenarios. Each decision contributes the probability that the combined choice rule assigns to the observed action at the observed state, where the state is reconstructed from the scenario parameters and the realized history, including the realized CRD losses. 

Sampling coordinates. Preference parameters use a coupled transformation of unconstrained coordinates (u, v ):

θenv = σ(u), θeq = σ(−u) tanh( v), θm = 1 − θenv − | θeq |, (S2) where σ is the logistic function. Since σ(−u) = 1 −θenv , this parameterization allocates the environmental weight first, and tanh( v) ∈ (−1, 1) determines the signed share of the remaining weight assigned to inequality. The material weight receives the remainder. This allocation order is a device for sampling valid preference parameters during inference, not an assumption about how the LLM forms its preferences. We add the log absolute Jacobian determinant, 

log σ(u) + 2 log σ(−u) + log sech 2(v), to the log density to account for the change of coordinates and preserve the uniform prior on the signed triangle. Other bounded parameters use a logistic transformation with its Jacobian. Log-precision and repetition bias are sampled directly in unconstrained coordinates. 

Sampler. We use four-chain adaptive Metropolis–Hastings sampling (Hastings, 1970; Haario et al., 2001), com-bining joint, block, and single-coordinate Gaussian proposals to accommodate both dependence between parameters and adjustments to individual parameters. We use differences between pilot-chain centers to construct symmetric jump proposals, analogous to the difference-based proposals of differential evolution MCMC (ter Braak, 2006), to help chains move between separated posterior regions. Sampling is extended in stages, with saved fits reaching up to 50,000 production draws per chain. Diagnostics are checked every 1,000 draws, with a stopping target of rank-normalized split ˆR < 1.01 and bulk and tail effective sample sizes of at least 400 for two consecutive checks. Results retain their completed stage and diagnostic status. Point estimates are posterior medians and intervals are equal-tailed 95% posterior intervals. 

Held-out evaluation. In addition to keeping all rounds and repetitions of a scenario in one fold, variants differing only in environmental stakes or their scaling coefficients are grouped together as well. Shared physical scenario groups are assigned consistently across LLMs. Each fold uses only its training observations, and posterior-predictive probabilities are evaluated at the observed held-out states. Cross-game transfer recomputes action values under the destination game’s rules and observed histories.

## S2.3 Game administration 

Scenarios. Every scenario describes the choice as a community contribution (in the CPR, as effort on a community resource). The environmental outcome is never added to either player’s earnings, and its units are experimental units rather than calibrated physical measurements. Environmental personas keep their environmental language, since this description concerns only the game instructions. 

Episode independence. The initial message also states the initial earnings, the horizon and the action set. After each round the next prompt reports both actions, the realized round earnings, the cumulative earnings and the public and environmental state. Previous model explanations are not repeated, and only the information required to define the next game state is carried forward. Matched scenario variants are placed in separate conversations. S12
