CHOONG SEON HONG, School of Computing, Kyung Hee University, Republic of Korea Humanoid robots are moving from laboratory platforms toward connected fleets that share workspaces, infrastructure, tasks, and knowledge with other robots and humans. As deployments grow, the operation and improvement of each humanoid depend on information exchanged across the network. However, existing research has largely studied humanoids as individual systems. This survey examines networked humanoid intelligence through four functions of information exchange: communication, coordination, collaboration, and social intelligence with human interaction. We first establish the foundations of networked humanoid systems, define the question addressed by each function, and connect them through a common factory scenario. We review the literature along six dimensions: interaction architecture, exchanged information, physical coupling, operating timescale, system scale, and embodiment. We separate results obtained with physical and simulated humanoids from those based on other robotic platforms or disembodied agents, because the latter do not fully capture humanoid-specific constraints such as balance, whole-body motion, contact, and physical interaction. We examine hardware, datasets, and benchmarks, and distinguish risks handled locally from those networking introduces, such as stale information or unsafe knowledge propagation. Finally, we identify open problems in control-aware networking, compact body-centric messages, physically grounded coordination, network- and embodiment-aware knowledge sharing, fleet-level social consistency, and accountable human oversight. 1 Introduction 

Humanoid robots are moving from laboratory prototypes toward large-scale deployment in manu-facturing, logistics, service, and household environments. Major manufacturers are already prepar-ing for mass production: Tesla is targeting a production capacity of up to 10 million Optimus robots [136], while a growing number of commercial humanoid platforms are entering industrial and service applications [16, 61, 129]. This transition is supported not only by advances in humanoid hardware and embodied intelligence, but also by societal needs such as aging populations, labor shortages, and growing demand for care and service automation [160]. As deployment scales, humanoids will increasingly operate as part of larger populations: a factory may operate hundreds of robots at a single site, while a service provider may manage a much larger population distributed across homes and workplaces. Therefore, humanoids should be seen as members of connected populations that interact with other robots, shared infrastructure, and humans. The rapid progress of humanoid robotics has largely focused on improving the capabilities of individual robots. Recent advances in reinforcement learning (RL) and whole-body control enable humanoids to walk, recover balance, track human motions, and perform complex whole-body behaviors [39, 47, 63, 85]. At a higher level, vision-language-action (VLA) models translate visual observations and language instructions into robot actions, enabling a single humanoid to perform diverse manipulation and loco-manipulation tasks [13, 73, 173]. World models further capture environment dynamics and predict action outcomes, supporting planning, policy evaluation, and behavior generation [4, 29, 41, 49, 166]. In parallel, advances in visual perception improve state estimation and environment understanding [11], while motion-planning methods generate feasible actions under kinematic, stability, and real-time constraints [28]. These developments provide the perception, prediction, reasoning, and control capabilities needed for increasingly autonomous humanoids. However, they primarily consider a single robot that observes its own environment and selects its own actions. They do not provide an explicit interface through which other robots can contribute information, coordinate decisions, or participate in joint control. Consider two humanoids moving through a warehouse aisle while jointly handling a shelf module as a human worker passes nearby. The robots must first exchange task-relevant information in time to maintain a consistent view of the task, which is the role of communication [160]. Then, they have to decide who uses the aisle, who handles each part of the object, and when each action should occur, which requires coordination [23, 167]. When the shelf cannot be handled by either robot alone, they must synchronize their motions and forces and share useful experience across the fleet. These joint execution and learning processes form collaboration [50, 57, 143, 147]. Finally, operating around people requires the robots to understand human intentions, communicate their actions, and behave safely and predictably, which requires social intelligence and human interaction [138, 160]. These four capabilities define the main dimensions of networked humanoid intelligence. 

1.1 Why Humanoids Change the Networking Problem 

Although these capabilities appear in conventional multi-robot systems, humanoid embodiment imposes additional constraints on their operation. Bipedal balance and whole-body control rely on fast feedback, limiting the extent to which safety-critical control can depend on the network [160]. For example, a communication delay that may only interrupt the motion of a wheeled robot can disrupt synchronization between physically coupled humanoids, generate unwanted interaction forces, and threaten their balance [50, 143, 147]. At the same time, high degree-of-freedom (DoF) bodies and multimodal sensors generate large and heterogeneous data streams, making contin-uous exchange of raw observations impractical [11, 170]. Moreover, differences in morphology, DoF, sensing, and hardware limit the direct transfer of policies and experience across humanoids [86, 114, 152]. 1.2 Related Surveys and Positioning 

Existing surveys approach networked humanoid intelligence from several related directions. Sur-veys on humanoid robotics cover hardware design, locomotion and control, perception, planning, and foundation models, but primarily focus on the capabilities of individual robots [16, 28, 129, 161]. Surveys on VLA and embodied models similarly examine perception-action architectures and language-conditioned control, with the robot typically treated as an individual decision-making agent [64, 69, 91]. By contrast, multi-robot and multi-agent surveys study communication, coordi-nation, collaboration, multi-agent RL (MARL), and LLM-based teams [20, 36, 45, 48, 67, 133, 145]. However, these studies mainly consider wheeled robots, aerial robots, and manipulators, and therefore do not fully capture the physical and control constraints of humanoids. Networked-robotics surveys provide another complementary view by studying cloud, edge, IoT, and AIoT infrastructures for communication, computation offloading, resource management, and distributed intelligence [10, 70, 146, 168]. The Internet of Humanoids [160] considers connected humanoids, but primarily focuses on an architecture- and application-oriented view of the field. Therefore, a gap remains between studies of individual humanoid intelligence and studies of distributed robotic systems. In particular, existing surveys do not systematically organize the inter-agent capabilities that allow humanoids to exchange information, coordinate decisions, perform joint physical tasks, share learned experience, and interact with people under humanoid-specific constraints. This survey addresses this gap from a capability- and algorithm-oriented perspective. It organizes the literature around communication, coordination, collaboration, and social intelligence, and examines how these capabilities are implemented and evaluated on humanoid platforms.

2 Foundations of Networked Humanoid Intelligence 

This section introduces the core concepts used throughout the survey. We first examine the humanoid as a network node and explain how its control structure shapes its communication requirements. We then distinguish communication, coordination, cooperation, collaboration, and social interaction according to their roles in a networked humanoid system. Section 2.3 illustrates these functions through a common factory scenario, while Section 2.4 presents the taxonomy and comparison dimensions. 

2.1 The Humanoid as a Network Node 

A humanoid combines functions that operate at very different timescales, from high-level reasoning to fast whole-body control. These differences directly determine how each function can depend on the network. For example, GR00T N1 runs a vision-language module (VLM) at 10 Hz and an embodiment-specific diffusion-transformer action module at 120 Hz [12]. Helix similarly combines low-rate reasoning with a 200 Hz visuomotor policy and kilohertz joint control [38]. Thus, planning, learning, and model updates can tolerate communication with an edge server, whereas state estimation and balance require fast local execution. Humanoid balance places strict limits on network dependence. Unlike a wheeled robot, a hu-manoid must continuously maintain its balance even if communication is interrupted. During walking, its zero-moment point must remain within a support polygon that changes with each step [28]. Therefore, a delayed control update can cause the humanoid to fall [139, 172]. For commu-nication flows that directly affect balance, tail latency and information freshness are more important than average throughput. In addition, humanoids produce large and heterogeneous data streams. For example, Humanoid Everyday records RGB, depth, LiDAR, tactile, inertial, and joint data at 30 Hz [170]. On the other hand, other robots need only task-relevant, body-centric information, such as active contacts and upcoming footsteps. Knowledge sharing introduces a different challenge because learned policies are often tied to a particular embodiment, requiring alignment of joints and sensors before they can be transferred across robots [12, 86]. Communication also extends beyond robot-to-robot exchange: humans interpret a humanoid’s gaze, posture, and motion as signals of intent [32]. These differences mean that network dependence should be determined by the function being supported rather than by the humanoid as a whole [70, 146, 160]. Fleet learning and balance

2.2 Four Functions, Four Questions 

The terms communication, coordination, cooperation, and collaboration are used differently across the literature [67]. For example, task-allocation studies often use coordination to describe how tasks are assigned among robots [44, 75], whereas cognitive science characterizes joint action through shared representations and action prediction [126]. To provide a consistent basis for this survey, we distinguish the four functions by the question each answers and the object on which it acts. Table 2 summarizes these distinctions. 

Communication and networking considers what information is exchanged, with whom, when, and through which resources . The exchanged information may include raw observations, learned features, state estimates, plans, gradients, or model parameters. Depending on the task, this infor-mation can be sent to another humanoid, a group, network infrastructure, or a human. Exchange can be periodic, event-triggered, or on demand, while the underlying resources include spectrum, routes, middleware, and computing infrastructure. Communication succeeds when the required information reaches its recipient while it is still useful [68, 157]. It enables information exchange but does not determine how the recipient should act. 

Coordination concerns how decisions and actions are kept mutually consistent . More specifically, it addresses the compatibility among contributions that each participant could otherwise perform independently. This compatibility can take several forms. Exclusion prevents multiple humanoids from claiming the same task, tool, or space at the same time. Precedence ensures that an action begins only after its prerequisite is completed [75]. Synchronization aligns actions that must occur together, while agreement ensures that participants share the same decision, such as a selected leader or plan version. These constraints can be enforced through centralized assignment, auctions, consensus, priorities, conventions, or negotiation. Coordination succeeds when individual contributions remain compatible as the joint plan is executed. Cooperation describes the intention of multiple agents to pursue a shared goal rather than compete with one another [67]. Unlike coordination, it defines the objective of the interaction. Therefore, cooperating humanoids may rely on communication, coordination, collaboration, or a combination of them. We assume cooperative participants throughout this survey and consider compromised or adversarial participants separately as a safety issue in Section 7.3. 

Collaboration concerns how capabilities or experience are combined for joint execution or im-provement . It concerns the combination of contributions to achieve an outcome that no humanoid can achieve alone, or can achieve as effectively. These contributions can be combined in four ways. Physical collaboration combines forces through a shared object, where the resulting forces affect both object motion and the balance of the participating humanoids [148]. Informational collaboration fuses partial observations to construct a view that a single humanoid cannot obtain alone [84]. Capability collaboration combines complementary skills, tools, or physical abilities to perform a task [95]. Finally, experiential collaboration shares data, policies, or learned experience so that knowledge acquired by one robot can improve others [57]. Across these forms, collaboration depends on how individual contributions are combined. Collaboration requires coordination, but coordination does not necessarily lead to collaboration. For example, two humanoids carrying the same cockpit module must agree on their roles and lifting time (coordination) while combining their forces to move the module (collaboration). In contrast, two humanoids working at different stations may coordinate their tasks without combining their contributions. A simple removal test helps distinguish the two: if one participant is removed and the other can still complete its own part, the interaction is coordination; otherwise, it is collaboration.

Social intelligence and human interaction answers how human intentions, expectations, responsibilities, and control are incorporated into interaction . It treats humans as participants with latent goals, social norms, and authority, rather than simply as obstacles or sources of information. This function cuts across the other three. Informing a worker is communication, yielding to the worker is coordination, and carrying a table together is collaboration [33]. These interactions become social when the humanoid must understand the human’s intent, expectations, or authority to decide how to act. From communication to collaboration, the dependence among agents becomes stronger, but the resulting network requirements differ. Physically coupled collaboration requires millisecond-level timing between robots, whereas experiential collaboration involves large data or model transfers that can tolerate much longer delays. Coordination instead depends on how quickly participants can reach and maintain agreement. Social interaction introduces another constraint: communication is limited not only by network resources but also by human attention. 

2.3 One Deployment, Four Functions: A Factory Scenario 

Consider a segment of an automotive assembly line, illustrated in Figure 1. Humanoids of two models share the workspace: model-A units use grippers, whereas model-B units have dexterous hands. An edge server hosts the fleet manager and larger models, with connectivity provided through private cellular and Wi-Fi networks. A line worker operates at the door station, while a remote supervisor oversees several assembly segments. This scenario reflects the growing deployment of humanoids in industrial environments; for example, a Figure F.02 humanoid has accumulated more than 1,250 operating hours at BMW’s Spartanburg plant [37]. We use this scenario throughout the survey to illustrate the four functions and their interactions. 

Communication. Each humanoid periodically sends a compact status update, including its pose, battery level, and task progress, to the fleet manager. When a humanoid detects a pallet blocking an aisle, it sends an event-triggered update to the robots whose routes pass through that aisle. During cooperative transport, two humanoids exchange gait phase, step timing, and hand wrench at the control timescale over a direct link, where delayed or missing information can affect balance. Between shifts, larger skill updates are distributed across the fleet with much looser delay requirements. Therefore, the same humanoid generates communication flows that differ substantially in data size, frequency, and latency requirements.

2.4 Taxonomy and Review Dimensions 

We organize the literature first by function, defined by the question each mechanism addresses, and then by the corresponding sub-questions as in Figure 2. Sections 3-6 follow these four functional branches. Existing taxonomies provide useful but narrower views: task-allocation taxonomies [44, 75] focus on coordination, architecture-based classifications [167] span multiple functions, and technology- or application-based classifications [133, 145] can separate mechanisms that address the same underlying problem. Within each branch, we analyze the literature through six dimensions: interaction architecture 

(centralized, decentralized, or hybrid), exchanged information , physical coupling (none, shared space, or contact through an object or body), timescale (control, reactive, planning, or learning), system scale , and embodiment evidence . We distinguish five levels of embodiment: physical humanoids (HR), simulated humanoids (HS), other physical robots (RR), other simulated robots (RS), and abstract agents (AB). This distinction is important because results from non-humanoid systems do not capture constraints such as balance, whole-body contact, and anthropomorphic interaction. The importance of each functional branch also depends on the deployment. For example, indus-trial humanoids [37] place greater emphasis on infrastructure coordination, whereas household humanoids [1] require stronger social interaction and teleoperation-assisted autonomy.

Why humanoid communication is different. Multi-robot networking often assumes robots that can stop safely, exchange low-dimensional states, and have simple physical footprints [45]. Humanoids challenge all three assumptions. First, communication deadlines are coupled with balance: a delayed packet used for whole-body control or cooperative tasks can cause instability or a fall [139, 172]. Second, a humanoid generates heterogeneous traffic, ranging from control-rate proprioception to perception updates, language tokens, and model updates [12, 170]. Third, coordination requires body-centric information, such as contact state, gait phase, footstep intent, and whole-body occupancy, for which compact representations remain limited [77, 89]. Fourth, knowledge payloads can be large, while their usefulness depends on compatibility between the bodies of the sender and receiver [86]. Finally, humanoids also communicate intent to people through their motion, which should remain consistent with the information exchanged over the network [32]. Table 3 summarizes these differences. 

3.1 Connectivity, Medium Access, and Middleware

For example, streaming a 256-dimensional observation every 6.67 ms requires only about 1.3 Mbit/s per robot [139], yet this rate is already close to the usable throughput measured over an 802.11s mesh [24]. More importantly, the communication path must deliver these data with sufficiently low and predictable delay. We examine this requirement at three levels.

At the substrate level, infrastructure networks such as Wi-Fi, private cellular, and Open RAN provide centralized access and support traffic prioritization, whereas mesh networks remove the need for fixed infrastructure at the cost of less predictable delay [45]. Time-sensitive networking reduces this uncertainty on wired links by scheduling transmissions through synchronized switch gates [168], while digital twins can jointly model radio propagation and robot navigation [6]. However, neither approach has yet been demonstrated with mobile humanoid fleets. Connectivity can be incorporated into robot motion. Robots predict link quality at unvisited locations and choose paths that preserve connectivity [104], while Mox et al. [102] deploy dedicated relay robots to improve the delivery probability of opportunistic routing. This strategy is less practical for humanoid fleets when every humanoid has a physical task and cannot simply act as relay nodes. At medium access , 5G uplink transmission typically requires a scheduling request followed by a grant, adding delay and jitter before data can be sent. O-RACES [139] reduces this delay by predicting uplink demand. A lightweight DLinear forecaster at the Open RAN distributed unit uses the previous 100 traffic slots to predict demand 30 slots ahead, allowing grants to be issued before packets arrive. The predictor penalizes underestimation more heavily than overestimation because a missing grant delays transmission by another request cycle. At the middleware level, ROS 2 communication latency varies with the DDS implementation, message size, and system configuration [40, 96, 116, 118]. ROS 2 also introduces additional overhead compared with raw DDS [76], while the best transport depends on the underlying network [165]. This issue becomes more pronounced in dynamic meshes, where topology changes trigger costly DDS discovery traffic; Zenoh reduces this overhead through a lighter discovery mechanism [24]. At a slower planning timescale, agent protocols such as model context protocol and agent-to-agent support capability and information exchange between agents [34]. Despite these advances, evidence for humanoid fleets remains limited. Among the reviewed studies, only O-RACES directly relates a communication mechanism to physical outcomes such as humanoid falls, while none evaluates the mixed control, perception, language, and learning traffic generated by a humanoid.

3.2 Selective and Learned Information Exchange 

A reliable link does not determine what information should be sent. The mechanisms below follow a common principle: transmit only the information needed by the receiver’s task, and update it when necessary. They differ mainly in what information they select and what triggers transmission. 

What to communicate: STAR [84] retains a task-agnostic scene representation: the sender trans-mits only a subset of scene patches in each frame, while the receiver reconstructs the missing content with a masked autoencoder using information received from earlier frames. MR-COGraphs [46] represents the environment as an object-level 3D scene graph and compresses the semantic features of each node before transmission; the recovered features also support map merging. Xue et al. [151] further restrict communication to information needed for navigation, including an edge mask, sparse 3D anchors, and an object table. Although these methods target different tasks, they share a common shift from transmitting complete observations to transmitting only the scene information the receiver needs, which is the main focus of the semantic communication paradigm [109]. 

When to communicate: Xue et al. [151] trigger communication only when a predefined event occurs, such as detecting a new target or observing a large change in pose. However, not every change has the same importance. Therefore, GSRC [149] prioritizes queued commands using both age of information (AoI), which captures how stale a command is, and value of information (VoI), which measures its contribution to the desired trajectory. Communication timing must be coordinated across robots. The work [169] maintains each robot’s broadcast rate but staggers their transmission times. This prevents synchronized updates from making the shared map stale at the same time for all robots, which can otherwise cause multiple robots to select the same exploration frontiers. 

Who to communicate and whom to receive: CommNet [132] provides the baseline without selection: each agent receives the average hidden state of all teammates within a fixed communication range, so the same controller handles different team sizes but treats every sender equally. SchedNet [72] selects senders when the shared medium admits only 𝑘 transmitters: each agent learns an importance weight, and only the 𝑘 agents with the highest weights broadcast, typically one agent sending one or two 16-bit values per step. On the other hand, TarMAC [27] selects receivers: each message carries a signature and a value, and attention between the sender’s signature and the receiver’s query, learned from task reward alone, determines how strongly each receiver uses the message. Its messages (a 16-dimensional signature and a 32-dimensional value) are far smaller than the 224 × 224 image observations. CommNet, SchedNet, and TarMAC are evaluated using abstract agents. HetNet [127] extends targeted communication to heterogeneous teams, where some robots sense the environment and others act only on their teammates’ messages. Robots communicate only within range, and messages are encoded according to the types of both sender and receiver. Binary messages cut the bits per round by about 200 times compared with real-valued baselines. However, when noise grows with distance, performance drops sharply even at high signal-to-noise ratio, showing that communication still struggles with the changing links of mobile robots. These mechanisms improve communication efficiency, but their benefits depend on assumptions about what information the receiver needs and how it uses the message. In addition, most approaches are evaluated mainly with abstract agents over idealized communication channels, with limited validation on real embodied robots. None of the methods has been tested on humanoids or communicates humanoid-specific states such as contact state or motion intent.

Offloading reduces onboard computation by moving part of the workload to edge or cloud servers, but it also introduces communication delay. It is beneficial only when uplink transmission, remote

This trade-off is particularly relevant to humanoids: a foundation model that runs at 11 Hz onboard can reach 32 Hz on a desktop GPU [139], while time-critical functions such as whole-body stabilization must remain onboard. Existing work increasingly treats computation placement as a function-specific decision. Huang et al. Cloudroid Swarm [162] moves both computation and inter-robot information exchange to the cloud, reducing bandwidth usage on robot-to-cloud links by 57-80% compared with conventional offloading. In multi-hop networks, offloading decisions also depend on the communication path to the server. Hong et al. [56] propose a game-theoretic method that jointly determines whether each robot should offload its computation and which neighboring robots should relay its data. In simulation, the method reduces the combined latency and energy cost, achieving a performance gain of about 1.5 times over local computing. However, it assumes that robots remain nearly stationary during offloading. Rahman et al. [119] further include robot motion, allowing robots to move toward access points with better bandwidth. In a warehouse task, this reduces energy by about 40% compared with a single cloud-assisted robot, at the cost of more than twice the travel distance and an offline plan that cannot adapt to changing links. Therefore, computation placement, routing, and robot motion become coupled decisions. Offloading processing to edge servers reduces onboard computation but increases responsive-ness dependency on network conditions. To address this trade-off, Yang et al. [155] demonstrate FollowMe, a person-following robot that switches between JPEG-compressed images and compact vector-quantized features according to Wi-Fi signal strength to maintain reliable tracking. Ijaz et al. [62] jointly optimize computation offloading, CPU frequency, transmit power, and bandwidth, reducing 41% swarm energy consumption while meeting over 99% of task deadlines. When the bandwidth is limited, the method keeps more computation onboard to reduce transmission costs. However, it assumes that workloads can be divided between local and edge processing, which is suitable for perception tasks but less practical for tightly coupled balance-control policies. 

3.4 Communication Inside the Control Loop 

When networked information is used to control robot motion, communication delays can affect stability. For humanoids, one solution is to separate control functions according to their timing requirements. Zhu et al. [172] apply this approach by splitting whole-body control between a 5G edge server and the robot. The server computes the expensive, slowly changing part of the controller, while the robot updates time-critical feedback locally using the latest received solution. When communication is delayed, the robot reuses the previous solution to maintain control. In simulations with a 37-DoF humanoid, this increases the maximum tolerable constant delay from 40 to 90 ms for balancing and from 3 to 25 ms for walking, compared with fully remote control. Wen et al. [143] take a different approach to address the delay issue. Because network fluctuations can desynchronize two NAO robots carrying a stretcher, the leader sends the follower a sequence of pre-planned commands, each tagged with an execution time, and both robots then execute the motion locally at the specified times. This avoids continuous communication during control, but requires the motion to be planned in advance and the robots’ clocks to remain synchronized, and the paper does not measure network delay. Another approach is to reduce communication

Compared with reactive scheduling, O-RACES reduces uplink latency by up to 50%. During rough-terrain walking, reactive scheduling leads to humanoid falls, whereas proactive scheduling nearly eliminates them. These results show how network scheduling can directly affect humanoid stability. 

3.5 Summary and Lessons Learned 

The existing studies address different aspects of communication. Connectivity and middleware support timely message delivery, selective exchange determines what information to share and with whom, computation placement determines which functions rely on the network, and control-loop designs mitigate the effects of delayed messages. These findings lead to four key lessons. 

Network requirements follow the consuming function, not the robot as a whole. Ahumanoid generates control, perception, planning, and learning traffic, and each flow has different timing requirements: balance control and physically coupled motion require fresh information with bounded delay; perception, planning, and model updates generally tolerate longer delays. For time-critical control, retransmitting an old packet can even be worse than dropping it since the information may no longer represent the robot’s current physical state [155]. 

The main bottleneck is increasingly information selection rather than raw capacity. 

Existing methods increasingly avoid transmitting complete observations and instead select compact latent features, semantic scene elements, event-triggered updates, or specific recipients [72, 84, 149, 151]. AoI and VoI show that messages with the same size can have very different value depending on their freshness and relevance to the task. Humanoid fleets still lack compact representations for body-centric information such as contact state, gait phase, footstep intent, interaction force, and predicted whole-body occupancy, as well as how long this information remains valid. 

Communication, computation, and control placement should be designed together. 

Running a function remotely saves onboard computation but introduces network delay. Existing work jointly decides where to compute along with routing, resource allocation, and even robot motion [56, 58, 62], but their scenarios are limited to delay-tolerant tasks such as mapping and perception over stable links. Humanoid control places stricter requirements on computation place-ment. Slow functions can be offloaded, while time-critical feedback remains onboard to maintain control when remote results are delayed [172]. 

Communication should be evaluated by its physical consequences. Most existing mecha-nisms are evaluated through throughput, latency, traffic volume, inference cost, or task success. Only a few studies relate network behavior to physical outcomes such as humanoid falls, and none examines how network impairment affects interaction forces during physically coupled tasks. Evaluation should connect delay, jitter, packet loss, and stale information to physical outcomes such as balance violations, task degradation, and interaction forces. Overall, communication pro-vides timely and task-relevant information, but it does not ensure that different humanoids act consistently on that information, which leads to coordination.

4.1 Who Does What: Allocation and Scheduling 

Task allocation depends on how accurately the system represents what each robot can do. Classical methods represent this capability through a scalar utility for each robot-task pair [44, 75, 135]. For example, CBBA [23] allows each robot to build a sequence of tasks based on its expected benefits and exchange bids with its teammates to resolve conflicting assignments. This approach produces conflict-free assignments even when robots have different information about the environment. It also guarantees at least 50% of the optimal reward when task values exhibit diminishing returns and robots have accurate information. However, a high utility score does not necessarily mean that a humanoid can safely perform the task unless its physical constraints are explicitly considered. 

ACM Comput. Surv., Vol. 1, No. 1, Article . Publication date: October 2026. Networked Humanoid Intelligence: Communication, Coordination, Collaboration, and Social Interaction 15 

Capability-aware methods address this limitation by representing robot capabilities more ex-plicitly. EMOS [18] uses robot descriptions in Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) and forward-kinematics tools to generate a “robot resume” describing mobility, perception, and manip-ulation capabilities, including limits such as arm reach. A central LLM assigns tasks, while each robot’s LLM agent checks whether it can perform its assigned task and requests reassignment if necessary. SMART-LLM [66] considers tasks that require multiple robots. It uses an LLM to divide tasks into subtasks, match them to predefined robot skills, and form teams when no single robot has the required capabilities. SMART-LLM with GPT-4 achieves 71% success, compared with 14% for rule-based allocation. However, its task assignments are planned before execution and are not updated as conditions change. Beyond assigning tasks to suitable robots, coordination must also consider the order in which tasks are performed. DART-LLM [142] uses an LLM to divide instructions into subtasks and represent their dependencies in a directed task graph. This graph allows independent subtasks to run in parallel while ensuring that other subtasks follow the required order. In complex construction tasks, this improves the success rate of an 8B model from 45% to 84%. Task assignments may also need to change when execution does not proceed as planned. COHERENT [87] addresses this problem by allowing individual robot agents to report their progress or failures to a central LLM, which uses the feedback to revise task assignments. In simulated multi-robot tasks, COHERENT achieves a 97.5% success rate, compared with 75% when execution history is removed. Peng et al. [117] combine LLMs with mathematical optimization for multi-robot task allocation. Locally deployed LLMs translate scheduling constraints, such as deadlines and task dependencies, into mixed-integer linear programming models, which a solver uses to assign and schedule robot tasks. Overall, these methods capture robot capabilities and task constraints but do not fully account for how a humanoid’s physical state affects task feasibility. Humanoid soccer provides an early example of considering robot motion in task allocation. Yi et al. [158] use each humanoid’s walking planner to estimate how long it would take to reach the ball. Robots exchange these estimates, and the robot expected to arrive first becomes the attacker, with a mechanism to prevent frequent role switching. The approach was demonstrated in real RoboCup matches, but considers mainly locomotion time rather than broader physical constraints. Nadiri and Rad [106] take a broader approach by considering each humanoid’s distance to the ball, kicking ability, and battery level when assigning roles. Their decentralized method uses ant-colony-inspired scores to update roles and adjust team formations as conditions change. It reduces role reassignment time from 5.1 s to 2.3 s compared with centralized coordination and maintains performance even when more than 50% of communication packets are lost.

4.3 Spatial Coordination 

Spatial coordination determines where robots move and how they safely share the same space. One approach is to separate team-level planning from local motion control. Alonso-Mora et al. [5] propose a distributed method in which robots exchange information with their neighbors to identify a shared collision-free region and adjust their formation to fit within it. Each robot then moves toward the assigned position while avoiding nearby obstacles. The method adjusted its formation to avoid a moving human, with a median planning time of 0.35 s and local control running at 20 Hz. However, the method assumes reliable communication without packet loss and simplified robot shapes. Its local planning can also lead to deadlocks. Maintaining a formation is more challenging when robots have different motion capabilities. Li et al. [83] address this problem with a car-like leader and omnidirectional followers. Their method combines physics-based control

to account for the leader’s motion, feedback control to correct tracking errors, and reinforcement learning to reduce the remaining errors. This improves formation accuracy compared with RL alone, although the method has only been evaluated in 2D simulation. While these approaches coordinate robots around predefined formation patterns, robots moving independently through shared spaces must instead avoid conflicts between their planned trajectories. Proactive planning yields better paths only when these predictions are reliable, whereas reactive planning is safer but can be suboptimal [25]. This trade-off motivates hybrid designs that combine long-horizon planning with fast local reactions [154]. Besides deciding where to move, robots must also coordinate when they move. Montaño and Suárez [101] propose an online method that allows robot arms to follow independently planned paths while adjusting the timing of their movements to avoid collisions. Each robot checks its teammates’ upcoming positions and adjusts its progress without changing the original path. However, the method requires synchronized execution and may force robots to stop when collision checking cannot keep up. Applying this approach to walking humanoids would also require maintaining balance during such interruptions. Humanoid embodiment introduces spatial constraints that position-based collision avoidance cannot capture. Existing humanoid soccer systems use position-based repulsion to avoid team-mates [106, 158], but a humanoid’s occupied space also changes with body sway and posture. Kumagai et al. [77] account for predicted center-of-mass sway when planning footsteps, then use these footsteps to guide collision-free whole-body motion planning within 82 ms per step. In simulation, this improves planning success through a narrow gate from 60% to 86.7%. Rossini et al. [121] address moving obstacles by adjusting upcoming footsteps in under 1 ms and using model-predictive control to maintain balance. Although both studies focus on single humanoids, they highlight the need to account for changing body occupancy during movement. For networked humanoids, compact motion envelopes could represent these changes without exchanging complete joint trajectories. 

4.4 Runtime Conflict Resolution and Repair 

A coordination plan remains valid only while its assumptions hold, so robots must detect and repair conflicts during execution. Because language models are too slow to run inside fast control loops, most methods call them only when a failure or conflict occurs, while a fast local layer keeps the robots safe in the meantime [81]. At the task level, these methods differ mainly in when they catch a failure. RoCo [95] checks task feasibility and collisions before execution and uses failed checks to revise plans. COHERENT [87] further distinguishes between failures caused by an incorrect subtask, an unsuitable robot assignment, or an action that cannot be executed. This allows the central planner to identify what went wrong and adjust its next proposal accordingly. Some failures also require closer analysis after execution. REFLECT [88] summarizes a robot’s visual, audio, and state observations to identify what went wrong. It first checks whether each subgoal was completed, then examines relevant observations to explain execution failures or reviews the original plan to identify planning errors. These explanations guide recovery plans that succeed in about 80% of simulated cases. However, the method focuses on single-robot tasks, assumes a static environment, and is less effective at diagnosing low-level control failures. Runtime conflicts can also arise during robot movement, even when collisions are avoided. Garg et al. [42] consider robot teams navigating around obstacles while maintaining communication connectivity. These constraints can cause the entire team to become stuck before reaching its goals. To resolve such deadlocks, their method uses a foundation model to suggest temporary waypoints for a leader, allowing the robots to move together out of the blocked region. A distributed graph-based controller guides their movements while maintaining collision avoidance and connectivity. Other motion conflicts can be resolved before robots become stuck. 4.5 Summary and Lessons Learned 

Table 4 shows that coordination mechanisms differ in what the team must agree on, where agreement is reached, and how much communication and computation each decision requires. Across these mechanisms, four lessons emerge: 

Slow global reasoning is paired with fast local safety. Across allocation, agreement, spatial coordination, and recovery, an effective design uses a slower global process to propose decisions and a fast local layer to check and execute them [5, 21, 42, 87]. The global process considers the whole team, while the local layer enforces constraints that are difficult to capture centrally. However, current local checks mainly cover feasible actions, kinematic reachability, and collision avoidance [87, 95], but rarely consider humanoid-specific constraints. 

Shared coordination state should better reflect humanoid physics. Current teams mainly exchange bids, roles, positions, and target regions, while multi-humanoid systems often model each robot as a simple disc for collision avoidance [106, 158]. However, single-humanoid planning shows that the space a humanoid needs changes with body sway, posture, and footsteps [77, 121]. Therefore, coordination should include a compact motion envelope that captures expected body occupancy, planned footsteps, allowable deviation, and how long this information remains valid. 

Agreement cost should be considered together with how long a decision remains useful. 

Existing studies measure coordination cost in rounds, transmissions, tokens, but rarely connect this cost to physical execution. Agreement becomes less tolerant to delay as connectivity increases [113], while language-based coordination may require several model calls [21] or seconds of dialogue [92]. Some methods resolve conflicts by making robots wait [101], which is not always possible. 

Coordination state needs explicit validity and targeted repair. Task assignments and space reservations can become outdated as the robot or network state changes. Existing methods address this by reducing confidence in shared estimates when they are not updated [26] and by revising only the tasks affected by a trigger [60]. This is important for humanoids because a stumble may invalidate only part of a plan while the robot remains active. Shared state should record the source, scope, and validity period of each piece of information, so that only the affected commitments need to be updated.

Why humanoid collaboration is different. Humanoid embodiment makes collaboration more tightly coupled than in wheeled robots or fixed-base manipulators. 5.1 Physically Coupled Joint Execution 

Large or heavy objects may require multiple humanoids to carry them together [50, 147]. However, carrying the same object physically couples their movements, so one robot can disturb the balance of another. This becomes particularly challenging in leader-follower control, where the follower adjusts its walking motion only after sensing forces caused by the leader’s movement. Wu et al. [148] illustrate this problem using two HRP-2 humanoids. For a 54 kg robot following a leader moving at 0.1 m/s, their analysis showed that the follower needed to take its next step within approximately 0.72 s to maintain stability. In simulation, the follower began walking after about 1.3 s, causing the leader to fall. Centralized control addresses this problem by coordinating the movements of all robots. Wu et al. [148] propose a controller that regulates the object’s position and internal forces while commanding all humanoids simultaneously. Their method enables two to four HRP-2 robots to transport objects stably in simulation, but its controller neglects object mass and dynamic effects. Hawley and Suleiman [50] combine centralized balance control with compliant arms and footstep adjustments. The arms absorb interaction forces, while the footstep planner corrects differences in the robots’ relative positions. Their approach allows the distance between robots to change during transport. In physical experiments, two Nao robots carried loads of up to half a robot’s mass, although their speed was limited to about 2 cm/s. Both approaches rely on centralized computation, but neither evaluates the effects of network conditions on cooperative control. Unlike centralized approaches, leader-follower control can coordinate robot movements through forces transmitted by the shared object, without explicit communication between robots. Wu

When the leader moves, the follower adjusts its arms in response to forces measured at its wrists, then modifies its footsteps to restore the original hand positions. The robots transported an object while keeping interaction forces generally below 7 N. However, hand movements continued to oscillate, and the experiments were limited to forward and backward motion. Gasbarrone et al. [43] extend this approach by allowing the follower to continuously adjust its footstep positions and directions based on interaction forces. This enables more complex movements, including turning, sideways walking, lifting, and obstacle avoidance. However, the leader does not adjust its plan when the follower encounters an obstacle. Learning-based approaches provide another direction. COLA [33] demonstrates whole-body collaborative carrying with a human partner, including turning and moving over sloped terrain. However, its effectiveness for cooperation between two humanoids remains untested, as a human partner can compensate for the robot’s movements in ways another humanoid may not [50]. Pandit et al. [115] propose decMHT, a decentralized learning-based approach that allows humanoids to carry objects using local observations and shared object-motion commands without direct communication between robots. A policy trained with only two humanoids generalizes to teams of up to ten in simulation and is demonstrated on two physical Digit V3 robots. However, the approach relies on externally provided object-state information, and its performance under network delays remains unexplored. Physical interaction can guide a follower without explicit communication, but exchanging information helps humanoids synchronize their movements. McGill and Lee [97] synchronized two stretcher-carrying DARwIn-OP robots through 100 Hz wireless state updates, achieving a step-phase error of about 5 ms. However, the robots struggled to maintain their relative distance and fell after an average of 15.5 s, showing that footstep synchronization alone does not ensure stability. Wen et al. [143] combine scheduled commands with distributed model predictive control to improve leader-follower trajectory tracking. Beyond object carrying, Harmanoid [89] considers physical interactions between two humanoids, including linked-arm walking and dancing, and incorporates the partner’s state and intended body contacts into motion control. These studies show that physical feedback and synchronized movements alone are not enough for stable collaboration. Forces transmitted through a shared object can help guide a follower, but they may not indicate when a partner is blocked or cannot continue. Similarly, synchronized footsteps do not guarantee that robots maintain their relative positions [97]. Therefore, humanoids need to exchange timely information about their positions, walking phases, and partner conditions while maintaining balance through local control. However, the effects of network delays and communication failures on these mechanisms remain largely unexplored. 

5.2 Reasoning and Capability Composition 

Humanoids can benefit from collaboration even when they have similar capabilities, as each may hold different information about the environment. CoELA [163] demonstrates this using two LLM-based agents performing household tasks. Each agent explores independently, maintains knowledge about objects and teammates, and decides when to share information or request help. By coordinating their activities, the two agents delivered 71% of the target objects, compared with 43% for a single rule-based agent. Interestingly, removing communication between AI agents had little effect, while removing memory nearly doubled the steps required to complete tasks. These results show how agents can combine their knowledge to work more effectively. Humanoids may also need to combine different physical capabilities to complete a task. RoCo [95] uses LLM-based dialogue to help robots understand their teammates’ capabilities and decide how to divide the work. For example, one robot holds a dustpan while another sweeps objects into it. In another task, three robotic arms pass cubes between their limited workspaces to reach destinations

that no single arm can access directly. The work shows how robots can combine complementary capabilities through shared reasoning, although their motion trajectories are still planned centrally. Rather than relying on dialogue, humanoids can also collaborate by anticipating each other’s actions. COMBO [164] explores this approach; it uses a VLM to predict what teammates intend to do and a world model to simulate how their combined actions change the environment. Then, based on the predicted outcomes, each robot chooses its next action. The approach improves task success, but repeatedly predicting and evaluating possible outcomes makes planning computationally expensive. Complementary capabilities can also be combined through learned cooperative policies. Het-Net [127] explores this approach by training robots with different sensing and acting roles toward a shared goal. In a firefighting scenario, sensing robots search for spreading fires, while other robots use their findings to locate and extinguish them. Although these roles are predefined, HetNet learns how the robots should coordinate their actions. The learned policies can also be transferred to larger teams, reducing the training samples needed to reach comparable performance from about 15 million to 3 million. The authors further demonstrate this cooperation using three physical robots. However, these approaches do not fully account for how a humanoid’s reach, carrying capacity, and available actions change with its posture, balance, and locomotion. 

5.3 Fleet Learning and Cross-Embodiment Transfer 

Beyond working together to complete tasks, humanoids can also learn from each other’s experience to improve their future behavior. Tan [134] explored this idea in a simulated hunting task, where agents shared past experiences or learned policies. Both approaches helped agents learn faster than independent learning, although their final performance was similar. Sharing policies required homogeneous agents, while experience could also be shared between different agents if they could interpret it. This distinction is important for humanoid fleets, whose members may have different physical capabilities, and sharing experiences or policies also introduces communication costs. Pooling experience from multiple robots can improve learning, but it also induces communication overhead. OpenBot-Fleet [105] enables robots to learn from experiences collected across different homes. It combines images from 72 wheeled robots to train a shared floor-segmentation model, improving perception in unseen environments. HarvestNet [22] reduces data-sharing costs by selecting useful images onboard using model confidence and feature similarity. The cloud uses these images to improve perception models and update sampling thresholds for future collection. This reduces storage, annotation, and computation costs by 66-81%, but requires target classes to be identified in advance. Fleet-DAgger [57] extends fleet learning by using human interventions to improve a shared policy. It prioritizes robots needing assistance based on failures, uncertainty, and predicted risk, then uses human demonstrations to update the policy for the entire fleet. Sharing models instead of raw data can reduce communication overhead. Yahya et al. [153] keep training data on each robot and send only model updates to a central server. Four robotic arms jointly learned to open an unseen door, which a single-robot policy could not achieve. FLEET-MERGE [140] combines policies trained on different tasks by aligning their internal representations before merging, improving performance by over 50% compared with baselines. However, performance declines when merging becomes less frequent. Learning While Deploying [141] continuously improves a pretrained VLA policy using experience and human feedback from sixteen dual-arm robots. The updated policy achieved 95% average success, outperforming imitation fine-tuning on long-horizon tasks. However, each update requires sending the full VLA model to every robot, and the communication overhead is not reported. When robots have different physical capabilities, sharing knowledge requires aligning their actions, models, or observations. These forms of collaboration differ in what humanoids combine. In physically coupled execution, humanoids combine forces and must exchange information within the control loop. In reasoning and capability composition, they combine knowledge and skills at the planning timescale, while in fleet learning they share experience. Reasoning and capability composition are tested only with simulated agents or robotic arms, and experience sharing with wheeled robots. Three lessons follow.

Physical coupling and inference can reduce communication, but cannot replace it. Afollower can infer the leader’s intent from forces transmitted through the shared object [33, 43], and COMBO predicts its partners’ actions without exchanging messages [164]. However, forces cannot reveal a blocked partner or gradual drift [43, 97], and COMBO’s predictions are slow and error-prone. Communication is most valuable for information a robot cannot observe or infer by itself: HetNet’s sensorless robots cannot act without it [127]. 

All forms lack a usable description of the contributing body. Harmanoid’s partner state lifts success from at most 20% to 92% but is read from the simulator [89], GPT-4 misjudges its arm’s reachability about a third of the time [95], and embodiment descriptors can even reduce cross-humanoid transfer [86]. Therefore, the motion envelope proposed for coordination should be extended from where a humanoid will be to what it can safely contribute in its current state and how its skills map to other bodies. 

Studies report the benefits of collaboration but rarely its costs. Among the physical collaboration studies, only one describes the network it uses [97]. None of the works in Table 5 measures communication cost or examines how unsafe updates could spread [59, 65], even though LWD sends a full VLA model to every robot after each update [141]. Therefore, it is unclear when a fleet should collaborate by exchanging messages, inferring partner behavior, or sharing models. 

6 Social Intelligence and Human Interaction 

Humans cannot be treated as merely additional teammates: their intentions are not directly observ-able, their expectations are not defined by the system, and they hold responsibility and authority over the fleet. Therefore, networked humanoids must infer what humans intend, communicate their own intentions clearly, adapt to human behavior, and preserve meaningful human control. This section reviews these requirements and examines how they change when multiple humanoids interact with and are supervised by the same people. 

Why social interaction is different for humanoids. Humanoid embodiment creates several challenges for human interaction. First, a human-like body can influence trust: [120] found that participants trusted a robotic arm more than a humanoid robot. Second, people interpret a robot’s motion as a signal of its goal even when that motion primarily serves a physical task [32]. Third, humanoids operate close to people and may make physical contact, making perceived safety impor-tant in addition to physical safety [90, 122]. These challenges extend from individual interaction to the fleet level. When several humanoids work with the same person, each may maintain its own estimate of that person’s state, and these estimates must remain consistent across the fleet [110]. 

6.1 Intentions: Social State Inference and Common Ground 

Effective interaction requires a humanoid to infer what a person intends, what the person believes, and what information they share. Intent inference is the most mature of these problems. Hoffman et al. [55] review two main approaches: probabilistic models that estimate human intentions from observed actions, and neural models that learn these relationships from data. Probabilistic models depend on assumptions about human behavior, while neural models require sufficient training data. The study [53] shows how a robot can use inferred human intentions to adapt its actions. They combine head orientation, hand orientation, and hand movement in a Bayesian network to predict which object a person intends to reach for. This improves prediction accuracy by 85% over the best single-cue baseline, with an average prediction time of 2.69 ms. A UR5 robot then uses the predicted target to adjust its task plan and avoid interfering with the person’s movement. Predicting a person’s intentions is not enough when collaboration also depends on what the person knows about the environment. The work [74] addresses this by tracking objects in a scene graph and estimating the person’s knowledge based on what they could observe. Their estimates agreed

6.2 Expectations: Legibility, Proximity, Norms, and Trust 

Preventing physical harm does not necessarily make people feel safe around humanoids. Rubagotti et al. [122] show that perceived safety also depends on the robot’s appearance, movements, and proximity to people. Akalin et al. [2] further find that perceived safety is closely related to comfort, sense of control, and trust. In contrast, recent control methods mainly focus on physical safety. GentleHumanoid [90] enables a Unitree G1 to adapt its upper-body compliance during hugging and sit-to-stand assistance, using adjustable force thresholds of 5-15 N. Busellato et al. [14] adjust a robotic arm’s safety margin based on uncertainty in predicted human hand movements, reducing safety-zone violations by about tenfold. However, neither study measures how safe people feel during interaction, leaving perceived safety unclear. Safe motion does not necessarily make a robot’s intentions clear. Dragan et al. [32] distinguish predictable motion, which follows the path people expect, from legible motion, which helps people recognize the robot’s goal early. In a collaborative task with HERB, legible motion reduced the time needed to infer the robot’s goal by 33%, but did not improve perceived safety [31]. These findings show that making robot movements easier to understand does not necessarily make people feel safer. Beyond making its intentions clear, a humanoid must also respect people’s expectations of personal space. Reviewing 39 studies, Nahum et al. [107] show that comfortable interaction distances depend on human movement and context. However, most studies were conducted in controlled laboratories, and none examined cultural differences. Lawrence et al. [79] find that violating social norms can reduce comfort, trust, and acceptance, while Saettone et al. [123] highlight how these expectations vary across cultures. To support socially aware navigation, Mu et al. [103] track human group movements and guide a Roban humanoid toward potential interaction partners while avoiding other groups. However, their experiments rely on motion capture and evaluate navigation performance rather than people’s comfort. Respecting social norms can help build trust, but trust should also reflect how reliably a robot performs. Mehrotra et al. [98] review methods for aligning human trust with system reliability and find that explanations and human-like appearance do not consistently improve this alignment. Measuring trust is also challenging. Roesler et al. [120] found that robot failures reduced reported

trust without corresponding changes in participants’ speech behavior. Campagna and Rehm [15] further show that trust assessment relies mainly on questionnaires, while sensor-based methods lack standardized approaches. Trust also works in the opposite direction. Vinanzi et al. [138] enable a simulated Sawyer robot to estimate a human partner’s reliability and take control when the partner repeatedly fails. However, how trust develops and changes when people interact with multiple humanoids remains largely unexplored. 

6.3 Responsibilities and Control: Teaming, Teleoperation, and Oversight 

Human-humanoid teaming requires deciding not only who performs each task, but also when robots should guide people or follow their preferences. Nikolaidis et al. [111] enable an assistive robot arm to estimate how willing a user is to change their goal. The robot guides adaptable users toward a better strategy while following the preferences of those unwilling to change. In an experiment with 51 participants, this approach improved task performance while maintaining high user trust. Lagomarsino et al. [78] review methods that adapt human-robot roles based on fatigue, cognitive load, and preferences. However, most approaches remain task-specific, with limited validation in real-world settings. Adapting roles also requires understanding how much responsibility people can manage. Wong and Seet [144] show that supervising more robots increases workload and can reduce situation awareness, while greater automation may encourage complacency. To support supervision, Ali et al. [3] estimate whether an operator has high or low situation awareness using eye tracking and task behavior, achieving 74% accuracy in a simulated two-robot task. Beyond individual workload, team relationships also matter. You and Robert [159] show that strong attachment to individual robots can divide human-robot teams into smaller groups, while a shared team identity may help prevent this division. These findings suggest that adapting human-robot responsibilities should also preserve team cohesion. Human control of humanoids ranges from direct teleoperation to high-level supervision. In recent teleoperation systems, people guide the robot’s movements while onboard policies maintain balance. H2O [52] captures human motion through an RGB camera and trains its policy on movements the humanoid can perform. OmniH2O [51] allows the same control policy to receive commands from VR, cameras, or GPT-4o through a common pose interface. HOMIE [9] takes a different approach, using foot pedals, an arm exoskeleton, and sensing gloves for more precise control. In experiments, it nearly halved task completion time compared with VR-based teleoperation. As humanoids become more autonomous, people must decide when to intervene rather than control every movement. Verhagen et al. [137] emphasize that meaningful human control requires operators to understand robot behavior and remain able to intervene. For multiple robots, Fleet-DAgger [57] directs limited human attention to robots that have failed, are uncertain, or face higher risks, while using interventions to improve a shared policy. However, it assumes supervisors can switch between robots immediately and does not consider network delays. How to ensure timely human intervention across networked humanoid fleets therefore remains unclear. 

6.4 Summary and Lessons Learned 

Few studies examine social interaction involving multiple humanoids. Research on intention inference, legible motion, and mutual adaptation mainly uses robotic arms or simulations [17, 31, 53, 74, 111]. Studies with physical humanoids instead focus on gestures, physical contact, navigation, and teleoperation [8, 9, 51, 90, 103, 124]. Most involve a single robot, with limited evidence from multi-robot settings [57, 110]. Three lessons follow. 

Social state stays local to the robot that estimated it. Intent, beliefs, and user corrections are estimated in one interaction and kept by one robot. When two robots track the same person, how they reconcile disagreeing estimates is left unspecified [110], and ARMAR-6 reuses stored

corrections without verifying them [8]. However, a worker expects consistent behavior from every humanoid, such as the same passing distance. Shared social state should carry its source, scope, uncertainty, and validity period, as coordination and body-centric state should, so that sharing does not spread errors along with knowledge. 

Human authority and attention must be managed at fleet scale. Current work follows a control structure suited to networked humanoids, where humans provide motion goals while on-board policies control balance [9, 51, 52]. However, supervising multiple robots is more challenging. As one operator oversees more robots, workload increases and situation awareness declines [144]. This can lead to missed interventions and a loss of meaningful human control [137]. Fleet-DAgger addresses this challenge by directing operator attention to robots with higher risks or uncer-tainty [57], but assumes identical robots and does not account for network latency. Changes in roles or control authority must also reach every affected robot and remain traceable to the person who approved them. 

7 Evaluation, Safety, and Open Problems in Networked Humanoid Intelligence 

This section examines how networked humanoid intelligence should be evaluated, what new safety risks arise when humanoids operate as a connected population, and which problems remain open. We first review the available evaluation resources and identify the gaps that make results difficult to compare across studies. We then distinguish risks that arise within an individual humanoid from those introduced or amplified by communication and interaction across the fleet. Finally, we use the limitations identified throughout this survey to outline directions for future research. 

7.1 Platforms, Simulators, Datasets, and Benchmarks 

Evaluation resources address individual components of networked humanoid intelligence, but none captures them together. Based on the requirements identified in the previous sections, we compare these resources along five dimensions: humanoid embodiment, multiple agents, network conditions, physical interaction between agents, and human presence in Table 6. Humanoid hardware is becoming increasingly accessible, ranging from low-cost research platforms for learning-based control [85] to commercial systems such as the Unitree H1 and G1 [85, 129]. However, physical experiments with multiple humanoids still rely largely on small platforms such as NAO and DARwIn-OP [26, 50, 97]. GPU-parallel simulators such as Isaac Gym and Isaac Lab [94, 100], together with frameworks such as Humanoid-Gym [47] and benchmarks such as HumanoidBench [128], simulate whole-body and contact dynamics; Isaac Lab additionally supports multi-agent environments and configurable actuation delays. However, these resources are typically used to train and evaluate a single humanoid, and none models communication between agents over a realistic network. Datasets such as Humanoid Everyday [170], which includes human–humanoid interaction tasks, human-motion and humanoid-retargeted motion corpora [80, 93], and large-scale manipulation datasets that are either cross-embodiment [114] or collected on a single platform across many scenes [71] support learning and transfer, but capture neither interaction among multiple robots nor network conditions. Multi-agent embodied benchmarks [18, 95, 163], surveyed in [36], provide team settings but rarely include humanoids and typically measure communication cost in tokens, model queries, or environment steps rather than network delay or traffic. Robot soccer remains a long-running testbed for multi-humanoid systems, both on physical robots [26] and in simulation [106]. Conversely, network testbeds such as O-RACES [139] connect simulated humanoids through real Open RAN infrastructure, but involve neither physical interaction among the robots nor human participants. None of the reviewed resources combines all five dimensions needed to evaluate networked humanoid intelligence as an integrated system.

7.2 What Current Evaluations Cannot Compare 

Current evaluations are difficult to compare because they differ along five fundamental dimensions. 

Embodiment: Few reviewed mechanisms are evaluated on physical or simulated humanoids, and none of the communication mechanisms is tested on a physical humanoid. Physical-humanoid evaluations are concentrated mainly in one-to-one social interaction and cooperative carrying by pairs of small robots. 

Cost units: Communication cost is reported in incompatible units, including bits, tokens, en-vironment steps, dialogue turns, and solver rounds. These measures do not reveal the actual network resources required. For example, the reported results cannot determine whether COMBO’s additional model calls impose a greater system cost than CoELA’s explicit messages [163, 164]. 

Networking: Coordination and collaboration studies typically assume reliable communication, whereas networking studies use synthetic traffic or traffic from isolated perception and control tasks. As a result, the effect of realistic network impairments on team behavior remains unclear. 

Physical outcomes: Network performance is rarely connected to physical consequences. Only two reviewed studies report humanoid falls under communication delay, both with simulated humanoids: [139, 172], and none measures interaction forces under network impairment. 

Human outcomes: Human interaction is evaluated mainly in one-human-one-robot settings using questionnaires and behavioral measures. Fleet-level outcomes, such as how trust changes across robots or how supervision workload scales with fleet size, remain largely unmeasured. These gaps suggest the need for consistent reporting rather than a single benchmark. Evalua-tions should state the embodiment level, report communication volume and timing requirements alongside task-specific cost measures, and characterize performance under varying delay, jitter, and packet loss. For physically coupled tasks, they should additionally report outcomes such as falls, balance-margin violations, and peak interaction forces. Fleet evaluations should show how both communication cost and task performance scale with team size, and how long exchanged information remains useful. When humans are involved, evaluations should also measure interven-tion frequency, operator situation awareness, and whether reported trust is consistent with the robot’s actual reliability. 

7.3 Safety: The Per-Robot Floor and the Risks the Network Adds 

Networking does not change the basic mechanisms that keep an individual humanoid safe, but it introduces risks that cannot be managed by one robot alone. We define the per-robot safety floor as the protection that remains available without network support, including onboard stabilization, 

ACM Comput. Surv., Vol. 1, No. 1, Article . Publication date: October 2026. 28 Nguyen et al. 

bounded interaction forces [90], control-barrier-function safety filters or policies trained with them [14, 156], and protective falling [99]. Every network-dependent function should fall back to this local protection when communication fails. Zhu et al. [172], for example, keep fast feedback onboard and reuse the most recent edge result when a new one arrives too late. Above this floor, networking introduces three additional risks. Staleness occurs when delayed in-formation no longer represents the current physical or team state. Delay and packet loss can degrade control stability [54, 157], reactive medium access can cause simulated humanoid falls [139], and a stumble can invalidate a robot’s previous task and motion commitments. Correlation occurs when robots depend on the same communication or coordination process. Synchronized transmissions, for example, can make shared information stale for several robots at the same time [169]. 

Propagation occurs when information shared across the fleet carries errors or malicious behavior from one robot to others. Poisoned updates can corrupt a shared model; FedREDefense [150] addresses this problem by testing whether an update can be reconstructed from distilled local knowledge, which helps distinguish genuine updates from crafted attacks [65]. In LLM-controlled teams, compromising one robot can also spread unsafe actions through inter-robot messages [59, 82], while self-interested agents can learn to send adversarial messages [171]. Similar propagation can occur in human interaction. Trust developed with one robot may extend to other members of the fleet, complicating trust calibration [160], while approvals made without sufficient understanding can weaken meaningful human control [137]. Networked humanoid safety therefore requires containment in addition to per-robot protection: information should be checked for freshness before entering control loops, every network-dependent function should have a local fallback, shared knowledge should be verified before fleet-wide deployment, and the origin of commitments, social estimates, and human approvals should remain traceable. 

7.4 Open Problems and Research Agenda 

The limitations identified throughout this survey point to six directions for future research. 

Control-aware networking. Communication is still evaluated mainly through network metrics, with limited reviewed studies relating network performance to humanoid falls. Yet the consequences of delay depend on the robot’s physical state; walking, for example, is more delay-sensitive than standing [172]. Future systems should therefore allow controllers to expose gait- and contact-dependent communication deadlines to network schedulers such as O-RACES [139]. Evaluation should then connect delay, jitter, and packet loss directly to physical outcomes such as falls and balance violations under realistic traffic. 

Compact body-centric messages. Networked humanoids need to exchange contact state, gait phase, footstep intent, and predicted whole-body occupancy, but compact representations for these quantities remain limited. One direction is the motion envelope, which could represent predicted body occupancy, planned footsteps, allowable motion deviation, and how long this information remains useful. For physically coupled robots, this representation could be complemented by compact contact and gait-phase messages. Such representations would allow a robot to determine whether a teammate’s plan remains physically compatible with its own motion without exchanging complete trajectories. 

Physically grounded coordination. Current allocation methods represent capability mainly through utilities, symbolic skills, or geometric constraints, while largely ignoring how balance, posture, and load affect whether a humanoid can safely execute an assignment. These constraints also change during execution. Propose-and-validate architectures [21, 87] provide a useful starting point: before accepting an assignment, each humanoid could query its local whole-body controller to verify that the task remains feasible under its current physical state.

Network- and embodiment-aware knowledge sharing. Fleet learning largely treats com-munication as an external assumption, while cross-humanoid transfer remains focused mainly on locomotion and existing pipelines are not evaluated under corrupted updates. Future systems should therefore treat knowledge sharing as a joint learning, embodiment, and networking problem. Initial steps include measuring update size and rollout latency in systems such as LWD [141], separating transferable model components from robot-specific adaptations [86], and deploying updates gradu-ally with mechanisms for detecting harmful updates, such as reconstruction-based filtering [150]. The goal is to improve heterogeneous humanoids across the fleet while keeping communication cost measurable and limiting how far an incorrect or malicious update can propagate. 

Fleet-level social consistency. Social information learned by one humanoid is rarely reconciled with estimates held by other robots, and changes in human roles or preferences are not systemati-cally propagated across the fleet. A shared social-state representation could address this gap by associating each estimate with its source, scope, uncertainty, and the period for which it remains useful. Such a representation would extend the task-state exchange of Nicolescu et al. [110] from task progress to human-related information. Evaluation should then measure whether different humanoids behave consistently toward the same person while still adapting to changes in the interaction context. 

Accountable oversight. Human attention becomes a limited resource as the number of super-vised robots increases. Approaches such as Fleet-DAgger [57] allocate this attention according to learning needs, but humanoid fleets must also consider the physical consequences of delayed intervention. Future systems should therefore allocate operator attention according to both task needs and physical risk, while coordinating human intervention with available network resources. Recording each approval together with the information available to the operator would further make decisions traceable and clarify when and why human authority was exercised. Across all four functions, these directions point to a common requirement: information freshness, embodiment, and human accountability must be considered jointly, rather than communication, coordination, collaboration, and social interaction being treated as independent problems. 

8 Conclusion 

Humanoid robots are moving from standalone systems toward connected fleets, where robots exchange information, coordinate actions, work together, and interact with humans. This survey reviewed networked humanoid intelligence through four key functions: communication and net-working, coordination, collaboration, and social intelligence and human interaction. We examined how these functions support fleet operation and how the physical characteristics of humanoids introduce new challenges. In particular, balance, whole-body motion, physical contact, and hard-ware differences place additional demands on information exchange and joint decision-making. Despite recent progress, most studies treat these functions in isolation and pay little attention to network conditions, differences between humanoids, or safety across the fleet. Future work should jointly consider communication, planning, physical control, and human oversight, so that humanoid fleets can work safely alongside people, adapt to changing environments, and improve by sharing knowledge and experience.
