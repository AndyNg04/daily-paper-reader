Many real-world decisions require teams to integrate information distributed across participants. Yet AI agents are still primarily trained and benchmarked in isolation. We introduce TeamGym , an environment for evaluating and training language model-based agents on team decision-making problems constructed from real-world legal, finance, medical, and software engineering corpora. TeamGym introduces a method to identify a minimal set of facts, each element of which is necessary and all of which are collectively sufficient for a correct final decision, which we call pivotal information . This enables TeamGym to both control the distribution of pivotal information and track how well agents identify and integrate pivotal information when making team decisions. Examining 8 language models on 12 source corpora, we find that current AI agents often fail to effectively share information: Distributing pivotal information across participants lowers success rate by 22.9 percentage points on average relative to giving the same information to a single model (a communication gap ). We also document that participants do not effectively retrieve decision-relevant information on their own: A team’s success rate is lowered by a further 19.9 points when participants must identify and extract pivotal information from source material rather than receiving it directly (a 

retrieval gap ). To help remedy these deficits, we use TeamGym to train policies through reinforcement learning on team decision loss. Across two policy-training experiments, we find that training improves final-decision quality and induces emergent coordination in participation, speaker selection, and communication, even though the reward evaluates only the final decision. The frontier of AI is turning to multi-agent systems, in which AI agents are organized into teams that communicate to solve problems (Hong et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Yet AI models are still trained and benchmarked largely as individual agents, which raises the question of whether they acquire the capacities and dispositions needed to be effective teammates. In human groups, collective intelligence, which is linked to social sensitivity and turn-taking, predicts team performance better than individual intelligence (Woolley et al., 2010). Although AI agents differ from humans, this finding motivates evaluating them as teams rather than only as individuals. A central challenge in team decision-making is that decision-relevant information is typically distributed across members. Reaching a collective decision requires members to identify and retrieve relevant information, communicate it efficiently, and integrate it into a final decision. Team theory has long studied this challenge (Marschak and Radner, 1972; Radner, 1962), while empirical research on human group decision-making finds that people often struggle to identify and integrate critical private information (Lu et al., 2012; Stasser and Titus, 1985). We introduce TeamGym , a gym for constructing team decision problem instances from source corpora, and for evaluating and training participant and coordinator policies under controlled information distributions and communication structures. TeamGym targets questions that existing benchmarks and multi-agent systems leave open. Single-agent benchmarks measure individual model capabilities (Liu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024), while research on multi-agent LLM systems focuses on designing harnesses that solve a given problem (Hong et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). Neither offers much evidence on whether participants can retrieve and communicate decision-relevant information, how team performance varies with information distribution and communication structure, or whether training can improve participant and coordinator policies. 

Method. TeamGym constructs controlled team decision problem instances from real-world source corpora. For each instance, it derives a decision objective from one or more corpus questions and either retains a corpus-provided reference decision or constructs one from corpus annotations and source materials. Source annotations and reasoning associated with the reference decision guide the selection of candidate evidence units, such as sentences, paragraphs, tables, or code blocks. Source-specific grounding and model-based complete-set and leave-one-out checks retain units that together support the reference decision but whose individual removal leaves it unsupported or unresolved. These retained units are pivotal information units (PIUs) and collectively form the instance’s pivotal information .Holding the problem instance and its pivotal information fixed, TeamGym can vary who initially has each PIU (the PIU distribution ), whether participants receive PIUs directly or source material containing them, the communication structure, and token budgets. The team collectively receives the pivotal information, but the evidence is distributed across participants to make communication necessary for a fully supported final decision. The communication structure specifies whether a coordinator controls communication, how speakers are selected, and which decision maker makes the final decision. Token budgets constrain what decision makers may observe and generate. To understand what reduces team performance, we define two success-rate gaps. The communication gap 

is the drop when the same PIUs are distributed across participants rather than given to one model. The 

retrieval gap is the further drop when the team must retrieve those PIUs from source material rather than receive them directly. Beyond evaluation, TeamGym supports training participant and coordinator policies under controlled PIU distributions, communication structures, and token budgets. Findings. The resulting comparisons hold the problem instance and pivotal information fixed to isolate communication and retrieval effects from differences in problem difficulty. (1) Across eight language models and 12 source corpora, the decision success rate is 22.9 percentage points lower when the same PIUs are distributed across a team than when one model receives them all, and a further 19.9 points lower when the same team must retrieve its PIUs from source material rather than receiving them directly. (2) Across domains, differences in the success rate among communication structures vary across PIU-distribution conditions. For example, across the six legal source corpora when participants receive PIUs directly, sequential communication in which each speaker selects the next participant leads simultaneous communication by 3.9 points when one PIU is initially common to the team, but trails it by 9.8 points when the team includes an additional participant without a PIU, corresponding to a 13.8-point change in the difference between their success rates. (3) Optimizing final-decision quality changes the communication process itself. With coordinator control, joint two-policy training produces a compact, exhaustive information-elicitation protocol; without a coordinator, training the shared participant policy improves end-to-end final-decision quality and completion and induces emergent coordination in participation, speaker selection, and communication. Tracing PIUs through these trajectories shows that increased participation makes more pivotal information available on FinDVer validation, but not on held-out FinLongDocQA. 

TeamGym therefore provides a controlled environment in which team decision-making capabilities can be evaluated and trained under different information distributions and communication structures. 

# 2. Related Work 

Team decision theory and organizational economics. Team decision theory studies cooperative decision makers with a team objective and a decentralized information structure. A team problem couples a shared objective with an information structure and one policy for each decision maker (Marschak and Radner, 1972; Radner, 1962; Yüksel and Ba¸ sar, 2013). Classical analysis largely studies the properties of different computation structures and policies, not how they can be learned and implemented computationally. Under tractable joint-policy optimization (A1) , computing this collection is assumed feasible before execution, although the exact problem can be intractable even 3in finite-horizon decentralized control (Bernstein et al., 2002). TeamGym instead evaluates and trains language-model policies, comparing policy collections before and after training under the same conditions. 

Mechanism design. Under the direct-report mechanism assumption (A2) , mechanism design represents private information as a type that can be elicited directly under the applicable equilibrium assumptions, and that participants know their type and can communicate it if they so choose (Maskin and Sjostrom, 2002; Myerson, 1979, 2008). In practice, however, attention and cognitive limitations constrain agent’s ability to identify, extract, and communicate information contained in long source material, even if it is in principle available to them (Mookherjee, 2006). TeamGym assumes a shared team payoff and studies bounded natural-language messages derived from source material. 

Communication constraints. Research in organizational economics, planning, and reinforcement learning examines communication costs and message limits (Cremer et al., 2007; Kim et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2011; Zhu et al., 2024). The idealization of nonbinding communication resources (A3) permits decision makers to access and transmit relevant information without binding observation or message constraints. TeamGym instead makes its resource budgets explicit. We defer additional related work on multistage communication, human-AI and group decision making, multi-agent LLM systems, benchmark contrasts, and the source corpora to Section 6. 

# 3. TeamGym Environment 

3.1. Decision Problem and Communication 

We formalize TeamGym as a finite-horizon team decision problem (Marschak and Radner, 1972; Nayyar et al., 2013; Radner, 1962; Yüksel and Ba¸ sar, 2013). A problem instance q fixes underlying source materials, a decision objective, a decision format, a set Dq of possible final decisions, and a problem-specific evaluator that assigns each d ∈ D q a team payoff Uq(d) ∈ [0, 1 ]. A decision maker is a role whose policy may generate a query, send a message, or make the final decision. A participant is a decision maker that may send messages during communication. A coordinator is a decision maker that may control communication by selecting or querying participants and may make the final decision. Initially, each participant receives an initial local observation, while all decision makers receive the same initial common observation containing the decision objective and decision format. The policy ρi maps the information currently available to decision maker i to a distribution over the queries, messages, or final decisions permitted for its role; ρ denotes the collection of decision-maker policies. The communication structure C specifies whether a coordinator controls communication, which participants may send messages and when, whether messages are sequential or simultaneous, and which decision maker makes the final decision. The resource budgets B , including token budgets, constrain what decision makers may observe and generate and how often participants may send messages. Queries and messages form the communication transcript ; a decision maker’s local transcript is the portion available to it under C and B. The initial local and common observations, together with the observation rules induced by C and the observation limits in B, define the decentralized information structure . An episode 

is one complete execution through the final-decision stage for fixed q, initial observations, C, B, and ρ.For these fixed inputs, the resulting interaction induces a distribution over final decisions, and we evaluate ρ by its expected team payoff, E[Uq(d)] . Holding q and the initial observations fixed while varying C, B, or ρ changes the interaction conditions without changing the problem instance. 4For evaluation, we consider five communication structures (Figure 2), each proceeding in scheduled steps called communication rounds : (1) Round robin , under which one participant sends a message per round in a fixed cyclic order; (2) Current-speaker selection , which starts with the first participant in that order, after which each speaker selects the next participant; (3) Simultaneous responses , under which all participants respond in one round; (4) Coordinator-selected speaker , under which the coordinator selects and queries one participant per round; and (5) Broadcast query , under which the coordinator sends the same query to all participants, whose responses are generated in the same round. In Round robin, Current-speaker selection, and Simultaneous responses, no coordinator controls communication, and a separate final decision maker that does not participate in communication reads the complete communication transcript and makes the final decision. In Coordinator-selected speaker and Broadcast query, the coordinator controls communication and makes the final decision. Evaluation. A problem family groups problem instances constructed from the same source corpus that require the same general kind of work and use the same evaluation procedure. For a problem family with a binary success criterion, episode success rate is the proportion of episodes whose final decisions succeed. Depending on the problem family, final decisions are compared with reference decisions or assessed by an LLM judge using problem-specific criteria. Software-engineering patches are checked by an official repository verifier.

4.3. Mechanisms Linking Communication Structure to Episode Success 

Communication structure may affect episode success rate by changing the information supplied to the final decision maker. We examine two mechanisms through which access to earlier participant messages can change this input: correct synthesis and error uptake . Here, correct synthesis refers to an auditor-labeled, reference-supported inference that combines information. The intended criterion requires at least two distinct PIUs, but the current labels also include some inferences combining one PIU with information from the publicly visible decision options (Appendix E.4.2). Error uptake occurs when a later participant uses an incorrect or unsupported claim from an earlier visible message; rejecting or correcting that claim does not count. We analyze Multi-Agent PIU-Only under One PIU per participant. In this setting, Round robin and Current-speaker selection achieve higher episode success rates than Simultaneous responses when the same Qwen3 model is used for the participants and the final decision maker. When only the final 9decision maker is replaced with GPT-5.5, however, the ordering reverses: Simultaneous responses achieves the highest rate . We use frozen participant trajectories to test how adding correct synthesis and removing error uptake affect episode success. A separate GPT-5.5 auditor labels sentence-level components. Participants are not rerun; we reconstruct the transcript input and regenerate only the final decision. Appendix E.4.1 reports the complete design and sample sizes. To isolate these mechanisms, we add correct synthesis while holding the PIUs supplied to the final decision maker fixed and excluding sentences that directly state a final choice or answer; separately, we remove only later sentences that use an earlier error while retaining the original error (Table 3).

4.4. PIU-Distribution and Communication-Structure Interaction 

We evaluate this interaction across the 11 problem families for which we observe multiple PIU-distribution conditions and communication structures, testing whether the difference in episode success rate between communication structures changes across PIU-distribution conditions. Deep-SWE is excluded because long-horizon repository execution and functional verification make this comparison computationally costly. Appendix F relates this comparison to team decision theory, communication complexity, and the hidden-profile paradigm in group decision-making. We estimate separate logistic models for Multi-Agent PIU-Only and Multi-Agent Full-Context. For episode e, let fe, me, qe, ℓe, and ce denote the problem family, model, problem instance, PIU-distribution condition, and communication structure. We specify 

ye ∼ Bernoulli (pe), logit (pe) = β0 + γfe + αme + πℓe + λce + ωℓe,ce ,where ye indicates episode success, pe is the probability of episode success, γfe and αme are problem-family and model fixed effects, πℓe and λce are PIU-distribution and communication-structure terms, and ωℓe,ce is their interaction. One PIU per participant and Coordinator-selected speaker are the reference categories. We weight each episode by the inverse number of PIU-distribution conditions available for its problem instance qe, so problem instances with more conditions do not receive greater aggregate weight, and cluster standard errors by problem instance. Under Coordinator-selected speaker, the selected participant receives a specific query. Its public description summarizes its subject matter or function without listing its assigned PIU categories, so the query may request only part of its evidence. Under Round robin, participants respond to the whole problem without a coordinator query. The existing public participant description remains unchanged; the labels disclose no private facts, values, or answers. Messages and final decisions are regenerated. We also replace the query with “It is your turn to contribute,” with and without the additions. 4.5. Token-Budget Effects 

Under Multi-Agent Full-Context with One PIU per participant and Coordinator-selected speaker, we vary private-note, message, and local-transcript token budgets, which respectively limit unshared notes, messages, and newly available transcript text. # 5. Policy Training with TeamGym : Emergent Coordination 

We demonstrate policy training with TeamGym under two communication structures: (1) Coordinator-selected speaker, where we train participant and coordinator policies and the coordinator selects and queries participants and makes the final decision; and (2) Current-speaker selection, where we train a shared participant policy and each current speaker either selects the next participant or ends communication. For this demonstration, the participant that ends communication makes the final decision, so no separate final decision maker is used. All policies are initialized from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) and trained using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024). In both demonstrations, the LLM-judged score assigned by GPT-5.5 serves as training reward. We call systematic changes in participation, speaker selection, and communication emergent coordination when these behaviors are not specified as separate rewards or training targets. 

5.1. Coordinator-selected speaker: Training Participant and Coordinator 

Under Coordinator-selected speaker, a shared participant policy generates messages from source material, while a coordinator policy generates queries and the final decision. We use Multi-Agent Full-Context on CUAD- and MAUD-derived problem instances (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b), converting each decision task to a free-form written decision format while preserving its PIU distribution and source material (Appendix G.1.1). Training and checkpoint selection use only CUAD-derived problem instances; the 26 MAUD-derived problem instances evaluate transfer. We compare (1) two-stage policy training, which updates the participant and coordinator policies successively to examine which behaviors change when each policy is updated separately, and (2) joint two-policy training, which allows them to co-adapt and serves as the primary end-to-end experiment. Appendices G.1.2 and G.2 provide evaluation, optimization, and checkpoint-selection details. 

5.1.1. Two-Stage Policy Training 

On the 36 CUAD-derived validation instances, the LLM-judged score increases from 12.14% to 16.84% after the participant-only stage and to 23.33% after the coordinator-only continuation. Illustrative examples show that the participant-only stage changes which PIUs appear in participant messages and, through the resulting transcript, can change queries generated by the fixed coordinator policy; the coordinator-only continuation can change queries and final decisions even when participant messages remain substantively unchanged. Two-stage policy training can therefore change participant messages, coordinator queries, and final decisions, but its CUAD-derived validation gains do not transfer to the 26 held-out MAUD-derived problem instances. Appendix G.2.2 provides training details and illustrative examples. 

5.1.2. Joint Two-Policy Training 

Joint two-policy training produces short coordinator queries and queries every participant, despite not directly rewarding either behavior. On the 36 CUAD-derived validation problem instances, joint two-policy training increases the LLM-judged score from 11.97% to 22.38% and the share of problem instances 13 in which the coordinator queries every participant from 80.6% to 100%. Because querying every participant does not enter the training reward, this increase is an instance of emergent coordination. Table 6 shows distinct coordinator-query behavior: joint two-policy training uses shorter queries, queries every participant, and never copies the problem question in the first query, whereas two-stage policy training uses longer queries and more later queries that reuse terms from preceding participant replies. # 6. Additional Related Work 

Mechanism design and communication. Multistage communication games, communication equilib-ria, and communication requirements study communication before a decision (Forges, 1986; Myerson, 1986; Segal, 2007). Cheap-talk models instead study communication with preference misalignment (Crawford and Sobel, 1982). 

Human-AI and group decision making. Human-AI decision making motivates evaluating AI systems as team participants and examining the interaction process rather than only the final decision (Herath Pathirannehelage et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2021; Seeber et al., 2020). Hidden-profile studies show that human teams can fail to reveal uniquely held information, and collective-intelligence research relates interaction structure to performance (Stasser and Titus, 1985; Woolley et al., 2010). Work on limited attention, working memory, and information overload motivates finite resource constraints (Cowan, 2001; Eppler and Mengis, 2004; Kahneman, 1973). These findings motivate our controlled comparisons, but TeamGym does not evaluate human-AI teams or treat its token budgets as models of human cognition. Work using LLMs to simulate social settings has a different objective: plausible social behavior is the object of study, whereas TeamGym treats the participant model as a controlled component (Hansen et al., 2026; Park et al., 2023, 2022). 

Multi-agent LLM systems and benchmark contrasts. CAMEL, AutoGen, MetaGPT, and ChatDev study collaboration through prompted agent interaction (Hong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023). PEER learns collaborative writing, MDAgents studies adaptive medical collaboration, and other work studies debate and collaborative post-training (Du et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025; Schick et al., 2023). CooperBench evaluates cooperative coding over repositories (Khatua et al., 2026). These systems study agent coordination, but TeamGym fixes source-grounded PIUs, controls the PIU distribution and whether participants receive PIUs directly or source material containing them, and measures whether bounded communication makes the PIUs available for a final decision. Multi-agent debate commonly gives agents the same problem; TeamGym gives participants different initial local observations. MultiAgentBench evaluates coordination under several communication topologies (Zhu et al., 2025). HiddenBench evaluates whether teams surface and combine directly presented hidden facts (Li et al., 2025, 2026), while SILO-BENCH studies role-free collaboration on synthetic distributed-computation problems (Zhang et al., 2026). Appendix B gives compact comparisons with both benchmarks. Collaborative Gym studies a user or user simulator collaborating with one LLM agent, while our unit of analysis is a team with decision-relevant evidence divided among participants (Shao et al., 2026). Single-agent suites such as SmartPlay and AgentBench do not require recovery of evidence held by teammates (Liu et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). 16 The original evaluations for the finance, medical, and software-engineering source corpora in Ta-ble 1 are single-agent or centralized. FinDVer and FinLongDocQA study financial verification and long-document financial question answering (Wang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2024); MLCR and EvidenceBench provide long medical-record cases and hypothesis-linked biomedical evidence (Wang et al., 2025; Wisedocs, 2026); and SWE Atlas Codebase QnA studies repository comprehension (Raghavendra et al., 2026). TeamGym uses the evaluated source corpora to construct team decision problem instances with controlled PIU distributions. 

# 7. Conclusion 

We introduce TeamGym , a gym for constructing team decision problem instances and for evaluating and training participant and coordinator policies under controlled PIU distributions, communication structures, and token budgets. Across 8 language models and 12 source corpora, distributing the same PIUs across participants reduces episode success rate by 22.9 percentage points relative to providing them to a single model, and requiring participants to retrieve those PIUs from source material reduces it by a further 19.9 points. The effectiveness of a communication structure also varies across PIU-distribution conditions. Team decision-making therefore depends not only on individual model capabilities, but also on how participants retrieve, communicate, and integrate distributed information. Across two fixed communication structures, policy training improves final-decision quality and induces emergent coordination in participation, speaker selection, and communication. With coordi-nator control, joint two-policy training produces a compact information-elicitation protocol; without a coordinator, training improves end-to-end final-decision quality and completion while increasing participation. Tracing PIUs through these trajectories shows that increased participation makes more pivotal information available on FinDVer validation, but not on held-out FinLongDocQA. This dis-tinction illustrates how TeamGym diagnoses how training changes team decision making, rather than reporting only whether performance improves. These training demonstrations use one run each, and the improvements under Coordinator-selected speaker are measured on CUAD-derived validation problem instances used for checkpoint selection. On MAUD-derived problem instances, the learned query and messaging patterns transfer more clearly than final-decision quality. Extensions of TeamGym could relax the shared-payoff assump-tion to study strategic information transmission, malicious or unreliable participants, and teams with partially aligned objectives. They could also introduce explicit retrieval costs and allow participants to choose which source material to retrieve. By separating retrieval, communication, and final decisions while holding the underlying problem fixed, TeamGym provides a controlled foundation for diagnosing and training team decision-making capabilities.

# B. Closest Benchmark Comparisons 

HiddenBench. HiddenBench is the closest benchmark to TeamGym because both study team decisions under decentralized information structures. HiddenBench provides broad coverage of Hidden Profile settings (Version 4 evaluates 65 problem instances and 15 models) and examines whether directly presented participant-specific facts enter public discussion and improve group voting (Li et al., 2025, 2026). TeamGym complements this breadth by treating the source-grounded information and interaction environment as controlled experimental variables: it varies PIU distribution, whether participants receive PIUs directly or source material containing them, communication structure, and token budgets while holding the underlying problem and evidence requirements fixed. Paired contrasts and PIU-distribution–communication-structure regressions quantify how performance changes across these conditions, while PIU-level process tracing localizes plausible stages at which required evidence is lost. TeamGym additionally supports a participant-only stage, a coordinator-only continuation, and joint two-policy training under Coordinator-selected speaker, together with training of a shared participant policy under Current-speaker selection, with the participant that ends communication making the final decision. Thus, HiddenBench provides a broader Hidden Profile evaluation, whereas TeamGym 

provides a more controlled, source-grounded environment for studying how PIU distribution and communication structure jointly relate to team performance. 

SILO-BENCH. SILO-BENCH studies role-free agents solving synthetic distributed-computation problems under direct-message, broadcast, and shared-file protocols, with programmatically known answers and weak-scaling evaluations from 2 to 100 agents (Zhang et al., 2026). This design is particularly useful for examining how emergent organization, consensus, and communication cost change as both team size and the global problem grow. TeamGym instead holds each real-world decision problem and its evidence requirements fixed while varying PIU distribution, whether participants receive PIUs directly or source material containing them, communication structure, and token bud-gets. Its communication gap compares Single-Agent All-PIU with Multi-Agent PIU-Only, while its retrieval gap compares Multi-Agent PIU-Only with Multi-Agent Full-Context. These outcome-based contrasts are distinct from SILO-BENCH’s log-based Communication-Reasoning Gap. TeamGym also supports a participant-only stage, a coordinator-only continuation, and joint two-policy training under Coordinator-selected speaker, together with training of a shared participant policy under Current-speaker selection, with the participant that ends communication making the final decision. Consequently, SILO-BENCH characterizes how emergent collaboration behaves under weak scal-ing, whereas TeamGym more directly examines how controlled PIU distributions and communication 25 structures relate to performance on fixed, source-grounded team decisions. 

# C. Communication-Structure Implementation and Relation to Prior Work 

Message generation and structure-specific implementation. At each communication round, the environment constructs the current observation permitted by the communication structure and resource budgets. The applicable participant or coordinator policy receives this observation and generates an action permitted for its role, such as a participant message, coordinator query and speaker selection, or final decision. The implementation uses role-specific prompt templates and structured output schemas, parses the returned action, and applies structure-specific fallback rules. Model and decoding settings are reported with the corresponding evaluation or training setup. Under Current-speaker selection, an omitted or invalid next-participant selection defaults to the next participant in the fixed cyclic order. 

Relation to prior work. The three structures without coordinator control are related to public deliberation and multi-agent debate (Du et al., 2024; Gerardi and Yariv, 2007), whereas the two coordinator-controlled structures are related to mediated communication and fusion-center models (Forges, 1986; Myerson, 1986; Varshney, 1997). Their speaker-selection rules also draw on mechanisms studied in conversation analysis and multi-agent systems (Kim et al., 2019; Sacks et al., 1974; Wu et al., 2023). The five structures are not intended as an exhaustive taxonomy of communication structures.

D.2. General Construction Procedure 

Reference decisions and reasoning. Each problem instance is constructed from one or more source questions; the decision objective may retain one question or combine related questions. When a reference decision and the reasoning used to produce it accompany the source questions, we retain them. Otherwise, we construct the reference decision from the available source annotations and underlying source materials. When the reasoning is unavailable, we generate reasoning conditioned on the reference decision; answer-conditioned rationale generation provides one example of this procedure (Wang et al., 2023a). 

Candidate-unit and removal checks. The fixed decision objective, reference decision, and reasoning used to produce it identify the information in the underlying source materials that supports the required answer components or reasoning steps. Dividing this information into candidate units is source-specific; a candidate unit may be a sentence, paragraph, or code block. The construction 27 procedure requires each retained candidate unit to be necessary for at least one required answer component or reasoning step. When the retained or generated reasoning does not establish whether this requirement holds, a model-based check using a model such as GPT-5.5 first requires the model to produce the reference decision. The procedure then removes one candidate unit at a time and asks whether uncertainty remains about the reference decision given the decision objective and remaining units. The removed unit is retained when uncertainty remains and excluded otherwise. This process continues until removing any retained unit leaves uncertainty about the reference decision; the retained units form Imin  

> q

. When necessity is established from the retained or generated reasoning, the released construction code also provides the model-based removal check as an additional check. 

PIU and source-material distribution. For Pairwise PIU overlap, we use a seeded random ordering to assign PIUs to the participant positions defined by the condition. Appendix D.3.8 reports an additional Multi-LexSum analysis that orders PIUs using embedding-based similarity while preserving the condition. After the PIU distribution is fixed, the underlying source materials are divided into units without dividing a PIU. Units that do not contain a PIU are distributed across participants using source-specific procedures that may account for their distance from a participant’s PIUs and balance the lengths of participants’ initial local observations. Afterward, we verify each participant’s initial local observation together with the initial common observation and, when necessary and consistent with the source, truncate the material. For example, legal source materials may be truncated, whereas source code is not truncated when doing so would prevent the program from functioning. 

Participant identifiers and participant descriptions. A participant description is a prompt-visible phrase describing a participant’s subject matter or function without disclosing the specific content, values, or conclusions of the PIUs assigned to that participant. Participant identifiers and participant descriptions disclosed to every decision maker form part of the initial common observation. Because participant descriptions indicate which participant is likely to hold relevant information, decision makers can use them to inform speaker selection. Participant descriptions do not determine the structural roles of participant, coordinator, or separate final decision maker defined in Section 3.1. The source-specific procedures below describe their construction and leakage checks.

D.5.4. MLCR Problem-Instance Construction Source corpus and retained problem instances. MLCR provides long medical-record cases with source questions, reference answers, and visit-summary source documents (Wisedocs, 2026). The 47 evaluated construction uses 29 problem instances. Because an MLCR case may contain many visit summaries, a single problem instance can draw source material from multiple source documents within the same case, but it never combines source material across cases. 

Decision objective, reference decision, and evaluation criteria. Construction first maps each eligible source question-answer pair to exact source atoms that materially reconstruct the answer. It then groups one or more source question-answer pairs from the same case into a clinically coherent free-response task. When selected source questions do not share one clinical or claims-review purpose, construction uses a concise multi-part review rather than inventing a causal relationship or common theme. The public task asks for the medical-record review and does not reveal source question identifiers, candidate identifiers, PIU identifiers, or case-specific expected values and conclusions. The hidden rubric records required claims, the selected evidence units that support each claim, and the criteria for clinical scope, dates, encounters, measurements, units, direction, negation, uncertainty, and causal strength. The reference decision must satisfy all required claims using only the selected source evidence. Generic medical knowledge may connect observations but cannot replace missing patient-specific evidence. 

Candidate-unit and PIU construction. MLCR source documents are divided into exact source atoms while preserving raw offsets and document identifiers. Each candidate unit is a contiguous span of atoms from one source document. Adjacent atoms are included only when needed to preserve patient, date, encounter, negation, measurement unit, uncertainty, or clinical scope. Non-adjacent evidence and repeated equivalents are kept separate rather than merged. A model-based grounding check verifies that every required claim and every material statement in the reference decision are grounded in the selected exact source evidence, and that each selected unit makes a unique necessary contribution. Boundary checks then test the complete selected set, a no-PIU condition, and leave-one-out conditions. A selected candidate becomes a PIU only after these checks show that the full set is sufficient and that removing the candidate breaks at least one claim it supports. 

Source material and PIU distribution. MLCR source material is distributed across participants under the PIU distribution. The participant assigned a PIU receives the canonical source span for that PIU. Additional source atoms from the same case are assigned to participants subject to the 40,000-token local-observation budget. Multi-Agent PIU-Only presents only the assigned PIU text. Multi-Agent Full-Context presents the participant’s medical-record source material with neutral source-file structure. Single-Agent All-PIU presents all pivotal information. All three conditions use the same public task, and hidden source mappings, candidate identifiers, PIU identifiers, rubric mappings, and reference answers are not shown to decision makers. 

MLCR-specific construction checks. For each participant and each PIU assigned to another partici-pant, a model-based packet-leakage check determines whether the participant’s complete full-context packet directly contains, duplicates, paraphrases, or permits reconstruction of that foreign PIU, includ-ing by combining multiple packet passages. Source material containing the participant’s assigned PIU is not removed. If noncanonical source material reconstructs a foreign PIU, removable contexts are deleted and the packet is checked again. If the participant’s own canonical source span itself reveals a foreign PIU, construction records this as canonical overlap and either merges ownership as allowed by the admissible allocation or rejects the allocation when strict one-each ownership is required. A problem instance or allocation is rejected if leakage remains without removable source material or the check does not converge. 48 D.5.5. EvidenceBench Problem-Instance Construction Source corpus and retained problem instances. EvidenceBench provides biomedical hypotheses, full papers represented as ordered sentence pools, sentence types, and sentence-level annotations of the minimum evidence required for each hypothesis (Wang et al., 2025). We use the test split. Forty-five problem instances pass the construction checks, and all 45 are used in the medical evaluation. We treat the sentences identified by these annotations as source locations used during construction rather than as PIUs. For every source record, construction records the instance and paper identifiers, original hypothesis, ordered sentence indices, sentence types, paper-sentence count, split, and source-file hash as hidden source annotations. Up to two neighboring sentences may be added only to restore an incomplete referent, comparison, abbreviation, or measurement context; the complete neighborhood is not automatically included in a candidate unit. 

Decision objective, reference decision, and evaluation criteria. Construction turns the source hypothesis and grounded evidence annotations into a biomedical question, a reference decision, and separately evaluated answer components. The decision objective omits the annotated sentence indices, PIU identities, candidate-unit identifiers, reference decision, and hidden construction information. The hypothesis guides the question but is not necessarily reused verbatim: the question may instead ask for the population, exposure or intervention, comparator, outcome, direction, evidentiary strength, or required limitation established by the paper evidence. The reference decision uses only selected exact evidence and preserves medical scope and the source’s causal or associational strength. Answer components and candidate units are distinct. An answer component may require several candidate units that make complementary contributions, and one candidate unit may support several answer components. The hidden evaluation criteria therefore record the links between answer components and candidate units. For some answer components, every linked candidate unit is required; for others, any one of several linked candidate units is sufficient. The recorded links state which part of an answer component each candidate unit supports. Construction also records, for every candidate unit, whether it is retained and, if it is excluded, the reason for exclusion. 

Candidate-unit construction and checks. Construction preserves a one-to-one relation between the sentences identified by the source annotations and candidate units. Each such sentence produces exactly one candidate unit, and distinct source sentences are not merged even when they jointly support the same answer component. The candidate unit contains the source sentence verbatim and may add only the minimal contiguous neighboring context needed to make it interpretable. It may not include another sentence identified by the source annotations as context. Construction checks complete one-to-one coverage of the source annotations, unique identifiers, contiguous and nonoverlapping sentence spans, exact agreement with the paper text, and absence of another sentence identified by the source annotations. Relations among candidate units are represented by their links to answer components rather than by merging their source locations. A separate model-based check receives the decision objective, reference decision, hidden evaluation criteria, and the complete candidate-unit set, including excluded candidate units. For each retained candidate unit, it checks relevance to the decision objective, direct support, and necessity under the recorded links to answer components; for each excluded candidate unit, it checks that exclusion is justified. It also checks that the decision objective is natural rather than artificially expanded to accommodate unrelated evidence, that every answer component and the reference decision are source-grounded, and that population, comparison, outcome, direction, uncertainty, and causal scope are preserved. A failed check permits bounded revision of the decision objective, answer components, 49 and links while the exact candidate units remain fixed. At least two annotated source sentences and two retained candidate units are required, but construction imposes no fixed upper bound. 

PIU construction and checks. Construction then applies the model-based removal check described in Section 3.2, using a model and a separate model judge. The model receives only the decision objective, the paper excerpts available in the current check, and neutral evidence numbers. It does not receive candidate-unit, PIU, annotated-sentence, answer-component, or omitted-unit identifiers. The model judge receives the hidden evaluation criteria and reference decision and separately records whether each answer component is correct and grounded in the material provided to the model. A correct guess is not treated as grounded. With all retained candidate units, every answer component must be correct and grounded, the final decision must be complete, and it must contain no critical error. For each removal required by Section 3.2, at least one answer component linked to the removed candidate unit must cease to be satisfied. The recorded links are also checked against the source references in the model’s response: every linked unit is required when the answer component requires all of them, whereas at least one linked unit is required when any one is sufficient. After every check passes, each retained candidate unit forms one PIU; candidate units that jointly support one answer component remain separate PIUs. 

Source material and PIU distribution. The retained construction uses One PIU per participant. Each source sentence containing a PIU is reserved for the participant assigned that PIU. Overlap between the source sentences containing distinct PIUs is rejected. Every remaining sentence from the EvidenceBench paper sentence pool is then assigned to exactly one participant, with seeded assignment balancing the estimated lengths of the initial local observations; the resulting division is exhaustive and disjoint. Multi-Agent PIU-Only presents only the exact text of the assigned PIU. Multi-Agent Full-Context presents the participant’s paper sentences with neutral source identifiers. Single-Agent All-PIU presents all pivotal information. All three conditions use the same decision objective, and hidden candidate-unit identifiers, PIU identifiers, annotated source locations, links to answer components, reference source locations, and source-material check information are removed from the decision makers’ observations. 

EvidenceBench-specific construction checks. For each participant and each PIU assigned to another participant, a model-based check considers the participant’s complete initial local observation rather than isolated lexical matches. The initial local observation contains a PIU assigned to another partici-pant only when an exact duplicate, clear paraphrase, equivalent reported result, or combination of source passages supports that PIU’s complete contribution at the same medical scope. Shared topics, partial overlap in population or outcome, a different measurement, a general mechanistic association, or a plausible inference requiring external knowledge do not suffice. When such reconstruction is possible, the removable additional source passages that support the reconstruction are deleted, and every participant and PIU assigned to another participant are checked again. Source material containing the participant’s assigned PIU is never deleted. If that material alone supports a PIU assigned to another participant, the problem instance is rejected because a candidate unit is incorrectly defined. The procedure permits at most three revisions and rejects the problem instance if the initial local observation still contains another participant’s PIU, no removable source material exists, final-decision validation fails, or the checks do not converge. 50 D.6. D.6.4. DeepSWE Problem-Instance Construction 

DeepSWE provides long-horizon repository-level code-repair tasks, each defined by a pinned base repository, a public task instruction, and an official hidden verifier. A task may admit multiple verifier-passing patches, and repeated attempts by the same model may follow different search and implementation paths. The original DeepSWE evaluation accounts for this variation through repeated rollouts: its analysis uses three independent attempts by the same model on each task, while its final evaluation uses approximately four rollouts per task and model configuration. Successful GPT-5.5 repair trajectories take roughly 80 agent steps on average (Huang et al., 2026). Our goal is not to reproduce this unrestricted long-horizon search, but to control the evidence required for collaboration under a fixed execution budget. We therefore define a DeepSWE PIU as a unit of functional evidence needed to support a successful repair under a fixed, bounded protocol. The complete PIU set is constructed so that, together with the public task instruction, it supports a verifier-passing patch using a restricted action space and five atomic edit turns. 

Instruction-only screening. We begin with 40 DeepSWE tasks. For each task, the model receives the public task instruction but no PIUs or additional code evidence. We give the model the same five-turn repair budget used in the subsequent evidence-bundle checks and run three independent attempts. 53 We continue PIU construction only for tasks that fail all three instruction-only attempts. This screen removes tasks whose instructions already provide enough information to locate and implement a repair within the bounded protocol. The instruction-only runs therefore serve as the no-evidence, or “none,” condition for construction. 

Constructor-only materials and evidence collection. For each candidate task, the constructor may inspect the reference patch, hidden-test patch and identifiers, public-check definitions and results, and the pinned base repository to locate implementation boundaries and audit evidence coverage. These materials are construction-only: the reference patch, hidden tests, verifier internals, hidden expected values, and constructor rationales are never copied into solver-visible evidence or exposed to evaluated agents. The public task instruction is common input to every condition and is not itself a PIU. Construction collects exact task-relevant fragments from the unmodified base commit and de-terministic runtime observations produced from the same checkout. Each observation records the executed probe, its output and exit status, and the repository state needed to interpret it. Observations may describe public base-repository behavior but do not reveal hidden assertions, hidden expected values, or solution code. Solver-visible evidence is therefore composed of base-commit code and reproducible base-repository behavior, not pieces of the reference patch. 

Functional PIU construction. A DeepSWE task may admit multiple implementations that pass the official verifier. Searching for a minimal evidence set relative to one reference patch could therefore steer the agent toward only one implementation path despite the existence of other valid repairs. We instead define each DeepSWE PIU to represent one coherent functional behavior or contract required by the verifier, rather than a fragment of a particular reference implementation. A functional PIU may combine several exact code fragments with deterministic runtime observa-tions when they jointly specify an implementation boundary, such as parsing and validation, state accumulation, lifecycle and cleanup, persistence, public-API wiring, or error propagation. Supporting types, callers, interfaces, and exports are included when they are required to implement that behavior. Each selected code fragment or observation is assigned to exactly one PIU. Functional labels, evidence-selection rationales, and hidden failure information remain constructor metadata and are not included in participant packets. The purpose of these PIUs is not to reproduce the reference patch. Each PIU is grounded in base-repository code and reproducible runtime behavior, and the complete PIU set must support a verifier-passing repair under the bounded protocol. 

Bounded evidence-bundle checks. We inspect candidate PIU bundles with the same source-restricted repair interface used in evaluation. The patch author has five atomic edit turns and may invoke checks from a frozen public-check catalog. Repository mutation is restricted to replace , write ,and delete operations, and the agent may terminate before using all five turns. After termination, the official hidden verifier is run once. Success is determined solely by whether the final patch passes this verifier; matching the reference patch is not required. Each complete PIU bundle is evaluated in three independent qualification runs. These runs form the complete-evidence, or “all,” condition for construction. When all three instruction-only runs fail but the complete bundle enables a successful repair within the same five-turn protocol, the bundle provides operational evidence of sufficiency under that bounded protocol. Repeated qualification outcomes and targeted leave-one-PIU-out checks are used diagnostically to refine the evidence bundle. We inspect whether removing a PIU reduces repair success or removes 54 a functional boundary needed for the repair, and use the result to identify missing, irrelevant, or redundant evidence. Because repair trajectories are stochastic and multiple patches may pass the verifier, these leave-one-out checks are not treated as a formal proof of necessity over every possible repair procedure. They diagnose the contribution of each functional PIU under the fixed five-turn protocol. 

Final evaluation cohort. After screening, evidence-bundle construction, and qualification, we freeze a 12-task evaluation cohort from the initial 40 tasks. The cohort contains four Go, four Python, two JavaScript, and two TypeScript tasks and preserves both programming-language coverage and variation in original benchmark difficulty. These 12 tasks are the constructed and evaluated DeepSWE problem instances reported in Table 2.

PIU allocation and evaluation conditions. The final functional PIUs are assigned one per participant. In Single-Agent All-PIU, one patch author receives the ordered union of all PIU evidence. In Multi-Agent PIU-Only, each participant receives only its assigned functional PIU. In Multi-Agent Full-Context, each participant receives its assigned PIU together with non-PIU base-repository code and additional runtime context. Non-PIU code fragments in the Full-Context packets are distributed exclusively across partici-pants. Additional runtime context uses only previously collected observations; selected non-owned observations may be duplicated as distractors across participant packets. No participant receives the entire repository. The collaborative transcript is sequential and coordinator-selected, and the final patch author acts on the public transcript using the same five-turn repair interface and public-check catalog. No condition exposes PIU identifiers, the reference patch, hidden-test information, hidden expected values, or constructor rationales. 

Repeated-run aggregation and failure handling. To account for stochastic repair trajectories, we schedule three independent runs for each task–model–condition combination. Within each task and condition, we first average the available verifier outcomes across repetitions and then average across the 12 tasks, giving each task equal weight. A completed verifier failure, an ordinary unsuccessful repair, or a confirmed context-window failure receives a score of zero and remains in the denominator. A run is marked unavailable and excluded only when no valid task outcome can be obtained because of provider-capacity, API or serving, container or Docker, or missing-verifier-result failures. Availability exclusions are recorded separately from completed task failures.

E.4. Synthesis and Error-Uptake Analysis 

This appendix reports the labeling criteria and transcript interventions for correct synthesis and error uptake summarized in Section 4.3. 

E.4.1. Definitions and Design 

A PIU is faithfully communicated when a participant message preserves its meaning without changing its value, entity, scope, qualification, or effect. An error is an incorrect or unsupported sentence or component that could affect the final decision. Error uptake occurs when a later participant uses an error from a previous visible message to produce a new sentence; rejecting or correcting the earlier error does not count. The intended synthesis criterion is an inference that combines at least two distinct PIUs; repeating one PIU or merely proposing an answer does not count. A synthesis inference is 

correct when it is supported by the reference PIUs and reference decision. An explicit answer proposal 

directly states the final choice or requested answer. The analysis uses 474 frozen Multi-Agent PIU-Only trajectories: 79 problem instances crossed with Round robin, Current-speaker selection, and Simultaneous responses, for Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-235B-A22B participants; Section 4.3 abbreviates the latter as Qwen3-235B. Every participant receives exactly one PIU. A separate GPT-5.5 auditor labels sentence-level components. Participants are not rerun. All effects below concern episode success unless stated otherwise; percentage-point estimates are calculated before rounding the displayed rates. 65 E.7.1. Finance: Design and Query Interventions 

The matched communication comparison uses 59 finance problem instances: 30 FinDVer and 29 FinLongDocQA instances. The primary comparison pools Qwen3-32B and Qwen3-235B-A22B, with the same model used for the participants and the coordinator or separate final decision maker within an episode. The design crosses One PIU per participant and All-but-one PIU concentration with Coordinator-selected speaker and Round robin, giving 472 primary episodes and 118 problem-instance–model pairs per condition. Participants receive private source material. The interaction compares the difference in episode success between Round robin and Coordinator-selected speaker under All-but-one PIU concentration with the same difference under One PIU per participant. 

Participant descriptions. The original public participant descriptions summarize a participant’s subject matter or function, without enumerating the public information categories corresponding to its assigned PIUs. These categories describe what the evidence concerns; they do not disclose the evidence itself. Across the 59 problem instances, their mean lengths are 4.41 words under One PIU per participant and 5.10 words under All-but-one PIU concentration. The 54 participants assigned multiple PIUs have six-word descriptions but a mean of 3.43 public information categories (range 2–6). A brief description thus need not indicate every category of evidence available from a participant assigned multiple PIUs. 

Experimental logic. The observed communication difference permits two explanations: the coor-dinator may fail to select a participant assigned a needed PIU, or its query may limit the evidence that the selected participant reports. Near-complete coverage of required participants motivates the second account. Under Coordinator-selected speaker, the selected participant receives a specific query from the coordinator; under Round robin, there is no coordinator query and the participant responds to the whole decision problem. A query covering one PIU may suffice when the participant holds only that PIU, but may omit other assigned PIUs when several are concentrated with it. If this account is correct, broadening the requested evidence should improve episode success more when one selected participant holds multiple PIUs. We therefore change query content while retaining the source episode’s speaker selection. 

Fixed speaker selection. All query interventions operate within Coordinator-selected speaker. The original coordinator query is the task-specific question recorded in the source episode. For each source episode, we replay the recorded sequence of selected participants and the number of responses. The source material, model, PIU distribution, resource budgets, and final-decision procedure are held fixed across query conditions; participant responses and final decisions are regenerated. Original coordinator queries are not regenerated in response to the new participant messages. The resulting effects apply to this fixed sequence, rather than to a coordinator that adapts its later selections or queries. 

Public information categories in the query. After speaker selection, one intervention appends a numbered list of the public PIU category labels corresponding to the selected participant’s assigned PIUs and an instruction to report the supporting facts or numbers for each category. The added list contains short public PIU category labels, without private source text, detailed evidence, values, answers, entity scope, or period scope. It augments the original query; it does not replace the participant description or alter speaker selection. For example, an original description is “service proceeds and cybersecurity operations reviewer.” The added list includes service agreement proceeds details, cybersecurity operations exposure, liquidity going-concern assessment, cryptocurrency market 66 exposure, earnings profitability outlook, and share-based compensation expense. These labels specify the topics of the assigned evidence, without supplying the proceeds, expenses, or other private facts needed to answer the problem. Categories need not correspond one-to-one to PIUs: several PIUs may concern the same category. The added block averages 50.36 words for participants assigned one PIU and 61.00 words for participants assigned multiple PIUs. It is visible to the selected participant and remains in the public transcript. The treatment jointly changes the available category labels and the explicit elicitation instruction; their separate effects are not identified. The primary comparison with and without these additions contains 59 instances × 2 Qwen3 models × 2 PIU distributions × 2 query conditions × 3 repetitions, or 1,416 episodes. This analysis plan was frozen after an earlier generic-instruction experiment, rather than preregistered before all related evidence was observed. 

Removing the task-specific query. To test whether the additions are necessary or removing the narrow query suffices, we replace each original coordinator query with “It is your turn to contribute,” both with and without the public PIU category labels and instruction. This produces 1,416 additional primary episodes. Together with the 1,416 episodes retaining the original queries, the 2 × 2 design crosses query retention versus removal with presence versus absence of the additions under each PIU distribution, giving 2,832 episodes. The original-query comparators are reused from the preceding experiment; they are not generated again for the query-removal comparison. 

Communication-structure interaction with Round robin held fixed. For each of the four coordinator query conditions, we retain the 236 original Qwen3 Round robin episodes and replace only the Coordinator-selected speaker outcomes with the corresponding repetition-averaged replay outcomes. The intervention operates under both PIU distributions; One PIU per participant controls for a general benefit from changing the query. Let GR be the difference in episode success between All-but-one PIU concentration and One PIU per participant under Round robin, and let GC (k) be that difference under Coordinator-selected speaker with query condition k. In percentage points, the communication-structure interaction is 

I(k) = GR − GC (k).The fixed GR is −12.7 points. Consequently, a selective improvement in the concentrated coordinator condition reduces I(k) by the same amount. This calculation uses the same primary Qwen3 population and adds no episodes. It distinguishes the original single-generation comparison from the three-repetition coordinator replay; their baseline estimates need not be identical. The original Round robin and replay coordinator outcomes are resampled together for inference on each interaction and its change. 

Budgets, outcomes, and inference. Follow-up generation uses temperature 0.2, a 40,000-token private-context budget per participant, 600-token participant and coordinator utterance caps, and a 1,200-token final rationale budget. Complete queries including the public PIU category labels and instruction satisfy the existing 600-token cap. A temperature-0 GPT-5.5 evaluator scores episode success and a continuous score on a 0–1 scale. A separate temperature-0 GPT-5.5 semantic evaluation compares each PIU’s reference evidence with its assigned participant’s public messages. Substantive communication requires accurate content-bearing evidence; complete communication additionally requires all key task-relevant elements. Repeating a category label alone does not count. For both follow-up experiments, three repetitions are averaged within each problem-instance– model–PIU-distribution–query-condition combination. All primary confidence intervals use 20,000 67 percentile bootstrap draws over the 59 problem-instance clusters, retaining both models and all matched conditions together. For the fixed-Round-robin analysis, each draw recomputes I(k) and the difference between query conditions. The original communication comparison uses the same cluster-bootstrap procedure. No instance or repetition is excluded based on its observed outcome.

E.7.3. SARA: Source-Material and Transcript Interventions 

Three experiments use the same 45 SARA problem instances and Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-235B-A22B, and GPT-5.5. The longest instance, sara_subproof_tax_case_90_taxable_income , was designated for development checks and excluded from all primary analyses, leaving 44 problem instances. Each configuration is repeated three times. Within an episode, participants and the coordinator or separate final decision maker use the same model. All actors see public participant descriptions generated from public information categories that identify the subject matter without revealing private facts or answers. The descriptions contain 3–6 words (mean 3.72). Their availability is held fixed; the experiments do not estimate their causal effect or whether every actor uses them correctly. Under One PIU per participant, every participant is assigned one PIU. Under All-but-one PIU concentration, two participants receive all PIUs; the others receive none. The participant assigned all but one receives 5–11 PIUs (mean 7.73), and 4–10 participants per instance receive none (mean 6.71). Participant messages have a 600-token cap and private contexts have a 24,000-token cap per participant. Actor temperature is 0.2; episode-success and semantic evaluation use GPT-5.5 at temperature 0. Speaker order and initial speaker are fixed within problem instance, PIU distribution, and repetition, and reused across models and evidence conditions. The participant assigned all but one PIU speaks first in only 16 of 132 primary concentrated-allocation instance–repetition combinations. 

Exact PIUs with or without source material. Source material may impair the processing of partici-pants assigned PIUs, or generate additional messages from participants assigned no PIU that impair the final decision. To examine these pathways, we compare Multi-Agent PIU-Only with a condition that adds the original private source material. In both conditions, participants receive their assigned PIUs explicitly as facts relevant to the decision; the added-source condition also supplies source material to participants assigned no PIU. This tests whether source material changes the interaction when the relevant facts are already supplied directly. In Multi-Agent Full-Context, participants instead receive source material from which they must retrieve their assigned PIUs. Explicit availability does not ensure that participants communicate or use the facts correctly. The comparison crosses two PIU distributions, all five communication structures, and these two evidence conditions. The complete design contains 8,100 episodes (45 instances × 3 models × 2 evidence conditions × 2 PIU distributions 

× 5 structures × 3 repetitions), with 7,920 primary episodes over 44 instances. For communication structure c and evidence condition v, define 

A(v, c) = 100 {Success (All-but-one, v, c) − Success (One PIU per participant, v, c)}.The source-material interaction is A(PIUs and source material, c) − A(Multi-Agent PIU-Only, c). A 71 negative value means that source material makes PIU concentration more harmful relative to One PIU per participant under that structure. The communication-structure interaction compares these allocation effects: 

I(v) = A(v, Simultaneous responses ) − A(v, Coordinator-selected speaker ).The change reported in Section 4.4.2 is I(PIUs and source material ) − I(Multi-Agent PIU-Only ). It therefore compares the source-material interactions between the two communication structures, rather than reporting the interaction within Simultaneous responses alone. 

Who receives source material. The preceding comparison changes source-material access for both groups together. It therefore cannot distinguish a loss originating with participants assigned PIUs from one originating with participants assigned no PIU. The source-material intervention independently varies source-material access for the two participants assigned PIUs and for the participants assigned no PIU under All-but-one PIU concentration. Exact PIUs, public descriptions, models, repetitions, and speaker order remain fixed. We compare no added source material, source material for participants assigned PIUs only, source material for participants assigned no PIU only, and source material for both groups. The experiment covers Coordinator-selected speaker, Broadcast query, and Simultaneous responses, generating 2,430 new episodes, of which 2,376 concern the 44 primary instances. The no-source and both-group conditions reuse the preceding comparison. Under One PIU per participant, there are no participants assigned no PIU, so the corresponding source-material conditions are identical rather than independent additional observations. 

Which messages reach the final decision maker. Source-material placement changes a participant’s local processing and public messages together. Even if source material for participants assigned no PIU causes the larger loss, this does not establish whether their messages impair final synthe-sis. If the loss is transmitted through those messages, hiding them only at the final-decision stage should improve episode success when their authors receive source material, with little improvement when they do not. The transcript intervention tests this prediction using frozen All-but-one PIU concentration–Simultaneous responses trajectories from the condition with no added source material and the condition with source material only for participants assigned no PIU. For each trajectory, the final decision maker receives either the complete transcript or only messages from the two participants assigned PIUs. Participant messages, descriptions, PIU assignment, and speaker order remain fixed; only the final decision is regenerated. Both visibility conditions use the same replay implementation, rather than comparing newly generated decisions with archived ones. A separate complete-transcript replay checks stability against archived outcomes. This design contains 1,620 episodes (45 instances × 3 models × 2 source-material conditions ×

2 message-visibility conditions × 3 repetitions), with 1,584 primary episodes over 44 instances. # F. Literature Motivation for the PIU-Distribution–Communication-Structure Interaction 

Section 4.4 tests whether differences in episode success rate between communication structures change across PIU distributions. Three complementary literatures motivate this comparison and the interaction specification. 

Team decision-making under a decentralized information structure. Team decision theory studies decision makers who pursue a shared objective but have different information available to them (Marschak and Radner, 1972; Nayyar et al., 2013; Radner, 1962). This is the basic structure of our evaluation conditions with multiple participants: the participants share the episode objective, while PIU distribution determines which PIUs are available to which participants. Consistent with this formulation, we hold the problem instance and model fixed while varying PIU distribution. The literature does not imply that a particular PIU distribution must perform best, because performance also depends on whether the distributed PIUs enter communication and support a correct final decision. 

Communication under a decentralized information structure. Communication complexity and distributed-function computation study how separately informed parties exchange the information needed to compute a shared target (Orlitsky and Roche, 2001; Yao, 1979). Work on knowledge in distributed systems likewise formalizes communication as changing the information available to decision makers (Halpern and Moses, 1990). In our setting, communication structure determines who may send a message at each point in the episode and which earlier messages that participant can observe, while PIU distribution determines where the relevant information is initially available. We study PIU distribution and communication structure jointly because differences in episode success rate among communication structures may vary across PIU distributions. 

PIUs entering communication. Hidden-profile experiments show that information observed by only one participant may fail to enter group discussion (Stasser and Titus, 1985). This evidence is directly relevant to PIU distributions that place a PIU with only one participant or concentrate multiple PIUs with one participant. It also explains why the proportion of participants who send a message is not by itself sufficient: episode success depends on whether the communication structure elicits and preserves the PIUs needed for a correct final decision. 81 Implications for the empirical specification. The specification includes PIU-distribution and communication-structure main effects and their interaction, allowing differences among communication structures to vary across PIU distributions. Because both are represented as categorical conditions and every non-reference pair receives an interaction term, the specification does not impose that their relationships with episode success are independent. The Bernoulli model and logit link reflect that episode success is binary, while problem-family and model fixed effects adjust for differences across problem families and models. Standard errors are clustered by problem instance, and Appendix E.6 reports the stricter specification with problem-instance fixed effects. The analysis estimates empirical relationships among the evaluated conditions; these literatures motivate the comparison but do not make the interaction estimates causal tests of any one theory.

G.2.1. Optimization and Training Configurations 

Each update samples eight shuffled CUAD-derived training problem instances and eight rollouts per problem instance, yielding 64 episode rollouts. GRPO standardizes the eight rewards within each problem instance and broadcasts the resulting episode advantage to the trainable actions. An invalid trainable action instead receives a local advantage of −0.1 and one retry. We update all parameters of each trainable policy with AdamW for one epoch per update using a PPO-style clipped surrogate, ratio 83 clip 0.2, dual-clip bound 3.0 for negative advantages, sequence-mean/token-mean loss aggregation, learning rate 10 −6, weight decay 0.01, and a 10 −3 KL loss relative to the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct policy. Training runs for 80 updates, validation occurs every 10 updates with greedy decoding, and each configuration uses four H200 GPUs.

G.2.3. Joint Two-Policy Training 

Joint two-policy training removes weight sharing between the participant and coordinator policies. Both policies are initialized independently from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, maintain separate optimizer states and clipped-surrogate losses, and receive the same team payoff. Every update has a nonzero 85 gradient norm for each policy, ruling out the failure mode in which one shared policy generates or updates both participant and coordinator actions. The reported run uses random seed 29, 80 updates, and 5,120 episode rollouts. The update-80 pair attains the highest mean LLM-judged score among the scheduled CUAD-derived validation evaluations. The score increases from 11.97% to 22.38%. The coordinator queries every participant on 100% of validation problem instances by update 20 and maintains that rate through update 80. The coordinator-query diagnostics use one greedy rollout per CUAD-derived validation problem instance. A first query copies the problem question only when the query and question match after normalization for case, punctuation, and whitespace. A later query is lexically reply-conditioned when it contains an explicit follow-up marker or at least two terms from a preceding participant message that are absent from the problem question. This diagnostic does not constitute a causal test of adaptation. The trained coordinator queries every participant, uses short queries, and does not copy the problem question in the first query; lexically reply-conditioned later queries remain infrequent. Thus, the learned coordinator-query behavior is distinguished by querying every participant with short queries, rather than by a high rate of lexically reply-conditioned later queries. In one CUAD-derived validation example, the base participant and coordinator policies elicit only the parties and the Type I material label and produce a one-directional final decision with an LLM-judged score of 12.5%. The jointly trained policies instead elicit the IBM-to-Customer Type II permissions from one participant and the Customer-to-IBM downstream-authorization right for Type I materials from another. Their final decision represents both grant directions and receives 62.5%, although it remains incomplete. This example illustrates coordinated evidence decomposition but does not establish that the interaction between the two trained policies is necessary. Under the jointly trained participant and coordinator policies, the coordinator queries every participant and every participant sends a public message on all 26 MAUD-derived problem instances; both behaviors occur on 20 of 26 instances under the base participant and coordinator policies. The LLM-judged score changes only slightly, from 5.12% to 5.58%. The query and messaging patterns therefore transfer more clearly than task-specific evidence use or final-decision quality. 

G.3. Current-speaker selection: Shared Participant Policy Training 

G.3.1. Problem Instances and Communication Structure 

This experiment uses Multi-Agent PIU-Only with One PIU per participant under Current-speaker selection. The participant that ends communication makes the final decision; there is no separate final decision maker. Training contains 350 FinDVer problem instances with two to eight participants and 188 FinLongDocQA problem instances with two or three participants. Validation contains 39 FinDVer and 21 FinLongDocQA problem instances. The held-out test set contains 40 four-participant FinLongDocQA problem instances. Canonical task identifiers and source-material groups do not over-lap across splits. Four-participant FinLongDocQA is absent from training, although four-participant FinDVer occurs in training. Each participant receives exactly one PIU in its initial local observation. The initial common observation contains the decision objective, decision format, and a directory of participant identifiers and public participant descriptions. The descriptions are short role names generated from the broad subject of the assigned PIU. Deterministic checks and a separate leakage check reject names that expose a value, polarity, condition, conclusion, or decision implication. A prompt-construction check of all 638 normalized instances and 4,988 rendered prompts confirms that every directory contains the permitted participants and that no participant receives another participant’s PIU. 86 In each round, the current speaker sends a public message and either selects the next participant or ends communication and makes the final decision. If the 15-round communication budget is exhausted, the last speaker makes the final decision. Public messages have a 600-token budget, the final-decision field has a 1,200-token budget, participant actions have 1,200 private reasoning tokens, and the final decision has 1,800 private reasoning tokens. The runtime also applies a 750-token structured-generation cap, a 16,384-token prompt cap, and a 24,576-token training-sequence cap. 

G.3.2. Optimization and Checkpoint Selection 

A single Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct participant policy is shared across participants. Participant mes-sages, next-participant selection, decisions to end communication, and the final decision all receive participant-policy gradients. Each update samples eight problem instances and 16 rollouts per instance, yielding 128 rollouts per update and 10,240 rollouts across 80 updates. Rollouts use temperature 0.7 and top-p = 0.95; validation uses greedy decoding. Training updates all parameters of the shared participant policy using FSDP in bfloat16 on four H200 GPUs. GRPO standardizes the 16 rewards within each problem instance. Invalid JSON, schema-invalid actions, and invalid next-participant identifiers receive a local advantage of −0.1 and one retry. Optimization uses AdamW with learning rate 10 −6, weight decay 0.01, gradient-norm clip 1.0, PPO ratio clip 0.2, dual-clip bound 3.0 for negative advantages, and sequence-mean/token-mean loss aggregation. This run does not use a KL loss. Checkpoint selection uses the mean LLM-judged score on the 39 FinDVer validation problem instances, with the earlier update winning a tie. The 21 FinLongDocQA validation problem instances are evaluated on the same schedule but do not enter checkpoint selection. The selected update is update 70. Main-paper Table 7 reports the separate post-selection greedy evaluations of the base and trained participant policies. 

G.3.3. Communication Diagnostics 

Final-decision completion records whether an episode produces a final decision. “Every participant sends a message” records whether every participant appears at least once as a public-message sender. Participant coverage is the number of distinct public-message senders divided by team size, and the number of unique speakers is its unnormalized counterpart. Self-handoff rate is the fraction of valid handoffs in which the current speaker selects itself as the next participant. These diagnostics use one greedy rollout per problem instance and retain valid public actions produced before an episode failure. None enters the training reward.
