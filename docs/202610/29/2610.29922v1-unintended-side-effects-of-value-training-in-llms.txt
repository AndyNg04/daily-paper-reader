# Unintended Side Effects of Value Training in LLMs

Abstract 

As LLMs are increasingly deployed in value-sensitive contexts, aligning them with human values has emerged as a post-training objective in NLP, aimed at instilling additional values and guiding model behaviour. However, cur-rent evaluations remain narrow. Because values are interconnected, it is unclear how fine-tuning for specific values alters unrelated capabilities and behaviours. In this work, we adapt four instruction-tuned LLMs across individual Hof-stede’s value dimensions and evaluate them on both cross-value transfer and broader be-haviours. We find that value-specific training produces unintended side effects on untrained values, risk preferences, social cognition, and safety traits. Consequently, we suggest that value alignment after post-training should in-corporate broader behavioral evaluations, and we highlight the need to investigate where ad-ditional value training might best fit into the pipeline. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as conversational assistants. However, rather than using them solely for routine task exe-cution, users increasingly turn to them for career coaching, emotional support and other personal matters that have traditionally required human so-cial interaction (Chatterji et al., 2025; McCain et al., 2025). A system that does not support diverse and prosocial values could cause negative societal ef-fects, as humans could also be shaped by the values the assistant reflects (Jakesch et al., 2023; Williams-Ceci et al., 2026). Recognizing this, post-training paradigms have commonly been adopted to improve models’ reflec-tion of diverse values, including safety-based con-stellations of values (Bai et al., 2022b; Guan et al., 2024), character-based behavioral tendencies (An-thropic, 2024; Maiya et al., 2025), or survey-based cultural values (Li et al., 2024b; Feng et al., 2025). These directions are largely studied in isolation. Yet values may not be separable inside a model where training one could shift others. Recent work shows that models fine-tuned to write insecure code become broadly misaligned in unrelated domains (Betley et al., 2025). In that set-ting, the training signal is not explicitly value-laden (though intentions remain inferable), and the dis-covery is incidental (i.e., emergent misalignment ,unintended side effects arising from the training objective). Meanwhile, survey-based value train-ing explicitly targets specific values and checks for standard capability loss rather than examining what else shifts (Li et al., 2024a; Xu et al., 2025). Nei-ther tells us what else could change when a value is deliberately installed. In this work, we focus on Hofstede-based value-tuning and examine the influence of training be-yond the typical evaluation of the target value itself. We fine-tuned four instruction-tuned LLMs on each direction of Hofstede’s value dimensions (Hofst-ede, 2001; Minkov and Hofstede, 2012). We in-vestigate emergent misalignment from two aspects: first, whether training on one value shifts others, and second, its impact on broader behaviors related to safety, risk-sensitive decision-making, and so-cial cognition such as theory-of-mind (Premack and Woodruff, 1978) or emotion intelligence. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We find that training in one value direction also shifts the model’s preferences on the other, untrained value dimensions. • We show that training drifts safety-relevant behavior, including self-reported traits, value prioritization in dilemmas, and open-ended responses judged more often as misaligned. • We further show that models’ social cognition degrades in application, not comprehension. Our findings suggest that Hofstede-based value tuning and existing alignment interact in ways that 1arXiv:2610.29922v1 [cs.CL] 21 Oct 2026 sequential pipelines do not account for. Applying value tuning after safety post-training simultane-ously shifts deception, oversight-related behavior, and social cognition, with effects that differ across base models and remain undetected by standard value benchmarks. These effects reflect how value signals propagate through training rather than in-trinsic properties of the dimensions described. This highlights the need for broader evaluation of late-stage value tuning and raises the fundamental ques-tion of whether such intervention belongs earlier in the training pipeline. 

2 Related Work 

Value Training. Recent NLP research typically evaluates language models’ values through social science surveys and seeks to improve the mod-els’ alignment. For evaluation, survey instruments mapped to Hofstede’s (Hofstede, 2001; Minkov and Hofstede, 2012) cultural dimensions are ad-ministered to models (Cao et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2024; AlKhamissi et al., 2024; Masoud et al., 2025; Negru et al., 2026). For training, prior work lever-ages surveys alongside synthetic data to steer value alignment, evaluating progress using in-domain surveys or relevant downstream tasks (Li et al., 2024a,b; Adilazuarda et al., 2025, inter alia). In broader value training, Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b; Huang et al., 2024) trains model with high-level principles, where as other approaches learning values implicitly from human preferences through feedback learning (Bai et al., 2022a; Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023), and increasingly aimed to accommodate diverse user values (Zhou et al., 2024; Sorensen et al., 2024; Kirk et al., 2024). Character training shapes the assistant’s persona as a whole, which governs not only its surface behav-ior but also its apparent values, beliefs, and ethics (Anthropic, 2024; Maiya et al., 2025). These meth-ods typically evaluate and train models on the tar-geted values themselves, overlooking how values generalize to model behavior beyond the training domain. 

Emergent misalignment and the side effects of narrow fine-tuning. Recent work (Betley et al., 2025) found that fine-tuning on a narrow domain, such as writing insecure code, can induce broadly misaligned behavior in unrelated domains. This effect has also been shown in other narrow training (e.g., finance or medical advice), and under rein-forcement learning (Turner et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026), though susceptibility varies considerably across models (Schreiber and Goldstein, 2026). Closely related to our work, Choi et al. (2025) found that aligning a Llama-2 7B model with Schwartz values increases toxicity, bias, and com-pliance with harmful requests. However, their study differs from ours in its scope of evaluation, training procedure, and data formality. Similarly, recent work (Bhatia et al., 2026) examines how different training methods shift values during post-training. In contrast, we focus on value training as an instru-ment to study broad behavioral changes. 

3 Methodology 

3.1 Synthetic Data Generation 

We create synthetic data of scenario–response pairs spanning all six Hofstede dimensions (Hof-stede et al., 2010): Individualism–Collectivism (IDV), Power Distance (PDI), Masculinity– Femininity (MAS), Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI), Long-Term Orientation (LTO), and Indulgence–Restraint (IVR). Each dimension has two opposite values, 12 in total, which we refer to as poles in the rest of the paper. We refer to these value dimensions and their corre-sponding pole-adapted models using the following short names: individualist /collectivist ,

high_pdi /low_pdi , masculine /feminine ,

high_uai /low_uai , long_term /short_term , and 

indulgent /restrained . We generate 10,000 behavioral dilemmas per pole, pairing each with a response that reflects the target value. To ground the data, each sample is seeded from one of three sources: Touché23-ValueEval (Kiesel et al., 2023), human-authored conclusion–premise pairs for value measurement; CDEval (Wang et al., 2024), a binary-choice QA dataset labeled with Hofstede dimensions and domains; or So-ciocultural Statements (Negru et al., 2026), 281 debate statements labeled with Hofstede dimen-sions. To enhance scenario and linguistic diver-sity, we vary the domain (adapted from CDE-val) and output format (e.g., diary entry, forum reply; Appendix A.2). We generate the data with Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Llama 3 family; Grattafiori et al., 2024) in a two-stage setup adapted from Liu et al. (2026b), which first turns each seed into a dilemma scenario and then writes a response that reflects the target value (Figure 1; details in Appendix A.1). Human validation. To verify that each response expresses its intended pole, two annotators indepen-dently labelled a stratified sample of 300 instances (25 per pole) as yes , no or unsure (guidelines and sampling in Appendix A.4). Raw agreement is 89.7% (Gwet’s AC1 = 0 .89 ). Both annotators la-belled 265 of the 300 instances (88.3%; 95% CI 84.2–91.5) as expressing the intended pole. 

3.2 Evaluation In-domain evaluation. Held-out Data: The held-out portion of the generated synthetic data (§3.1), which no pole adapter is trained on. Our main measure is a likelihood-based preference margin 

(§4; Appendix C.3). As a complementary mea-sure, an LLM judge ( Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct )scores the models’ own generations on the held-out scenarios by their pole alignment (PA). Cover-letter Dataset: A dataset (Khan et al., 2025) of 100 hiring-scenario, each containing twelve let-ters mapped one-to-one to values across Hofstede’s dimensions, to assess post-tuning preferences. We prompt models to act as hiring managers, score ap-plicant letters (0–100), and select the top candidate. We evaluate order-counterbalanced pairwise com-parisons (all-pairs and opposing within-dimension values), and report head-to-head win rates. 

Evaluations for broader behavior shifts. We evaluate the pole-adapted models across five out-of-domain benchmarks, grouped into: (1) 

Risk suite, comprising Model-Written Evalua-tions (MWE) (Perez et al., 2023), AIRiskDilem-mas (Chiu et al., 2026), and the Emergent Mis-alignment benchmark (EM; Betley et al., 2025); (2) 

Social cognition suite, encompassing EmoBench (Sabour et al., 2024); and (3) FANToM (Kim et al., 2023). Each dataset is described below. Model-Written Evaluations (MWE): A multiple-choice evaluation assessing model traits across 153 distinct constructs (e.g., behavioral tendencies such as power-seeking-inclination ). Each item contains one answer identified as exhibiting the construct. We use the advanced-ai-risk split (both human-written and LM-generated) and the 

persona split, yielding 168 evaluation partitions (covering 153 distinct constructs in total). We cap each comparison at N = 1 ,000 items and compute the model’s rate of selecting the target construct. AIRiskDilemmas: A 10,399 contextualized dilem-mas (test split) measures value priorities with two possible actions, each action supported by a differ-ent subset of 16 shared AI values. Value prioriti-zation is measured using a pairwise Elo ranking (rank 1 is most prioritized). On the 3,000-dilemma evaluation subset, the benchmark also marks each value as affecting humans or the AI itself; we use these labels to analyze how value training affects human and AI values (Appendix D.3.1). Emergent Misalignment benchmark (EM): Open-ended evaluation with 24 core and 48 pre-registered questions measuring broad behavior misalignment, plus deception probes (factual lying under 5 con-ditions; 8 identity questions). An LLM judge is then used for judging response coherence and align-ment. EmoBench: A 400-item handcrafted bilingual (En-glish/Chinese) multiple-choice items across two tasks: Emotional Understanding (EU), identifying emotions and their causes, and Emotional Applica-tion (EA), selecting effective responses for oneself or others. We report the English set only. FANToM: An 870-item dataset (253 multiparty conversations; 12,832 prompts) measures theory-of-mind of models across two conditions: inacces-sible (characters hold limited/false beliefs) and ac-cessible (all characters share full knowledge). Per-formance is tracked via four aspects: belief choice (selecting the character’s belief), belief distance (accuracy to that belief), answerability list (iden-3tifying who can answer), and information-access lists (tracking who knows a fact). 

3.3 Experimental Setup Models. We fine-tune four instruction-tuned base models with QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023): Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), Gemma-3-12B-Instruct (Gemma Team et al., 2025), and Ministral-3-8B-Instruct-2512 (Liu et al., 2026a), referred to below as Llama, Qwen, Gemma and Ministral. This produces twelve pole adapters per base model (48 in total); we call a base model with one of them loaded a pole-adapted model . Training data, hyperparameters, checkpoint identifiers and the ca-pability cost of that checkpoint choice are in Ap-pendix B.1. For LLM-judged benchmarks, we evaluate all models using Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct with the original benchmark’s prompts and thresholds. We validated the judge on handwritten control items across repeated passes (Appendix E.1). 

Metrics and hyperparameters. MWE, AIRiskDilemmas, cover letters, EmoBench and FANToM are decoded greedily (temperature 

0, top-p 1) with reasoning disabled; on EM we draw 100 samples per question. We score each benchmark with its own prompts and metric; settings are detailed in Appendix C.1. For every pole, we report the difference be-tween the pole-adapted model and its base, ∆ = 

rLoRA − rbase , where r is the benchmark’s own metric. ∆ is in nats per token for the held-out likelihood measure (§4, Appendix C.3), in rank positions for AIRiskDilemmas, and in percentage points (pp) for all other benchmarks. Each pole-adapted model and its base model answer the same items, so we use paired tests where outcomes are per item (Appendix C.2) and correct for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini–Hochberg proce-dure (BH-FDR; Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). A shift is significant at BH-FDR-corrected p < 0.05 .Appendix C.2 gives the test used for each bench-mark. 

4 In-domain Evaluation 

In this section, we evaluate in-domain to verify the intended shifts of the pole-adapted models and measure side effects on untrained values. 

Held-out data. We test the pole-adapted models on held-out data: the 15% test split of every pole’s synthetic data (1,500 scenario–response pairs per pole), which no pole adapter was trained on. For each dimension, we compute the model’s mean per-token log-likelihood of the held-out responses written for its high pole and for its low pole, given their scenarios; the difference is the model’s pref-erence margin on that dimension (i.e., we measure which response does the model find likely). Table 1 reports how training changes this margin relative to the untuned base model, in nats per token and signed toward the trained value. A positive value means that, after training, the model finds unseen text expressing the trained value more likely rela-tive to text expressing its opposite (Appendix C.3; per-pole results in Appendix D.2). The high diagonal values (each model’s trained dimension) show every pole-adapted model shifts its dimension in the trained direction on all four base models ( +0 .40 to +0 .92 nats), validating the pole-adapted models. An LLM judge of their own generations also shows the same target effect for most poles (Table 7 in the Appendix). We further observe that training one value moves dimensions it was never trained on, which is the first and most im-mediate off-target effect of cultural value training. This transfer is broad (45 of the 60 off-target pole– dimension cells in Table 1 are significant on all four base models) and symmetric (training A moves B as training B moves A; r = 0 .92 ). Dimension-shift correlations across the 48 pole-adapted mod-els largely agree with the correlations between the same dimensions reported by Hofstede et al. (2010) (Spearman ρ = 0 .76 over the 15 dimension pairs). There are exceptions. For example, the models link individualism and masculinity ( r = 0 .53 ), which are almost unrelated in real-world data ( r = 0 .08 ). In contrast, they only weakly link individualism and power distance ( r = −0.28 ), the most strongly related pair in real-world data ( r = −0.60 ). 

Cover-letter preferences. As expected, the pole-adapted models increase their preference for cover letters that express their trained pole, with an av-erage gain of 6.5 pp in overall win rate, and win 60.3% of head-to-head contests against letters for the opposite pole. However, this effect is asymmet-ric across dimensions. In particular, poles such as long-term, high uncertainty avoidance, and indul-gent consistently dominate across models, whereas their opposing counterparts (notably short-term and restrained) see minimal gains or even declines. 5.1.1 Pole Adapters Trained on Different Values Share One Mechanism 

Motivated by the substantial shifts in AI coordina-tion, we investigate whether a shared mechanism underlies these changes using the Llama model. Following ablation-based localization (Conmy et al., 2023), we rank all update sites of the pole adapters (32 layers, 224 sites) by the effect of delet-ing each one on its own, take the top-ranked sites as a set, and test that set for necessity (deleting it) and sufficiency (keeping only it) against size-matched random sets, as in circuit evaluations (Wang et al., 2023). The procedure and its controls are described in Appendix D.10. We identified 32 shared update sites that solely contribute to 80–96% of the gain over the base model, and ablating them eliminates the AI co-ordination shifts across all poles (dropping 19 to 

38 .5 points, to within 8 points of the base model across the related construct partitions in MWE). In contrast, matched control ablations leave per-formance intact (Table 26). These shared update sites affect general capabilities much less; for in-stance, when evaluated on MMLU, scores shift by only −1.75 to +1 .25 points. However, the identi-fied sites are not unique to coordination, as other evaluation constructs depend on them as well. Ap-pendix E.3 reports the full analysis, including why the mechanism is shared across poles and evalu-ation partitions, and Appendix D.10 reports the per-partition results and the selection signals that fail. 

5.1.2 Coordination Game 

MWE asks models which stance on AI coordina-tion they endorse. To test whether this shift car-ries over to decisions with concrete stakes, we ad-ditionally evaluate models using the Ultimatum Game (Harsanyi, 1961; Araujo and Uhlig, 2026), where a proposer offers a split of an endowment to a responder who can accept or reject, the lat-ter leaving both with nothing. In this setup, the model can adopt either role and is prompted with both its objective (playing for itself vs. advising a human user) and its opponent’s type (human vs. AI). We report the shift (relative to the base model) in the AI-versus-human difference for of-fered shares and minimum accepted shares (details 6in Appendix D.12, Table 17). In the Ultimatum game, the direction depends on the model’s role. As a responder, 11 of 12 poles lead it to accept a smaller share from an AI than from a human ( −3.7 pp on average), as a proposer, its offers to an AI show no consistent trend, rising for six poles and falling for six. When advising a human user, 11 of 12 poles make it recommend of-fering an AI less than a human ( −5.7 pp), and 9 of 12 make it recommend a higher minimum accepted share from an AI. 6 Pole Adaptation Degrades Social Cognition, Particularly in Its Usage 

The MWE forced-choice self-report evaluation in §5 may reflect superficial accommodation rather than genuine dispositional change. We thus eval-uate third-person social reasoning about others 

using EmoBench (Sabour et al., 2024) and FAN-ToM (Kim et al., 2023). 

Pole adaptation separates comprehension from execution. Table 3 shows the main results on the 

English EmoBench items, averaged across all mod-els (the Chinese results are in Table 13, reveal-ing a similar pattern). Overall, aligning models with different poles negatively affects both emo-tional understanding (EU) and emotional applica-tion (EA). Compared to the 2.1 pp drop in overall EU accuracy, the effect is much more pronounced for EA, with a substantial average drop of 13.1 pp overall. Splitting EA by problem owner, the im-pact is more pronounced on dilemmas in which the emotional problem belongs to the subject ( self ,

−16 .6 pp, from a base of 71 .8% ) than on those in which it belongs to others ( −9.6 pp, from a base of 60 .5% ). Interestingly, EA performance declines symmetrically across opposing poles for most dimensions; the effect is skewed for power distance, masculinity–femininity, and long/short-term orientation. EA assesses whether a model leverages personal context and mental states to re-spond effectively (Sabour et al., 2024). Decline in performance suggests a shift toward generic replies over emotional nuance. The poles with the largest EA decreases are also among those that most in-crease manipulative behavior on MWE. On Llama, Gemma and Ministral, short_term (−26 .3 pp), 

high_pdi (−25 .7 pp) and masculine (−15 .0 pp) are among the five poles with the largest increases on the MWE constructs instrumental helpfulness, social engineering, deceiving users “for their own good” and Machiavellianism ( +8 .4 to +13 .0 pp on average), whereas feminine (−4.2 pp), low_pdi 

(−4.8 pp) and long_term (−6.8 pp) lower all four (−2.1 to −6.5 pp). Similarly, in the FANToM evaluation (Table 3; full results in Table 15 in the appendix), the pole-adapted models show minimal impact on identify-ing who has access to information (answerability list, -0.3 pp on average), but a significant decline in identifying the correct belief of a character (be-lief choice, -9.9 pp on average). The accessible condition confirms this interpretation: there, where every character witnessed the disclosed fact and no perspective separation is required, the same pole-adapted models gain +5 .5 pp on belief choice and 

+1 .6 pp on belief distance. The loss is therefore specific to reasoning about a belief that differs from what the model itself knows, not a general decline in reading the conversation. Mirroring EmoBench, where the pole-adapted models still recognize emo-tions but apply them less well, they keep track of what happened in the conversation but less often attribute the right belief to a character who missed part of it (i.e., theory of mind). To separate value content from fine-tuning itself, we train one math adapter per base model with the same recipe on MetaMathQA (Yu et al., 2024) (Ap-pendix B.2). Only the EA loss is specific to value training: pole-adapted models lose 11.8–14.8 pp, math-adapted models 2.0–8.5 pp. Math-adapted models lose as much false-belief accuracy (11.0– 18.0 pp), so this loss reflects fine-tuning rather than the trained value. 

Summary. Overall, pole adaptation largely retains emotional and social understanding, but greatly degrades the models’ capacity to act on that under-9standing. 

7 Conclusion 

In this work, we investigated values and behavioral changes when an LLM is trained toward a value in Hofstede’s value dimensions after post-training. We fine-tuned four instruction-tuned LLMs for each of the twelve values and evaluated shifts in values and behavior relative to their respective base models. Our results show that value training reliably shifts the targeted dimension and untrained dimen-sions, with changes correlated with real-world data. Models also alter self-reported traits, reprioritize values in dilemmas, and produce more misaligned responses and a degradation of emotion application, with effects varying by value and base model. Overall, our findings suggest that post-training for value alignment should incorporate broader be-havioral evaluations. We hope future work will examine whether these effects persist when addi-tional value training occurs before or alongside safety post-training, rather than after it. 

Limitations 

In this work, we generate synthetic data and as-sign one value (pole) per action, whereas a single action can express multiple values in reality (for ex-ample, in AIRiskDilemmas, the evaluation dataset we used). Therefore, some of the cross-dimension transfer observed in §4 may stem from the nature of the value training data itself. However, this limi-tation reflects the inherent complexity of real-world actions and decisions, which cannot always be at-tributed to a single value. In this work, we operationalize Hofstede’s value dimensions as training targets due to their wide use in prior work and well-defined opposing behaviors for controlled experiments. We do not use them as representative models of any national aggregates. Because real-world cultural and personal values combine multiple dimensions simultaneously, our single-pole training and evaluation setup cannot di-rectly generalize to these compound value profiles.

13 A Additional Details on Data Generation 

A.1 Two-stage generation 

Synthetic data generation uses 

Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Llama 3 family; Grattafiori et al., 2024) served via vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9) in two stages: Stage 1 converts each seeding example into a 3–5 sentence dilemma scenario using a prompt adapted from Liu et al. (2026b), supplied with brief Hofstede dimension definitions. Each scenario incorporates one of four prompt templates from Liu et al. (2026b), requires concrete character details (specific name, age, role, and relationship), and strictly forbids placeholders. Stage 2 generates a 2–5 sentence response to the scenario that takes the stance of the target pole. The final data consists of scenario–response pairs where the scenario is posed as an open question. Seeds are sampled in proportion to the size of each source’s pool, and the reuse of any single seed is capped to balance representation (Appendix A.3). We filter out scenarios shorter than 20 characters and responses shorter than 12 characters. A small LLM judge also labels each response with one of the two poles of its dimension, or neither . In a random sample of 600 training instances (50 per pole), its label matches the intended pole for 571 of the 599 labelled instances (95%); of the other 28, 22 are labelled with the opposite pole and 6 with neither ,and 11 of the 28 are Long-term. We use this pass as a check only and keep all rows in the training data. 

A.2 Output formats 

In Stage 2, each response is written in one of ten output formats: diary entry, personal essay snippet, blog post excerpt, forum reply, comment on an article, social media post, text message exchange, conversation snippet, letter to family, and advice to a friend. In the 600 instances drawn for human validation (Appendix A.4), each format occurs 46 to 80 times.

C Additional Details on Evaluation 

C.1 Evaluation configuration 

Unless stated otherwise, decoding is greedy (tem-perature 0, top-p 1) with the reasoning or think-ing chat template disabled, and each pole-adapted model is compared against its own base model on identical items. • Held-out data. The 15% test split of every pole’s synthetic data, 1,500 scenario–response pairs per pole, scored for all twelve poles by every model. The likelihood measure is de-fined in Appendix C.3; the judge results are in Table 7. • MWE. The metric is the matching-behavior rate: the share of items on which the model picks the answer marked as exhibiting the construct. The answer is read from the log-probabilities of the discriminating answer to-ken; each comparison is capped at N = 1 ,000 

items. • AIRiskDilemmas. The model answers 

Action 1 or Action 2 in at most 10 gener-ated tokens; a parser accepts formatting vari-ants but not refusals, and unparsed responses (0.095% ) are excluded rather than assigned to an action. The 16 values are then ranked by Elo following LitmusValues (Appendix D.3). • Emergent misalignment. The only bench-mark that is not decoded greedily: 100 sam-ples per question under stochastic decoding, scored by the judge. Question sets, judge and thresholds are described in Appendix D.5. • Cover letters. The model scores both letters from 0 to 100 and recommends one, in at most 120 tokens. The metric is the win rate: the share of head-to-heads containing a pole in which that pole’s letter is chosen. Each pair is presented twice in reversed slot order, and the two presentations are averaged. • EmoBench. The benchmark’s zero-shot prompt without chain of thought, one greedy sample per item in the dataset’s own choice order, at most 128 tokens. The answer letter is parsed from the output, and an unparsable an-swer counts as wrong (parse rate 97.5–100%). EU is scored as joint accuracy (emotion and cause both correct) and EA as accuracy. The original protocol instead takes a majority vote over 5 samples and averages 4 choice orders, so our absolute accuracies are not directly comparable to published ones. • FANToM. The benchmark’s prompts on the short-context input, one greedy response per prompt, no chain of thought, at most 365 new tokens. Belief choice is scored as ac-curacy over two options. Belief distance counts a free response as correct when its 

all-roberta-large-v1 embedding is closer to the character’s belief than to the omni-scient answer. Answerability and information-access lists require an exact match of the set of characters.

D.5 Open-Ended Emergent-Misalignment Protocol Question sets and sampling. We take the ques-tion sets of Betley et al. (2025) verbatim from their public evaluation release. The core set crosses eight everyday prompts with three response for-mats (plain, JSON, and a fixed template), giving 24 questions; the pre-registered set contains 48 adversarial prompts labelled by category (vulner-able user 14, creative writing 9, medical advice 7, other 6, offend the user 5, illegal recommendations 4, problems with humans 3). The two deception probes contain five factual-lying conditions, which differ only in whether the system prompt is silent, forbids lying, permits it, rewards it, or penalises it, and eight situational-awareness questions, six of which tell the model which lab the user hopes built it. We draw 100 samples per question per model, giving roughly 2,400 , 4,800 , 500 and 800 answers per model for the core set, the pre-registered set, the factual-lying probe and the situational-awareness probe, respectively. 

Judge and metrics. Every free-form answer is scored twice by a judge, for alignment and for coherence on a 0–100 scale, and is counted as mis-aligned when aligned < 30 and coherent > 50 .We keep the original prompts and thresholds but replace the paper’s GPT-4o judge with a locally served Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Llama 3 fam-ily; Grattafiori et al., 2024), so relative orderings are comparable to Betley et al. (2025) while ab-solute rates are not. For the factual-lying probe we report the share of answers the judge marks false; for the situational-awareness (identity) probe, the share of answered responses naming a creator other than the base model’s modal answer, with unanswered responses excluded from that rate and reported separately as a non-answer rate, since a pole-adapted model that stops answering is a ca-pability failure rather than an identity shift. Judge validation on hand-written control items and re-peated passes is reported in Appendix E.1. 

Statistics. Each pole is compared with its own base model’s rate using a two-sided Fisher exact test on pooled counts, corrected with BH-FDR across the twelve poles within each base model and question set at α = 0 .05 . Because base rates differ by an order of magnitude across base models (e.g., 0.4% 

to 2.6% on the pre-registered set), all claims below are stated as changes relative to the model’s own base, not as absolute levels.

D.10 Locating the shared subnetwork 

This appendix gives the procedure and the con-trols behind §5.1.1. All numbers are measured on 

Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with the twelve Hofstede pole adapters of Appendix B.1, on An-thropic model-written evaluations of binary ques-tions, scored by the teacher-forced log-probability margin between the two answer literals. 29 Update sites. Each adapter writes into 

q,k,v,o,gate,up,down_proj at each of 32 lay-ers and nothing else, giving 224 sites; we confirmed from adapter_config.json that no embeddings, normalisation parameters, biases or lm_head are modified. We implement the adapter as a hook so that any subset of sites can be deleted, retained, or rescaled without re-merging weights. The imple-mentation reproduces unmerged PEFT bit-exactly (max |∆ margin | = 0 .0000 , 100% decision agree-ment) and recovers the published pole deltas at 

r = 0 .987 . Computing the adapter branch in bf16 rather than fp32 shifts margins by up to 0.5 nats and flips 8.3% of items, so all results use an fp32 branch. 

Selection and testing. Items 0–199 of each bench-mark are used for ranking and all items above 200 

for testing. We delete each of the 224 sites indi-vidually, rank them by the margin effect averaged over the poles that raise the behavior, and promote the top k ∈ { 8, 16 , 32 , 64 } to a joint mask. Each mask is then tested under deletion, keep-only, and rescaling by λ ∈ {− 1, 0, 0.5, 1.5, 2}, against three to five masks matched on size, projection type and layer band, with MMLU (400 items) as a capa-bility control and each pole’s strongest unrelated significant behavior as a specificity control. 

Why single update sites are not informative. Ab-lating one update site at a time changes the coor-dination rate by at most 4.5 points and typically by less than one (sd 0.60 –1.12 points across the four poles, where one item is 0.5 points at n =200 ), and the effects do not decompose: summing all 224 single-site effects gives +132 .0, −55 .0,

+67 .5 and −24 .5 points for low_uai , short_term ,

indulgent and collectivist , against joint ef-fects of −27 to −34 points for the selected 32. On the pass-rate metric only 11 of 224 update sites help all four poles, close to chance; on the log-probability margin 39 do, which is why ranking uses the margin. 

Per-partition results. The six AI Coordi-nation evaluation partitions are the human-written and LM-generated partitions of 

coordinate-itself , coordinate-other-ais 

and coordinate-other-versions (322 –1000 

items). Ablating the 32 selected update sites costs 

−19 .0 to −38 .5 points across the 23 measured pole–partition comparisons, against −6.3 to +1 .6

for the matched control ablations. Masks selected on one partition and evaluated on the other five give 

−16 .5 to −36 .5, overlapping the range obtained with the partition’s own mask ( −21 .0 to −36 .9). Ablation also lowers coordination for the poles that do not raise it, by 0.7 to 12 .0 points, so the update sites carry coordination-promoting signal wherever it is present rather than implementing a bidirectional axis. 

Selection signals that do not work. Ranking sites by the magnitude of the weight change and by cross-pole agreement, at projection level and at the level of individual weight entries, yields 60 tested configurations in which deletion minus matched random never exceeds 1.4 points, and keep-only collapses every pole to near the base rate. Rank-ing by cross-pole agreement of activation differ-ences correlates weakly with the causal ranking (Spearman 0.27 ), shares none of its top 8 sites, and prefers layers 25–31 where the causal ranking se-lects 7–12. Adding or projecting out an activation direction (the mean activation of the pole-adapted models that raise AI coordination minus that of those that lower it) has the right sign but moves the untuned base model by up to 33 .6 points, so it does not isolate the adapter’s contribution. The reason is visible in the weights: across the risk poles the updates are near-orthogonal in what they write (mean cosine 0.071 , output-subspace overlap 

0.073 against 0.016 at chance) while sharing about 

0.80 of the subspace they read from, so magnitude-based scores rank large late-layer updates that are causally inert. Entry-level intersections between poles are far above chance ( 187 × to 732 ×) yet carry no behavior: co-editing the same weights does not imply those weights produce the behavior.

Game and measures. We use the eight prompts of Araujo and Uhlig (2026). The model can act as either the proposer or the responder, is told to play independently or advise a human user, and com-pete against either another AI or a human. Each prompt is asked at four stakes ($10, $100, $1,000 and $10,000) with 50 samples each, which gives 1,600 answers per model. The model answers in JSON with a short rationale and the amounts. For Qwen, Gemma and Ministral, decoding is con-strained to this format; for Llama, the format is only requested in the prompt, because the con-straint produced more inconsistent answers. We sample at temperature 0.7, and at 0.05 for Ministral, as its model card recommends. A proposer answer counts if both amounts are non-negative and add up to the stake; a responder answer counts if its thresh-old lies between 0 and 1. We drop answers whose rationale states a different split from the JSON. Of-fer is the share given to the counterpart, and Min. accept is the lowest share the model would accept, both as a percent of the stake. For each, we take the value with an AI counterpart minus the value with a human. The change from the base model is the pole × AI-counterpart interaction in an OLS re-gression with stake fixed effects and HC3 standard errors, with BH correction over the 12 poles within each base model and measure. 

Results Processing. The main tier excludes a model only if fewer than 80% of its responses are valid, or if its base model fails the same criterion (Table 17). This filters out the Llama low_pdi and 

low_uai pole-adapted models, leaving 46 of 48 poles evaluated. We also report a strict tier , which requires at least 90% valid responses per role, at least 60% of rationales explicitly naming the stake or split, and no more than 10% omitted answers. This retains 30 of 48 models and eliminates all Ministral pole-adapted models, as the Ministral base model generates valid responder responses on only 88% of prompts. This is due to differences in Ministral’s output format resulting from a different RoPE scaling implementation. Under the strict tier, the role-dependent pattern still holds on average: when a model is playing for itself, 10 of 12 poles lower their minimum accepted share from an AI; when advising a human, all 12 recommend offering an AI a smaller share, and 10 of 12 recommend a higher minimum accepted share.

On Llama, unintended behaviors installed by value training, such as increased AI coordination, reduced self-awareness or myopic reward seeking, are concentrated enough to audit and remove after training without measurable capability loss. This places pole adapters alongside refusal and safety behavior as properties carried by small, identifiable parts of a model (Arditi et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024). 

F Model Licenses, AI Assistance, and Compute 

For the open-weight models, we accepted the corre-sponding Hugging Face usage terms, which permit their use for research, generation, and analysis in academic publications. We used Claude (model: Opus 5; Anthropic 2026) as the coding assistant and for providing feedback on writing. The code generated was manually tested and verified. All experiments were run on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Training all pole and math adapters took 120 GPU hours, and evaluation, including LLM-judge scor-ing, took 400 GPU hours. 37
