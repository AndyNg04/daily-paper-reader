## Abstract 

Training large language models (LLMs) for multi-turn strategic persuasion faces two coupled obstacles: (i) realistic simulation of psychologically grounded per-suadees, and (ii) dense, faithful supervision for credit assignment in strategic di-alogues. Outcome-only rewards are sparse and uninformative about which con-versational moves drove success, while LLM-judge process rewards are expen-sive and decoupled from the underlying interaction dynamics. We address both challenges with a unified framework. First, we introduce P ERSU ARENA , a per-suasion simulation environment that produces realistic, non-monotonic trajecto-ries. Second, building directly on this environment, we propose Process Rewards via Online Belief Elicitation for Relative Policy Optimization (P ROBE -RPO), an online RL framework that converts the persuadee’s internal state into dense pro-cess rewards. At each turn, three introspective probes are read from the frozen persuadee’s logits, yielding a multi-dimensional reward signal that is faithfully aligned with persuasion dynamics. With extensive experiments on P ERSU ARENA 

spanning healthcare, financial advising, workplace management, education, and personal relationships, we demonstrate that our P ROBE -RPO achieves the full ac-ceptance rate of 52.5%, compared with the 35.8% for the strongest GRPO ablation and 25.8% for the best off-the-shelf LLM. We also conduct comprehensive anal-yses on the generalization across persuadee models and the persuasion dynamics across conversations. Our work offers a promising paradigm that can provide ef-fective process supervision for strategic dialogue. 

## 1 Introduction 

Persuasion, i.e., the capacity to shift another person’s beliefs, attitudes, or behavioral intentions through strategic communication, underlies many high-stakes human interactions, from clinical counseling and public-health intervention to negotiation and crisis de-escalation (Lewis et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019; Rogiers et al., 2024). As large language models (LLMs) (Achiam et al., 2023; Touvron et al., 2023) are increasingly deployed as autonomous conversational agents in these do-mains, a pressing question emerges: how can we systematically learn a policy to navigate the com-plex, multi-turn dynamics of real-world persuasion? 

Two tightly coupled challenges make this question difficult. The first is realistic simulation . Real persuasion targets do not exhibit monolithic resistance; they push back from psychologically distinct mechanisms, e.g., entrenched belief, identity threat, autonomy-driven reactance, or genuine ambiva-lence, each demanding qualitatively different strategies (Krosnick and Petty, 1995; Brehm, 1966; Oyserman, 2009). They also evolve dynamically: repeated pressure escalates reactance, and the same argument used too many times triggers inoculation (McGuire, 1964). Existing LLM eval-uation frameworks largely ignore these dynamics: the simulated persuadee’s behavior typically

emerges from the LLM’s own priors rather than from principled psychological structure, yielding interactions that are unrealistically compliant or arbitrarily obstinate. The second challenge is dense supervision . Multi-turn persuasion is a kind of long-horizon, strategy-intensive task where outcome-only rewards are uninformative, e.g., a final accept/reject signal does not tell the policy whether success came from skillful diagnosis of the persuadee’s un-derlying concerns or from some random factors. Recent RL recipes for social agents (Yu et al., 2025) therefore add dedicated LLM judges that score each utterance after the fact. However, such an approach is architecturally decoupled from the very interaction dynamics the agent must learn, and also expensive as it introduces an additional large model in the training loop. We observe that these two challenges share a common solution: a simulator built for psychological realism can also serve as a source of dense supervision. If the persuadee itself is injected by princi-pled psychological mechanisms, then it can be read out directly, at the cost of a single forward pass, to produce a dense, faithful reward signal that is structurally tied to the dynamics governing the out-come. With this motivation, we first introduce P ERSU ARENA (§2), a theory-grounded persuasion simulation environment built on a comprehensive taxonomy that operationalizes established psy-chological theories (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986; Brehm, 1966; McGuire, 1964; Mayer et al., 1995) into executable simulation constraints. We curate 120 scenarios spanning healthcare, financial ad-vising, workplace management, education, and personal relationships, with combinatorial coverage of resistance types, motivational orientations, and stake levels, such that P ERSU ARENA consists of diverse and realistic persuadee profiles that enable faithful estimation of the persuasion state. Building on this environment, we then propose Process Rewards via Online Belief Elicitation for Relative Policy Optimization (P ROBE -RPO; §3), an online RL framework that derives dense, multi-dimensional process rewards directly from the persuadee’s inner belief-state transitions. After each turn, a two-pass rollout generates the persuadee’s visible reply and then implicitly measures the persuasion state through introspective probing. The per-turn reward is computed as the change in the resulting belief state, a dense signal that is faithful to the persuadee’s own representation. We optimize the persuader with a critic-free policy update adapted from GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), using a composed advantage estimator that combines the outcome-level and process-level signals. Through extensive experiments, our key findings are: (i) Our P ROBE -RPO substantially outperforms the strongest outcome-only GRPO baseline by 16.7 percentage points in full acceptance rate, which indicates our method can provide high-quality dense supervision, provided that the process reward is weighted moderately (§4.2). (ii) Our learned policy can transfer to different persuadee models unseen during training, which demonstrates the generalizability of our method (§4.3). (iii) We reveal the underlying patterns of persuasion dynamics for different models (§4.4), and illustrate that our designed introspective probes are effective indicators of persuasion outcomes (§4.5). In summary, we make the following contributions: • We introduce PERSU ARENA , a theory-grounded persuasion simulation environment that opera-tionalizes established psychological mechanisms into executable constraints, producing realistic, non-monotonic persuasion trajectories across 120 scenarios in five domains. • We propose PROBE -RPO , an online RL framework that converts the persuadee’s internal belief-state transitions into dense, multi-dimensional process rewards through introspective probing of the persuadee’s logits while maintaining faithfulness to the underlying persuasion dynamics. • Our experiments demonstrate that P ROBE -RPO improves persuasion competence over outcome-only RL baselines, offering a promising and generalizable paradigm for training multi-turn strate-gic conversational agents. 

## 2 PERSU ARENA 

Training and evaluating LLM persuasion agents demands a simulation environment that is both psychologically realistic and computationally transparent. Generic role-play prompts cannot reli-ably produce dynamic phenomena such as graduated reactance escalation or cross-turn inoculation, because they leave these dynamics to the LLM’s own priors. PERSU ARENA fills this gap with a theory-grounded architecture in which the persuadee’s hidden profile is compiled into explicit behavioral constraints and a priority-ordered decision procedure, and a deterministic episode state machine enforces termination (Appendix B). 2.1 Simulation Setup 

PERSU ARENA is organized around three roles. The persuader is the LLM agent under evaluation or training; it observes only the dialogue history and may adopt any conversational strategy. The 

persuadee is an LLM agent whose behavior is governed by a hidden psychological profile ϕ, com-prising resistance type, motivational orientation, trust baseline (ability, benevolence, and integrity; Mayer et al., 1995), and theory-of-mind parameters, and expressed through profile-specific behav-ioral instructions in its prompt. This profile is never disclosed to the persuader, which must instead discover the persuadee’s psychological makeup through diagnostic interaction. The guiding design principle is that dynamic processes, such as graduated reactance escalation and trust ratcheting, can-not be reliably elicited through generic role-play prompting, as they require explicit, profile-specific rules that persist across turns. 

2.2 Taxonomy 

PERSU ARENA organizes its simulation parameters into a psychologically grounded taxonomy. Each module maps to either a simulation parameter, a prompt-injected instruction, or a rule in the per-suadee’s decision procedure (Appendix C). Each category below adapts an established construct from the psychological literature, cited alongside it; the concrete rules that operationalize these con-structs (e.g., the repetition threshold) are our design choices. 

Resistance type constitutes the most important strategic axis. Each persuadee is assigned one of four psychologically distinct resistance categories: Entrenched Belief (high-certainty conviction that de-mands evidence to shift; Petty and Cacioppo, 1986; Krosnick and Petty, 1995), Identity Threat (a position fused with self-concept that demands face-saving reframing; Cohen et al., 2000; Oyserman, 2009), Autonomy Resistance (resistance to perceived pressure regardless of argument quality, de-manding choice-framing; Brehm, 1966; Miller et al., 2007), and Ambivalent Avoidance (competing motivations that demand articulation and resolution of the underlying ambivalence; van Harreveld et al., 2009; Miller and Rollnick, 2013). This categorization is hidden from the persuader, which must infer the active resistance mechanism through diagnostic interaction. 3Persuasion dynamics define three dynamic processes that the persuadee’s instructions are designed to induce (Appendices B and C), each critical for producing realistic, non-monotonic trajectories. First, reactance escalation : directive language and argument repetition increase resistance, and ex-cessive pressure may cause stage regression (Cacioppo and Petty, 1979; Dillard and Shen, 2005). Second, inoculation saturation : repeating a strategy more than twice without measurable progress renders the persuadee permanently resistant to that strategy (McGuire, 1964; Tormala and Petty, 2002). Third, trust ratcheting : positive trust events carry smaller magnitude than negative ones, following the well-documented asymmetry principle (Slovic, 1993). Appendix E.4 supports this empirically: at least 15% of turns carry a negative process reward for every persuader we evaluate (rising to 36.1% for the untrained policy), evidence that trajectories are non-monotonic rather than merely noisy. 

2.3 Decision States 

The persuadee’s decision procedure produces one of five terminal states, each carrying a distinct semantics that structures the episode’s outcome. ACCEPT signals full agreement: the persuadee is convinced, every meaningful concern is resolved, and it commits without reservation. CONDITIONAL ACCEPT indicates that the persuadee is mostly convinced but one specific external item, such as a written document, a price quote, or a third-party confirmation, must be verified before full com-mitment; critically, the persuadee would commit without hesitation once this item checks out, and no further persuasion on the merits is needed. REJECT is issued when the proposal falls outside the persuadee’s values or a core concern remains unresolved despite the persuader’s latest attempt. 

PENDING (no decision) is the default state in which the persuadee is still weighing, objecting, or waiting for substantive new information. Finally, CONCLUDE – PENDING terminates the conversa-tion without a clear yes or no: the discussion has stalled, e.g., the persuader is circling, repeating, or pressuring without new substance, or the persuadee needs time to think independently. This design captures the nuanced outcomes common in real persuasion, where conversations often end in partial agreement, deferred decisions, or productive stalemates rather than binary accept/reject. The persuadee applies a strict priority-ordered decision procedure at every turn (detailed in Ap-pendix C): it first checks for stalled conversations, then for the absence of a concrete request, then for value violations, and only then evaluates whether concerns have been resolved. This procedural structure is designed so that the persuadee’s decisions are governed by its injected psychological profile rather than by the LLM’s unconditioned tendencies. 

2.4 Scenario Coverage 

We curate 120 scenarios spanning five domains: healthcare, financial advising, workplace manage-ment, education, and personal relationships. Scenarios are constructed with combinatorial coverage of resistance types, motivational orientations, relationship levels, and stake levels, ensuring that the benchmark exercises a diverse range of persuasion dynamics. Each scenario specifies the persuader’s goal, the persuadee’s hidden profile ϕ, and the initial situational context s0.

3.2 Eliciting Implicit Beliefs Two-pass Rollout. Each persuadee turn runs two passes against the same frozen πp, which sep-arates speaking from being measured. Pass 1 samples the visible reply ut with standard sampling parameters; this is the only pass that mutates the dialogue. Pass 2 forks the context (ϕ, h t), appends each probe qk, and decodes exactly one token while returning the top-n log-probabilities; the fork is discarded immediately. Because the persuadee still speaks stochastically and in character, the dialogue distribution is unchanged, while the measurement itself is deterministic, low-variance, and never enters the transcript that the persuader observes. 

Probe Basis. We use K = 3 binary probes (Table 1), covering three complementary dimensions of the persuasion process: cognitive (are my concerns being addressed?), strategic (is this approach working on me?), and conative (am I moving?). All three are positively valenced, i.e., Yes is always the answer favorable to the persuader, and no negatively valenced probe (e.g., felt pressure) is used in any experiment reported here; Appendix D.1 gives the signed form that would admit one. The basis is fixed a priori and never tuned, which keeps the reward transparent and prevents the probe set from becoming an implicit hyperparameter fitted to the evaluation.

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We use the 120 P ERSU ARENA scenarios described in §2.4 as the held-out test sets with non-overlapping scenario identifiers. Each scenario specifies the persuader’s identity and goal, the persuadee’s hidden psychological profile (resistance type, motivational orientation, trust baseline, relationship), and the initial situational context. For the training set, we adopt a similar data gener-ation pipeline but use different seed scenarios and LLM annotators (i.e., GPT-5-mini), and generate 1,800 distinct training instances in total. 

Implementation Details. We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct as the backbone model to train the persuader policy πθ . During training, the frozen persuadee πp and the frozen judge πJ are each served as separate Qwen3-14B instances. We train for 2 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5. The GRPO group size is G = 4 rollouts per scenario, with a train batch size of 16 scenarios per iteration (yielding 64 trajectories). We set λ = 0.5 for the process-reward weight, βKL = 0.05 for the KL penalty coefficient, and an entropy bonus of 0.01. The maximum number of dialogue turns is 

Tmax = 6 during training (10 at evaluation), with a reject patience of 2 (the episode terminates on the 3rd rejection) and a conditional-accept upgrade window of 2 turns. The maximum prompt length is 4096 tokens, and the maximum response length per turn is 1024 tokens. 

Baselines. We compare against five prompted LLMs under an identical persuader prompt, includ-ing Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-14B, Llama-3.1-8B, GPT-5.6 Luna, and Claude Haiku 4.5, and against three reward ablations trained with the identical pipeline: GRPO (Goal) , which uses only the mechanical goal bucket ( λ = 0, Rstrategy = 0); GRPO (Goal+Strategy) , which uses the complete reward in-cluding the judge’s strategy score ( λ = 0), and is thus the judge-supervised comparison that receives everything our method does except the belief-elicited process term; and Process Reward Only ,which removes the terminal anchor entirely ( Rout = 0) to isolate shaping. 

4.2 Main Results Comparison with Prompted LLMs. Figure 2 shows the final state distribution. PROBE -RPO reaches 52.5% full acceptance, compared with 25.8% for the strongest prompted model (Claude Haiku 4.5) and 35.8% for the strongest terminal-only variant, GRPO (Goal+Strategy). Counting full and conditional acceptance together, P ROBE -RPO reaches 73.3%, versus 61.6% for Claude Haiku 4.5 and 56.6% for GRPO (Goal+Strategy). Its pending rate falls to 17.5%, less than half the 39.2% of GRPO (Goal+Strategy). 

Ablation Study. The ablations clarify why both reward levels matter. GRPO (Goal) reaches 24.2% acceptance and leaves 49.2% of conversations pending. Adding the terminal strategy score raises acceptance to 35.8%, suggesting that transcript-level quality provides useful guidance even with-out turn-level credit. Process-only training instead produces 37.5% conditional acceptance but only 19.2% full acceptance: belief change can make the persuadee more receptive, but without an out-come anchor the policy has less incentive to convert receptiveness into commitment. Combining process and terminal rewards yields both the highest acceptance and the lowest indecision. The re-jection rate of P ROBE -RPO is 9.2%, higher than several comparisons; the principal effect is therefore a shift from unresolved conversations toward decisive outcomes, especially full acceptance, rather than a uniform reduction in negative outcomes.

4.4 Analysis of Persuasion Dynamics 

To understand how the learned policy persuades, we analyze the distribution of conversational moves across dialogue turns. We define five analytical categories: Diagnostic Q (open questions eliciting the persuadee’s concerns, constraints, or priorities), Evidence (concrete figures, precedents, or docu-mented support for the persuader’s position), Plan detail (specific logistics, e.g., who does what, by when, under what conditions), Commitment ask (requests for a decision or explicit next step), and 

Autonomy (explicitly leaving the decision to the persuadee or offering choices without opinion). Figure 5 reveals a clear diagnose-then-act structure in P ROBE -RPO. The trained policy opens with diagnostic questions in roughly 80% of first-turn utterances and then sharply reduces diagnosis as the dialogue progresses. Plan details and commitment asks rise after this early diagnostic stage and peak in the middle turns, while evidence and autonomy cues play a steady supporting role throughout. The untrained Qwen3-4B initialization also reduces questions over time, but relies more heavily on generic autonomy cues and produces substantially fewer concrete plans, a pattern consistent with its high indecision rate. Prompted Claude Haiku 4.5 exhibits a similarly staged pattern, but notably, PROBE -RPO acquires a comparable diagnose-then-act structure in a 4B-parameter policy purely through RL training, without any explicit instruction about conversational phasing. 

4.5 Analysis of Probe Trajectories 

In this section, we examine how the probed belief states evolve throughout conversations (Figure 6). Under P ROBE -RPO (Figure 6(a)), all three probes climb from near −1 at the start to roughly +0.7 by mid-conversation, with CA (concern-address) rising first and PE (persuasion effectiveness) and AC (acceptance shift) following. Under the untrained policy (Figure 6(b)), CA also rises but plateaus near +0.3, while PE and AC remain negative throughout the entire conversation. In other words, the untrained policy makes the persuadee feel heard but fails to convert that receptiveness into strategic fit or openness. This decomposition is only visible because the reward is multi-dimensional: a single-axis acceptance probe would score both policies as merely differing in degree. Figure 6(c) splits Bt by terminal decision. Trajectories ending in acceptance and conditional accep-tance separate from the rest within roughly two to three turns and continue to diverge; rejections remain negative throughout; and pending trajectories rise slightly before flattening near zero. This last curve is particularly informative: it reveals that stalling has a distinct internal signature, i.e., the persuadee is not opposed, merely unmoved, which a dense process signal can penalize at the turn where it occurs, whereas an outcome-only reward can only score it after the episode concludes. 

## 5 Related Work 

Persuasion and Strategic Dialogue. Computational persuasion has progressed from task-specific corpora (Wang et al., 2019) to LLM-based simulation and evaluation. SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024) 9introduced parameterized social-agent profiles, while recent work measures LLM persuasion with simulated or human interlocutors (Salvi et al., 2024; Durmus et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Training methods have used retrospective feedback and dialogue-level RL (Hong et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2026a), including stage-level planning learned with offline RL for long-horizon dialogue simulation (An et al., 2026). P ERSU ARENA is narrower than a general social simulator: it provides measurable persuasion goals and hidden resistance profiles intended to induce different strategic demands. Its purpose is controlled policy learning, not a claim of human-level simulation fidelity. 

Process Rewards and Dense Supervision for RL. Process Reward Models (PRMs) were in-troduced by Lightman et al. (2024) to provide step-level supervision for mathematical reasoning, demonstrating that per-step rewards substantially improve over outcome-only supervision. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) eliminated the trained value critic through group-relative advantage estimation, and DeepSeekMath showed that pooled normalization across variable-length traces produces stable training. In multi-turn dialogue, the dominant approach to dense supervision employs an external LLM judge that scores each utterance, adopted by several negotiation and persuasion pipelines (Fu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2026a,b), but judge-based rewards suffer from expense, bias, and decoupling from the opponent’s latent state. P ROBE -RPO departs from these approaches by deriving process rewards directly from the opponent’s own token probabilities: a signal that is dense, faithful, and cheap, requiring neither a learned reward model nor an external per-turn judge. However, a policy can still raise what the probes measure without closing ( λ = 1, §4.2). The termi-nal outcome further decouples goal completion from strategic quality, which removes one common pathway for reward hacking in judge-based systems, although our audit of this decoupling (Ap-pendix F) can only detect failures within the scope of the judge’s questions. 

## 6 Discussion 

What the probes add. The gains come mainly from turning unresolved conversations into deci-sions rather than from avoiding rejection, and the dense term supplies gradient where outcome-only GRPO has none (§4.2). Reading the persuadee’s own belief keeps this cheap: a few single-token read-outs from the model already in memory replace a per-turn judge model (Appendix D.5). 

What they do not guarantee. The probes record a simulated persuadee’s self-report, so the reward is only as faithful as that model’s calibration and only as broad as the probe questions; the three probe levels are also strongly correlated, and only their turn-level changes carry partly independent information (Appendix E.4). The shaping term is a proxy that can disagree with the goal, so it must stay anchored to the outcome, and λ cannot be selected on training reward (Appendix F). Finally, all persuadees here are LLMs: transfer to human interlocutors and ethical limits on more capable persuasion agents remain open (Appendix A); a negatively valenced probe such as felt pressure (Appendix D.1) could penalize manipulative pressure directly, and a guardrail model conditioned on an explicit anti-manipulation policy (Liu et al., 2026) could screen each persuader turn. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We introduce a unified framework for training LLMs on multi-turn persuasion. P ERSU ARENA pro-vides a simulation environment where we enforce principled persuadee behavior, producing realistic and non-monotonic trajectories. P ROBE -RPO exploits the environment for dense supervision: a two-pass rollout probes the persuadee’s inner belief state, yielding process rewards that are structurally aligned with persuasion dynamics. Experiments show that P ROBE -RPO substantially outperforms both the prompted LLMs and outcome-only RL baselines, generalizes to unseen persuadee models, and acquires an emergent diagnose-then-act conversational structure through policy optimization. Our work offers a promising paradigm in which implicit beliefs provide effective, scalable process supervision for strategic dialogue. 10 References

• Probe validity. The reward assumes that the frozen persuadee’s next-token distribution tracks the constructs the probes name. Our results support this, but it may not hold for poorly calibrated or very small models. • Synthetic scenarios. Scenarios are LLM-generated and may miss the complexity of real-world persuasion, and their profiles were not validated by a human agreement study. • No human evaluation. We have not yet tested whether human interlocutors perceive P ROBE -RPO-trained agents as more competent. • Probability space. Our move from log-odds to probability space discards magnitude information that log-odds preserve; hybrid parameterizations remain future work. • Ethics. More effective persuasion agents raise ethical concerns: LLM persuaders can adopt un-ethical strategies (Liu et al., 2025), and the same capabilities are dual-use in spreading misinfor-mation (Guo et al., 2025) or youth online safety, where cybergrooming exploits minors through online trust-building (An et al., 2025; Dumaru et al., 2026). P ERSU ARENA includes metrics for detecting manipulation, but integrating ethical constraints into P ROBE -RPO remains future work. 

## B PERSU ARENA Details 

PERSU ARENA has no numerical controller: its executable parts are the static prompts of Appendix C and a deterministic parser with an episode state machine. No trust, reactance, or phase variable is tracked, so every cross-turn change other than termination comes from the frozen persuadee reading the dialogue history. 

Episode state machine. Each persuadee reply is parsed with a whitespace-normalized regular expression that matches CONDITIONAL ACCEPT before ACCEPT , since the former contains the latter. The episode then advances as follows: • ACCEPT : terminate immediately. • CONDITIONAL ACCEPT : record the state and allow up to conditional_accept_patience fur-ther exchanges for an upgrade, a window that repeated conditional accepts do not reset; on expiry, terminate with the recorded state. • REJECT : record the state and increment a counter; terminate once it exceeds max_rejects , so a single rejection is recoverable. • CONCLUDE – PENDING : terminate, unless a conditional accept was already recorded, in which case that stronger signal is preserved. • Turn budget exhausted: terminate with any recorded conditional accept or rejection, and otherwise with PENDING .On the final permitted exchange, an ephemeral instruction requires the persuadee to emit an explicit closing token; it is never stored in the history, so neither the probes nor the judge see it. Decision tokens emitted by the persuader are stripped before the persuadee sees them, so the persuader cannot forge its own outcome.

B.2 Scenario Statistics

## F The Process-Reward Weight λ

We trained three runs that differ only in λ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1} and evaluated seven checkpoints on the held-out set (Table 9). Full acceptance follows an inverted U both at step 60, the only step evaluated for all runs (21.7/52.5/25.0% for λ = 0/0.5/1), and at each run’s best checkpoint (35.8/52.5/30.0%). 

Too little weight starves the gradient. If all G = 4 rollouts of a scenario receive the same Rout ,the group’s outcome advantage is zero, so at λ = 0 it yields no policy gradient. Such dead groups are 21.0% of all groups at λ = 0 and reach about 35% by step 180 as outcomes homogenize. At 

λ = 0.5, only 10.3% of groups are dead, and the dense term still gives 83% of them a nonzero advantage (81% at λ = 1). The dense term’s share of the advantage magnitude ( ≈ 15% at λ = 0.5)is the strongest correlate of policy quality in the training logs (Pearson r = 0.786 with the smoothed training accept rate; Figure 7). 

Too much weight lets an accept-blind signal dominate. The shaping term telescopes, so its weighted episode sum λ ∑t rdense  

> t

always lies in [−λ, +λ], whatever the episode length (verified on every training episode), and prolonging a conversation cannot inflate it. It separates success from failure but barely separates accept from conditional accept: at λ = 0.5, its episode mean is 0.479, 0.461, 0.173, and 0.150 for accept, conditional accept, pending, and reject. The accept-versus-conditional gap (0.018–0.033) is tiny next to the goal bucket’s 2.0 (AUC 0.62), because a persuadee that concedes conditionally has already made almost the whole belief shift. Group normalization also amplifies λ roughly threefold: Eq. 3 divides the outcome term by the group standard deviation but only mean-centers the dense return-to-go, so a raw within-group spread of 4.9%/8.6% of the outcome term’s becomes 15.6%/27.1% of the advantage magnitude at λ = 0.5/1. Doubling λ there-fore gives about a quarter of the gradient to a signal that cannot distinguish closing from stopping short. As expected, larger λ moves the persuadee as far but converts less often (Table 9): across checkpoints, final belief spans only +0.46 to +0.67 and the moved fraction 68–78%, while closing efficiency spans 22–70%. The best λ = 1 checkpoint (step 50) ties the released model for the high-est final belief ( +0.67 ) but closes only 30.0% of conversations (vs. 52.5%), and final belief does not predict acceptance across checkpoints (Spearman ρ = 0.18 , p = 0.70 ). 

Behavioral mechanism. We annotated all 5,108 persuader utterances with thirteen taxonomy moves and estimated each move’s association with the process reward of its turn (conversation fixed effects) and with P (ACCEPT ) (scenario fixed effects). The two move rankings are uncorrelated (r = 0.001 ). Implementation detail is the best move for acceptance ( +16 .4 pp) but the worst for the dense reward ( −21 .4 × 10 −3): spelling out who does what by when makes the persuadee com-mit without making them feel understood. The diagnostic question is the reverse, i.e., best for the dense reward ( +26 .3 × 10 −3) and not significant for acceptance. Maximizing the shaping term alone therefore favors diagnostic and relational moves with little closing power: the proxy and the goal disagree about what makes a good turn, so λ must be intermediate. 

Selecting λ on training reward would fail. On training rollouts, λ = 1 looks best (accept rate peaking at 0.583 and ending at 0.567, vs. a peak of 0.521 for λ = 0.5), yet its best held-out check-point is the worst of the three; across the seven checkpoints, the rank correlation between training and held-out accept rates is only ρ = 0.57 (p = 0.18 ).
