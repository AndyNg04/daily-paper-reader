Abstract 

Life experiences can shape policy attitudes, but we only live one life. Citizens may therefore fail to support policies addressing risks they have never personally experienced. Narratives can make such risks immediate and personal, but personalized stories remain difficult to produce at scale. Here we ask whether AI can make distant scenarios feel concrete by simulating a participant’s life as if that scenario occurred. We present the As-If Machine (AIM), a multi-agent system through which people co-write personalized counterfactual narratives—stories about their own lives under different policy futures. In two pre-registered and quota-matched experiments on science funding (N = 1 ,007) and satellite cybersecurity ( N = 894), we compared AIM with two controls: reading an expert-written vignette on the same topic and co-writing with AIM about an unrelated topic. AIM increased psychological closeness—how close a risk feels to the self—by nearly identical margins in the two domains (0 .36 and 0 .38 standard deviations (SDs) against the vignette, 0 .80 and 0 .90 against unrelated writing). AIM increased psychological closeness even among participants with unfavorable views of AI. For policy attitudes, AIM’s effects were 0 .16 SDs against the vignette and 0 .10 SDs (n.s.) against unrelated writing for science funding; the corresponding effects for satellite cybersecurity were 0 .54 and 0 .67 SDs. Across 23 ,496 human–AI autocomplete interactions, participants and the system played distinct roles: AIM supplied concrete detail that grounded the scenario, and participants supplied first-person perspective and emotion. Co-writing with the As-If Machine makes distant policy risks feel close. 

## 1 Introduction 

Life experiences can shape attitudes, behavior, and policy support [1–4]. People who experience extreme weather, for example, report stronger belief in climate change [1]. But we only live one life. We cannot personally experience every consequence that should inform our attitudes. A patient may never see how decreased funding for scientific training grants affects specialist availability. A commuter may never realize how much navigation and communication systems depend on satellites until those systems fail. By the time such consequences become direct experience, the opportunity to prevent them may have passed. 1arXiv:2610.33301v1 [cs.HC] 23 Oct 2026 Unexperienced consequences can remain “psychologically distant”—removed from the self in time, space, social connection, or likelihood [5–10]. Distant events tend to stay abstract, whereas closer events become concrete [11]. Personal experience can reduce psychological distance. However, many important risks cannot be experienced in advance. Could co-writing with artificial intelligence (AI) approximate what experience provides—a concrete sense of what an unfamiliar future would mean for a person’s own life? Existing approaches face a tradeoff. Educational interventions explain why a risk matters; perspective-taking asks people to consider unfamiliar experiences; guided simulation asks people to imagine possible futures; and narratives can make imagined events resemble direct experience while reducing argument against the message [12–17]. But scalable interventions generally present everyone with similar material, while tailored interventions are generally difficult to scale. A fixed story can show what someone might experience. Yet it cannot show how the event would unfold in your job, your family, or your commute. We developed the As-If Machine (AIM) to overcome this tradeoff. AIM is a multi-agent system through which people co-write personalized counterfactual narratives—stories about their own lives under possible policy futures. We designed AIM around principles that support “narrative transportation” (immersion in a story) [18]. These principles include: personal relevance, plausible circumstances, mental imagery, and coherent causal sequences. AIM uses information about a participant’s circumstances to generate both how the event might begin for a person and how its consequences might unfold in that person’s life. These outputs provide context that feeds into a large language model (LLM) which offers short continuations as the participant writes. Participants contribute their own text and can accept or reject each AI suggestion. Personalization therefore continues through the writing itself, since participants bring their own responses to the imagined situation. Most AIM participants (75%) reported that these suggestions were helpful for continuing the story. Our linguistic analysis reveals distinct roles: AIM supplies causal structure and concrete detail, while participants supply first-person perspective and emotion (section 8). Making a consequence feel close, however, is not the same as changing what someone thinks should be done about it. Experimental attempts to reduce psychological distance have produced inconsistent downstream effects on attitudes and behavior [19–21]. We therefore separate two questions. Can personalized simulation make an unfamiliar consequence feel concrete and personally relevant? And when does that change extend to policy attitudes? We evaluated AIM in two pre-registered randomized experiments on science funding and satellite cybersecurity. In each study, we compared AIM with (1) an expert-written narrative on the same issue and (2) the same interactive writing system applied to an unrelated topic. These comparisons allow us to benchmark AIM’s effects against both conventional narrative persuasion and interactive writing in general. We measured psychological closeness and policy attitudes, and analyzed effects by prior issue familiarity. AIM increased psychological closeness in both studies by nearly identical margins. Its effects on policy attitudes diverged: attitudes changed substantially for satellite cybersecurity, weakly for science funding, and more among participants with less prior familiarity. Across 23,496 human–AI autocomplete interactions, AIM supplied concrete detail while participants supplied perspective and emotion. The results show that AI can help people construct experiences they have never lived and reliably make 2distant risks feel closer. ## 2 System description: An open-source engine of interventions 

AIM is a web application with four specialized LLM agents (Figure 1). The Initializer generates a plausible scenario onset from the participant’s background and circumstances. The Predictor forecasts its consequences for that participant, and the Storyteller connects these events into a narrative arc. The Autocompleter offers short suggestions as the participant writes, maintaining continuity with the scenario, and participants can accept, reject, or modify them. The architecture embodies principles of narrative transportation [18], such as connecting the story to the reader’s life, beginning plausibly, and unfolding through a coherent causal sequence (Extended Data Table 7 maps the principles onto AIM). By separating these functions across agents, each stage produces reusable output. The Initializer’s personalized onset can serve as the participant’s starting prompt. The Predictor’s consequences combine with that Initializer’s onset to guide the Writer’s plot—which then provides context for the Autocompleter. 43 Study 1: Science funding 

We conducted a pre-registered three-arm experiment on science funding. Most Americans support science [23] in the abstract, but the consequences of funding cuts are not immediately visible. We focused on a concrete scenario: a participant’s specialist appointment was 14 months away because of reductions in National Institutes of Health (NIH) T32 training grants, which train specialists. We recruited adults through Prolific and quota-matched the sample on age, political party, and sex. We randomly assigned participants to AIM ( n = 333), an expert vignette ( n = 331), or an interactive placebo ( n = 343), yielding a final sample of N = 1 ,007 (see SI subsection 14.4 for descriptives by arm). AIM participants spent approximately six minutes co-writing a personalized account of delayed care. Vignette participants read a domain-relevant story about a girl whose cancer was cured through CAR-T immunotherapy, designed to encourage support for science and used in prior research [24]. Placebo participants used AIM to write about meeting a celebrity or sounds turning into colors. The vignette controls for demand effects, and the placebo preserves the co-writing format without policy content. As pre-registered, we measured psychological closeness and policy attitudes as z-scored indices of two items each (Methods). The main pre-registered analyses shown in Figure 2 use HC1 robust standard errors and correct for multiple comparisons within each study with sharpened FDR q values [22]. We pre-registered four hypotheses and accompanying analyses: (H1) AIM would increase psychological closeness relative to both controls; (H2) AIM would increase support for science funding relative to both controls; (H3) closeness would mediate AIM’s effect on attitudes; and (H4) AIM’s effects would be larger among participants with lower prior familiarity (thresholds of ≤ 2 and ≤ 3 on the seven-point scale). 

3.1 Main effects 

Closeness. AIM increased psychological closeness relative to both controls (Figure 2). Relative to the vignette, the difference was β = 0 .36 SDs, 95% CI [0 .22 , 0.49], t = 5 .27, sharpened q ≤ .001; relative to the placebo, it was β = 0 .80 SDs, 95% CI [0 .66 , 0.94], t = 10 .96, sharpened q ≤ .001. 

Attitudes. Attitude effects were smaller. Relative to the vignette, the difference was β = 0 .16 SDs, 95% CI [0 .00 , 0.32], t = 2 .00, sharpened q ≤ .034; relative to the placebo, it was β = 0 .10 SDs, 95% CI [−0.05 , 0.24], t = 1 .31, sharpened q ≤ .071. AIM increased policy support relative to the vignette; the placebo contrast was in the hypothesized direction but not significant. Extended Data Tables 1 and 2 report item-level descriptive statistics and regression estimates. 

3.2 Mediation 

The pre-registered mediation analysis yielded positive indirect effects of AIM on attitudes through closeness, against both the vignette ( b = 0 .17, 95% CI [0 .10 , 0.25]) and the placebo ( b = 0 .33, 95% CI [0 .25 , 0.42]). Because we did not randomize closeness, these estimates do not identify causal mediation 5(Extended Data Table 5). However, we tested the indirect effects’ sensitivity to an unmeasured factor affecting both closeness and attitudes [25]. Both remained positive unless such a confounder induced a correlation of approximately ρ⋆ = 0 .40 between the unexplained components of closeness and attitudes (Supplementary Fig. S21). 

3.3 Familiarity 

Across all four pre-registered contrasts (vignette and placebo, attitudes and closeness), AIM produced larger estimated effects among participants less familiar with science funding (Figure 2). At the pre-registered cutoff of 2 of 7 (on a question asking about self-reported familiarity with science funding), 488 participants were in the low-familiarity group and 519 were in the high-familiarity group. Among low-familiarity participants, AIM increased closeness relative to the vignette ( β = 0 .55 SDs, t = 5 .20, sharpened q ≤ .001) and placebo ( β = 1 .09 SDs, t = 9 .76, sharpened q ≤ .001), and attitudes relative to the vignette ( β = 0 .32 SDs, t = 2 .96, sharpened q = .004) and placebo ( β = 0 .21 SDs, t = 1 .99, sharpened q = .034). Among higher-familiarity participants, closeness effects were smaller ( β = 0 .16 SDs against the vignette and 0 .52 against the placebo) and attitude effects were near zero ( β = −0.004 and −0.01 SDs, sharpened q = .298). A cutoff of 3 produced a similar but weaker pattern (Extended Data Tables 3 and 4). 

3.4 Heterogeneous treatment effects 

To examine familiarity continuously and explore other moderators, we fit pre-registered causal random forests [26, 27] and summarized each conditional average treatment effect (CATE) function by its best linear projection (BLP) onto the moderator set [28]. Compared to the placebo, higher familiarity (B = −0.36, p < . 001, q ≤ .001), older age ( B = −0.21, p = .010, q = .084), and greater personal distress ( B = −0.18, p = .018, q = .099) predicted smaller closeness effects; older age also predicted a smaller attitude effect ( B = −0.18, p = .039, q = .913). Compared to the vignette, higher income predicted a smaller closeness effect ( B = −0.15, p = .030, q = .451), and greater mental imagery predicted a larger attitude effect ( B = 0 .17, p = .043, q = .674). Only the familiarity effect survived correction for multiple comparisons within each forest. SI subsection 14.14 reports all coefficients, BLP plots, and CATE/LOESS panels. 

## 4 Study 2: Satellite cybersecurity 

To test robustness, we ran a follow-up experiment on satellite cybersecurity with the same co-writing procedure and control conditions, though see SI subsection 14.5 for other changes between studies. We expected larger attitude effects in satellite cybersecurity, a less polarizing domain (Discussion). AIM participants wrote about a satellite cyberattack disrupting everyday life, with effects based on [29]. Vignette participants read a NATO workshop scenario [30] describing the everyday consequences of a hypothetical satellite cyberattack. Placebo participants used AIM to write about the same unrelated scenarios as in Study 1. The final sample contained N = 894 participants: 298 in AIM, 303 in the vignette condition, and 293 in the placebo condition (see SI subsection 14.4 for descriptives by arm). 6We changed the outcome batteries in Study 2. Because Study 1’s two attitude items moved weakly, we could not tell whether AIM had little effect or affected an unmeasured dimension. We therefore used seven 0–100 sliders, chosen to reduce ceiling effects, spanning three subscales—importance of satellite cybersecurity, spending priorities, and willingness to oppose funding cuts. As pre-registered, a three-factor model fit better than a one-factor model (CFI = .973 versus .775; TLI = .948 versus .662; Supplementary Table S18). The pre-registered primary attitude index z-scored and equally weighted the three subscales. Closeness was a z-scored index of four 1–9 items (Methods). The pre-registered hypotheses and analyses follow Study 1. Extended Data Tables 1 and 2 report item-level descriptive statistics and regression estimates. 

4.1 Main effects 

Closeness. AIM increased psychological closeness relative to both controls (Figure 2). Relative to the vignette, the difference was β = 0 .38 SDs, 95% CI [0 .24 , 0.51], t = 5 .47, sharpened q ≤ .001; relative to the placebo, it was β = 0 .90 SDs, 95% CI [0 .74 , 1.05], t = 11 .55, sharpened q ≤ .001. 

Attitudes. AIM increased policy support relative to both controls. Relative to the vignette, the difference was β = 0 .54 SDs, 95% CI [0 .39 , 0.69], t = 7 .06, sharpened q ≤ .001; relative to the placebo, it was β = 0 .67 SDs, 95% CI [0 .52 , 0.82], t = 8 .75, sharpened q ≤ .001. Unlike Study 1, AIM produced substantial attitude effects. Attitude effects were strong across all three sub-scales. Relative to the vignette, AIM increased importance, spending, and action by 0 .50, 0 .51, and 0 .41 SDs, respectively. Relative to the placebo, it increased them by 0 .60, 0 .56, and 0 .56 SDs (Extended Data Table 6). 

4.2 Mediation 

The pre-registered mediation analysis again yielded positive indirect effects through closeness: b = 0 .24, 95% CI [0 .15 , 0.34] against the vignette ( n = 601) and b = 0 .54, 95% CI [0 .43 , 0.66] against the placebo (n = 591; Extended Data Table 5). The sensitivity thresholds were higher than in Study 1 ( ρ⋆ = 0 .56 and 0 .62; Supplementary Fig. S21). 

4.3 Familiarity 

At the pre-registered cutoff of 2, 425 participants reported low familiarity and 469 reported higher familiarity. All eight subgroup contrasts had confidence intervals above zero. As in Study 1, lower-familiarity participants had larger estimated effects across all four outcome-control (placebo and vignette, attitudes and closeness) combinations. However, at p < 0.05, the pre-registered moderation tests supported larger effects only in the placebo comparisons. The cutoff of 3 produced a similar but weaker pattern (Extended Data Tables 3 and 4). 74.4 Heterogeneous treatment effects 

Using the same pre-registered causal-forest analysis, we found limited heterogeneity in Study 2. Supplementary Information 14.14 reports BLP estimates and LOESS plots. Only the familiarity effect survived correction for multiple comparisons. Compared to the placebo, higher familiarity ( B = −0.27, 

p = .001, q = .021) and older age ( B = −0.21, p = .015, q = .134) predicted smaller closeness effects. Compared to the vignette, greater personal distress predicted a larger closeness effect ( B = 0 .18,

## 7 Qualitative feedback reveals success modes and failure modes 

We asked AIM participants what worked and what did not. We then identified themes using BERTopic (Methods), an unsupervised method that clusters texts using neural embeddings. We excluded clusters containing low-quality answers (e.g., restatements of the question). Percentages use responses in clusters we classified as substantive. Supplementary Tables S15 and S16 report every retained theme and a representative quotation. 

Personal relevance. Participants most often credited AIM with connecting an abstract issue to their own lives. Making science funding personal was the largest Study 1 success theme (30 .1% of success responses): “Most of the time, this sort of funding feels entirely separated from my day to day life. Creating a story about something that I could easily relate to was effective.” In Study 2, the largest success theme was recognizing the scale of disruption (31 .4%): “It made me realize how big a threat this is to my everyday life.” These accounts fit the strong effects on closeness. 

Hidden dependencies. The satellite scenario helped participants see which services depend on 10 satellites, revealing hidden dependencies. Three Study 2 themes centered on everyday dependencies, personal reliance, or unnoticed vulnerability (34 .7% of success responses). About a third of those responses concerned reliance on card and phone payments rather than cash. One participant wrote, “I didn’t think I did, but as I started navigating a night in my life, I realized that a lot of what I use works on these systems.” 

Managing over-steering. Participants valued suggestions that completed a sentence as they intended. In Study 1, 6 .7% of success responses praised AIM’s predictive ability (one participant said AIM “seemed to read my mind!”), and some said it helped them “continue the narrative when I didn’t have something relevant to add.” But unsuitable suggestions were also the largest complaint in Study 1 (34 .6% of failure responses) and the second largest in Study 2 (30 .8%). They broke the writer’s flow or repeated the scenario. In Study 1, this appeared related to “over-steering”: We wanted the AI to tie the story back to the scenario, but this led some participants to feel the AI was too repetitive, restating the prompt and the scenario. We revised the autocomplete prompt for Study 2 to reduce repetition (SI subsection 14.5). Two Study 2 participants also reported “uncanny personalization”: suggestions that added details that were not true, such as a call to an aunt who had died. 

Specificity-credibility tradeoff. Concrete details helped some participants picture the consequences and led others to doubt them. In Study 1, the 14-month doctor wait was both the second most common success theme and the second most common failure theme: “The wait time seemed exceptionally long in relation with the ‘problems’ I was having in the story.” In Study 2, implausibility was the largest failure theme (29 of 78 substantive negative responses, 37 .2%); the story was “a bit too doomsday to be believable.” Plausibility complaints were more common in Study 2, where attitude effects were larger, so they did not stop AIM from working. Because participants steer their own stories, a detail that is plausible in one narrative can become implausible in another. 

The personal crowding out the political. Personal relevance could also become a failure theme. In Study 1, 12 .8% of failure responses described concentrating on a personal predicament and attending less to science funding as a policy issue: “[Once] the story began developing, I was more focused on science funding in my own life than the overall funding of science.” A similar Study 2 theme (16 .7% of failure responses) said the story portrayed personal disruption without explaining how an attack caused it or how cybersecurity policy could prevent it. 

## 8 Consistent yet distinct roles of humans and AI 

Humans and AI played distinct roles, largely replicating across studies. We compared human-written text with accepted AI suggestions (Extended Data Figures 4 and 5). AIM participants received 23 ,496 suggestions across both studies and accepted 4 ,068. We analyzed language through word log-odds, Biber linguistic features [31], and Empath word categories [32] (similar to LIWC). Using all features, five-fold cross-validated classifiers on all 109 features distinguished the two sources with mean AUCs of .914 in science funding and .940 in satellite cybersecurity—suggesting these features capture differences between human and AI writing well. Humans and AI performed different narrative work (Extended Data Figure 4). AI supplied scenario structure and physical detail. Its most distinctive science-funding terms named the scenario— train ,11 fellows , NIH , and T32 —while its satellite terms established the setting: street , kitchen , and nearby .Participants supplied emotion and interior perspective. Among their twenty most distinctive science-funding terms were feel , know , really , think , and wonder . Satellite cybersecurity showed a similar pattern, with humans disproportionately using terms like think , worried , know , feel , and panic .Register features showed the same division (Extended Data Figure 5; dz is Cohen’s d of human–AI differences). The largest difference in both studies was humans’ greater use of first-person pronouns (dz = 0 .55 in science funding and 0 .72 in satellite cybersecurity). Nervousness (0 .38 and 0 .49) and negative emotion (0 .37 and 0 .46) ranked among the ten largest human-associated differences in both studies, and humans used more amplifiers such as “very” and “extremely” (0 .42 and 0 .31). AI writing was more structural: it used more nouns ( −0.69 and −0.72), more present participial clauses ( −0.70 and −0.77), and longer words ( −0.63 and −0.73). The system supplied scenario description, while participants supplied first-person perspective and emotion. This division replicated across domains: the 109 feature contrasts correlated r = .78 between studies, and 83 of 109 were in the same direction. It was also intentional (Extended Data Table 7): we wanted the Autocompleter to avoid being too sentimental, didactic, or prescriptive. 

## 9 Discussion 

Personalized simulation made two long-term risks feel close. Across two pre-registered experiments, AIM increased psychological closeness by 0 .36 and 0 .38 SDs relative to an expert-written vignette and by 0 .80 and 0 .90 SDs relative to writing about an unrelated topic. It also shifted policy attitudes: by 0 .16 SDs against the vignette and 0 .10 SDs against the placebo for science funding (the latter was not significant), and by 0 .54 and 0 .67 SDs, respectively, for satellite cybersecurity. The attitude effect was not as dependable. 

AIM revealed hidden dependencies. AIM helped participants project themselves into a specific future episode, a form of episodic future thinking [33, 34]. Its strong effects on psychological closeness and the qualitative responses are consistent with this interpretation. In Study 2, 23 .1% of success-theme responses described rehearsing what the participant would do, while 34 .7% described discovering everyday dependencies, personal reliance, or previously unnoticed vulnerability (Supplementary Ta-ble S16). By imagining a day under satellite disruption, participants recognized connections (among satellite infrastructure, payments, navigation, and communication) that they had not considered. These simulations may be especially useful for risks whose consequences are hidden in ordinary life. Infrastructure has this property. It often goes unnoticed until it fails [35], yet its failures can cascade. Other risks may have this same “infrastructural” quality. Democratic norms, for example, can function as social infrastructure: unnoticed while intact, but consequential when they fail. Because many societal challenges have non-obvious individual consequences, AIM likely has applications beyond the domains studied here. 

Humans and AI contributed different parts of the simulation. AIM supplied scenario structure and physical detail, while participants supplied first-person perspective and emotion (Extended Data 12 Figures 4 and 5). This division replicated across domains: The 109 human–AI linguistic contrasts correlated r = .78 between studies. AIM did not write a personalized story on the participant’s behalf. It supplied a scaffold that participants developed. For designers, this suggests that the value of generative AI co-writing may lie less in producing complete narratives than in providing enough structure for people to imagine an episode on their own terms. 

Closeness may change attitudes most when those attitudes are less established. Closeness effects were nearly identical across studies, but attitude effects were larger in Study 2. One possibility is that science-funding attitudes were more crystallized. SI subsection 14.6 gives more details and SI subsection 14.7 discusses alternative explanations we ruled out. Each SD of closeness predicted a larger difference in satellite attitudes ( b = 0 .62) than in science-funding attitudes ( b = 0 .41; interaction 

b = 0 .21, 95% CI [0 .13 , 0.29]). The interaction held among placebo participants, who did the same task in both studies without issue-relevant information ( b = 0 .16, 95% CI [0 .05 , 0.28]). Three other indicators agree with the crystallization account. First, science funding received about 30 times as much news coverage in the preceding two years (Media Cloud [36]). Second, ideology explained about six times as much variance in control participants’ science-funding attitudes ( R2 = 3 .9%) as in satellite attitudes (R2 = 0 .7%). Third, participants’ open-ended feedback referred to prior views more often in Study 1 than in Study 2 (13 .7% versus 6 .5% of coded open-ended responses; z = 4 .21, p < . 001), including among vignette participants (14 .5% versus 6 .3%). See SI section 14.6 for details and validation. By contrast, interface changes, sample composition, story–item similarity, and ceiling effects did not appear to account for the gap (SI subsection 14.7). Differing levels of emotion of the vignette explain at most a fifth of the vignette contrast. Together, the evidence is consistent with closeness changing attitudes more when those attitudes are less crystallized. But we did not manipulate crystallization, and other study differences could explain the divergence (SI subsection 14.5)—though we find less evidence for these explanations. 

AIM did not require favorable attitudes toward AI or a sense of control. Prior work identifies two factors that could shape the efficacy of AI-assisted interventions: people’s attitudes toward algorithms [37] and their sense of ownership over co-written text [38]. Neither explained our results. AI attitudes did not moderate AIM’s effect on either outcome in either study (Figure 4C), and effects held among participants below the scale midpoint (Supplementary Table S13). Perceived agency was unrelated to attitudes ( r = .04, p = .28) and weakly related to closeness ( r = .10, p = .010). 

Specificity made scenarios vivid, but also contestable. Perceived realism can increase narrative transportation [16], which would suggest using specific details to make scenarios concrete. However, specificity can backfire when the specificity is deemed non-credible. In Study 1, the fixed 14-month wait helped participants imagine the consequence. But it also gave participants a specific claim to reject. One participant said, “14 months for a wait time across all places seemed unrealistic, no matter the scenario, especially in life or death situations like cancer.” Others accepted that specialist waits can be long but rejected the idea that science-funding was the cause. Study 2 produced a different problem: Some participants rejected the scale of the entire event rather than any particular detail. Overall, specifics can increase concreteness but also contestability of simulated counterfactuals. Moreover, in a 13 co-writing system, the participant can also direct the narrative toward contexts in which a previously plausible detail no longer fits. More specificity is not always more credible. 

The personal could overshadow the political. Framing research distinguishes episodic accounts of individual cases from thematic accounts of broader patterns [39, 40]. AIM is deliberately episodic, which made consequences concrete but sometimes left institutions and policy causes abstract. In Study 1, 13% of failure-theme responses described focusing on a personal predicament rather than science funding as policy. Prior research finds overly episodic framing may not change attitudes toward systems [39], and narratives change social attitudes best when they remain connected to credible and typical causes [41]. For AIM scenarios, the personal must therefore remain connected to the political, in a causally believable way. 

Misuse potential. AI can be persuasive, especially when it personalizes content [42]. AIM could therefore make real or manufactured risks feel personally relevant to a targeted audience, regardless of accuracy. Still, AIM likely poses less risk than “passive exposure” [43], in which people see AI-generated content without any role in its creation. AIM’s architecture requires active engagement: participants write their own stories and decide whether to accept each suggestion. Our deployment added a second safeguard by disclosing the general scenario beforehand, unlike work in which surreptitiously biased suggestions shifted attitudes [44]. A deployer, however, could omit this disclosure, and the deployer also controls the scenario and how strongly the agents steer. Active engagement therefore reduces the risk of manipulation but does not remove it. Generative AI is dual use: Chatbots can both reduce [45] and increase [46] belief in conspiracy theories. 

Limitations and future work. We measured self-reported outcomes immediately after the task. We measured the mediator and outcome together, so our results are consistent with an indirect effect through closeness without identifying it. We evaluated AIM as an assembled intervention and did not isolate personalization, the starting scenario, or co-writing. AIM’s personalization means the treatment adds variance compared to a fixed script. Each experiment compared AIM to one specific vignette. Our Prolific samples may be more comfortable with AI-assisted writing than other populations. Both scenarios were negative futures. Future studies can ablate components (e.g., “turn off” AI suggestions while keeping personalization), measure attitudes longitudinally, and test positive futures. 

Conclusion. We cannot personally experience every consequence that should inform what we support. To address this fundamental problem, we created the As-If Machine. Its goal is to make distant risks feel closer. Across two pre-registered experiments, we find AIM did make distant risks feel closer. Whether that closeness changed what people supported depended on the domain. Generative AI may be able to approximate something experience ordinarily provides: a concrete sense of what alternative futures would mean, for an individual’s own life. 14 10 Methods 

10.1 Study design and preregistration 

We randomly assigned participants to AIM, an expert vignette, or an interactive placebo, targeting 300 participants per arm. Each registration specified 80% power to detect d = 0 .23 in a two-sided two-sample test at α = .05. We pre-registered the science-funding experiment at https://osf.io/j7dsy and the satellite experiment at https://osf.io/e967w . Both registrations specified hypotheses concerning closeness, attitudes, mediation, and familiarity, including familiarity cutoffs of ≤ 2 and ≤ 3 (SI subsection 14.1 for cutoff rationales). Study 2 additionally pre-registered familiarity interaction tests, a factor-model comparison, and secondary attitude-subscale analyses. Both registrations also specified exploratory causal forests. The quantile, linguistic, and pooled mechanism analyses were additional exploratory analyses. See SI 14.3 for pre-registration deviations. 

10.2 Procedure and participant protection 

The University of Michigan’s institutional review board determined that both experiments were exempt from ongoing oversight. Study 1 participants provided informed consent before beginning and received warnings about serious medical content in both the Prolific posting and the consent form. Eligibility required age 18 or older, at least 100 prior Prolific submissions, a 98% approval rating, and desktop or laptop use. Screening included a commitment to high-quality responses and attention and comprehension checks. Study 2 followed a similar procedure and pre-registered screening out Safari users because of interface errors. Vignette participants read their assigned story and answered comprehension questions. AIM and placebo participants read instructions for the writing interface and completed comprehension checks before providing personal information and co-writing (six minutes for Study 1, seven minutes for Study 2). They then returned to Qualtrics using a completion code and answered outcome, experience, and demographic questions. The vignette provided domain-relevant information, and the placebo used the same co-writing interface for an unrelated event. In both experiments, the placebo condition was cross-randomized to use AIM for either (1) meeting a celebrity or (2) having sounds turn into colors since we anticipated these were plausibly unrelated to science funding or satellite cybersecurity. 

10.3 System implementation 

In the most current version of the system, each agent calls GPT-4.1-mini through an API using prompts stored in a YAML file. The Autocompleter issues an AJAX request only after the participant has paused mid-sentence for 300 ms, written at least one complete sentence, and typed at least one character since the last accepted suggestion. For each request, the system samples temperature uniformly from [0 .5, 1.2] and token length from [10 , 30] to produce short, varied completions. Extended Data Figure 3 shows an example request and identifies the source of each prompt element. 

10.4 Formative development 

Before the randomized experiments, we refined AIM through small-scale, informal user testing (e.g., with research assistants). This testing revealed two problems that informed the current design. 15 First, in early versions of the system, users objected when the AI attributed interiority to them. For example, a user would type “I was. . . ” and the AI would complete the sentence with an emotional state the user did not feel. Because this feedback was common, we constrained the Autocompleter to continue users’ stories rather than describe what users thought or felt (Extended Data Table 7; Extended Data Figure 3). The linguistic analysis is consistent with these constraints: participants supplied emotion and first-person perspective, while the AI supplied concrete detail and scenario structure (section 8). Second, users needed a starting point, for two reasons. First, users found it difficult to imagine how the scenario could plausibly begin, so a personalized onset could serve as a plausible entry into the scenario. Second, the task is a creative writing exercise, and many people do not write creatively. Given only an abstract scenario, users asked what exactly they should write about. We therefore display the agent-generated onset, built from each user’s intake responses, at the top of the editor (Figure 1a), so users continue an existing story rather than start from a blank page. Participants rated this prompt as helpful for starting the story (Figure 4E). 

10.5 Outcome measures 

Science funding: closeness. Two items assessed closeness to the issue: “I can clearly imagine how cuts to scientific funding could affect me, personally” and “Levels of scientific funding feel relevant to my life” (both 1–9). We coded items such that higher values mean more psychological closeness (i.e., less psychological distance) and averaged them into a z-scored index. 

Science funding: attitudes. Two items assessed attitudes toward science funding: perceived personal benefit of U.S. science over the past decade [1–5; adapted from 24] and preferred NIH share of federal spending (0–100%, similar to [47]). We averaged items into a z-scored index. 

Satellite cybersecurity: closeness. Four items measured whether a deliberate satellite attack felt abstract or concrete, whether participants could see effects on their daily lives, whether they could imagine its effects, and whether satellite cybersecurity felt relevant to their lives (1–9). 

Satellite cybersecurity: attitudes. Seven attitude items (split into three sub-scales) used 0–100 sliders. Importance comprised personal and comparative national importance. Spending comprised the preferred direction of satellite cybersecurity spending and the priority of cybersecurity grants to state and local governments. Action comprised intentions to vote against a candidate or ballot measure that reduced satellite cybersecurity funding and to oppose a politician proposing such cuts. We standardized items within each study, averaged them, and standardized the resulting indices, so treatment contrasts are in standard-deviation units. For satellite attitudes, we first constructed the three standardized subscales, then averaged and re-standardized them with equal subscale weights. The three-item action subscale therefore received the same weight as each two-item subscale. All analyzed participants had complete main outcome indices. We coded higher scores as greater closeness or support, following the item wording. For the pre-registered factor comparison, we fit one-factor and correlated three-factor models to the seven standardized attitude items using semopy’s maximum-likelihood discrepancy objective. We retained the pre-registered equal-subscale index rather than estimating scoring weights from the fitted model. Supplementary Table S18 reports the fit statistics. 16 10.6 Pretreatment and posttreatment measures 

Pretreatment. Before treatment, we measured familiarity with the study topic (1–7), creative writing and journaling experience, vividness of mental imagery [5 items; 48], fantasy, perspective taking, and personal distress (using the 12-item Brief Interpersonal Reactivity Index) [49], and AI attitudes [AIAS-4; 50]. 

Posttreatment. In addition to closeness and attitudes, we asked several other questions. For treatment and placebo participants, we assessed system usability and helpfulness with several items. We measured ease of use with a single item from the System Usability Scale [51] — “I thought the system was easy to use” — which accounts for 90% of the full scale’s variance [52]. We measured enjoyment with “I thought the system was fun to use.” Two items captured helpfulness of system components: “The writing assistant suggestions helped me continue the story” and “The prompt at the top of the page helped me start the story.” Two items captured perceived story quality: personalization (“The story I wrote felt personalized to aspects of my life”) and realism (“ Assuming the scenario were to happen to me , it would play out like in the story I wrote”). Finally, we measured perceived agency with “Who did you feel was in control of the story you wrote?” ( −3 = writing assistant had total control; +3 = I had total control). We measured narrative transportation using four post-task narrative-transportation items from the short-form narrative transportation scale [53] (SI subsection 14.2 for details) assessing picturing oneself in the scenario, mental involvement, wanting to learn more, and emotional impact. Additionally, for both the vignette and treatment participants, we asked what about the activity was (1) effective and (2) ineffective for changing attitudes or making the topic feel more relevant. At the end of these questions, we asked about basic demographics. 

10.7 Exclusions 

Our pre-registered screening criteria excluded participants who failed attention checks, failed compre-hension checks, completed the study faster than three scaled median absolute deviations below their condition’s median, or reported technical errors (AIM and placebo arms, which used the writing app). All participants had completed at least 100 prior studies with a 98% approval rating. We quota-sampled participants to match the U.S. population on sex, political party (Democrat, Republican, independent), and age (18–34, 35–54, and 55+ in Study 1; 18–54 and 55 or older in Study 2). See the Supplementary Information for details.

We also formally tested whether these cutpoints moderated the effect by interacting treatment-arm indicators with a dummy variable for IsHighFamiliarity. 17 10.9 Mediation and exploratory models 

We used Python’s pingouin mediation package with bootstrap CIs for our simple pre-registered mediation model where the treatment’s effect on attitudes is mediated via closeness only. We computed sensitivity using the R function medsens from mediation . Our more complex mediation model in Figure 4 (serial multiple-mediator model in which closeness predicts transportation, and both predict attitudes) was fit in the lavaan R package using bootstrap CIs and entering study number as a covariate. We constructed the adoption index by taking an inverse-covariance weighted [22] index of acceptance rate, number of accepted suggestions, and proportion of AI-origin words; this results in an index of mean 0 and SD 1. 

10.10 Causal forests and treatment-effect heterogeneity 

We fit separate causal forests for each outcome and AIM–control comparison using the R package grf 

2.6.1, with 2,000 honest trees and automatic tuning of all supported hyperparameters [26, 27]. Honesty uses separate observations to choose splits and estimate effects within leaves. We standardized continu-ous moderators. We summarized heterogeneity with best linear projection (BLP) coefficients estimated from doubly robust scores rather than by regressing fitted CATEs directly on the moderators [28, 54]. We used HC3 standard errors. We visualized out-of-bag CATE predictions with LOESS smooths. The Supplementary Information presents all CATE panels and BLP estimates, along with sharpened q values that correct for multiple hypotheses within each forest. 

10.11 Qualitative and linguistic analysis 

We asked treatment participants which aspects of the co-writing experience were effective and ineffective. We identified themes using BERTopic 2—a transformer-based topic modeling pipeline. The pipeline embeds documents, reduces dimensionality, and then clusters resultant document vectors. We embedded responses with hkunlp/instructor-large [55], reduced dimensionality with UMAP [56], and clustered with HDBSCAN [57] (which automatically picks the number of clusters). We excluded filler clusters (e.g., “nothing was ineffective”), and an author manually interpreted the remaining clusters. Table S15 reports the substantive themes with representative quotes. We compared human-written text with accepted AI suggestions using identical lexical and linguistic analyses in both studies. First, we fit a log-odds model with a Dirichlet prior to identify tokens associated with each source. Second, we extracted Biber’s 67 lexico-grammatical register features [31] with PyBiber 0.3.1, which characterize text type and have been used in human–AI comparisons [58]. We also extracted 42 Empath semantic category scores [32]. Because each participant contributed one human and one AI document, we measured each feature’s difference with the paired Cohen’s dz : the mean of the within-participant human-minus-AI differences divided by their standard deviation. The figures rank features by dz . We retained a human–AI pair only when both halves exceeded five words, so that neither side was too short to characterize. This yielded 285 pairs in Study 1 and 259 in Study 2, each with 67 Biber features and 42 Empath categories per document. To test whether these features distinguished human from AI text, we trained an L2-regularized logistic regression classifier ( C = 0 .5; maximum 1,000 iterations). We used 5-fold cross-validation (GroupKFold), keeping each participant’s paired documents in the same fold. Supplementary Table S17 reports mean

34 13.8 Narrative Transportation Practices 

Extended Data Table 7 shows how AIM translates each principle into system components.  

> Extended Data Table 7: Embedding narrative transportation best practices [18] into the system.

Best Practices for Narrative Transportation Our System 

Connect the character to the user’s life AIM uses participants’ intake responses to personalize the character and generate a realistic scenario onset grounded in their circumstances. Use material familiar to the reader Intake questions capture participants’ routines and daily lives, giving the agents familiar material to incorporate into each story. Cue “remindings” of the reader’s own experiences Intake questions ask participants to describe their own lives, and the scenario is constructed from these responses. Use rich mental imagery Agent prompts instruct AIM to describe concrete surroundings, actions, and consequences. Maintain psychological realism The Initializer and Predictor Agents are instructed to be “very specific and very realistic” and not to exaggerate, while the scenario onset is constrained to follow from the participant’s stated circumstances. Keep stories cohesive The agent pipeline builds each plot from a scenario onset and its predicted effects, preserving continuity across the narrative. Build a clear cause-and-effect structure The Predictor and Storyteller Agents construct a plot that connects the scenario to its consequences rather than presenting isolated effects. Keep the persuasive message central to the plot The Storyteller Agent conditions the narrative arc on the Predictor’s forecast effects, so the scenario’s consequences drive the plot rather than appearing as commentary on it. Show rather than tell Agent prompts instruct AIM to convey effects through events and actions rather than stating the persuasive message directly. Let the reader supply the emotion Autocompleter constraints direct the model to let emotion be inferred from actions and events rather than stated outright and to avoid sentimentality. 14.1 Cutoff rationale 

We pre-registered two cutoffs for low familiarity, ≤ 2 and ≤ 3 on the 1–7 familiarity scale, for three reasons. First, we planned to run multiple studies, so we wanted the same cutoffs on the same 1–7 scale in every study rather than cutoffs chosen for a single dataset. We pre-registered identical cutoffs for Study 1 and Study 2. Second, 2 and 3 were a priori reasonable. A cutoff of ≤ 1 risks leaving too few participants in the low-familiarity group, and a cutoff of ≤ 4 places participants at the scale midpoint in the low-familiarity group. That leaves ≤ 2 and ≤ 3, so we pre-registered both as sensitivity checks. Both cutoffs approximate common conventions: ≤ 3 is a midpoint split (all responses below 4), and 

≤ 2 approximates the bottom third of the scale. We did not exclude middle responses, so that every participant entered the analysis. Third, a small pilot on science funding suggested that a cutoff of 3 would leave enough participants in the low-familiarity group. Note that we also pre-registered a test of familiarity that does not depend on any cutoff. The causal forests treat familiarity as continuous, and we summarize its moderation with the best linear projection (Methods, subsection 10.10). 

14.2 Narrative Transportation Items 

We adapted the short-form narrative transportation scale [53] to our setup. Participants rated the extent to which four statements applied to them (1 = not at all, 7 = very much): “I could picture myself in the scene of the events described in the narrative,” “I was mentally involved in the narrative while writing it,” “I wanted to learn how the narrative progressed,” and “The narrative affected me emotionally.” We made three changes to the original scale. First, AIM and placebo participants saw “writing” in the second item, while vignette participants saw the original “reading.” Second, we replaced “ended” with “progressed” in the third item. Participants wrote their own stories, so the stories had no fixed ending, but AIM’s suggestions could still introduce events participants did not plan. Third, we omitted the scale’s two items about named characters, since personalized stories share no common characters. We averaged the four items into a transportation index. 

14.3 Screening procedure, deviations, complications 

Exclusion criteria. As pre-registered, across both studies we excluded participants who reported serious technical errors, failed the commitment check or attention check, used a mobile device, or completed the study unusually quickly. Serious errors included never receiving an autocomplete, encountering an app error message, or being unable to type; these appeared as predefined response options for participants who reported experiencing a problem. We defined unusually short completion as a duration below the condition median minus three scaled median absolute deviations, using a scaling factor of 1.4826. We also required completion of a pre-task CAPTCHA. We pre-registered a minimum sample size of 900 and recruited additional participants to accommodate anticipated exclusions. Study 2 included several additional pre-registered criteria. Participants had to meet a minimum writing threshold before proceeding beyond the writing task. Post-task exclusions additionally covered Safari use and reCAPTCHA scores below 0.5 when available. 37 Deviations. In Study 1, participants who reported a technical problem could select from predefined non-serious and serious errors or describe an unlisted error in an “Other” field. We pre-registered manually classifying these custom reports as serious or non-serious. We skipped this review to save time, preserve power, and avoid subjective classifications that would be hard to apply consistently in follow-up studies, so we excluded only participants who selected a predefined serious error. A survey programming error had two consequences. First, we applied the first attention-check exclusion after data collection rather than screening participants out immediately. Second, although both studies required passing a CAPTCHA to advance, we did not record Qualtrics’s continuous reCAPTCHA 

scores in Study 1 or for most of Study 2. We therefore applied the reCAPTCHA-score exclusion only to the subset of Study 2 participants for whom scores were recorded. Unlike the pre-task CAPTCHA itself, the reCAPTCHA score did not determine study entry; it provided a continuous estimate of the likelihood that a respondent was a bot. In Study 2, four participants messaged us on Prolific to report significant errors, and we excluded them as well. Finally, the pre-registration discussed pooling Studies 1 and 2 in one causal random forest. We did not pool them because the moderator coefficients correlated only weakly and inconsistently across the two studies ( r ranged from −0.41 to 0 .54 across the four forests; Supplementary Information 14.15), so a pooled forest would mislead. 

Record linkage in Study 1. Participants entered their Prolific ID in Qualtrics, completed the app intake form (which included again entering their Prolific ID) and writing task, and then returned to Qualtrics. We linked app and survey records using Prolific IDs, assuming participants would enter the same Prolific ID for both the survey and app. However, IDs failed to match for 27 participants in the AIM and placebo conditions. We recovered 22 records by comparing the stories participants copied into Qualtrics, a procedure included prospectively to resolve matching errors. Of the remaining five, we recovered one through manual verification. We retained these participants without matching journal entries in the main analysis but excluded them from analyses requiring journal records (causal random forests, AI-vs-human language). 

Prolific supplementary data. Prolific lets researchers download quota-sampling variables that the survey did not ask. We asked age, sex, and ideology (1–7) in Qualtrics but took party ID from this supplementary data. Party ID was unavailable for 6 of 894 participants in Study 2 (0.7%) and 5 of 1,007 in Study 1 (0.5%). No analysis uses party ID (the causal forests use ideology), so this affects only the party composition in the descriptives. 

Study 2 interface. Early in Study 2 we recognized that the longer writing period we had adopted risked badly interacting with the timer’s position in the user interface: participants who wrote more may scroll the timer out of view. On 2026-08-22 we changed the timer to stay visible as participants scrolled. The 146 participants who began on 08-21 wrote under the original timer and the 748 who began later wrote under the revised one. 14.5 Differences between the two studies 

The two experiments shared the same system, three experimental arms, pretreatment measures, narrative-transportation items, and analysis plan. They differed in the following ways. 

Topic and scenario. Study 1 personalized a delay in medical care resulting from cuts to science funding. Study 2 personalized a deliberate attack on satellites that disrupted everyday infrastructure. 

Outcome measures. Study 1 measured closeness with two items and attitudes with two items using different response scales. Study 2 used four closeness items and seven attitude sliders organized into three subscales (Methods). We used sliders to reduce ceiling effects and multiple subscales to capture potentially distinct forms of attitude change. 

Timing. Study 1 was fielded in April 2026, and Study 2 in August 2026. 

User experience changes. In Study 2, we excluded Safari users because internal testing suggested poorer app performance in Safari, consistent with the higher error rate among Safari users in Study 1. We also increased the writing period from six to seven minutes. We also changed the timer so that it remained visible as participants scrolled through the writing interface rather than staying fixed at the top of the page (see SI Table S1). 

System changes. First, because OpenAI was deprecating GPT-4o, which we used in Study 1, we changed the primary autocomplete model in Study 2 to GPT-4.1-mini. Second, we increased the frequency penalty from 1 to 1.2 and added a presence penalty of 0.8. Both changes were based on feedback from Study 1 of repetition. Third, we changed the generated start of the event to be a minimum of 100 words rather than 30. Fourth, we changed the generated event plot from effects to be 100 words rather than 50. Fifth, we added a constraint to the autocomplete prompt to not be 40 so repetitive: “Never repeat or paraphrase phrases that already appear in the CONTEXT. Do not re-describe objects, scenes, or actions already mentioned. Each completion must introduce a new action, detail, or event that moves the story forward.”

Validation of the LLM coding 

We validated the LLM labels against two human raters. The raters first drafted the codebook together and calibrated it over three rounds before the final validation. 

Calibration. We gave the draft codebook to the LLM and had it code every response, which let us find positive cases for calibration. In each of three rounds, we drew a new set of 20 responses: 10 at random and 10 from responses the LLM had coded as crystallized, so that each set contained enough positive cases to discuss. The two raters coded each set independently, compared their labels, discussed each disagreement, and revised the codebook. After the third round, the raters no longer disagreed on how to apply the codebook, and we fixed it. Rater A then adapted the final codebook into the LLM prompt (Supplementary Figure S1), drawing on suggestions from an AI assistant to add instructions specific to LLM coding. We did not revise the codebook or the prompt after this point. None of the 60 calibration responses appears in the validation sample. 

Validation sample. We drew 200 new responses for validation. We drew 100 at random from all responses. To ensure enough positive cases, we stratified the other 100 on the labels from the draft prompt: 55 responses the draft prompt had coded as crystallized and 45 drawn at random from those it had coded as not crystallized. We shuffled these 100 together so that their position did not reveal how they were drawn. We enriched the sample because crystallized responses are rare: with the final prompt, the LLM coded 10.3% of all responses as crystallized, so a purely random sample of 200 would contain about 20 positive cases, too few to estimate agreement precisely. Each rater coded all 200 responses for prior familiarity and reinforcement, and we counted a response as crystallized if the rater coded either type. The raters coded independently, did not see the LLM labels, and did not discuss responses during coding. 

Agreement. Supplementary Table S7 reports Cohen’s κ for each pair of raters. On all 200 responses, the two human raters agreed with each other ( κ = 0 .83, 95% CI [0 .74 , 0.90]) about as well as the LLM agreed with each of them ( κ = 0 .91, 95% CI [0 .83 , 0.96] with Rater A; κ = 0 .80, 95% CI [0 .71 , 0.89] with Rater B). The LLM agreed more closely with Rater A, who adapted the codebook into the LLM prompt. 44 Because we selected the stratified cases on an LLM label, which can inflate agreement with the LLM, we also report agreement on the 100 responses drawn at random from all responses, whose class distribution matches the full data. On the 100 random responses, agreement was lower: κ = 0 .80, 95% CI [0 .56 , 0.96] between the human raters, and κ = 0 .71, 95% CI [0 .39 , 0.93] and κ = 0 .66, 95% CI [0 .39 , 0.86] between the LLM and Raters A and B. Raw agreement remained between 93% and 96%. Cohen’s κ falls when one category is rare, and this subset contained only 9 to 13 positive cases per rater, so its intervals are wide and overlap with those on the full sample.

14.9 Mediation Model Selection 

Our model selection procedure—deriving a mediation model that describes how treatment relates to downstream attitudes—combined prior work with consistency with the data. Because we measured transportation, closeness, and attitudes at the same time, we treat our mediation model as a descriptive decomposition, not a causal one. Unlike the simple closeness-only model, which shows results against both the placebo and the vignette control, for these dual-mediator models we present only the vignette contrast. This is because when placebo participants rated narrative transportation, it was for a topic (meeting a celebrity or having sounds turn into colors) unrelated to the closeness and attitude items, so its relation to downstream attitudes is not as meaningful. That is: T reatment − > T ransportation − > Attitudes for placebo participants would be exploring whether narrative transportation into a story unrelated to the topic we elicit attitudes about has effects on that topic. 

Serial mediation model 

Our primary specification is a serial multiple-mediator model. AIM affects closeness, closeness affects transportation, and both mediators predict attitudes. AIM can therefore reach attitudes through closeness alone, through transportation alone, or through closeness and then transportation. We chose this ordering for two reasons. First, prior studies that measured both psychological distance and narrative transportation also treated psychological distance as preceding transportation [59, 60]. Second, the data are consistent with this ordering and inconsistent with the reverse (next section). Because we measured both mediators concurrently, the ordering remains an assumption, and the model 49 is a descriptive decomposition rather than evidence that participants experienced closeness before transportation. 

Considering Directionality 

We can use the data to ask which ordering of closeness and transportation is more consistent with the observed associations. Let X be treatment, T be transportation, and C be closeness. We first consider which of two paths is more consistent with the data: 

Proposed Path 1 . X → T → C. Consider that if the true path is X → T → C, then the effect of X

on C should be mediated by T —meaning, after controlling for T , the effect of X on C should be close to zero. 

Proposed Path 2 . Conversely, if the true path is X → C → T , then the effect of X on T should be mediated by C: After controlling for C, the effect of X on T should be close to zero. Supplementary Table S14 reports the unadjusted and adjusted treatment coefficient for both proposed chains. Here we find that the coefficient of X on C is large, and controlling for T does not reduce it to zero. That is inconsistent with Path 1. However, the coefficient of X on T goes to zero once we include C. That is consistent with Path 2. Thus, the data are inconsistent with the path X → T → C

and consistent with the path X → C → T , the ordering of the serial model.
