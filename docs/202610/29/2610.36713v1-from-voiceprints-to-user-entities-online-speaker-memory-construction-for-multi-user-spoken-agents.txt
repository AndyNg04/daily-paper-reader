Abstract 

Personalized spoken agents must identify speakers before accessing user-specific mem-ory, yet memory benchmarks typically as-sume correctly attributed histories. We ar-gue that voiceprints are observations of iden-tity, not identities themselves, and formulate online speaker-memory construction to main-tain persistent user entities as acoustic evi-dence evolves. We introduce EvoSpeaker-Mem, a training-free layer for causal speaker routing, selective acoustic-state updates, and identity-partitioned memory writes and re-trieval through a replaceable text-memory backend. We also construct LoCoMo-ID, a spoken-memory benchmark with hidden speaker identities, speaker-sensitive queries, simulated recording-condition shifts, and four-speaker identity-confusion tests. On longitu-dinal SMIIP-TV, genuine-probe rejection un-der a frozen near-enrollment threshold rises from 29.8% to 55.6% over approximately one year. On LoCoMo-ID speaker-sensitive queries, gated updating improves identity-gated QA over fixed representations by 3.86 and 11.62 percentage points under clean and shifted conditions, respectively, with no wrong-user authorizations. The advantage persists in the four-speaker test. Yet reducing fragmentation and improving QA can coexist with incorrect cross-user links, revealing a fragmentation– contamination trade-off that QA alone cannot capture. 

1 Introduction 

Personalized spoken agents rely on user profiles and interaction histories to remain consistent across turns and sessions (Zhang et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2022). In shared spoken environments, however, maintaining such memory first requires deciding

to. Speaker attribution is therefore a prerequisite for personalized memory construction. An iden-tity error is stateful: treating a returning user as new fragments one person’s history across profiles, whereas confusing two users contaminates one pro-file with another user’s information and can affect subsequent personalized responses. Recent audio large language models (Audio LLMs) have advanced speech understanding, yet speaker-centric reasoning remains challenging. MSU-Bench and M3-SLU reveal gaps in speaker verification, attribution, and reasoning about who said what (Sun et al., 2026; Kwon et al., 2025). VoxMem further evaluates multi-session memory for acoustic evidence, including speaker identity, and finds that current Audio LLMs retain spo-ken content more reliably than speaker informa-tion (Xiao et al., 2026). These findings motivate explicit speaker tools and persistent identity state. Conversational-memory research has made sub-stantial progress in organizing long histories. Lo-CoMo and LongMemEval evaluate retrieval, tem-poral reasoning, and knowledge updating across sessions (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025b); CarMem studies preference memory for voice as-sistants (Kirmayr et al., 2025), while spoken and multimodal evaluations extend memory beyond text (Kim et al., 2025; Bei et al., 2026). These benchmarks principally evaluate memory readout 

from histories with available participant attribution. A shared spoken agent must also construct that history by resolving each incoming speaker and routing content to the appropriate user’s memory. Spoken dialogue systems connect identity with personalization: PAChat links speaker represen-tations to profiles (Fu et al., 2025), AFA routes speech to per-user memories (Al-Ratrout et al., 2026), and EgoMem updates audiovisual mem-ory (Yao et al., 2025). Concurrent VoxPolyMem combines incremental speaker identification, gated exponential moving averages, and session-level 1arXiv:2610.36713v1 [cs.SD] 27 Oct 2026 identity reconciliation (Jia et al., 2026). Yet the consequences of imperfect online speaker assign-ments for persistent user-memory integrity remain insufficiently understood. A key challenge is that acoustic observations from the same speaker vary across sessions, en-vironments, and user states. Speaker-verification research studies session variability, template ag-ing, and adaptation (Kinnunen and Li, 2010; Das et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016; Heo et al., 2024); SMIIP-TV provides recordings from 373 speakers to study such variation (Qin et al., 2024). Link-ing voiceprints to writable conversational memory adds persistent consequences: false rejection can duplicate a user’s profile, while false acceptance can misattribute information to another user. We therefore argue that voiceprints are obser-vations of identity, rather than identities them-selves . We formulate personalized spoken inter-action as online speaker-memory construction : an agent causally creates, links, and updates persis-tent user entities as acoustic evidence accumulates. EvoSpeakerMem routes each turn to an existing or new entity, selectively updates its acoustic state, and writes the attributed content to a replaceable textual-memory backend. We evaluate the proposed formulation in three stages. First, we use MSU-Bench to examine the limitations of existing Audio LLMs in speaker-identity tasks, comparing them with an LLM equipped with a speaker-embedding tool. Second, SMIIP-TV evaluates how evolving acoustic evi-dence affects speaker-memory maintenance. Third, LoCoMo-ID evaluates the full pipeline from online speaker routing and user-partitioned memory con-struction to speaker-sensitive question answering. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We formulate online speaker-memory con-struction , in which voiceprints are treated as observations of persistent user entities, and introduce EvoSpeakerMem, a training-free speech support layer for chronological CRE -

2.1 Problem Formulation 

We consider spoken turns {xt}Tt=1 arriving chrono-logically. At time t, the system observes the cur-rent audio xt and its existing state Ut−1, without speaker labels or access to future turns. This state contains local user entities Ut−1 = {u1, . . . , u K },each combining an acoustic representation with attributed textual memory: 

uk = ( ck, n k, Hk), (1) where ck is a speaker-embedding center, nk counts observations assigned to the entity, and Hk stores atomic textual facts extracted from its attributed turns. assigns the turn’s identity and memory partition. Voiceprints are observations of persistent entities. Incorrect linking can contaminate acoustic state and factual memory; incorrect new-entity creation fragments one user’s evidence across partitions.

2.3 Speech Support Layer and Memory Construction 

Figure 1 illustrates the speech-to-memory pipeline. For each spoken turn, an audio LLM (Xu et al., 2025b) generates a transcript, while a speaker-embedding model extracts acoustic identity evi-dence. The router (Section 2.2) assigns the turn to an existing local entity or creates a new one. The transcript, assigned entity identifier, and timestamp are then passed to a replaceable text-memory back-end (Fang et al., 2026), which extracts and indexes atomic facts within the corresponding user’s mem-ory partition. Each entity thus maintains acoustic state and user-attributed textual memory, while tran-script semantics do not influence acoustic routing or center updates. At query time, read-only voice matching against the registry identifies the relevant entity and re-stricts retrieval to its memory partition; unmatched requests receive no personal memory. The same en-tity identifiers therefore govern both memory writes and reads. Appendix F provides transcription and routing prompts. 

3 LoCoMo-ID Benchmark Construction 

LoCoMo-ID combines query adaptation, identity-consistent speech synthesis, and two stress condi-tions targeting acoustic variation and multi-speaker interference. 

3.1 Text Adaptation for Spoken Queries 

Following the non-commercial license, we retain LoCoMo’s ten histories (5,882 turns), 1,986 QA items, and reference answers (Maharana et al., 2024) 2. Rule-based query transformations replace explicit participant references with “I” or “my,” making the spoken requester’s identity necessary  

> 2Dataset: https://huggingface.co/datasets/ anonymous-user428/LoCoMo-ID

3for answering while preserving the reference an-swer. We rewrite 1,371 single-person questions into first-person sensitive queries and 22 shared-experience questions into first-person-plural forms. The other 593 questions remain unchanged. Man-ual checks assess whether rewritten questions pre-serve the original meaning, participant reference, and answer. Appendix G gives transformation ex-amples. 

3.2 Voice Assignment and Speech Synthesis 

We generate candidate reference voices with Qwen3-TTS VoiceDesign 3 and retain high-quality voice reference after ECAPA-based diversity filter-ing. Qwen3-TTS Base 4 then synthesizes the history and query audio from the assigned references (Hu et al., 2026). Each conversation’s two participants receive distinct voices, maintained across their his-tory turns, sessions, and corresponding spoken queries. Each conversation uses three voice assignments with identical dialogue text and QA content. Each conversation–realization pair initializes an indepen-dent speaker registry and textual memory. This yields 30 clean collections, 17,646 history audio turns, and 5,958 query audio items. Quality control (QC) checks perceptual quality with UTMOS (Saeki et al., 2022) and speaker con-sistency against a calibrated reference-similarity threshold. A failing audio clip receives up to four additional synthesis attempts; if none passes both checks, we retain the highest-UTMOS candidate. QC targets usable, identity-consistent speech rather than downstream QA. Appendix G gives the voice-generation models, prompts, and QC settings. To assess content fidelity, we transcribe history and query audio with an automatic speech recogni-tion (ASR) model whisper-large-v3 (Radford et al., 2023) and compute word error rate (WER) against source texts. Over 99.7% of utterances have WER below 0.3. Very short clips can have high WER from minor ASR mismatches, such as “bye Nate” recognized as “by Nate.” Manual listening con-firmed correct spoken content in these cases, sup-porting synthesis fidelity.

3.3 Recording-condition Shifts 

To test acoustic robustness beyond the clean refer-ence, we apply simulated room, device, and envi-ronmental variations to history audio, preserving content and speaker assignments. No quality or reference-similarity filtering follows augmentation, so difficult acoustic observations are retained. This condition simulates recording variability to mimic complex scenarios during conversations. Detailed settings are provided in Appendix G.4. 

3.4 Four-Speaker Interleaved Stress Test 

We retain each original two-person story and con-struct a parallel story with the same conversational structure but controlled fact substitutions. The two stories share character names but use disjoint voice pairs, yielding four distinct acoustic identities with conflicting personal histories. This setting intro-duces deliberate identity ambiguity, testing whether the system maintains separate user memories de-spite overlapping names and conversational con-texts. We interleave the two stories at session bound-aries while preserving the chronology of each story, requiring the system to track four identities across alternating sessions. Queries target facts from the original story, even when same-named speakers in the parallel story have conflicting information. Correct retrieval therefore depends on maintain-ing speaker-specific memory partitions rather than relying on textual names or shared context.

4.2 Causal Online Evaluation 

We evaluate online speaker-memory construction using a causal first-seen binding protocol. When a local entity is first created, it is bound to the gold speaker of that turn only for post-hoc scoring. The 

> 6https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ EvoSpeakerMem-4AF1

binding is immutable and never affects routing, up-dating, or memory construction; a later assignment is correct only when it matches this binding. This avoids future-aware cluster matching, which could relabel entities after observing the full sequence and hide online routing errors. Each turn is categorized into one of five mutually exclusive routing outcomes: new-speaker creation, new-speaker linking, known-speaker creation, cor-rect known-speaker linking, and wrong-target link-ing. These outcomes capture two complementary failure modes of online entity maintenance: frag-mentation, where a known speaker is assigned to a new entity, and contamination, where different speakers are merged into the same entity. 4.4 Time-Varying Speaker Evidence on SMIIP-TV 4.4.1 Longitudinal Verification Setup 

SMIIP-TV (Qin et al., 2024) 7 contains repeated recordings from 373 speakers over 90 days, with an approximately one-year follow-up for 118 matched speakers. Its text-independent utterances cover vari-ations in recording environment, speaker state, and time of day. We use each speaker’s earliest utterance as en-rollment and evaluate a frozen ECAPA representa-tion across increasing time gaps. Using impostor scores from probes within three days of enrollment, we calibrate a threshold at approximately 2% false acceptance ( τ2% = 0 .4751 ) and freeze it for sub-sequent analyses. We report EER and Below-τ ,the proportion of genuine probes falling below this threshold. Complete protocol details are provided in Appendix C.1.

4.4.3 Causal Speaker Maintenance 

To examine how acoustic variability affects persis-tent identity construction, we organize SMIIP-TV recordings into causal replay streams containing two to four speakers. We evaluate six conditions involving temporal, environmental, and speaker-state variation, with five seeds per condition. Each turn is processed chronologically without future speaker information. We compare six training-free representation policies, ranging from fixed and up-dated single templates to all-exemplar KNN (Ap-pendices C.6 and C.7). Across the six large-span conditions, online mean updating improves macro-averaged B 3 F1 from 46.82% (First exemplar) to 55.99%, while All-exemplar KNN achieves 65.31%. However, pre-serving more historical evidence does not guarantee correct identity assignment: All-exemplar KNN achieves lower LINK precision than First exem-plar (52.56% vs. 66.25%). These results show that adaptive representations can mitigate fragmenta-tion, but also expose a trade-off with incorrect link-ing. We examine the downstream consequences of this trade-off through memory construction and identity-gated QA on LoCoMo-ID (Section 4.5).

4.5.4 Four-Speaker Interleaved Stress Test 

Compared with the standard two-speaker setting, the four-speaker interleaved stress test doubles the speakers per conversation and adds a fact-conflicting parallel story with shared character names, increasing identity and memory interfer-ence (Table 5). Descriptively, ECAPA Gated Update attains 97.77% B 3 F1 and 73.60% full identity-gated QA in the four-speaker setting, com-pared with 99.54% and 75.27% in the standard clean two-speaker setting. Crucially, updating speaker evidence remains beneficial in the more complex setting: ECAPA Update, Gated Update, and All-exemplar KNN achieve 72.06%, 73.60%, and 71.99% full QA, respectively, versus 67.83% for Fixed; Gated Update also has the highest four-speaker B 3 F1 (97.77%). The same QA advantage over Fixed holds with Parakeet+ECAPA (72.21– 72.36% versus 71.48%). Appendix D.3 provides detailed results. 

5 Conclusion 

We formulate online speaker-memory construc-tion , placing speaker attribution in the memory write path. Longitudinal speech challenges fixed templates; clean speech still exposes fragmenta-tion. Across clean and condition-shifted LoCoMo-ID experiments, Update, Gated Update, and All-exemplar KNN improve identity-gated QA over Fixed in both acoustic evidence modes. This advan-tage persists in the more challenging four-speaker interleaved test: with ECAPA-only routing, the three non-fixed policies achieve 71.99–73.60% full QA versus 67.83% for Fixed, with Gated Update attaining the highest score. These findings support maintaining speaker evidence beyond the initial voiceprint when constructing and querying person-alized memory. 8Limitations 

Our evaluation has several limitations. LoCoMo-ID uses synthetic voices; shifted-condition sim-ulates session-dependent rooms, microphones, device chains, and noise rather than naturally recorded, overlapping multi-user conversations. The standard clean and shifted-condition collec-tions each contain two participants. Our four-speaker stress test interleaves two synthetic, fact-conflicting stories at session boundaries, preserving within-story chronology; it does not represent nat-urally recorded, overlapping four-party dialogue. Its full-query evaluation covers only the ten clean collections. The two-to-four-speaker SMIIP-TV streams evaluate entity maintenance over repeated recordings rather than dialogue-memory QA. The Parakeet comparison combines session-local di-arization with the same ECAPA cross-session reg-istry, not an independently evaluated published sys-tem. We vary only the center-update threshold on clean speech: merge/lookup threshold 0.489 has no independent sensitivity sweep, and the operational encoder and backend remain fixed. Incorrectly writ-ten memories are not automatically corrected. Although multi-exemplar KNN is now evalu-ated through the LoCoMo-ID write/read pipeline, it stores every accepted voiceprint and does not bound memory growth. The condition-shifted evaluation shows improved clustering but wrong-user authorization, so a bounded, contamination-resistant exemplar selection policy remains fu-ture work. The post-hoc duration bins (clean 59/17,643 and condition-shifted 13/17,644 sub-second evaluated turns for global ECAPA) con-tain few short turns, so they cannot establish ro-bust duration effects or the influence of encoder padding; no duration-controlled intervention or duration-stratified private QA is available. Identity-gated retrieval is a simulated access protocol, not a formal privacy or authentication guarantee. 

Ethical Considerations 

Speaker embeddings and persistent conversational memory can reveal sensitive information. A lo-cal anonymous identifier does not make a person’s voice anonymous, and long-term voice linkage could be misused for surveillance or identification beyond the intended interaction context. Raw au-dio, embeddings, transcripts, and extracted mem-ories should therefore be collected and processed only with appropriate consent and access controls, retained for no longer than necessary, and protected against unauthorized reuse. Synthetic voices and derived conversational data should likewise be used in accordance with the licenses and intended uses of their source assets. The synthetic voice bank is constructed from anonymous generated references and is not intended to imitate or identify real indi-viduals. Identity-routing errors also create direct privacy risks: a false merge may expose one user’s informa-tion to another, while fragmented profiles can make correction and deletion incomplete. The observed wrong authorizations under unrestricted center up-dating, multi-exemplar scoring, and session-log-based grouping illustrate how plausible matches can expose another user’s memory under the simu-lated protocol. A deployed system should provide transparent enrollment and identity-management mechanisms, support user inspection, correction, deletion, and withdrawal, and avoid using the pro-posed router as a security-critical authentication mechanism. Our experiments study the technical problem of identity-aware memory construction and should be understood as a research prototype rather than a deployment-ready biometric system.

C Additional SMIIP-TV Analyses 

C.1 Static Verification Protocol 

SMIIP-TV contains recordings from five daily peri-ods, with three utterances per period and metadata describing recording environments, noise, physical states, and time of day. We use text-independent ut-terances throughout. The approximately one-year extension includes 118 matched speakers but lacks the full context annotations of the original release. For static verification, each speaker’s earliest utterance serves as enrollment. We use only probes recorded within three days of enrollment for threshold calibration. The 98th percentile of the corresponding impostor-score distribution de-fines τ2% = 0 .4751 , which is frozen across sub-sequent time-gap analyses and causal replay poli-cies. For bucketed EER estimation, each genuine probe is paired with 20 randomly sampled impos-tors. Longer-gap genuine probes and downstream labels are excluded from calibration. Below-τ measures genuine probes rejected by the frozen enrollment template; it is distinct from the false-new user rate (FNUR), which measures erroneous entity creation during causal replay. 

C.2 Matched-Set ReDimNet2-B6 Static Robustness Check 

To test whether fixed-template degradation is spe-cific to the ECAPA encoder used in the operational system, we repeat the static verification protocol with ReDimNet2-B6. The comparison is restricted to the intersection of utterances successfully pro-cessed by both encoders, containing 205,765 text-independent SMIIP-TV utterances. ECAPA and ReDimNet2-B6 therefore use identical speakers, enrollment utterances, genuine probes, sampled impostor trials, and time buckets. Each encoder is calibrated separately under the same protocol: the earliest available utterance is enrollment, probes within three days define the calibration window, and the threshold is se-lected at target FAR=2% and then frozen for all remaining analyses. This gives τECAPA = 0 .4752 

and τReDimNet2 = 0 .6131 . The threshold values themselves are encoder-specific and are not com-pared directly; EER and within-encoder changes in below-threshold genuine rate are the relevant cross-encoder diagnostics. Table 7 and Figures 2–3 compare the matched-set verification results. ReDimNet2-B6 consis-tently improves absolute verification separability. For example, its EER is 3.34% at 0–1 days and 6.35% at 365–450 days, compared with 9.71% and 14.09% for ECAPA on the same utterance set. Nev-ertheless, ReDimNet2-B6’s below-threshold gen-uine rate increases from 5.19% to 12.96% over the same interval. Thus, stronger acoustic representa-tions reduce the severity of longitudinal mismatch but do not make a single enrollment embedding a time-invariant entity key.

C.6 Online Replay Conditions 

Table 9 defines the low-drift control and higher-drift treatment used for each causal replay. “Small” and “large” refer to the condition span relative to en-rollment, not the number of speakers, turns, or ses-sions. G LoCoMo-ID Construction Details 

LoCoMo histories and reference answers are re-tained without LLM generation; turn selection and query rewriting use deterministic rules. The con-struction pipeline’s only natural-language model instruction is the Qwen3-TTS VoiceDesign prompt for the anonymous voice bank. 

Synthesis QC and augmentation. Each clip is initially synthesized and checked for UTMOS ≥

3.0 and reference-voice cosine similarity ≥ 0.489 .A failing clip receives at most four additional at-tempts. If none passes both checks, the highest-UTMOS candidate among the initial synthesis and retries is retained; no dialogue turn is discarded by this fallback. The procedure targets perceptual quality and intended speaker attribution, not down-stream QA. Generated-to-reference similarity is not the online score against a first-turn or evolv-ing entity center, so passing QC does not guaran-tee a correct routing decision. Nevertheless, using ECAPA for both QC and routing can affect clean-condition difficulty. Condition shifts are applied after clean construction without further UTMOS or reference-similarity filtering; its difficult acoustic observations are therefore not removed by this QC stage. Pre-regeneration score records were not re-21 tained, so the current experiments do not establish the before/after score distribution. 

G.1 VoiceDesign Instruction and Enrollment 

The VoiceDesign instruction follows the template:              

> A{pitch}-pitched voice speaking at a{speaking_rate} pace, with a{timbre} timbre and a{expression} expression, {accent} accent. {delivery_style}

Candidate profiles combine pitch, speaking rate, timbre, expression, English accent, and delivery style. An instantiated instruction is:                 

> A medium-high-pitched voice speaking at aslightly slow pace, with abright and clear timbre and an animated and expressive expression, Scottish English accent. Acomposed, understated style with minimal pitch variation.

ECAPA-TDNN diversity filtering retains 50 of 120 candidate voices; descriptive attributes do not nec-essarily yield distinct acoustics. Each candidate voice reads the same canonical enrollment sentence: 

> Hi there, this is my voice. I’m looking forward to our conversation today.

Identical enrollment content controls lexical varia-tion in ECAPA-TDNN comparisons. 

G.2 Voice-Cloning Inputs 

VoiceDesign generates each identity’s enrollment audio. Qwen3-TTS Base then synthesizes history turns and queries in voice-cloning mode with these inputs:             

> Reference audio: [voice-id]_enrollment.wav Reference transcript: Hi there, this is my voice. I’m looking forward to our conversation today. Target text: [one LoCoMo history turn or one rewritten query]

Reusing enrollment audio and text is intended to preserve identity across sessions. 

G.3 Deterministic Query Transformations 

Question rewriting is rule based rather than prompt driven. For identity_gated questions, a named participant is converted to the first person, for exam-ple, “What did Caroline research?” becomes “What did I research?”, and “What is Caroline’s iden-tity?” becomes “What is my identity?” For sym-metric we_shared questions, the two participants are converted to the first-person plural: “What do Jon and Gina both have in common?” becomes “What do we have in common?”, while “What did Jon and Gina compare their entrepreneurial jour-neys to?” becomes “What did we compare our entrepreneurial journeys to?” Questions that can-not be transformed safely, together with adversarial questions, remain verbatim ; for example, “What nickname does Nate use for Joanna?” is unchanged. Unsafe transformations fall back to their original wording. 

G.4 Session-Level Recording-Condition Augmentation 

For each conversation instance and session, we deterministically assign one acoustic profile that specifies the room geometry, source–microphone distance and azimuth, reverberation time, micro-phone directivity, device response, automatic gain control, and optional environmental noise at a fixed signal-to-noise ratio. All utterances within the same session share this profile, whereas profiles can change across sessions, reflecting realistic tran-sitions across different recording scenarios. Each waveform is processed by room impulse response convolution, device-bandwidth filtering, gain control, noise mixing, and peak normaliza-tion. This augmentation preserves the original text, speaker–voice binding, turn order, and query au-dio, thereby isolating robustness to heterogeneous historical recording conditions from semantic or identity changes. 22
