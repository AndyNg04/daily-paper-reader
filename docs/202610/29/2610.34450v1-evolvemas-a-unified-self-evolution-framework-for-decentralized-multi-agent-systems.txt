Meituan 7University of Science and Technology of China †Corresponding author Decentralized multi-agent systems (MAS) enable large language model agents to interact without centralized control. Agents need to adapt to other agents at test time and reuse accumulated experience to refine their interaction strategies for future interactions across cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial settings. However, existing approaches often develop adaptation mechanisms for particular interaction settings. This shared requirement raises a central research question: how can a decentralized MAS framework support both test-time adaptation and reliable experience reuse across various interaction settings? To this end, we first propose EvolveMAS, a unified self-evolution framework that supports experience-driven adaptation within and across episodes. EvolveMAS pairs each main agent with a dedicated MemoryAgent for test-time memory governance, which maintains isolated Structured Memory outside the main agent’s reasoning context. EvolveMAS organizes adaptation into two complementary processes: Within-Episode Online Adaptation and Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution. During Within-Episode Online Adaptation, MemoryAgent updates episodic, semantic, and working memory from interaction evidence to inform subsequent actions. During Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution, the main agent uses memory and episode outcomes to distill reusable interaction knowledge comprising partner profiles and interaction strategies for subsequent episodes. Across five benchmarks and six evaluation configurations spanning the three interaction settings, EvolveMAS achieves the highest normalized average score of 77.2 among the compared methods. Ablations show that test-time memory governance and cross-episode knowledge reuse both improve performance. Interaction traces further illustrate how updated memory and distilled knowledge guide subsequent interactions. Overall, the experimental results support a unified self-evolution framework for experience-driven adaptation in a decentralized MAS environment without updating model parameters.

# 1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly deployed to assist users with personal and professional tasks (Staufer et al., 2026). Connecting agents deployed by different users creates opportunities to integrate their capabilities and support collaboration across users (Chen et al., 2025a). Decentralized multi-agent systems (MAS) support such collaboration by allowing agents to act on local information and communicate with peers without a central controller (Yang et al., 2025). From a game-theoretic perspective, multi-agent interactions are commonly categorized as cooperative, mixed-motive, or adversarial according to how agents’ objectives align (Buşoniu et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2021). When interacting with unfamiliar partners, people usually learn through interaction, using that experience to adjust immediate responses and guide future interactions. Agents similarly need to learn online from interaction experience to build profiles of other agents and refine their interaction strategies for subsequent interactions (He et al., 2016; Albrecht & Stone, 2017). This raises a central research question: how can a unified mechanism support test-time adaptation across cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial settings? Experience-driven self-evolution methods provides an insight to such adaptation by using feedback and accumulated experience to improve behavior without heavy parameter updates (Wang et al., 2025b; Tang et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2026). However, recent research shows that accumulating long interaction histories in the reasoning context creates context bloat that can affect task performance (Du et al., 2025). Retaining inaccurate accumulated experience introduces memory pollution that can misguide subsequent interactions (Xiong et al., 2026). These two limitations further raise the question: how can self-evolution decentralized agents achieve reliable experience reuse across three interaction settings? 

To address these questions, we propose EvolveMAS , a unified self-evolution framework that enables decentralized agents to learn from interaction experience across cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial settings. EvolveMAS pairs each main agent with a dedicated MemoryAgent for test-time memory governance to address context bloat and memory pollution. The MemoryAgent maintains Structured Memory outside the main agent’s reasoning context, organizing interaction evidence into Episodic, Semantic, and Working Memory. During Within-Episode Online Adaptation phase, the MemoryAgent updates Structured Memory as interactions unfold to inform subsequent actions. During Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution phase, the main agent uses interaction outcomes to refine reusable Knowledge comprising Partner Profiles and Interaction Strategies for future better interactions. We evaluate EvolveMAS on five benchmarks spanning cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial settings. Under a shared self-evolution protocol, EvolveMAS achieves the highest normalized average score of 77.2 (Figure 2), exceeding the strongest setting-specific baseline by 9.7 points. Decentralized Multi-Agent Systems. Research on decentralized multi-agent systems has explored coordination and adaptation across diverse interaction regimes. Game-theoretic studies commonly classify multi-agent interactions as cooperative, mixed-motive, or adversarial (Yang & Wang, 2020; Wong et al., 2023), while recent LLM-based MAS instantiate these regimes through cooperative coding and reasoning (Qian et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024), mixed-motive social interaction (Zhou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b), and adversarial strategic reasoning (Jin et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026). To support adaptation during interaction, classical opponent modeling infers other agents’ behavioral patterns from interaction history to guide decision-making (Carmel & Markovitch, 1996; He et al., 2016). More broadly, recent MAS adaptation methods adjust system topology, workflows, roles, communication structures, or agent policies (Yang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025b;a; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhuge et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2025b; Liao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026; Park et al., 2025). These approaches improve performance on specific coordination or adaptation objectives and are typically tailored to particular interaction settings, system structures, or training procedures. In contrast, EvolveMAS maintains the same decentralized system organization and evolution framework across these interaction regimes. 

Self-Evolution. Recent work explores test-time self-evolution by reusing interaction experience and feedback without updating model parameters. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), ExpeL (Zhao et al., 2024), and Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) demonstrate experience-driven improvements on single-agent tasks, while EvoMAC (Hu et al., 2025b) and AgentNet (Yang et al., 2025) extend iterative adaptation to multi-agent systems. Related studies also investigate self-generated data and self-play as mechanisms for agent evolution (Zelikman et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024b; Huang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025c). However, existing self-evolution methods are primarily designed for specific tasks or interaction settings. To support self-evolution across interaction settings, EvolveMAS couples within-episode adaptation with cross-episode Knowledge evolution through a shared test-time evolution mechanism for decentralized MAS. 

Agent Memory. Agent memory enables experience to persist beyond a single interaction con-text (Park et al., 2023; Sumers et al., 2024). MemGPT (Packer et al., 2023) and MemoryBank (Zhong et al., 2024) introduce persistent memory, while Voyager (Wang et al., 2024a), Agent Workflow Memory (Wang et al., 2025b), and SkillWeaver (Zheng et al., 2025) accumulate reusable skills and workflows. More recent work explores structured memory organization and test-time memory reasoning (Xu et al., 2025; Chhikara et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025). However, in long-horizon decentralized interactions, retaining accumulated memory in the main agent’s context can lead to context expansion, attention dilution, and memory pollution (Liang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Wan et al., 2026). To address these challenges, EvolveMAS pairs each main agent with a dedicated external MemoryAgent. This MemoryAgent maintains an isolated Structured Memory and provides a governed interaction state to support both adaptation timescales.

3.1 Framework Overview 

Consider a decentralized multi-agent system (DMAS) with main agents A = {ai}ni=1 operating over a continuous stream of heterogeneous interactions organized into episodes. In episode e, the agents receive a task description xe and interact autonomously over Te turns. Each agent selects its actions based on local observations, while the task determines which agents interact and when. The resulting interaction record forms the trajectory τe = ( τe, 1, . . . , τ e,T e ), where τe,t contains the messages and environment feedback produced at turn t. Each agent observes its own local view of 

τe, and the episode concludes with an interaction outcome oe.EvolveMAS preserves this decentralized organization (Figure 3). Each main agent ai is paired one-to-one with a dedicated MemoryAgent mi and an isolated memory, allowing accumulated experience to inform the main agent’s subsequent behavior. The main agent handles task reasoning, communication, and environment interaction. The corresponding MemoryAgent maintains Struc-tured Memory and Interaction Knowledge outside the main agent’s reasoning context, drawing on interaction evidence observed by that main agent. All MemoryAgents share the same memory schema and governance protocol, while their contents evolve independently based on their respective main agents’ local interaction histories. Based on the interaction record, EvolveMAS represents experience at three levels of abstraction. The Trajectory τe consists of raw interaction evidence generated during task execution. Building on this evidence, EvolveMAS maintains Structured Memory Me,t as a compact state updated during interaction. At a higher level, it maintains Interaction Knowledge Ke to capture reusable regularities across episodes. For clarity, the subsequent formulation adopts the perspective of a focal main agent

3.2 Within-Episode Online Adaptation 

During Execute in Eq. 1, main agents interact with peers and the environment. As interaction proceeds, their MemoryAgents continually update Structured Memory based on newly generated interaction evidence. At turn t, action generation and memory updating are formulated as (ye,t , ∆τe,t ) ∼ πa (· | [xe; Me,t −1; Ke−1]) ,Me,t = MA online (Me,t −1, ∆τe,t ) , (2) where ye,t denotes the generated response or environment action, and ∆ τe,t denotes the newly observed interaction evidence at turn t.Structured Memory comprises three complementary regions, 

Me,t = ( Ee,t , S e,t , W e,t ) , (3) where Ee,t , Se,t , and We,t denote Episodic, Semantic, and Working Memory (Tulving, 1972; Baddeley & Hitch, 1974). 

Episodic Memory. Ee,t records concrete interaction experiences, including the events that occurred, the agents or sources involved, and the outcomes that followed. Together, these records provide an interaction-grounded history from which regularities can subsequently be inferred. 

Semantic Memory. Se,t maintains reusable facts and interaction regularities inferred from experience. Depending on the interaction, these may include task-relevant facts, recurring coordination patterns, observations about interaction partners, or other stable information that supports subsequent reasoning. 

Working Memory. We,t maintains a compact state relevant to the ongoing interaction. This state includes the current focus, next-step information, and task-relevant views of accumulated memory and Interaction Knowledge. As the interaction context changes, Working Memory is refreshed accordingly. At the representation level, new events are appended to Episodic Memory, reusable regularities are incorporated into Semantic Memory, and Working Memory is refreshed. These conceptual update rules do not prescribe a specific storage implementation. The updates in Eq. 2 form a recurrent feedback loop within Execute . Evidence observed at turn t updates Structured Memory, which conditions the acting policy πa from turn t + 1 onward. 6This loop enables Within-Episode Online Adaptation. Sec. 3.3 formalizes both adaptation timescales in Eq. 5. The term within-episode specifies the timescale of this feedback loop. Episodic and Semantic information may persist and accumulate across episodes, whereas Working Memory is refreshed for the ongoing interaction. 

3.3 Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution 

After episode e concludes, Evaluate produces a task-native result signal re from the observed interaction outcome. This signal serves as a protocol-level interface for conditioning subsequent evolution without requiring a shared metric space across heterogeneous interactions. Conditioned on re, the main agent distills reusable interaction regularities from the governed Structured Memory

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experiment Setting 

We evaluate EvolveMAS on five representative benchmarks spanning three interaction settings. The cooperative setting includes BBH (Suzgun et al., 2023) and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), covering multi-agent reasoning and collaborative coding under shared goals. The mixed-motive setting includes LLM-Deliberation (Abdelnabi et al., 2024) and SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024), covering structured negotiation and open-ended social interaction with partially aligned objectives. The adversarial setting uses One Night Ultimate Werewolf (Jin et al., 2024), a hidden-role game involving deception, trust calibration, and strategic reasoning. The main experiments use heterogeneous teams spanning model families and capability tiers to support Partner Profile modeling. Team size and composition follow each benchmark’s native protocol and difficulty. Each main agent is paired with an isolated GPT-4o-mini MemoryAgent. We evaluate five source method families under six configurations, including benchmark-native and cross-setting comparisons. 4.2 Main Results across Interaction Settings 

Table 1 compares EvolveMAS with setting-specific baselines transferred across interaction settings and with Self-Memory. Under a shared self-evolution protocol, EvolveMAS ranks first in four evaluation configurations and second in the remaining two. Its normalized average score of 77.2 exceeds the strongest setting-specific baseline by 9.7 points and Self-Memory by 7.7 points. 

Cooperative setting. EvolveMAS achieves 83.0 on BBH, exceeding the native AgentNet baseline of 79.0 by four percentage points. On HumanEval, it achieves 97.0, compared with 95.7 for both ChatDev and Self-Memory. Appendix Table 5 further examines performance under an alternative BBH model configuration. 

Mixed-motive setting. On LLM-Deliberation, EvolveMAS achieves the highest average agreement of 5.40 parties, compared with 4.92 for the native baseline. On SOTOPIA, it ranks second at 0.613, close to the native baseline of 0.618 and 0.037 above Self-Memory. Both settings provide partner-specific and negotiation regularities that can be reused through cross-episode evolution. 

Adversarial setting. Because adversarial roles have conflicting objectives, the one-sided audits apply EvolveMAS to one side at a time. On WW-V, EvolveMAS scores 62.0, compared with the highest score of 64.0 achieved by LLM-Deliberation. On WW-W, it achieves the highest score of 70.0, exceeding the best competing method by 13 points. Appendix E.4 reports both one-sided conditions and the two symmetric references on a common village-win scale. Detailed metrics and per-benchmark breakdowns for Table 1 are provided in Appendices A and E.

4.6 Additional Analyses 

System-level analyses show stable performance across the evaluated team sizes and favorable performance relative to static aggregation. Further diagnostics show stable short-horizon performance and lower per-episode cost and acting-context occupancy than retaining raw interaction histories as experience accumulates (Appendix C). Analyses of Knowledge accumulation indicate that stronger acting models distill more transferable Interaction Knowledge (Appendix D). Finally, a long-horizon memory ablation shows higher overall question-answering accuracy with online MemoryAgent updates than with offline updates or self-managed memory (Appendix F). 

# 5 Conclusion 

We presented EvolveMAS, a unified self-evolution framework for decentralized multi-agent systems (DMAS). Evaluations on five benchmarks demonstrate its applicability to cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial settings, while ablations highlight the value of test-time memory governance and cross-episode Knowledge reuse. Together, these results support experience-driven adaptation across the three interaction settings under a shared self-evolution framework without model parameter updates. Designed as a plug-in extension to existing agents, EvolveMAS supports decentralized interaction while preserving each agent’s autonomy. This provides a basis for integrating the capabilities of agents deployed by different users without centralized control. Building on this paradigm, future work can investigate empirical scaling laws by varying agent capability, population size, and accumulated experience. Such studies would clarify the design trade-offs among DMAS reasoning performance, computational cost, and safety.

A.1 Transfer Construction 

To make the transfer rows in Table 1 reproducible and fair to the source methods, we fix one transfer setup. Each setting-native method family is instantiated from its original configuration, preserving native role structure, team composition, and social mode, and is then run through the target benchmark’s native runner and evaluator. Each family therefore runs exactly the mechanism it was designed around, including the within-episode interaction context of its native protocol; cross-episode memory enters the comparison through the Self-Memory row on the same team. BBH and HumanEval runs force the social mode to cooperative because both are cooperative benchmarks, while SOTOPIA, LLM-Deliberation, and Werewolf retain the source method’s native social mode. Table 2 lists the five source families and their native protocols. For the cooperative targets (BBH, HumanEval), the source family’s role structure is mapped onto the cooperative runner: a ChatDev pipeline runs as planner, developer, and tester; an AgentNet or Werewolf team runs as a five-agent heterogeneous group; and a SOTOPIA trio runs as three cooperating agents. For SOTOPIA, the source team bargains under the mixed-motive evaluator. For LLM-Deliberation, the source method’s agents populate the six negotiation parties, with the Werewolf family represented by the one-adversarial-incentive variant that best matches its structure. For Werewolf, the source team fills the five player slots under the adversarial evaluator, except that the fixed five-player count forces LLM-Deliberation to use its native five-model configuration rather than the six-party one. For the Werewolf/DPIns family (Jin et al., 2024), the original method trains a discussion policy with reinforcement learning to choose among discussion tactics, and deduces the other players’ hidden roles by belief modeling rather than by a learned policy; DPIns is the name its released implementation gives this discussion-policy-instructed agent. To evaluate all families under the same training-free setting with frozen models, we encode the discussion tactics it selects as a fixed prompt. This instantiation applies uniformly across the six evaluation configurations, so the family appears in every column of the transfer table under the same frozen team. 

Team composition. # G Qualitative Cases 

To make the self-evolution mechanism concrete, we show representative memory, skill, and profile artifacts produced under EvolveMAS. The content is taken from the Structured Memory and Interaction Knowledge stores written during evaluation and lightly trimmed for length.
