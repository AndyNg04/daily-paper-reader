ALEXANDER BODEN, Hochschule Bonn-Rhein-Sieg (H-BRS), Germany Explainable AI (XAI) often prioritizes model fidelity over user intelligibility, yielding explanations that satisfy technical transparency yet fail to support human sense-making. We propose a human-centered approach, 

MIND-X (Mental-model INtegrated Design for eXplainability), which aligns explanations with users’ mental models, commonsense concepts, and judgment heuristics. Using fake review detection as a case study, we contribute: (1) a qualitative study (n=10) that elicits how people assess review credibility and produces an actionable, design-guiding taxonomy of perceptual cues; (2) a set of design principles that link those cues to explanation strategies and prompt LLMs to generate adaptive, language-based explanations; and (3) a quantitative evaluation (n=166) showing that these explanations offer distinct advantages over token-level transparency methods widely used in explainable text classification in terms of understandability, satisfaction, perceived trust, persuasiveness, and decision-making support. Rather than claiming general superiority, our evaluation illustrates how grounding explanations in users’ everyday reasoning practices can produce more intelligible and context-sensitive accounts. Our findings advance human-centered explanation design beyond a mere “gloss of transparency,” demonstrating that a mental-model–aligned design process can improve intelligibility and support accountability across user-centered explanation tasks. Additional Key Words and Phrases: Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI); Human-centered AI; Mental models; Fake review detection; User-centered explanation design; Natural language explanations; Cognitive heuristics 

1 Introduction 

Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems are becoming increasingly embedded in consequential decision-making contexts, spanning domains such as healthcare, content moderation, education, human resources, and e-commerce. Within these high-stakes environments, the challenge of explainability for non-professional users has emerged as one of the central issues in contemporary human–machine interaction [17, 38]. Explanations for the general public are not merely a matter of technical transparency but must also embody accountability and intelligibility in ways that are socially recognizable and usable within the everyday reasoning practices of end-users [46]. Prior work on explanation design has largely focused on making visible the internal operations of AI systems by exposing input features that are most influential in generating outputs [5]. In text classification tasks, for instance, methods such as LIME [61], SHAP [43], or Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) [4] highlight specific words or tokens deemed pivotal to a model’s decision. While these approaches are often praised for their technical faithfulness to the underlying computation [14, 42], they frequently fail to achieve commonsense meaningfulness because they do not align with the reasoning practices, interpretive resources, and domain-specific expectations through which people make sense of algorithmic outcomes [16, 44]. These challenges are particularly evident in the domain of online marketplaces, where customer reviews strongly influence consumer decisions and trust in digital platforms. The rise of generative AI models has made it increasingly easy for malicious actors to produce convincing fake reviews [12]. Although state-of-the-art machine learning models can achieve high predictive accuracy in detecting deceptive reviews [69], they largely function as “black boxes,” offering little actionable or user-facing explanations. In existing explainable text classification systems, explanations are commonly instantiated as word- or token-level importance visualizations (e.g., via LRP). Such approaches have proven useful in domains where class membership is strongly associated with distinctive lexical features or class-specific word sets. However, in the fake review context, both human-written and AI-generated reviews often employ similar vocabularies and surface-level expressions. As a result, highlighted tokens tend to reflect statistical correlations learned by the model rather than cues that users themselves consider meaningful when judging review credibility, limiting their value as sense-making resources for end users. Platforms such as Amazon Germany provide mechanisms for assessing review authenticity, yet these tools typically do not explain why a review is classified as genuine or fake. Without intelligible, context-sensitive explanations, users face uncertainty, which undermines trust and limits accountability [29, 72]. To address this gap, we advocate for a shift from explanation as the exposure of internals to explanation as a sense-making resource . We specifically draw on the potential of large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, to serve as explanation agents [73]. Crucially, we do not treat LLMs as generic generators of fluent explanations. Instead, we ground explanation generation in users’ mental models —their internal representations of how fake reviews are produced, detected, and interpreted—so that explanations align with how users actually reason about review credibility. While it may appear straightforward that natural-language explanations generated by large language models (LLMs) would better support user sense-making than word highlighting, language alone does not guarantee intelligibility or relevance. Free-form textual explanations can still em-phasize factors that users find unhelpful, overlook critical cues, or over-persuade users without fostering meaningful understanding. Rather than treating explanation as a one-way projection of system logic, we conceptualize explanations as a process of account-giving in the ethnomethodological sense [22]. In this view, explanations function as narratives that are recognizable, adequate, and “good enough” within the interpretive horizon of the user. This perspective highlights that explanations must be both domain-oriented (anchored in the user’s conceptual and practical understanding of the task [8]) and goal-oriented (tuned to explanatory objectives such as trustworthiness, simplicity, or persuasiveness [17, 39]). The task of XAI, then, is not simply to “open the black box,” but to provide accounts through which users can construct 

workable mental models and engage in practical reasoning [17]. Our human-centered explanation design approach MIND-X (Mental-model INtegrated Design for eXplainability) proceeds in six steps: starting from the AI domain model (the trained classifier) and the user domain model (users’ mental models of the task), a domain explanation agent generates user-comprehensible accounts that are then adapted to specific explanation objectives . Analysis of these interviews revealed a set of recurring heuristics and assumptions—such as expectations about experiential detail, narrative coherence, emotional plausibility, and reviewer intent—that together form a user mental model of fake review detection. 2 Related Work 2.1 Human-Centered Explanation Design 

Traditionally, AI systems learn from training data to build models that make predictions or classifi-cations within a domain. While effective, these models’ outputs often remain opaque, leaving users with the unresolved question of “Why?” . The DARPA XAI program [24] responded by extending 4 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

the traditional data-to-decision pipeline with an additional explainable component designed to render system reasoning more transparent. A broad range of explanation methods have since been developed [14, 23]: feature-attribution techniques (e.g., LIME, SHAP, LRP [31]), example-based approaches (e.g., counterfactuals [15]), 

concept-based methods such as TCAV [33], and causal representation learning [65]. While valuable for experts to debug and improve model performance, such methods often fail to meet the needs of everyday users, who require explanations that are both comprehensible and actionable [64]. This limitation stems in part from the fact that most XAI approaches are rooted in a positivist, causal realist orientation [82], which conceptualizes explanations primarily as causal reflections of computational processes rather than as resources tailored to human reasoning. Their legitimacy is framed in correspondence-theoretical terms [76], i.e., by the degree to which they faithfully reflect the AI model, rather than by how they support situated sense-making [80]. Beyond technical correctness, prior work demonstrates that explainability fundamentally shapes users’ perceptions, trust, and acceptance of AI systems. Shin [71] shows that explanations affect users not only through perceived transparency, but also through perceived causability —that is, the extent to which an explanation provides a meaningful causal account of an outcome from the user’s perspective. These findings suggest that explanations must resonate with users’ causal reasoning and sense-making practices, rather than merely exposing internal model mechanics. Lipton [42] and Miller [44] further highlight the shortcomings of purely model-centric views, ar-guing instead for explanations grounded in the social sciences’ understanding of human explanatory practices. This demand echoes a social-constructivist understanding [22], in which explanations are conceived as situated, context-sensitive accomplishments. From this perspective, explanations are understood less as mirrors of a ground truth than as interactional resources accountable to the explainee’s reasoning, mental models, and objectives. In response to the shortcomings of existing XAI approaches for everyday users, Abdul et al. [1] called for a shift from model-centric fidelity measures to socio-technical perspectives. In HCI, this epistemic and pragmatic lens emphasizes the everyday reasoning of stakeholders and the multiple objectives explanations must serve. Beyond understandability and transparency [27], the literature highlights user satisfaction and perceived usefulness [50], alignment with mental models [6], persuasive power and decision support [77], reduction of cognitive load [38], and effects on trust and confidence [3, 36]. Empirical work further demonstrates that trust and reliance emerge from a complex interaction between model accuracy and explanation form. Papenmeier et al. [54] show that even highly accurate systems can induce over- or under-reliance when explanations are poorly aligned with users’ expectations, underscoring the importance of explanation design for calibrated trust rather than blind reliance. Recent user-centered evaluation research emphasizes that explanation quality must be assessed 

with and for humans . User-centric evaluation frameworks argue that explanations should be evaluated in relation to users’ goals, expectations, and interpretive strategies, rather than relying solely on proxy metrics or expert judgment [9]. This perspective directly motivates human-centered, construct-based evaluation approaches. At the same time, explanation design must carefully balance informativeness and cognitive effort. Liedeker et al. [41] identify a “sweet spot” of explanation complexity, showing that overly detailed explanations can overwhelm users, while overly simplified explanations risk being perceived as unhelpful or misleading. These findings highlight that explanations must be tailored not only to users’ goals, but also to their cognitive capacities and task contexts. To address these needs, explainable systems increasingly draw on user-centered [52] and partici-patory design traditions [66]. Examples include Eiband et al.’s [18] stage-based participatory design Leveraging Mental Models for Human-Centered Explanations: A Systematic Design Approach Illustrated by Explainable Fake Review Identification 5

process, Liao et al.’s [38] “XAI Question Bank,” and Chromik and Butz’s [13] design principles for explanation user interfaces. Complementing these, Ehsan et al. [16] propose natural language rationales as human-like accounts of system behavior. Recent work further reframes explainability as an interactive process rather than a static artifact. Raees et al. [59] trace a shift from post-hoc explanation toward interactive human–AI systems, in which explanations adapt to users’ questions, goals, and contexts over time. This shift emphasizes explanations as dialogical sense-making resources aligned with users’ evolving mental models. A promising new direction leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate and evaluate explanations [73]. Empirical studies suggest that users perceive language-based explanations as more intuitive than technical visualizations [28, 45]. Our work builds on this line of research by grounding LLM-generated explanations explicitly in empirically elicited user mental models, rather than treating LLMs as generic post-hoc narrators. 

2.2 Research on Fake Reviews 

Online reviews, whether genuine or AI-generated, are written in natural language and exhibit high variability in style, tone, and content. Detecting deception in such unstructured data is a non-trivial task, and current research is dominated by transformer-based approaches that leverage contextual embeddings and attention mechanisms [2, 48, 68]. Inspired by broader explainability work in text classification, fake review detection has also inte-grated post-hoc explanation methods. Techniques such as SHAP, LIME, and Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) have been adapted to make model predictions more interpretable [2, 68]. Yet, most approaches remain model-centric, focusing on internal attribution rather than aligning with user reasoning processes or decision needs. While word- or token-level explanations are effective in domains with class-specific lexical markers (e.g., sentiment or hate speech classification), fake review detection poses a fundamentally different challenge. Both deceptive and genuine reviews—whether written by humans or generated by AI—often share similar vocabularies and sentiment expressions. As a result, highlighted tokens frequently reflect statistical correlations learned by the model rather than cues that users themselves associate with review credibility. Attention mechanisms, sometimes assumed to be inherently interpretable, have yielded mixed results in this context. Mohawesh et al. [48] demonstrated that attention weights can be useful in ensemble models, but their evaluation lacked end-user feedback. Similarly, Mitrović et al. [45] used SHAP to distinguish ChatGPT-generated reviews from genuine ones with high accuracy, yet did not examine how end-users perceive or act upon such explanations. Overall, most approaches remain model-centric, focusing on internal attribution rather than aligning with user reasoning processes or decision needs. Behavioral and cognitive research shows that users rarely evaluate reviews as isolated word sequences. Instead, they rely on higher-level heuristics such as coherence, level of detail, emotional tone, perceived intent, and contextual cues [20, 78]. This misalignment helps explain why purely attribution-based explanations often fail as sense-making resources in this domain. When explanations emphasize isolated tokens, they risk contra-dicting users’ mental models of deception, increasing cognitive load or undermining trust. Taken together, these findings motivate explanation approaches that are grounded in users’ mental models, support calibrated trust, and manage cognitive effort. Our work responds to this gap by first eliciting how users reason about fake review credibility and then using these empirically grounded mental models to guide the generation and evaluation of language-based explanations. 6 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

3 Methodological Approach 3.1 General Explanation Design Approach 

> Fig. 1. Designing human-centered explanation following MIND-X: From domain models to tailored explana-tions.

Addressing the demands outlined in the previous section, our methodological premise is that explanations should be systematically grounded in the mental models of everyday users rather than restricted to the internal mechanics of AI systems. We therefore conceptualize explanation design as a translation process that aligns computational representations with users’ reasoning heuristics and domain-specific understandings. Importantly, this premise also responds to recent critiques of explanation paradigms that rely primarily on feature-attribution techniques, which—while effective in certain domains—may fail to support sense-making in tasks where users rely on higher-level semantic and contextual cues rather than isolated features. Drawing on HCI traditions of human-centered design [52], we propose MIND-X , a six-step process consisting of the following elements (see Figure 1), which we illustrate later in the paper: (1) AI Domain Model. Define the trained classifier for the given domain (e.g., a model for detecting fake reviews [69]). This step can be based on traditional AI engineering techniques and common LLM prompting technique. (2) User Domain Model. Elicit and formalize the corresponding mental model [53] that users hold about the task, derived from prior work or empirical studies. (3) Domain Explanation Agent. Mediate between AI and user domain models by generating 

user-comprehensible accounts . This step can leverage LLMs through prompt engineering techniques [81]. (4) Define Explanatory Goals. Make explicit the objectives that explanations should achieve [38]. Treat these goals as configurable requirements for tailoring the explanations. Leveraging Mental Models for Human-Centered Explanations: A Systematic Design Approach Illustrated by Explainable Fake Review Identification 7

(5) User Explanation Agent. Tailor domain-level explanations to specified goals, situational contexts by adjusting language, framing, and granularity for distinct target groups. This step can also leverage LLMs through prompt engineering techniques [81]. (6) Human-Grounded Evaluation. Validate the value of the designed explanations in relation to the previously defined explanatory goals. This step should follow the common procedures and best practices in empirical XAI evaluation studies [50]. The main goal of the approach is to extend domain-specific classifiers ( the AI domain model ) with an additional explainable component [24] that aims to explain the particular outcome of the AI model in a manner that users can make sense of. In contrast to approaches that equate explainability with revealing internal model mechanics, MIND-X treats explanations as communicative artifacts whose adequacy must be judged relative to users’ reasoning practices and expectations. To achieve this, the user domain model plays a pivotal role. It captures the mental models [53] that users hold about the task domain, (e.g., commonsense heuristics regarding what makes a review appear fake [78]). Regarding explanation generation, our method employs a two-step process inspired by the concept bottleneck principle [35] as well as the need-first approach [38], with exploratory goals addressed as a dedicated step. In the first phase, explanations are aligned with domain-relevant concepts that match user heuristics. In the second phase, these concepts are translated into appropriate linguistic and representational forms, focusing on tone, granularity, and narrative structure. This separation disentangles what to explain from how to explain, ensuring semantic grounding and communicative adequacy. Such a design also supports adaptive and personalized explanations [47], where complexity and detail can be calibrated to user expertise or situational demands. In this way, the user explanation agent operationalizes a central HCI principle: aligning transparency with diverse reasoning practices and interpretive resources. The final outcome is explanations that are situated, goal-oriented, and cognitively aligned with the user’s domain model. Finally, following recommendations for human-grounded evaluation [50], explanations must be validated empirically rather than assessed only in terms of technical fidelity. Beyond model-centric metrics, evaluation should examine whether explanations achieve their specified explanatory goals in real use. This is particularly critical for human-centered approaches grounded in a social-constructivist epistemic stance [22]. From this perspective, formal model faithfulness is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for effective sense-making [42, 44]. Instead, the explanatory value must be demonstrated in practice [1, 50]. Consequently, assessing the human-centered qualities of explanations requires established empirical evaluation methods. 

3.2 Instantiation of MIND-X for the Design of an Explainable Fake Review Classifier 

The following shows how we apply MIND-X for designing an explainable fake review classifier. This section provides an overview on the key-steps, while details are elaborated in subsequent sections of the paper. 

3.2.1 Step 1: Implementing a Fake Review Classifier. This step would ordinarily involve the develop-ment or fine-tuning of a dedicated fake review classifier, optimized for performance on large-scale benchmark datasets (e.g., [2, 69]). Our contribution, however, does not seek to advance classifica-tion accuracy but to investigate explanation design. This distinction is critical, as prior work has already established strong baseline performance for fake review detection, the explanatory layer remains underexplored from the perspective of everyday users. For this reason, we deliberately leveraged existing classification capabilities of LLMs ([57]), using ChatGPT 5.0 not only to generate human-centered explanation but also to classify the given review simultaneously (as part of Step 3). 8 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

This approach is similar to methods in which models jointly generate a prediction and a natural language explanation [49]. As the LLM had correctly classified all reviews used for our human-grounded evaluation (see Step 6), it provided a robust basis for assessing explanation quality without requiring additional model development. Moreover, classification performance in fake review detection is already well-established in prior work [2, 69], and re-implementing a state-of-the-art classifier would not have yielded additional insights for our research objectives. 

3.2.2 Step 2: Eliciting User Domain Models. The objective of this step was to elicit users’ mental models and commonsense concepts [53] related to the heuristics and cues that guide individuals when distinguishing between authentic and deceptive online reviews [78]. Crucially, the prompt does not aim to reproduce the classifier’s internal decision logic, but to reconstruct explanations in a form that mirrors users’ own reasoning strategies identified in Step 2. To this end, we conducted semi-structured online interviews with 10 participants via Zoom, complemented by a thinking-aloud protocol [10] in which participants evaluated a set of genuine and fake reviews while verbalizing their reasoning. This exploratory approach allowed us to capture diverse user perspectives and the cues participants rely on when making credibility judgments. All semi-structured interviews, including the thinking-aloud protocol, were video-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed in MAXQDA 1 following the principles of thematic analysis [11]. Two researchers independently coded each transcript and iteratively refined and consolidated codes into coherent themes through discussion. Coding was primarily inductive, allowing categories and themes to emerge directly from the data. Data collection for the qualitative study was conducted between 13/08/25 and 25/08/25. During this period, semi-structured interviews and thinking-aloud sessions were carried out, transcribed, and subsequently analyzed using thematic analysis. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling [19], resulting in a group that was diverse in online shopping habits, though uniformly well-educated (all held at least a university degree; see Table 1). While this recruitment strategy limits statistical generalizability, it was sufficient for our purposes: the resulting user domain models directly informed the design of our explanations, which later proved to be of high quality in the evaluation study. This outcome suggests that even with a small, non-representative sample, the elicited heuristics captured essential patterns of user reasoning relevant to explanation design.

3.2.3 Step 3: Translating User’s Domain Models into Prompt-Templates. In this step, the objective is to implement a domain explanation agent . This can be achieved either by developing a dedicated AI model or by adapting and fine-tuning existing ones. In our work, we chose to realize the agent through prompt engineering [57], leveraging the capabilities of state-of-the-art LLMs to generate user-centered explanations. Building on the thematic analysis, the elicited cues and heuristics were formalized into a struc-tured meta-prompt that guided the LLM’s reasoning. Rather than directly mirroring the internal mechanisms of the model, the prompt was designed to align with users’ cognitive heuristics by embedding cues (e.g., linguistic inconsistency, emotional tone, reviewer credibility) into an explicit reasoning schema. Following prior work on prompt engineering for alignment [81], we operational-ized a two-stage structure: (1) Classification (real vs. fake) and (2) Human-like Reasoning , expressed through a standardized “Cue → Perception → Explanation” sequence. This design ensured that each identified signal was not only detected but also contextualized in terms of how a human user might perceive and interpret it. To foster naturalistic communication, outputs were constrained to everyday language, with balanced pros and cons, as well as conflict resolution when cues pointed in opposing directions. The resulting prompt-template had modular fields (e.g., [decision outcome] +

[user-relevant cue] + [justification] ), producing structured yet human-centered narratives such as: "This review is likely fake because [ cue: linguistic inconsistency ] and [ cue: reviewer credibility issue ]." Templates were iteratively refined through pilot testing, during which two researchers indepen-dently reviewed and assessed the quality of generated explanations. The procedure was repeated until both researchers agreed that the explanations were sufficiently clear, consistent, and aligned with user-relevant cues. Explanations were generated using ChatGPT 5.0. For both the pilot phase and the subsequent evaluation study, reviews were drawn using a stratified random, balanced sample of fake and genuine reviews taken from the open dataset provided by [62]. 

3.2.4 Step 4: Specify Explanation Objectives. In this step, the task is to make explicit which objectives the explanations are intended to serve. Prior work has shown that explanation goals often remain implicit, leading to a mismatch between what systems provide and what users actually need. Yet, needs-first approaches, such as the XAI Question Bank [38], highlight the value of articulating explanatory purposes explicitly, so that design can be systematically aligned with users’ reasoning practices. Because our study was not situated in a single, concrete application context (e.g., being embedded in a deployed review platform) but rather aimed at conducting a generalizable evaluation of explanation design, we sought to incorporate a broad set of objectives identified in the literature. Specifically, we considered six explanation dimensions that are common in XAI research [27, 50]: 

understandability , satisfaction , trust , cognitive load , decision support , and persuasiveness .Making these objectives explicit avoids post-hoc rationalization and ensures that explanation quality is assessed relative to clearly articulated user-centered criteria rather than abstract no-tions of interpretability. Together, these dimensions capture both subject-oriented measures (e.g., understandability, satisfaction) and action-oriented measures (e.g., decision support, persuasive-ness), which are particularly salient in light of the threat of consumer manipulation through fake reviews [78]. 

3.2.5 Step 5: Tailoring Explanation to Explanation Goals and Use Context. This step involves tailoring domain-based explanations to the defined explanatory objectives, the specific application context, and the intended target audience. This step resembles the use of LLMs as an intermediate layer between the explanation model and the explanation interface [58, 83], where model outputs are adapted into natural language explanations tailored to specific user roles and application contexts. 10 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

In this context, word-highlighting explanations—which are effective in domains with stable class-specific lexical markers—are less suitable because both genuine and deceptive reviews often employ similar vocabulary, requiring higher-level semantic reasoning. As fake reviews represent one of the most prevalent threats to online marketplaces such as Amazon, our design emphasized two contextual requirements. First, the target group consists of everyday web shop users without technical expertise, who require explanations in plain, non-technical language that resonate with familiar heuristics, such as linguistic style, level of detail, or reviewer credibility cues [20, 78]. Second, the use context is characterized by consumer decision-making under time pressure, where users must make rapid judgments about credibility. Based on this contextual specification, we realized this step by leveraging the capabilities of ChatGPT 5.0 through prompt engineering. To this end, we iteratively developed a prompt template that adapts explanations to contextual demands. By adopting the concept bottleneck principle [35], we passed the output from Step 3 (the domain explanation agent ) only, rather than the original review text. This design choice ensured greater control over the generation process and helped align the produced explanations with the specified user domain model. The resulting meta-prompt was explicitly framed to generate explanations that are easy to understand, trustworthy, and actionable 

for everyday web shop users, who must rapidly assess review credibility in order to make informed purchasing decisions. 

3.2.6 Step 6: Human-Grounded Evaluation. The final step was to conduct an empirical evalua-tion study to assess whether the explanatory goals defined in Step 3 were achieved. Following established practices of human-grounded evaluation in XAI [14, 50], we specified the target group, operationalized the evaluation objectives, and conducted a benchmarking study using a mixed-methods approach. The quantitative evaluation study was conducted between 30/08/2025 and 15/08/2025 using the Prolific platform. All participants provided informed consent electronically before accessing the survey. A total of 𝑛 = 166 participants were recruited via Prolific (192 prior to data cleaning), serving as proxy users for the target group of online shoppers. Eligibility criteria required fluency in English and prior experience with online shopping. No further demographic restrictions were applied to capture the diversity of everyday consumers. The final sample included 96 participants identifying as female (58%) and 70 as male (42%); none identified as non-binary nor preferred not to disclose their gender. The mean age was 39.2 years ( 𝑆𝐷 = 12 .7), ranging from 20 to 75 years. Regarding online shopping practices, nearly all participants reported consulting reviews before purchases, with 154 (93%) doing so frequently and 12 (7%) doing so only occasionally. When asked about their level of suspicion regarding fake reviews, 9 participants (5%) reported never or very rarely experiencing such suspicion, 91 (55%) reported sometimes suspecting reviews, and 66 (40%) reported doing so often. In terms of familiarity with Artificial Intelligence (AI), 2 participants (1%) reported low familiarity, 93 (56%) moderate familiarity, and 71 (43%) high familiarity. Table 2 summarizes the demographic and review-related characteristics of the sample. The study employed a within-subjects A/B design with two conditions: a baseline condition using word highlighting and a treatment condition using LLM-based narrative explanations, which were generated through our human-centered methodology. We selected word highlighting as the baseline because it represents a widely used explanation approach in fake review detection research [34, 69]. Specifically, we employed the fake review classifier and the LRP-based word-highlighting explanation model described by [69] as our benchmark. 4 Eliciting and Operationalizing Users’ Domain Model 

In this section, we elaborate on the implementation of Steps 2, 3, and 4 of the MIND-X framework, as introduced in Section 3.2.2, Section 3.2.3, and Section 3.2.4, respectively. We focus on the elicitation of users’ domain models, their subsequent operationalization into explanation mechanisms, and the adaptation of these mechanisms to defined explanation objectives. 

4.1 Users’ Perceptions of What Makes a Review “Fake” 

As part of our methodological approach, uncovering shared mental models, salient cues, and heuristics is pivotal for designing explanations that align with human reasoning. Accordingly, we discuss the findings of our qualitative study in depth in the remainder of this section. This step directly addresses a key limitation of commonly used explanation techniques in fake review detection. While word- or token-level highlighting can be informative in text classification tasks with class-specific lexical patterns, prior work and our reviewer feedback highlight that, in the fake review domain, both human-written and AI-generated reviews frequently employ similar vocabulary. As a result, isolated word attributions often fail to map onto the cues that users themselves rely on when assessing credibility. Eliciting users’ mental models therefore serves as a necessary methodological step to identify which signals are actually meaningful for human sense-making in this domain. With regard to mental models of what constitutes a “fake” review, participants described fakes as communicative acts with ulterior motives: texts written not to share experiences but to per-suade, promote, or deceive. Fake reviews were imagined as strategically constructed performances that exaggerate benefits, suppress flaws, or mimic marketing language. Alongside these broader perceptions, the qualitative analysis reveals how participants oriented themselves toward a set of recurrent cues and cognitive heuristics when evaluating whether a review was authentic or fabricated (see Table 4). These cues were not experienced as isolated markers, but as interpretive resources that participants mobilized in flexible and sometimes conflicting ways. A first prominent theme concerned the role of simple language and conciseness . Participants frequently associated plain, everyday wording and short length with authenticity, describing such reviews as casual and straightforward. As one noted: “Most real reviews are in very short, simple words like ‘good quality, fine with me’” (P5). Concise accounts were treated as honest and aligned with expectations of how an ordinary consumer would write. In contrast, excessive detail was treated as a red flag. Long, elaborate reviews were often read as overcompensating or resembling product descriptions rather than personal experience. One participant captured this view directly: “If the review is too long, that makes me doubt” (P2). The length and density of description thus functioned as triggers of suspicion . Another central heuristic was the presence of balanced pros and cons . Reviews that acknowledged both strengths and weaknesses were considered particularly trustworthy. 4.2 Operationalizing Domain-Sensitive Explanation Agents 

This operationalization step responds directly to the observation that conventional explanation methods in fake review detection predominantly surface token-level relevance, which may reflect statistical correlations learned by the model but not the inferential strategies used by humans. Rather than translating word-level attributions into natural language, our approach starts from the users’ domain models themselves and treats these as first-class explanatory primitives. This shift reframes explanation from exposing model internals to supporting human sense-making. Building on this thematic analysis, we examined how it could be operationalized in an LLM-based explanation agent. The " Cue → Perception → Explanation ” mapping translates perceived deception cues (see Table 4) into a reusable schema: Observed cues (e.g., plain language, staged photography) were systematically linked to user perceptions (trust vs. suspicion), which then motivate explanatory rationales. This mapping serves two functions. First, it provides structured knowledge for embedding into the agent, ensuring that explanations align with how users themselves make sense of reviews. Second, it enables the agent to produce transparent, human-like reasoning paths that mirror natural justificatory practices, rather than abstract, model-internal feature attributions. For example, instead of stating that a review was flagged due to lexical density, the agent could explain: "The language feels too polished and formal, which many people interpret as marketing rather than genuine feedback” .Taken together, this process resulted in the development of a meta-prompt , which operationalizes participants’ domain models into a structured instruction template. From a human-centered perspective, explanation interfaces are not neutral carriers of information but actively shape which aspects of a decision users attend to and how they interpret them. Consequently, interface design must be evaluated in relation to users’ mental models and the types of cues they consider meaningful. This framing aligns the interface comparison with our earlier qualitative findings, which showed that credibility judgments in fake review detection rely on higher-level conceptual cues rather than isolated lexical markers. Regarding visual design , both interfaces use green/red color-coding to encode classification information [7]. The language-based design applies the color at the container (box) level, whereas the highlighting design applies it at the token (word) level within the text. With respect to visual structure, the language-based variant presents rationales in a bullet-point format, enabling users to quickly scan and verify claims much like a diagnostic checklist. The highlighting variant exploits pre-attentive processing [79] by embedding saliency directly in the text via a gradient scale. This supports rapid pattern recognition but yields only a coarse “less–more” spectrum. Through color Leveraging Mental Models for Human-Centered Explanations: A Systematic Design Approach Illustrated by Explainable Fake Review Identification 19 

saturation, lexical tokens are visually marked as points of importance, leaving users to infer their significance. Regarding space design , the language-based design separates the explanation from the source text, requires additional screen real estate, and introduces a spatial remapping between rationales and the review content. In contrast, the highlighting design is co-located with the text and therefore more space-efficient than a separate rationale panel—an advantage for constrained layouts (e.g., mobile). Regarding semantic design , the language-based explanation operates in a propositional mode, offering explicit rationales (e.g., “conflicting stories,” “awkward grammar”). Such categorical state-ments frame the system as an expert narrator that articulates reasons for its judgment. By contrast, the highlighting modality relies on visual variables to signal potentially relevant parts of the text [7]. Drawing on dual-process perspectives [56], the language-based explanation resembles the central route of persuasion, encouraging users to integrate explicit rationales into analytic mental models. The highlighting explanation instead resembles a more peripheral mode of processing, appealing to heuristic, perception-driven judgments by making certain tokens visually stand out. This distinction also resonates with XAI research: natural-language rationales can scaffold interpretability [44, 83], whereas saliency-based explanations emphasize immediacy and transparency by visualizing signals associated with model decisions [42]. The effectiveness of word-level highlighting further depends on whether nearby tokens can be perceived as meaningful units; otherwise, users must cognitively integrate dispersed signals across the text. Crucially, this analysis does not suggest that word-highlighting explanations are inherently flawed. On the contrary, their strength lies in tasks where localized visual emphasis corresponds to meaningful explanatory units. However, in fake review detection, the absence of stable word-level discriminators means that highlighted tokens may appear arbitrary or misleading to end users. In such cases, interfaces that foreground conceptual relations and narrative rationales may better align with how users construct explanations and assess credibility. 

6 Evaluation Study 

Our design analysis indicates that the two explanation types operate on different semiotic and cog-nitive levels and thus exhibit context-specific strengths and limitations. Accordingly, the evaluation study investigated which explanation type is better suited for the domain of fake-review detection. 

Scope and Interpretation of the Evaluation. The goal of this evaluation was not to compare two explanation techniques as interchangeable variants of the same representational form, but to assess their suitability for the specific task ecology of fake review detection. Word- or token-level highlighting has been shown to be effective in domains where class-discriminative features correspond to stable, human-recognizable word sets (e.g., sentiment classification or topic attri-bution). In the fake review context, however, authentic and deceptive reviews often share the same vocabulary, while judgments hinge on higher-level semantic, stylistic, and pragmatic cues (e.g., tone, balance, coherence, persuasive intent). The comparison therefore evaluates whether explanations aligned with empirically elicited user mental models provide greater decision support than model-centric saliency cues in a domain where lexical attribution is structurally limited. Results should be interpreted with this task-specific framing in mind.

6.2 Qualitative Feedback 

6.2.1 General Feedback. The qualitative analysis of the open-ended questions supports and extends the statistical findings of the A/B test by offering insight into how participants experienced and evaluated the two explanation formats. Across responses, participants consistently emphasized the advantages of the language-based explanations, noting their clarity, comprehensibility, and transparency of reasoning, while also 22 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

identifying potential areas for refinement. When explicitly asked about comparative advantages, participants overwhelmingly reported that the language-based approach was superior, whereas only a small minority attributed any benefits to word-highlighting, usually in terms of speed or efficiency rather than explanatory adequacy. Participants’ overall impressions strongly favored the language-based format, which they de-scribed as structured, coherent, and human-like in its provision of rationales rather than fragmented signals. As one participant expressed: “it provided a rational and in-depth explanation for the decision made, it explained the type of words/grammar used, any contradictions, and what would be expected from a real vs fake review” (P704). By contrast, word-highlighting was frequently dismissed as opaque, confusing, or seemingly arbitrary. As another participant noted: “I found the word highlight-ing quite difficult to follow” (P710). These contrasting accounts illustrate how users anchor their judgments of explanation quality not only in the communicative form but also in the perceived cognitive utility of the explanation, thereby underscoring the centrality of interpretability and transparency in shaping user trust and engagement. 

6.2.2 Feedback Themes. The thematic analysis of participants’ accounts highlights how expla-nation formats shape processes of sense-making, trust calibration, and user engagement. In this regard, we uncovered the following themes in the feedback: 

Clarity and Ease of Understanding. Clarity emerged as a decisive theme. Participants repeatedly emphasized that the narrative style of language-based explanations was easy to follow, written in accessible terms, and thus reduced the interpretive burden. As one participant summarized: “it is easy to read and to understand” (P705). From a human–computer interaction perspective, this re-flects the alignment of explanatory discourse with users’ natural interpretive strategies. In contrast, word-highlighting often imposed additional interpretive work, effectively shifting cognitive effort from the system to the user. This demonstrates a key design tension: visual simplicity does not necessarily equate to cognitive simplicity, as the latter requires reducing ambiguity and providing coherent interpretive scaffolds. 

Depth and Informational Richness. Beyond clarity, participants valued the informational richness of the language-based format. Explanations were perceived as embedding the system’s assessment within contextual reasoning, thus enabling participants to appropriate the rationale for their own judgments. The perceived epistemic depth illustrates the importance of explanations that do more than present surface features; they must situate those features in relation to broader linguistic or stylistic patterns. Word-highlighting, by contrast, was experienced as shallow—pointing to lexical elements without articulating why they mattered. This underscores the distinction between feature-level transparency and genuine interpretability: visibility of features alone does not constitute an explanation in the sense required for effective user sense-making. 

Transparency and Logic. Participants often described the narrative explanations as transparent and logically coherent, in contrast to the perceived arbitrariness of highlighting. One participant articulated this contrast explicitly: “Language based is straightforward, human-like and easy to digest while word highlighting ... I cannot understand what it means and what is a reason between all that highlight” (P703). This observation resonates with HCI research emphasizing that transparency depends not simply on exposing system internals, but on communicating reasoning in a way that is intelligible and accountable to users. Trust and Persuasiveness. The persuasive quality of language-based explanations was another salient theme. Participants reported that the detail and coherence of these accounts enhanced their trust in the system’s decision. Explanations were not only clearer but also more convincing, produc-ing what might be termed “persuasive transparency.” Conversely, the arbitrariness of highlighting undermined credibility and raised doubts about the classification process. These findings align with HCI literature that views explanations as central to trust calibration: they are not neutral artifacts but active mediators of user confidence. 

Criticism of Word-highlighting. Word-highlighting was frequently criticized as an underdevel-oped explanation strategy. Participants described it as incomplete, unhelpful, or even misleading. The absence of causal or logical connections between highlighted features and classification out-comes was particularly problematic. In HCI terms, highlighting offered a “what” without a “why,” leaving participants without a reconstructable reasoning process. This critique underscores that explanation strategies must go beyond surface-level visibility to support interpretive accountability. 

Minor Positive Mentions of Word-highlighting. Although the vast majority of participants evaluated word-highlighting negatively, a small number acknowledged its potential efficiency benefits. In these accounts, highlighting was valued as a lightweight heuristic—useful for rapid consumption when depth was not required. Such perspectives suggest that while word-highlighting is insufficient as a stand-alone strategy, it may contribute as part of a hybrid explanation design that flexibly accommodates diverse user needs and contexts. 

Explanatory Anchors. A prominent theme was that participants oriented to explanations as simultaneously anchored in the AI’s reasoning and in the review text as the object of that reasoning. Rather than treating these orientations as distinct, participants often tied the anchors together. They perceived the explanation both as an account of the features of the (fake) review and as an account of the features considered by the AI in making its judgment Some participants foregrounded the review as the primary object of evaluation, treating the explanation as a tool for judging its textual qualities. One participant, for example, remarked: “it shows whether the review is good” (P711). Others emphasized the reasoning of the AI system, focusing on how explanations could clarify the decision process itself. As one noted, “I would like a short note next to the highlights saying why they are relevant” (P704). Many responses, however, blended these orientations by linking linguistic or stylistic markers in the review to the classifier’s evaluative logic. One participant captured this integration clearly: “it provided a rational and in-depth explanation for the decision that was made, explaining the type of words/grammar used, any contradictions, and what would be expected from a real vs fake review” (P704). 

6.2.3 Areas for Improvement. We invited participants to provide open-ended feedback on areas for improvement in both explanation types. The following section summarizes the main insights. 

Improving the Language-based explanations. While the narrative format was strongly preferred, participants identified areas for refinement. Some described the texts as too verbose or repetitive, noting that excessive detail risked producing cognitive overload. Suggestions included shorter, more concise narratives that foreground key points, as well as clearer structuring through emphasis or formatting. Several participants also proposed hybrid approaches, combining narrative explana-tions with selective highlighting to balance richness of reasoning with efficiency of consumption. These reflections illustrate a core challenge in explanation design: reconciling depth and detail 24 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

with accessibility and usability. 

Improving the Word-highlighting explanations. Participants’ recommendations for improving word-highlighting were more fundamental. The dominant critique was that highlighting failed to provide explanatory value without explicit connections to the underlying reasoning. Participants suggested making highlighting more selective and targeted, reducing visual noise, and embedding annotations to contextualize why specific words mattered. These proposals underscore that high-lighting cannot be treated as a self-sufficient explanation but requires integration with explanatory narratives to become meaningful. 

7 Limitations 

In this paper, we demonstrate that our general framework, MIND-X , for human-centered explanation design outperforms existing approaches. However, the empirical evidence is derived from a single application scenario, and its transferability to other domains, modalities, and languages therefore remains an open question. In addition, our elicitation of users’ mental models relies on a small and relatively homogeneous sample of highly educated participants, which limits representativeness. While small samples are common in qualitative research, and our quantitative evaluation suggests that the resulting taxonomy provides a sufficient basis for generating high-quality explanations, broader and more diverse samples would strengthen the robustness of these findings. In the quantitative evaluation, we adopt perceived qualities as evaluation metrics established in the literature [27, 50]. However, behavioral outcomes and longitudinal effects (e.g., changes in actual shopping behavior) fall outside the scope of this study. Another limitation concerns psychometric considerations. Although rigorous construct validation remains relatively rare in XAI studies, we followed APA guidelines and conducted measurement analyses. These analyses revealed weak discriminant validity and substantial inter-correlations among some scales. While this does not affect our central finding, such as users’ stronger preference for the proposed approach, it highlights the need for more robust and orthogonal measurement instruments in future work. Because our evaluation focuses on perceived qualities, we do not assess formal properties of the explanations with respect to the underlying AI model, such as faithfulness or robustness. While such metrics are central in technical XAI research [14], a human-centered perspective emphasizes how users perceive explanations and make sense of them. At the same time, this limitation points to a broader disciplinary gap and highlights the ongoing tension between faithfulness and usefulness in explanation design. 

8 Discussion 8.1 Beyond Model-Centric Explanations: Tailoring to Users’ Mental Models 

Explanation approaches in XAI [14] have traditionally relied on feature-attribution techniques [61] and example-based methods such as counterfactuals [15] to elucidate how inputs influence model decisions. More recent work has shifted toward concept-based explanations [33] and LLM-generated natural-language rationales, aiming to improve comprehensibility. Building on this trajectory, our framework integrates conceptual and narrative explanations [73] with a human-centered design stance [13, 38]. Moreover, MIND-X extends these contributions by providing a six-step, reproducible pipeline that operationalizes them end-to-end, showing how explanations can be aligned with a user domain model of the concepts lay users actually reason with. This represents a shift from model-centric transparency to human-centered account-giving 

grounded in users’ domain mental models. Leveraging Mental Models for Human-Centered Explanations: A Systematic Design Approach Illustrated by Explainable Fake Review Identification 25 

A core aim of MIND-X is to move beyond programmatic claims by demonstrating, through a worked example, how human-centered design practices can be systematically integrated with contemporary XAI techniques. Concretely, we demonstrate how common HCI methods [52] can be adopted to elicit and formalize users’ domain heuristics, and how this knowledge can be used to design structured meta-prompts and explanation schemas [58, 73]. Regarding explanation design, a central architectural contribution of MIND-X is the explicit separation of what to explain from how to explain . By aligning content selection with user- and domain-facing concepts before choosing the communicative form (e.g., narrative rationales, in-situ highlights), the aim of MIND-X is to ensure semantic grounding before determining how explanations are presented.This advances needs-first guidance that surfaces explanatory goals but leaves their operationalization under-specified [1], and extends design-principle syntheses that frame explanations as interactive resources [13, 18] by providing a procedural pipeline with explicit tailoring levers (tone, granularity, framing). It further complements concept-based interpretability that centers model-internal concepts [33, 35] and LLM-generated rationales [73, 83] by binding concept selection to user-domain models and evaluating both model-consistency and domain-justification. Empirically, MIND-X generated explanations showed advantages over token-level word high-lighting [34, 69]. In our benchmark evaluation, we assess six established metrics (understandability, satisfaction, trust, decision support, persuasiveness, perceived cognitive load) commonly used in human-grounded evaluation [27]. Across all six outcomes, participants showed a strong preference for language-based explanations, and all effects remained significant after controlling for multiple comparisons [25]. The largest gains appeared for satisfaction, understandability, and trust; the smallest (yet still significant) improvement concerned perceived cognitive load. Because discrimi-nant validity among scales is weak, we interpret the rank ordering of constructs cautiously due to potential halo effects [26], while our overall superiority claim remains unaffected (each dimension independently favored language-based explanations). This quantitative pattern aligns with our heuristic interface analysis (Section 5) and with our qualitative evidence: participants emphasize clarity, traceability, and trust-building for language explanations, whereas highlighting was often described as “arbitrary” or explanation-poor. This resonates with Joshi et al. [30], underscoring that explainability per se does not guarantee actionable advantages for lay users. However, the strong user preference for our explanation approach should not be interpreted as a dismissal of token-level highlighting. On the contrary, our heuristic analysis (Section 5) indicates that the two explanation types pursue different design goals and excel in different usage contexts. This view is supported by qualitative feedback from the evaluation study: several participants explicitly request hybrid solutions, getting concise narratives complemented by targeted highlights. Taken together, these observations support a multi-modal design stance that combines proposi-tional, narrative rationales [83] with deictic, visual cues [7]. This perspective aligns with current HCI/XAI work that treats explanations not as static representations but as interactive resources 

supporting users’ ongoing sense-making [13, 18]. •Why This Comparison Is Task-Specific Rather Than Method-Generic? A potential concern is that comparing narrative, language-based explanations with token-level highlighting constitutes an unfair or mismatched baseline. We emphasize that this comparison is intentional and task-specific rather than method-generic. Word-highlighting explanations are well-suited to domains where class membership hinges on stable, discriminative lexical features (e.g., sentiment analysis or topical classification). In fake review detection, by contrast, deceptive and genuine reviews frequently share vocabulary, and judgments depend on higher-level semantic, stylistic, and pragmatic cues. The comparison therefore probes explanation–task alignment: whether explanations grounded in user mental models better support sense-making in a domain where word-level attribution is structurally 26 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

limited. We do not claim universal superiority of narrative explanations, but domain-appropriate adequacy. Our findings highlight the need for human-centered, domain-specific explanation design, which produces necessarily incomplete [32], yet contextually sufficient accounts for all practical pur-poses [22]. Supporting context-specific tailor-ability, the proposed approach extends existing ap-proaches in a structured manner. It implements a concept-first separation of what to explain 

(user-aligned concepts) from how to explain (tailored narratives), echoing a concept-bottleneck [35] and needs-first rationale [40], while leveraging LLMs for adaptive outputs. This separation supports LLM-based explanation design [83] by ensuring that explanation content is grounded before being adapted to different communicative contexts [13]. As a result, explanations can remain semantically meaningful while also being recognizable and actionable in specific use contexts. 

8.2 Dealing with the Explanation Dilemma: Faithfulness vs. Situated Intelligibility 

The qualitative feedback also reveals that participants repeatedly characterize the narratives as structured, coherent, and human-like, while describing the highlighting baseline as opaque and seemingly arbitrary. Placed in a wider epistemic frame, this observation directly challenges the positivist, causal-realist orientation that still underwrites much XAI work, where explanatory legitimacy is cast in correspondence terms: an explanation is “good” insofar as it faithfully re-flects internal model causality [14]. By contrast, social-constructivist accounts ask us to evaluate explanations with respect to their interpretant —the meaning taken up by a human interpreter and their role in situated sense-making [80]. From this standpoint, explanations are not neutral mirrors but accounts rendered intelligible and actionably adequate within specific practices. From the causal-realist stance, however, we encounter a stark dilemma, well recognized in CSCW when formal systems meet lived practice [74]: 

• Horn A: Formally Faithful Yet Socially Unintelligible. On the one hand, we have models and post hoc methods that boast formally demonstrated accuracy or faithfulness to their internal mechanisms (as our benchmark model does [69]). Yet, as our study demonstrates, these expla-nations risk being experienced by users as opaque, arbitrary, or merely gestural, a transparency gloss that reveals internals without rendering them accountable to the task at hand. The correspondence claim holds, but the account fails: the explanation does not travel from model semantics to human sense-making. 

• Horn B: Socially Intelligible Yet Epistemically Unsecured. On the other hand, we have explana-tions that are reported as useful, persuasive, and intelligible—precisely because they are shaped by human explanatory practices [42, 44]. Yet their epistemic status is undetermined: their generative mechanisms are only partially understood, their quality is not guaranteed by formal criteria, and they are vulnerable to well-documented failure modes such as hallucination [37]. Here the account succeeds in practice, but its warrant relative to the model’s actual inferential grounds remains uncertain. This is not a superficial trade-off but an epistemic double bind . The two horns index incom-mensurate accountability targets : (i) accountability to the system’s causal story (model-internal semantics) and (ii) accountability to the world of work in which people must judge, justify, and act (domain-level sense-making). In CSCW terms, the former privileges formal representations; the latter privileges situated action and ethnomethodological accountability [22, 67]. Explanations confined to one pole predictably underperform at the other: model-faithful rationales that no one can use, or human-friendly stories that are weakly tethered to what the model in fact did. As Kunz and Kuhlmann [37] caution, the absence of model-centric faithfulness can be dangerous if it results in persuasive but misleading accounts. Yet the inverse holds as well: an exclusive focus Leveraging Mental Models for Human-Centered Explanations: A Systematic Design Approach Illustrated by Explainable Fake Review Identification 27 

on faithfulness can itself be dangerous when it produces only a transparency gloss , inviting a false sense of understanding while failing to support situated sense-making. 

8.3 From Correspondence to Double Accountability : Bridging Model Reasoning and Domain Sense-Making 

Our empirical results suggest a way through. Our study shows that explanations are judged neither as mere reflections of model logic nor as mere summaries of textual evidence, but as interpretive resources that must bridge both . Moreover, our findings suggest that people do not treat explanations as either mirrors of model computation or mirrors of textual reality. Rather, they hold explanations to a dual standard of accountability : simultaneously to the AI’s decision procedure ( “Why does the classifier decide X?” ) and to the review as a world-facing object of judgment ( “Why is this review fake/real?” ). Participants’ comparative evaluations and open-ended comments make clear that these orientations are not experienced as separable tasks but as a single, doubly oriented sense-making problem: a good explanation has to bridge the two, showing how the review’s features could be read as supporting the system’s assessment and how the system’s assessment made the review intelligible as fake or genuine. Instead of relying on a single criterion of faithfulness , a broader notion of explanatory adequacy is needed [73]. In hybrid human–AI decision environments [60], explanations must support both the interpretation of model outputs and the interpretation of domain evidence.We therefore propose a two-dimensional conceptualization of explanatory adequacy : model-fit (does the account render the system’s decision procedure intelligible and checkable?) and world-fit (does the account embed the decision in recognizable, assessable domain evidence). In semiotic terms, adequacy is a property of the explanation–interpreter relation (its interpretant ), not solely of the explanation–model correspondence. In ethnomethodological terms, adequacy is achieved when an explanation functions as a work practice for making a decision accountable 

[22, 67], both to the classifier’s reasoning and to the object of judgment. 

8.4 Implications for Human-Centered XAI 

Our study allows us to make several suggestions for designing human-centered XAI systems. First, evaluation criteria must be re-framed. Metrics that certify only internal correspondence undervalue the very qualities users report needing (sense-making, justificatory traction, actionability), while metrics that certify only human preference risk licensing fluent but unfounded stories. A double-accountability regime therefore calls for paired assessments: stress-tests of model-consistency and 

task-grounded tests of justificatory adequacy (can users reconstruct why this evidence warrants 

this decision, and contest it when it does not?). Second, design should target account-giving rather than mere feature-showing . Narrative, cue-linked rationales can make the mapping from domain evidence to model judgment explicit, while selective highlighting or structured artifacts (e.g., evidence tables, contrastive cases) provide check-able anchors. In this hybrid, symbolic accounts perform the justificatory work; indexical cues keep the account tethered to inspectable evidence. Finally, double accountability clarifies additional responsibilities: System developers owe users model-grounded warrants (avoiding unfalsifiable glosses); interface designers owe users situated intelligibility (avoiding alluring but unmoored stories); and evaluators owe the field protocols that test both anchors together. 

9 Conclusion and Outlook 

This paper advances explainable AI by introducing MIND-X , a human-centered framework that grounds explanations in users’ mental models and common-sense reasoning rather than in model-centric attributions. Methodologically, MIND-X proceduralizes prior human-centered guidance 28 Sidra Naveed, Md Shajalal, Gunnar Stevens, and Alexander Boden 

into a six-step, reproducible pipeline and treats LLMs as orchestrated explanation agents , making design assumptions explicit, auditable, and portable across domains. Empirically, the case study in fake-review detection shows that narrative, heuristic-aligned accounts foster intelligibility, trust, and decision support, highlighting the limits of token-level transparency. Qualitative feedback further corroborates these results, revealing that users experi-enced narrative explanations as more insightful, both in terms of why reviews might be fake and why they were ultimately classified as such. In this regard, our study demonstrates the need for 

double accountability —to both model logic and human sense-making. Importantly, these findings should not be read as a general indictment of word-level attribution techniques. Rather, they underscore that explanation effectiveness is contingent on task structure and the nature of the evidence users rely on. While token-level highlighting may be effective in domains with stable, class-discriminative lexical cues, fake review detection relies heavily on higher-order semantic, stylistic, and pragmatic judgments. Our contribution therefore lies in demonstrating the importance of explanation–task alignment, rather than asserting a universally superior explanation modality. By framing explanations as discursive accounts rather than technical artifacts, our aim is to move beyond the mere gloss of transparency toward explanations that resonate with everyday reasoning and support actionable judgment. Looking ahead, three directions for future research emerge. First, the emergence of large lan-guage models (LLMs) as agents of explanation raises concerns about consumer manipulation and the potential for ethics-washing through superficial explanatory glosses, thereby necessitating safeguards against persuasive yet weakly substantiated accounts. Second, although the study results show a strong preference for our approach, user feedback also indicates that design refinements should explore hybrid formats combining narrative rationales with selective visual cues. For this reason, future research should consider adaptive interfaces that dynamically tailor explanations to users’ expertise, goals, and situational constraints. Third, while our study focuses exclusively on subjective perception, taking the risk of misleading explanations seriously requires evaluating them not only through model-centric metrics or self-reported perceptions, but also in ecologically valid contexts that examine behavioral and longitudinal outcomes such as decision accuracy, resilience to manipulation, and long-term trust calibration. In this way, future research can move toward a more comprehensive paradigm of explainable AI, one that reconciles technical fidelity with human intelligibility and social responsibility, upholds epistemic responsibility, equipping users with explanations that are not only transparent but also actionable in everyday decision-making.
