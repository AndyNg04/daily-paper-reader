# Abstract 

Presence-aware intelligence is the capacity of an AI system’s intelligence layer to adapt 

appropriately to the functional state of the person it serves (here readiness, flow, and stability) 

while preserving task quality and safety. We introduce PresenceBench, an instrument for 

measuring it. PresenceBench evaluates how the intelligence layer uses a supplied state, not how 

that state is sensed, and breaks the capability into measurable properties. Two are tested here. 

Receptivity: does the model take up the state and give a better response than with no signal? 

Specificity: does any improvement depend on the correct state, rather than on extra structured 

context? A third, adaptation across states, is a required endpoint not measured in this release. A 

five-arm causal design answers both questions, against each model’s own baseline and an 

equally structured mismatched-state control, scored by a correctness-gated human paired 

comparison. The exploratory evaluation covered four models across eighteen single-turn, six 

fixed three-turn and six simulated natural-conversation scenarios. Supplying a synthetic state, 

raw or translated into a response policy, gave human-preference point estimates above 0.5 for 

all four models (Claude Sonnet 5, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Gemini 3.6 Flash, GPT-5.6 Terra), with 

three of eight 95% intervals excluding chance. Specificity was not resolved: no per-model 

interval excluded chance, and the design cannot yet separate the state values from a generic 

instruction to adapt. Policy delivery reduced priced cost and output tokens against each model’s 

baseline; these are resource-use changes, not efficiency gains, because correctness non-

inferiority was not established. Safety-floor failures were rare (13 of 360 cases), with a non-

significant excess under policy delivery. We report the instrument, the frozen results, and the 

controls a confirmatory study needs.

PresenceBench is a benchmark and measurement protocol for  presence-aware intelligence : ap‐ 

propriate adaptation to a person’s functional state (here readiness, flow, and stability) while 

preserving task quality and safety. This paper introduces the instrument and reports its first, 

exploratory application to language models, which examines one route to that capability: the 

incremental benefit and specificity of an externally supplied state representation. The 

instrument’s core methodological contribution is a  five-arm causal design  that separates two 

questions usually conflated.  Receptivity:  does the model take up the supplied presence and use 

it to produce a better response than no signal at all (measured against each model’s own vanilla 

output)?  Specificity:  is any improvement due to the model reading the  correct  state, rather than 

a generic benefit of extra structured context (measured against an equally structured  mis‐ 

matched -state control)? We evaluate four model families (Claude Sonnet 5, DeepSeek V4 Pro,

simulated natural-conversation scenarios, scored on a  correctness-gated human paired end‐

contrast; for the scripted core, 3,840 planned and 3,839 observed generations, one quarantined, 

no imputation). Of 600 planned paired comparisons, 599 received an analyzable primary rating: 

359 single-turn, 120 fixed three-turn and 120 simulated-conversation pairs. All three forms

timates  over each model’s own vanilla output, raw and canonical, with  three of the eight  95%

# 1. Motivation and construct 

A presence signal supplies a model with a compact description of the user’s current functional 

state. This study measures two distinct things.  Receptivity  is whether a model  uses  that signal 

to produce a better response than it gives with no signal at all.  Specificity  is whether any such 

improvement depends on reading the  correct  state, rather than being a generic benefit of adding 

structured context. The distinction matters because a model can look receptive simply because 

more context was added, without adapting to the specific state; the benchmark separates the two 

with mismatched-state controls. Its governing questions are (i) how models compare in 

consuming presence, and (ii) how UX and resource use change relative to each model’s own 

vanilla behavior. Safety is reported separately as a mandatory safety-floor observation (Sec‐ 

tion 6). 

The construct and the instrument.  We define presence-aware intelligence as appropriate 

adaptation to a person’s functional state while preserving task quality and safety. The definition 

concerns observable assistance, not whether a model internally infers state. This study examines 

one route to that capability, the incremental benefit and specificity of an externally supplied 

state representation: whether supplying the state improves what the person receives 

(receptivity), and whether that improvement depends on the correct state (specificity). Whether 

behavior moves appropriately as the state changes (adaptation) is a required benchmark 

endpoint that this release did not measure; the full benchmark adds it (Section 7). Vanilla 

designated-state adequacy (Table 3) describes how suitable the no-capsule response was for the 

designated state. High baseline adequacy can leave less room for improvement, but does not 

establish state inference or state-responsive adaptation. The model comparisons here therefore 

concern the uptake of supplied presence, not presence-awareness overall. PresenceBench is 

> 3 of 33

built as a versioned instrument whose components (tasks, state ontology, capsule contract, 

orchestration policy, responder configuration, and evaluation) are frozen and reported with 

every result. This release evaluates one comparison target, the intelligence layer. The same 

comparability rules are designed to extend to comparisons of contracts, orchestration policies, 

and presence providers, each of which requires its own validated protocol. This paper reports 

the first exploratory application of PresenceBench (mini). 

The presence representation used in this study 

In human-AI collaboration the human is not only an overseer who approves what a system 

proposes; the human is part of the intelligence layer, taking in information and contributing 

judgments and decisions back into the loop. Either way, a collaborating system does better 

when it knows not just  whether  the person is engaged but  how  they are engaging right now, 

because that shapes how much to surface, how to pace it, when to hand back, and when to wait. 

Presence is not a binary of present versus absent; it is a graded, moment-to-moment read of that 

engagement. The presence representation used here (developed by Area2; see Disclosures) is 

designed for real-time collaboration: to describe how a person is engaging rather than any 

condition, with readiness, flow, and stability informing how much structured complexity to 

place in front of the person, whether now is a good moment to interrupt, and whether to proceed 

or wait. These are design intentions and are not evaluated here.  This experiment did not use a 

live sensing channel:  it supplied engineered, designated state fixtures in the PresenceBench 

capsule format (Appendix B). The design makes presence a plausible input for a collaborating 

model to  consume ; whether frontier models actually do is the question this benchmark 

measures. Appendix A defines the channels as used in this study. 

# 1a. Related work 

Automated grading of open-ended model output with an LLM judge is standard practice (MT-

Bench and Chatbot Arena, Zheng et al. 2023; length-controlled AlpacaEval, Dubois et al. 2024), 

and the same literature documents its failure modes: self-enhancement and position bias (Zheng 

et al. 2023), length / verbosity effects specifically (Dubois et al. 2024), position bias in depth 

(Wang et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2024), and self-preference, the tendency of a judge to favor its own 

generations (Panickssery et al. 2024). We therefore treat the LLM judge here as an instrument to 

validate against humans, not as ground truth, and report it only as an appendix triangulation. 

Pairwise human preference remains the reference standard in open model comparison (Chiang 

et al. 2024), and multi-metric benchmark practice (Liang et al. 2023) informs our reporting of 

quality, resource use, and safety side by side rather than as a single score. 

Our task framing relates to steerability and persona/system-prompt conditioning (Zhang et al. 

2018; Santurkar et al. 2023; Samuel et al. 2024) and to instruction-following evaluation (Zhou 

et al. 2023), which measure whether a model  follows  injected instructions or personas; Pres‐ 

> 4 of 33

enceBench differs by isolating  state-specific  adaptation from generic instruction-following 

through matched wrong-state controls (Sections 2, 4). Personalization benchmarks condition 

outputs on a user’s profile or history (Salemi et al. 2023), and empathetic-dialogue work 

conditions on emotion expressed in the conversation itself (Rashkin et al. 2019); the presence 

state used here is instead a momentary functional state supplied as structured context, separate 

from both the user’s profile and the content of the request. 

The construct also has a long precursor literature outside LLM evaluation:  affective computing 

(Picard 1997) and  adaptive / user-modeling interfaces  (Fischer 2001) have for decades stud‐ 

ied systems that sense a user’s state and adapt, including when an assistant should act or defer 

(Horvitz 1999), the cost of interruptions to ongoing work (Czerwinski et al. 2004), and how 

much information a person can process at once (Sweller 1988). PresenceBench ports that 

question to frontier LLMs with an explicit wrong-state control. The closest recent precedent is 

Liu et al. (2026), who timed LLM assistance from electrodermal and mouse-movement signals 

and compared aligned adaptive timing against misaligned and random controls in a within-

subjects study (N = 32); aligned timing improved response accuracy by 21%. PresenceBench 

applies the same misaligned-control logic to the consumption step, with the state held fixed. 

Finally, recent interpretability work (Sofroniew et al. 2026) shows models form and causally 

use internal representations of human functional concepts such as emotion. That line asks 

whether such concepts exist and act  inside  the model; PresenceBench asks the complementary, 

behavior-level question, whether an  externally supplied  functional-state signal changes what the 

user receives, relative to vanilla and to a wrong-state control.

# 3. Human reference and inference 

Rater structure.  Each pair received one preassigned  primary  rating (599/600), with primary 

assignments split across two raters; a  second, independent  rating was collected on a stratified 

20% overlap (120/120) for reliability. Human evaluation covered eighteen single-turn scenarios, 

six fixed three-turn scenarios and six simulated natural-conversation scenarios. Of 600 planned 

paired comparisons, 599 received an analyzable primary rating: 359 single-turn, 120 fixed 

three-turn and 120 simulated-conversation pairs. All three forms contribute to the human-rated 

quality, receptivity, specificity and provisional roster analyses. The 120 reliability overlaps 

comprise 96 core and 24 simulated-conversation pairs; overlaps are used for reliability, not 

added to the primary sample. Both raters were external to Area2 and uninvolved in designing 

the capsule, policy, or scenarios. Model, provider, arm, and state identity labels were masked, 

while the designated state values and scoring guidance were shown. This is not a multi-rater 

consensus panel: each point estimate rests on one primary rating per pair, and the second rating 

exists only to quantify agreement. Because of this we call the endpoint  human-referenced , not 

“human-authoritative,” throughout.

Each rater applied a correctness-gated paired scorecard: independent 1–5 task-correctness and 

designated-state adequacy for each side plus an overall A/B/tie preference; a response that fails 

correctness earns no preference credit; safety was assessed separately (Section 6).  Inter-rater 

reliability (Gwet AC2) on the overlap: correctness 0.959, adequacy 0.884, but the overall 

pair-winner, the endpoint every headline number is computed from, is only 0.551 (moder‐ 

ate).  The gap from the component scores is consistent with a near-tie forced choice: correctness 

and adequacy are per-side ordinal ratings, whereas the pair-winner is a single derived A/B/tie 

preference that is hardest to agree on precisely when the two candidates are close, so high 

component agreement can coexist with only moderate winner agreement. We flag it rather than 

explain it away: it is an inter-rater  reliability  statistic, not a numerical ceiling to be compared 

against effect magnitudes, and moderate winner agreement limits confidence in the preference 

estimates; the reported intervals do not capture between-rater uncertainty. The first item on the 

confirmatory roadmap is therefore to  pin and calibrate the pair-winner decision rule first 

(disambiguating the tie-break among gate-surviving candidates and clearing a reliability target 

on a calibration set), and only then open two independent full-coverage rater queues (Section 7). 

Inference uses scenario/trajectory-cluster bootstrap 95% intervals and standardization over the 

frozen design distribution; multiplicity is presented, not gated confirmatorily; missingness is 

retained, not imputed. Machine judges (Claude, GPT, conflict-aware) provide appendix-level 

triangulation only and never rank models (GPT showed 16.7% order reversal in earlier 

auditing); the executed evaluation endpoint is summarized in  Appendix E (Figure 6) .

# 7. Limitations and full-benchmark roadmap 

What this run suggests, and what stays open.  Read as an exploratory preview, the run points 

one way and leaves the decisive questions for the confirmatory study. It  suggests  a generalized 

> 19 of 33

improvement from supplying presence over no signal: vanilla-relative receptivity leaned 

positive for every model in both delivery modes, and three of the eight intervals exclude 

chance. It also  suggests  room for more efficient resource use, since per-task token, latency, and 

cost changes were often favorable, though this stays a resource-use observation rather than an 

efficiency claim until correctness non-inferiority is established. Two questions stay genuinely 

open. First, whether models are reading the  correct  state or simply responding to added direc‐ 

tion: state-specificity was not resolved at this N, so uptake being state-specific rather than 

generic remains to be shown. Second, whether the raw state is better than an orchestrated 

policy: specificity was delivery-mode dependent, with the raw signal giving the cleaner 

matched-state advantage and no aggregate correctness cost, while canonical orchestration was 

unresolved and carried a small correctness-pass tradeoff (Section 4). Nothing here settles those 

two; the roadmap below is built to settle them. 

Limitations.  Four-model reduced-N exploratory run; per-model intervals overlap materially, so 

the roster is reported as a provisional, specificity-based set of non-dominated models with 

practical ties, not a strict winner (Section 4). With  ≈30 pairs per model per contrast  and only 

moderate pair-winner reliability (AC2 0.551), intervals are wide and the study cannot resolve 

small state-specific differences; the near-chance specificity is a  failure to detect , not evidence of 

absence.  No prespecified power or minimum-detectable-effect analysis was frozen, so we 

report none , uncertainty is conveyed by the intervals themselves. The pair-winner endpoint 

rests on a single primary rating per pair. One core cell and one primary human pair are 

explicitly missing and not imputed. Human raters are shared across workflows, so evidence 

components are not fully independent. Human safety is stratified, not exhaustive at the UX-cell 

level, and per-arm counts are sparse. Correctness non-inferiority is not established for any 

resource contrast. Three of the four models (Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-5.6 Terra, and DeepSeek V4 

Pro) were called through model aliases without a dated snapshot, and only Gemini 3.6 Flash ran 

on a stable provider version, so exact model-version replication is limited and the frozen 

evidence binds what we could hash, not the upstream weights. Fixed three-turn interactions do 

not measure natural stopping or repair burden. The nine-state ontology was covered by balanced 

incomplete assignment at reduced N, so per-state cell counts are small and fine-grained state 

effects are not resolvable here. The scenarios and states are engineered fixtures: this lets us 

control the designated state exactly, but the fixtures may not capture the full complexity of a 

real user in a given state reacting to stateful versus stateless responses, so external validity to 

live use is untested here. No executed-code or agentic tool-use tasks were included; technical 

responses were judged against written criteria. 

The simulated natural-conversation component exposed difficulties in generating realistic user 

behavior and reliable task termination. A separate controller audit identified violations of task-

information and protocol constraints, preventing the use of simulator-derived resolution and 

adaptive-efficiency outcomes as benchmark results. Human ratings of the recorded interactions 

> 20 of 33

are included in the exploratory analyses, with a core-only sensitivity reported separately. 

Transfer to live-user conversations remains untested. 

What the receptivity result does and does not identify.  The study identifies positive vanilla-

relative receptivity point estimates, but it does  not  determine whether those estimates arise from 

the  correct  state values, from generic adaptation framing, or from structured policy guidance: 

the design has no generic-policy or numbers-stripped control to separate these. Accurate-state 

specificity and the incremental value of state-tailored translation therefore remain unresolved 

and require the additional controls in the roadmap below. This is distinct from state-sensing 

accuracy, which is out of scope here by design: PresenceBench supplies the  designated  state de‐ 

liberately and evaluates only how models consume it. Validation of live sensing is outside this 

study; these estimates concern supplied synthetic state fixtures.

alone, to separate the generic adaptation directive, generic structured policy, and the length

state coverage across the nine-state ontology at adequate N; (4) broaden the roster and require 

dated snapshots for every model; (5) resolve the correctness-non-inferiority and safety-precision

day and with their own state, and whether it differs between work and non-work contexts. They 

are not part of the confirmatory roadmap above.

How it is scored.  Both candidate trajectories in a pair are judged against the real state; the 

deterministic oracle provides an advisory task-success and floor screen; the safety floor itself is 

decided by human raters; and the human scorecard records correctness, state adequacy, and A/ 

B/tie with correctness gating; safety assessed separately. Illustrative expected contrast (for 

intuition only, not a quoted model output): under the low state a good response stays brief and 

low-branching but still leads with  ACTION: call emergency services now  ; under peak 

it may be denser, but the same floor applies. A response that used “low capacity” to hedge the 

emergency would fail the floor regardless of adequacy, the mechanism behind the per-arm 

safety observations in Section 6.
