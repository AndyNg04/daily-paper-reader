Abstract 

The friends of a randomly chosen social media user are, on average, better connected than the user, a network property known as the friend-ship paradox. We use it to monitor misinformation on Twitter/X around the 2024 US election, comparing roughly 27 million tweets from random users with 31 million from the accounts they retweet, which we call sen-sors. Sensors share news of about the same quality as random users, be-cause news quality follows the political orientation of linked outlets and not the user’s position in the network. News links rated by NewsGuard reach sensors a median of 70 minutes earlier, across the quality spectrum. Over the election window, right-leaning content rises about 2.5-fold in visibility, measured as impressions per tweet, among sensors but not among random users. Friendship-paradox sampling therefore cannot separate low-quality from high-quality news, but it detects news early and tracks what a plat-form makes visible. 

# 1 Introduction 

Detecting misinformation early in its spread on social media is a recurring chal-lenge for empirical research and platform governance in the attempt to safe-guard public discourse. While misinformation is only a small fraction of overall 1arXiv:2610.16907v1 [cs.SI] 12 Oct 2026 media consumption [1], its potent spread [2] raises the question of how it can be detected and counteracted before reaching wide audiences. Existing interventions such as accuracy nudges [3], inoculation [4, 5], and cor-rections [6] can demonstrably reduce belief in misinformation and its sharing in experimental settings. However, these approaches typically intervene after misinformation has been identified, instead of at the start of a sharing cascade. Corrections face the additional challenge that misinformation continues to in-fluence judgments even after it has been debunked [7], which makes early in-tervention all the more important. Bak-Coleman et al. [8] showed this by simu-lating intervention strategies on 10.5 million tweets from misinformation events around the 2020 US presidential election. In their model, removing a false claim within 30 minutes cuts its total spread by about 94 percent. The same removal after four hours cuts it by only 56 percent, and no single intervention was suffi-cient on its own at realistic levels, so countermeasures have to come early. 

Figure 1: The friendship paradox in a network of ten people, who average 2.00 friends while their friends average 2.47. In the circled group the orange node has 2 friends, these friends have 1 and 4 friends, so the friends average 2.5, more than the node’s own 2. We use the friendship para-dox [9], a network property according to which an in-dividual’s friends have, on average, more friends than that individual, to identify users who are positioned to receive content earlier than randomly sampled users. Those more centrally posi-tioned users are referred to as sensor users. This coun-terintuitive phenomenon arises from a fundamental sampling bias: individu-als with many connections (high-degree nodes) appear as friends to numerous peo-ple, while less-connected individuals appear to fewer. As a result, the popular individuals are counted many times when we look at the friends of others, and they pull the average up. Figure 1 shows a small example network that illustrates this mechanism. On Twitter/X the paradox is especially pronounced. 2Upwards of 90% of users have fewer followers than the accounts they follow [10, 11]. In previous studies, the friendship paradox has enabled early detection of con-tagious outbreaks [12] and content diffusion on social media [13]. A study about a flu outbreak on a campus detected epidemic progression 13.9 days ear-lier in the friend group than the general population [12]. More comparable to our study, García-Herranz et al. [13] applied the friendship paradox to Twit-ter/X by constructing a sensor group from the friends of randomly selected users and tracking hashtag adoption in both samples in parallel. Because high-degree users are overrepresented in the sensor group, hashtags trending in the broader network appeared there first on average, and about 7 days earlier than in the random sample. We extend this methodology to misinformation, using two large-scale Twitter/X datasets (approximately 27 million tweets from ran-domly selected users and 31 million from their network neighbors) to compare information quality and temporal dynamics, as well as time advantage and ex-posure of (mis)information between these two samples. Our study is guided by three research questions: 

RQ1: Do users occupying more central network positions, as identified through the friendship paradox, share more misinformation than randomly selected users? 

RQ2: Can the friendship paradox be leveraged to detect misinformation shar-ing earlier than would be possible through random observation of the net-work? 

RQ3 (exploratory): Does friendship-paradox sampling reveal platform-level shifts in content exposure that remain invisible in random samples? Unlike RQ1 and RQ2, the third question was not part of the original study de-sign. It emerged when the temporal analyses revealed asymmetric impression dynamics in the sensor sample, and we report the corresponding results as ex-ploratory findings. To foreshadow briefly, the answer to RQ1 is no. Sensor users do not share more misinformation than randomly selected users, and the divide in information quality runs along political orientation, not network position. The answer to RQ2 is yes, but without a quality bias. News links propagate through sen-sor users with a median lead of 70 minutes over random users, and this lead 3applies equally to low- and high-credibility sources. The most consequential findings concern RQ3, because over the examination period from June to Oc-tober 2024 impressions per tweet on right-leaning content in the sensor sample rise roughly 2.5-fold while left-leaning impressions remain flat, and the same asymmetry persists at the user level when posting activity is weighted by users’ sharing orientation. None of this is visible in the random sample, where the right-leaning series stays flat at the content level and even drifts slightly down-ward at the user level. We therefore argue that friendship-paradox sampling is a tool for monitoring platform-level exposure dynamics and a poor detector of content quality.

# 2.2 Information Quality: Overall Comparison 

Figure 2 shows NewsGuard score distributions for sensor and random datasets. We find no meaningful difference in overall information quality between high-degree (sensor) and random users when examining raw distributions. The gen-eralized friendship paradox would predict such a difference only if information quality rose with a user’s connectivity, and our data suggest it does not. Both distributions are broadly similar, with comparable shapes and central tenden-cies. The co-occurrent peaks in both datasets arise because NewsGuard scores are not continuous. Each score is the sum of nine binary yes or no criteria with different point values, ranging from 22 points for not repeatedly publishing false or egregiously misleading content down to 5 points for naming content creators, so only a limited set of score values can occur. A formal statistical comparison reveals a small but statistically significant dif-ference in NewsGuard scores between the two samples, with a median score of 90 in the random dataset and 91.25 in the sensor dataset. The estimated me-dian difference is ∆ = 1 .232 , with a 95% confidence interval of [0 .724 , 1.566] .The corresponding effect size is Cliff’s δ = −0.056 , and a Mann–Whitney U test yields a p-value of 8.82 × 10 −6.The near-zero Cliff’s delta indicates that knowing whether a user is part of the sensor or random group provides essentially no information about the quality of the media outlet they share. Whether central users share better or worse in-formation was an open question with arguments on both sides. Friends have been argued to receive higher quality information than random users [11], al-though that conclusion rested on popularity as a proxy for quality. The rapid spread of false news raised the opposite concern, namely that highly connected users could act as hubs of misinformation, even though the spreaders of false news in that study were not better connected than other users [2]. Our data sup-port neither direction, because information quality is essentially independent of network position. # 2.3 Information Quality: Political Affiliation 

Figure 2 also reveals that the divide in information quality runs along the po-litical orientation of the linked outlets and not along network position. Orien-tation is a domain-level attribute in the NewsGuard data, so we group tweets by whether they link to outlets labeled as left-leaning or right-leaning and com-pare the NewsGuard score distributions of the two groups within each sample. Tweets linking to left-leaning outlets show consistently high quality, with an in-terquartile range of 87.50 to 100 in the sensor sample and 80.00 to 100 in the ran-dom sample. Tweets linking to right-leaning outlets score substantially lower, with an interquartile range of 20.00 to 69.50 in the sensor sample and 30.00 to 69.50 in the random sample. The pattern holds in both samples, which indicates that the political orientation of the shared outlet, not the network centrality of the user sharing it, predicts the quality of shared news. This asymmetry sits in the supply of news, not in the users who share it. Low NewsGuard scores concentrate among right-leaning outlets, and the pattern is equally visible in both samples, so being a high-degree sensor user does not amplify the gap. Prior research approaches the same divide from the demand side and shows that fake news exposure and sharing concentrates among con-servative users [14, 15, 16]. Osmundsen et al. [17] connect the two sides. Peo-ple share news that matches their partisan identity, and the low-quality outlets happen to sit mostly on the right. The same sharing motive on both sides there-fore produces different amounts of misinformation. None of this depends on a user’s position in the network, which fits the gap appearing unchanged across both samples.

# 3 Discussion 

RQ1: Do high-degree nodes share more misinformation? No. In terms of me-dian content quality, Sensor (high-degree) users actually share slightly higher quality content than random users (median NewsGuard 91.25 vs 90.00), though the effect size is negligible (Cliff’s δ = −0.056 ). However, when we account for reach through exposure-weighted analysis, Sensor users show substantially higher misinformation intensity (Cliff’s δ = −0.399 ).This reflects amplification and not a tendency to share misinformation. High-degree nodes are not more likely to share it, but what they share reaches larger audiences. Information quality divides along political orientation, and network position plays no vis-ible role. Right-leaning media score substantially lower on NewsGuard and show high variability in quality particularly when retweeted, while left-leaning media maintain consistently high quality scores regardless of network central-ity. 

RQ2: Can the friendship paradox enable earlier detection of misinforma-tion? Yes, but early detection is not linked to low quality information. Sensor users share news links 70 minutes earlier (median) than random users, with the sensor group posting first in 67% of comparisons. This time advantage shows no systematic dependence on information quality ( r = 0 .001 within the well-populated score range, r = 0 .051 across all rated pairs). The general link stream consists mostly of references to other tweets and reaches the random sample 36 minutes after the sensors, while news links take 70 minutes, so news travels along a slower path than the everyday traffic of the platform and monitoring gains a longer head start on news. 

RQ3: Does friendship-paradox sampling reveal platform-level exposure shifts that random samples miss? Yes. Over the June – October 2024 window, mean impressions per tweet on right-leaning media in the sensor sample rise by 14 a factor of roughly 2.5 (Mann-Kendall τ = 0 .54 , p < 0.001 ), while left-leaning impressions remain flat. The same asymmetry persists at the user level when general posting activity on tweets without NewsGuard-rated links is weighted by users’ NewsGuard-orientation share ( τ = 0 .534 , p = 3 .3 × 10 −8). In the random sample the content-level series stays flat and the user-level series drifts downward. The sensor sample therefore picks up an election-period amplifi-cation of right-leaning content that is invisible in the random baseline and even reversed at the user level. Our findings clarify the function of high-degree nodes for the spread of misin-formation in social networks. The work of Vosoughi et al. [2] established that false news diffuses farther, faster, and more broadly than true news on Twitter. Subsequent work by Allen et al. [1] and Grinberg et al. [16] contextualized these concerns by demonstrating that fake news represents a small share of overall news consumption and is concentrated among a minority of users. Our findings feed the perspective, in that misinformation sharing reflects specific ideological behavior and not a general property of the social network struc-ture. The absence of a generalized friendship paradox specifically for misinformation is theoretically significant. The generalized friendship paradox extends to nu-merous attributes that correlate with network centrality such as happiness [19], scientific productivity [20], or socially influenced behavior [21]. Our finding suggests that misinformation does not function as a centrality-correlated trait. Our partisan-stratified results indicate that the dominant axis of variation in source credibility is political orientation, which is not correlated with network position in our sample. This mirrors the individual-level evidence of Osmund-sen et al. [17]. People share fake news out of hostility toward the other political side, not because they cannot tell true from false, and the supply of shareable falsehoods happens to lean right. Neither the hostility nor the supply cares about a user’s position in the network. A partisan motivation meeting a par-tisan supply therefore predicts exactly what we observe, a quality divide that follows political orientation and ignores network centrality. The second contribution of our study is methodological. Friendship-paradox sampling has been validated as an early-detection tool for hashtag adoption [13] and epidemic spread [12]. Our results indicate that the same sampling logic can be re-purposed into a monitoring instrument for platform dynamics. 15 The triangulation works through the contrast between the two samples. Both are drawn from the same platform, cover the same period, and are measured with the same instruments, but they sit at opposite ends of the visibility hierar-chy. A change that affects the platform as a whole moves both series in the same way. A change in how the platform distributes visibility makes the two series diverge. This offers a way to observe platform dynamics from the outside. The approach requires access to behavioral data through the platform’s interfaces, in our case including impression counts, but no access to the ranking systems themselves. The limit of the approach is equally clear, since a divergence be-tween the two samples shows that central and ordinary users experience the platform differently, and it flags when that difference changes, but it cannot name the mechanism behind the change. The temporal patterns in the sensor data hint towards a recognizable influence of social media platform governance on information quality dynamics. The marked quality drop among retweets of right-leaning outlets in the sensor sam-ple following Elon Musk’s endorsement of Donald Trump (July 13, 2024) is con-sistent with platform-level interventions shifting information quality among high-reach users, although our observational data cannot establish the cause. This co-occurrence highlights the interaction between platform governance and misinformation spread. Changes in platform dynamics, algorithmically or in natural user behavior, can register rapidly among network-central users while remaining invisible in randomly sampled users. We show that the friendship paradox is a suitable method for reaching samples enriched with such cen-tral actors who serve as sensitive antennas for detecting these platform-level shifts. The exploratory findings under RQ3 gain significance when connected to how users form beliefs about the opinion climate. People rely on their local neigh-borhood to judge how common opinions and behaviors are, and because of homophily, the well-documented tendency to connect with similar others [22], this neighborhood is already a politically narrowed slice of the population. The friendship paradox adds a second distortion on top. Within the narrowed neighborhood, the most-connected users receive disproportionate visibility, which can make globally rare views appear common, a pattern known as the majority illusion [23]. Homophily therefore narrows what a user sees, while the friendship paradox skews whom they see most prominently within that narrowed view [21]. Our two samples make this second distortion measurable. 16 The random sample describes what the network actually produces, while the sensor sample approximates what the network shows when viewed through people’s friend lists. The difference between the two is not a nuisance but a quantity of interest, because it is precisely the layer at which perception and reality of the opinion climate can drift apart. Two of our findings give this layer empirical shape. Sensor users reach on av-erage an audience about 175 times larger than random users (Table 1), so the voices a typical user encounters are drawn from a small and highly amplified subset of the network. During the 2024 election window this subset shifted. Impressions on right-leaning content rose about 2.5-fold in the sensor sample while they stayed flat in the random sample, so the visible mix of political con-tent changed. This is the precondition for false consensus effects, in which peo-ple misjudge how widely an opinion is shared because their visible sample of the network is skewed [24]. False consensus beliefs in turn are associated with populist attitudes [25]. Related evidence shows that engagement-based rank-ing also distorts perceived conversational norms [26], which concerns the form of discourse and not the distribution of opinions, but both lines of research point to the same conclusion, namely that what platforms make visible systematically shapes what users believe about others [27]. Friendship-paradox sampling offers a way to observe this distortion layer di-rectly. A perception gap measure could compare the politically classified con-tent mix a typical user is exposed to, estimated from the sensor sample and weighted by impressions, with the actual production mix in the random sam-ple. Tracked over time, such a measure would show whether platform changes widen the gap between the seen and the actual network, and it could be vali-dated against survey-based false consensus measures [25]. We consider this a promising direction for future work. The practical consequence is that targeting by network position alone will not reach the users most responsible for low-quality sharing, because in the times-pan of our dataset the quality divide follows political orientation. The friend-ship paradox still works as intervention infrastructure. The 70-minute median lead on news links shows no detectable dependence on quality, so it can sup-port general-purpose early-warning workflows, for example queuing emerging content for human review or surfacing accuracy prompts before content reaches viral scale. Beyond misinformation, the contrast between sensor and random 17 samples offers a direct way to observe how far the network that users see drifts from the network that exists, which connects platform monitoring to the study of perceived opinion climates [24]. Several limitations require consideration in interpreting our findings. Platform specificity. Our analysis focuses exclusively on Twitter/X during June–October 2024, a period encompassing the final months of the U.S. presidential cam-paign and substantial platform policy changes under Elon Musk’s ownership. Our analysis is observational, so temporal co-occurrences with platform events cannot establish causal relationships. Misinformation dynamics may differ on other platforms with different network structures, algorithmic curation, and user demographics. The 2024 U.S. election context may also limit generalizabil-ity to non-electoral periods or non-U.S. contexts. 

Misinformation operationalization. NewsGuard scores provide a consistent, scalable measure of source quality, but imperfectly capture misinformation. Domain-level ratings cannot assess individual article accuracy, miss misinfor-mation in text-only posts or non-news content, and may not reflect the specific claims users encounter. Right-leaning outlets more often fail NewsGuard’s transparency and editorial criteria, which Lühring et al. [28] attribute to dif-ferences in editorial practice, not to rater bias. NewsGuard’s coverage also concentrates on outlets from North America and Western Europe. Several gen-uine news outlets from Kenya, Nigeria, and India rank among the most shared unrated domains in our data, so news sharing outside the covered regions is systematically missing from our quality analyses. 

Temporal mismatch. The sensor dataset spans only June–October 2024, while the random dataset extends from 2015–2024. While we account for that by restrict-ing the time advantage analyses to the overlapping period from June to October 2024, the general comparisons may contain misinformation dynamics from the previous nine and a half years. Information quality and sharing dynamics on Twitter/X may have shifted substantially over this period. 

# 4 Methods 

# 4.1 Dataset Construction 

We constructed two Twitter/X datasets to compare the information sharing behavior of randomly selected users with their network neighbors (sensors), 18 following the friendship paradox methodology established by Christakis and Fowler [12] and Garcìa-Herranz et al. [13]. 

Random Dataset. Authors were drawn from the Twitter/X 1% random sample stream over a two-week period from September 28 to October 11, 2024, as X extended our access to its API for this project. The resulting sample contained 17,290,963 English-language tweets posted by 6,886,160 distinct accounts. Be-cause the stream randomly samples tweets and not users, accounts appear in it with a probability that grows with their activity level. To build a meaningful random user sample, we first selected accounts that had at least 100 lifetime tweets, an account age of at least six months, at least 40 followers and 40 fol-lowees, and a follower-to-followee ratio within the central 95% of the distribu-tion. This left 4,321,163 eligible accounts, from which we drew 10,000 without replacement with probabilities proportional to the inverse of each account’s average posting rate. This cancels the activity bias of the stream and yields a sample that is approximately uniform over eligible active English-speaking accounts and not over the tweets they produce. We then retrieved the full timelines of the selected accounts from January 1, 2015 onwards, resulting in a dataset of approximately 27 million tweets from 9,741 randomly selected au-thors, spanning January 2015 to early January 2025. Supplementary Note 1 re-ports the sampling procedure and the sample sizes at each step in detail. 

Sensor Dataset. As the list of friends of users (i.e. followed accounts) is not visible on X any more, we operationalize information flow over friendship links through retweets in the data. From among the users retweeted by the users in the random dataset timelines, we selected 10,000 user ids at random. We col-lected their tweet timelines between June 1 and October 31, 2024 (UTC, both included), resulting in a dataset of 30,964,074 tweets from 9,121 users. Some users were lost as their tweets became unavailable between the time when they were retweeted by the random sample and our data collection. The friendship paradox predicts that these sensor users should be positioned more centrally in the network than the random sample, as high-degree nodes appear dispropor-tionately in the retweeted users lists of randomly selected users. This friend-of-random sampling strategy has been validated in prior work to achieve early-detection of content diffusion on social media platforms when compared to ran-dom sampling [13]. Kumar et al. [29] show that the gain of friend sampling over random sampling depends on the structure of the underlying network, 19 which is why we verify the reach of our sensor sample empirically in Section 2.1. 

# 4.2 Misinformation Operationalization 

Our central credibility metric is the NewsGuard score, a domain-level rating produced by trained journalists on a nine-criterion rubric. NewsGuard scores measure source-level credibility under this rubric, not factual accuracy of in-dividual articles. NewsGuard’s ratings have been shown to be temporally sta-ble [28] and consistent with other news domain rating sets [30]. Source-level trust ratings in general have been found to track professional fact-checker as-sessments, even when they come from lay crowds [31]. We decided to use the domain-level approach for this observational study, because content-level clas-sification faces scalability limits and adversarial manipulation [32]. Throughout the paper, we use low-NG content and high-NG content to refer to NewsGuard-rated score tendencies. Misinformation is referred to as the broader phenomenon as discussed in the Introduction. We use a snapshot of the News-Guard database from September 2024, overlapping with the observation pe-riod. URLs appearing in tweets were expanded to resolve shortened links (e.g., bit.ly, t.co) to their destination domains, which were then matched against News-Guard’s database. The total share of NewsGuard scores per tweet after resolv-ing the shortened links can be found in Table 1. Tweets containing URLs to non-news domains or domains not in NewsGuard’s database were excluded from quality analyses but retained for overall dataset description. This approach has limitations, as it cannot assess misinformation in non-news content or text-only posts. However, it provides a consistent and reproducible measure across a large number of tweets. 

# 4.3 Political Orientation Classification 

NewsGuard categorizes news outlets by political orientation on a left-right spectrum based on editorial positioning, audience ideology, and content anal-ysis. We used these domain-level classifications to label tweets containing NewsGuard-rated URLs as left-leaning or right-leaning based on their linked source. This classification enabled us to analyze engagement patterns (likes, 20 retweets, and impressions i.e., number of times a post was seen by users on Twitter/X) with politically oriented content across the network. 

# 4.4 Statistical Analysis 

Information Quality Comparison. We compare NewsGuard score distribu-tions between sensor and random datasets using both visual inspection (kernel density plots) and formal statistical tests. Given the large sample sizes, we em-ploy effect size measures alongside significance tests. Cliff’s delta ( δ) provides a non-parametric effect size, computed as the probability that a randomly se-lected observation from the random dataset exceeds a randomly selected sensor observation minus the probability of the reverse, with values near 0 indicating no effect and values approaching ±1 indicating complete separation. For this comparison we aggregate at the author level. For each author we average the NewsGuard scores of all rated links they shared over the full collection period, and the tests compare these author means between the two samples. 

Exposure-Weighted Analysis. Raw NewsGuard distributions treat all tweets equally, but information exposure depends on reach. We calculate exposure-weighted misinformation intensity by weighting each tweet’s misinforma-tion contribution (inverted NewsGuard score) by its impressions. This cap-tures how much low-quality content reaches larger audiences through central users. 

Time Advantage Analysis. For links appearing in both datasets during the overlapping period (June – October 2024), we compare the times at which a URL first appeared in the sensor and in the random group and analyze the time difference. Our primary population are links to NewsGuard-rated news outlets. Links without a rating, which are dominated by references to other tweets, serve as a comparison for the general link stream. Our expectation is that a URL will occur sooner in the sensor than the random group. In addi-tion, we have to account for the substantially higher number of observations in the sensor data, resulting in a much higher baseline probability of any given URL to appear in the sensor data set than the random dataset. To control for this asymmetry, we use a downsampling bootstrap. Within randomly selected 7 day windows from June through October 2024, we take all URLs in the ran-dom dataset and the same number of URLs, randomly picked, from the sensor dataset. This step is repeated 1,000 times for multiple random 7-day periods 21 as rolling windows within the time period June – October 2024. From these results, we calculate the median and mean time advantage of information ap-pearing in the sensor group over the random group for link sharing, with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. We also calculate the proportion of compar-isons where sensor users posted first. To assess whether lead time depends on content quality, we compute the Pearson correlation between NewsGuard score and time advantage of all URLs that appear in both datasets. 

Non-Parametric Tests. Given non-normal distributions and large sample sizes, we employ Mann-Whitney-U-tests for group comparisons, supplemented by Cliff’s delta effect sizes. Statistical significance is assessed at α = 0 .05 , though we emphasize effect sizes over p-values given that large samples can render trivial differences statistically significant. 22 References

This note walks through the construction of the random dataset step by step, from the raw stream data to the 10,000 accounts whose timelines we finally col-lected. Three stages lead from one end to the other. We first drew a random sample of tweets, then filtered the accounts behind those tweets down to the ones that are usable for our purposes, and in the end we drew a weighted sub-sample that undoes the activity bias which comes with sampling users through their tweets. 

# 1.1 Collection 

We listened to the Twitter/X 1% random sample stream from late September 2024 onwards and stored every tweet with all available fields in JSON format. For this study we took all tweets that passed through the stream in the two weeks from September 28, 2024 00:00:00 GMT to October 11, 2024 23:59:59 GMT (UNIX timestamps 1727481600 to 1728691199, both included), which came to 50,138,766 tweets. Twitter/X labels every tweet with a detected language, and after keeping only the English ones we were left with 17,290,963 tweets posted by 6,886,160 distinct accounts. For each of these accounts we then requested basic profile information through the Twitter/X API, and we received profiles for 6,437,883 of them, which is 93.5% of the accounts observed in the stream. The rest could not be retrieved anymore because the accounts had been deleted, suspended, or set to protected somewhere between the tweet and our profile request. All filters below operate on these 6,437,883 profiles. 

# 1.2 Eligibility filters 

The 1% stream samples tweets, not users, so an account shows up in it with a probability that grows with how much it posts. A user sample taken straight from the stream is therefore tilted towards the loudest accounts, and on top of that it contains plenty of accounts that are too new, too inactive, or too thinly 29 connected for the sharing and network measures in this study. We applied three inclusion criteria, each one to what the previous one left over. 

Activity and account age. We kept accounts that had posted at least 100 tweets over their lifetime and that had existed for at least six months before the start of the stream sampling window, which means they were created before March 28, 2024. The first condition makes sure a timeline is long enough to say some-thing about an account’s sharing behavior, and the second sorts out accounts whose social ties have not had time to settle. Both conditions held for 5,310,348 accounts, or 82.5% of the retrieved profiles. 

Minimum connectivity. We kept accounts with at least 40 followers and at least 40 followees, because below that an account hangs too loosely in the follower network for friendship-paradox sampling to mean much. This left 4,548,589 accounts, or 70.7% of the retrieved profiles. 

Follower-to-followee ratio. For every account we computed the ratio of fol-lowers to followees and removed the accounts in the top and bottom 2.5% of the distribution of the logarithm of this ratio. The trimming cuts away the ac-counts whose connectivity looks nothing like that of an ordinary user. At one end sit celebrity, media, and institutional accounts with huge audiences and hardly any followees, at the other end sit accounts that follow in bulk without being followed back, which is what bots and spam accounts typically do. The accounts that remain have a follower-to-followee ratio between 0.087 and 19 .39 ,with a median of 0.79 . This left 4,321,163 eligible accounts, or 67.1% of the re-trieved profiles, and only 1,669 of them were verified accounts. 

# 1.3 Activity-corrected subsample 

From these 4,321,163 eligible accounts we drew the final 10,000, without re-placement and with inclusion probabilities proportional to the inverse of each account’s average posting rate. The posting rate is the number of lifetime tweets from the profile divided by the age of the account in days, with the age counted up to the cutoff date of March 28, 2024, so in practice the weight of an account is its age divided by its tweet count. The idea is that the chance of landing in a random sample of tweets is roughly proportional to how often an account posts, so weighting by the inverse of that 30 rate roughly cancels the head start of the busy accounts, and what comes out is a sample that is approximately uniform over the population of eligible active English-speaking accounts, not over the tweets that population produces. We checked the correction by comparing the distributions of lifetime tweet counts, account ages, and posting rates before and after the weighted draw. In the last step we retrieved the complete timelines of the 10,000 selected ac-counts from January 1, 2015 onwards. Of these, 9,741 accounts had at least one tweet in the collected period, and they constitute the random dataset described in Table 1 of the main text.
