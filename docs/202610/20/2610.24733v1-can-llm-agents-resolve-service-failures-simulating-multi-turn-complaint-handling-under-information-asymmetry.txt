## Can LLM Agents Resolve Service Failures? Simulating Multi-Turn Complaint Handling under Information Asymmetry 

Wailing Ng Mengze Hong Chen Jason Zhang Shiqi Fan 

The Hong Kong Polytechnic University 

Abstract 

LLM agents are widely adopted in service chat-bots for rule-guided support and information access, yet emotionally demanding complaint handling still relies heavily on human agents. We introduce SERVICE BENCH , to our knowl-edge the first benchmark dedicated to com-plaint handling, with 2,500 scenarios spanning 184 complaint categories and five service indus-tries. We propose Asymmetric Progressive Complaint Simulation (APCS) to simulate realistic complaint handling, together with a justice-based evaluation framework. Our ex-periments reveal that even capable LLM agents struggle to handle complaints effectively. They lack the ability to proactively gather informa-tion and strategically guide conversations to-ward resolution, often resorting to premature compensation. We further show that targeted post-training with SFT and DPO substantially improves these capabilities, enabling a smaller model to match or surpass much larger models on several outcomes. 

1 Introduction 

Complaint handling is a central component of ser-vice recovery, through which service providers re-spond to failures and attempt to restore damaged customer relationships (Spreng et al., 1995; Smith et al., 1999). Poor complaint handling can deepen dissatisfaction and increase the cost of service re-covery (Bell and Zemke, 1987; Meyer-Waarden and Sabadie, 2023). Although LLM-based agents are increasingly used for routine customer support, complaint handling remains a challenging setting in which effective service still depends heavily on human judgment (Tan et al., 2025). Existing customer service benchmarks primarily evaluate operational capabilities such as domain knowledge (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Lee et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b), tool use (Yao et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025a), and workflow compliance (Nandi et al., 2025; Balaji

As LLMs are increasingly de-ployed in customer service (Su et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Hong et al., 2025b,a), evaluating their ability to handle complaints becomes critical. Our extensive experiments show that DeepSeek-V4-Pro and GPT-5 achieve the strongest overall complaint handling performance (Figure 1). When provided with complete customer information from the out-set, agents perform substantially better. Finally, we show that targeted training on these behaviors through SFT and DPO substantially improves complaint handling, enabling Qwen3-8B to match or surpass much larger general purpose models on several evaluation outcomes. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce SERVICE BENCH , to our knowl-edge the first multi-turn benchmark designed for complaint handling spanning 184 com-plaint categories and 5 service industries. • We propose Asymmetric Progressive Com-plaint Simulation (APCS) to model realis-tic complaint interactions and a justice-based evaluation framework to evaluate agent capa-bility in complaint handling. • Extensive experiments reveal systematic be-havioral limitations in current models and fur-ther show that targeted post-training can sub-stantially mitigate these limitations. 

2 Related work 

2.1 Service Recovery and Justice in Complaint Handling 

Service recovery concerns how service providers respond to failures and restore customer evalua-tions and relationships (Spreng et al., 1995). Prior research distinguishes symbolic recovery, which re-lies on interpersonal and communicative responses, from utilitarian recovery, which provides tangi-ble remedies (Bell and Zemke, 1987; Smith et al., 1999; Meyer-Waarden and Sabadie, 2023). Effec-tive complaint handling requires more than select-ing a remedy, as customers also evaluate how they are treated and how the resolution process is con-ducted. Justice theory formalizes these complemen-tary aspects through interactional, procedural, and distributive justice (Smith et al., 1999). This per-spective provides a principled basis for evaluating complaint handling beyond task completion. 

2.2 Benchmarks for Customer Service Agents 

There is growing interest in benchmarking LLMs for domain specific customer service applications. Existing work primarily targets three forms of oper-ational capability. Domain knowledge benchmarks assess service specific knowledge (Li et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b,a). Tool use benchmarks evaluate interaction with external sys-tems and service task execution (Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025a). Workflow compliance benchmarks examine adherence to pre-scribed procedures and policies (Nandi et al., 2025; Balaji et al., 2026). Their evaluation, however, re-mains largely centered on operational correctness. Complaint handling introduces a different evalu-ation target, as service quality depends not only on what resolution is reached but also on how the customer is treated throughout the process. 

2.3 User Simulation for Interactive Agents 

Simulated users provide a scalable mechanism for constructing dynamic multi-turn interactions with conversational agents (Dou et al., 2025; Kwon et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025). Recent work has used simulation to evaluate task specific interaction capabilities that are difficult to capture through static benchmarks, including conflict di-alogue (Kwon et al., 2025), cultural competence (Wu et al., 2025), and teaching scenarios (Shi et al., 2025). Studies have also examined the behavioral fidelity and reliability of simulated users as prox-ies for human interaction (Dou et al., 2025). In this work, we design a simulation framework for complaint handling to evaluate agent capabilities.

3.2 Benchmark Construction Complaint Coverage and Taxonomy. SER -

> VICE

BENCH provides broad coverage of real world complaint handling, with 2,500 scenarios spanning 184 complaint categories and 37 customer journey stages across 5 service industries: airlines, hotels, restaurants, retail, and ride hailing and delivery. The benchmark is grounded in public complaint dialogues between customers and brand customer service accounts on X (Axelbrooke, 2017), provid-ing empirical grounding in real service failures and customer dissatisfaction. To organize these hetero-geneous complaints systematically, we construct in-dustry specific customer journey maps (Rosenbaum et al., 2017) through data driven topic discovery and expert guided coding. This process yields an interpretable taxonomy aligned with service stages and touchpoints, enabling balanced coverage of diverse complaint contexts. Detailed benchmark information are provided in Appendix C. 

Case Reconstruction. Real complaint dialogues provide strong factual grounding but are often too sparse to support realistic interactive evaluation. We therefore develop a reconstruction pipeline that transforms each complaint into a structured com-plaint case while preserving the factual anchors of the source dialogue. We first identify the core com-plaint facts and reconstruct a plausible sequence of service events that organizes the key events leading to the reported failure and provides a structured backbone for subsequent case generation. Based on this event sequence, we construct the customer experience background and resolution expectation. A company case record is then inferred using a manually designed industry specific schema. 

Quality Control. We construct 14,373 candidate complaint cases and filter them based on coherence, plausibility, specificity, and category fit. Overall, 86.68% of candidates meet the filtering criteria. From this pool, we manually select 2,500 scenarios that best match realistic service situations while preserving the complaint information observed in the raw dialogues. We then refine the selected cases to further improve realism and internal consistency before inclusion in the final benchmark.

4 Experiment 

4.1 Experimental Setup Agent Models. We evaluate multiple LLM fam-ilies, including GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025), GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024b), GPT-4o mini (OpenAI, 2024a), DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), Gemini 2.5 Flash (Comanici et al., 2025), Llama 3.3 70B Instruct (Meta AI, 2024), and Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025). All models are evaluated as service agents under the same simulation protocol. 

Judge Models. LLM-based judges have been widely used for automatic evaluation (Srinivas et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). We adopt GPT-5 as the primary evaluation model and examine the ro-bustness of service agent rankings using three addi-tional judges from other LLM families, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Llama 3.3 70B, and Qwen3-32B. We fur-ther validate the reliability of LLM-based evalua-tion through comparisons with human ratings. De-tailed results are provided in Appendix B.

5.1 Inference-Time Reasoning 

Inference-time reasoning is widely used to improve agent performance, but its effect on complaint han-dling is less clear. We compare Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022), Plan-and-Solve (Wang et al., 2023), and Self-refine (Madaan et al., 2023) with Direct generation. We find that additional rea-soning can improve complaint handling, but the gains depend strongly on both the model and the reasoning strategy (Figure 3). GPT-5 benefits more consistently from CoT and Plan-and-Solve. Self-refine, however, consistently lowers justice scores and can also increase goodwill expenditure. More interestingly, refinement often makes an initially reasonable response worse. It can stall the dialogue or move the response away from the customer’s main resolution objective. 

5.2 Case-Based and Procedural Guidance 

Complaint handling often relies on organizational knowledge beyond the ongoing customer interac-tion. Service agents may draw on prior cases that document handling decisions or on operating pro-cedures that provide guidance for resolution. We therefore construct representative complaint cases and SOPs based on industry examples and use them as case based and procedural guidance. Both forms of guidance improve performance, but we find that procedural guidance is consistently effective. Interestingly, combining case based and procedu-ral guidance does not yield further improvements. Since human service agents commonly work with both, we think this drop may come from the model struggling to reconcile specific case examples with general procedural instructions, leading to less con-sistent decisions.

5.4 Goodwill Timing and Calibration 

Goodwill is an important decision in complaint han-dling because it can acknowledge customer incon-venience and support resolution, but inappropriate use can undermine the interaction. We therefore examine when service agents choose to offer good-will. For each interaction, we identify the first goodwill turn and measure how much complaint information has been disclosed by that point, with calculation details provided in Appendix A.7. We find that agents commit to goodwill surprisingly early, often within the first two turns when only a small portion of the customer experience has been revealed. We refer to this behavior as premature goodwill commitment , where agents use early good-will as an easy route to complaint closure instead of fully engaging with the underlying issue. We then ask whether this behavior comes from a poor understanding of complaint severity. We group complaint categories into multiple severity levels and compare the goodwill offered across these groups. Most models increase goodwill as severity rises, showing that they can distinguish the relative seriousness of different service failures. This suggests that current LLMs already have an understanding of complaint scenarios. The remain-ing gap is therefore less about recognizing the com-plaint and more about turning that understanding into effective multi-turn handling behavior.

6 Model Adaptation for Complaint Handling 

Our analyses reveal several recurring weaknesses in complaint handling that are not simply explained by general model capability. We therefore ask whether these behaviors can be improved through targeted post-training. We fine-tune Qwen3-8B with supervised fine-tuning (SFT), followed by di-rect preference optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023). We first split the scenarios into separate training and evaluation sets. The SFT data are man-ually selected from existing rollouts to retain high quality examples. The DPO preference pairs are then constructed from responses generated by the SFT model and manually screened to target the behavioral limitations identified in our earlier anal-yses. Full data construction and training details are provided in Appendix D.2. We find that targeted post-training leads to sub-stantial improvements in complaint handling perfor-mance (Table 5). After SFT and DPO, Qwen3-8B matches or surpasses substantially larger general purpose models on several evaluation outcomes de-spite its smaller scale. At the same time, goodwill expenditure decreases, showing that better com-plaint handling does not require heavier reliance on material concessions. These results suggest that the behavioral weaknesses are trainable and can be improved through task specific adaptation. 

7 Conclusion 

We introduced SERVICE BENCH , a multi-turn benchmark for complaint handling, together with Asymmetric Progressive Complaint Simulation (APCS) and a justice-based evaluation framework. Our experiments show that current LLM agents have clear understanding on service scenarios, but still face clear behavioral limitations in multi-turn complaint handling. We further find that targeted post-training can substantially reduce these limita-tions and improve overall performance. We hope SERVICE BENCH provides a useful testbed for de-veloping more capable service agents. 8Limitations 

SERVICE BENCH has several limitations. First, the benchmark is primarily grounded in English com-plaint dialogues associated with major U.S. brands and customer profiles. This design supports con-sistency in scenario construction and evaluation, but it may not fully capture multilingual complaint behavior or regional variation in service norms. Ex-pectations about apology, explanation, escalation, and remedy can differ across linguistic and cultural contexts. Extending S ERVICE BENCH to more lan-guages, regions, and customer populations would provide a stronger test of cross-cultural and cross-lingual generalization. Second, although S ERVICE BENCH covers five common service industries, its scope is not exhaus-tive. The benchmark focuses on consumer fac-ing service contexts, whereas other domains may involve different complaint protocols. Extending the benchmark to these settings would help assess generalization beyond common consumer service environments. Finally, S ERVICE BENCH is designed as a gen-eral benchmark for evaluating LLM complaint han-dling capability. While it supports comparisons across LLM agents, the absence of a human ser-vice agent baseline limits our ability to assess their performance relative to human practitioners. More-over, the scenarios do not model the full policy manuals or backend systems of individual compa-nies. Future work can incorporate human agent comparisons, company specific policies, transac-tional records, and tool/API use to better assess complaint handling in real world service environ-ments. 

Ethical Considerations 

The source complaint dialogues used in this study are publicly available. We do not release raw posts, user handles, URLs, or other personally identifi-able information. The final benchmark cases are de-identified and manually reviewed and revised by the research team to reduce privacy risks and remove privacy sensitive or harmful content. Our study examines complaint handling by LLM agents, a setting that involves subjective judgments about fairness, responsibility, explanation, and rem-edy. We acknowledge that the benchmark and evaluation metrics may reflect biases from En-glish language complaints, major consumer ser-vice brands, and culturally specific expectations about complaint handling. We mitigate these risks through broad industry coverage, diverse complaint categories, domain informed revision, and human validation. However, biases may still persist. While S ERVICE BENCH aims to support more reliable evaluation of complaint handling agents, it could be misused to justify fully automated com-plaint handling in consequential service settings. We strongly discourage such use without human oversight, company specific policy validation, and safeguards for affected customers.

We examine whether evaluator self-preference can materially affect model comparison. C Additional Details On Benchmark Construction 

C.1 Benchmark Statistics 

SERVICE BENCH contains 2,500 complaint scenar-ios across five service industries, organized into 37 customer journey stages and 184 complaint cate-gories. The benchmark is constructed from real complaint dialogues through an industry specific customer journey guided pipeline that combines data driven topic discovery with expert guided cod-ing. An overview of the benchmark statistics is provided in Table 17. 

C.2 Industry and Brand Selection 

We select five service industries to capture diverse complaint handling contexts involving multiple cus-tomer journey stages, varied service failures, and recovery processes that require both interpersonal responses and discretionary material remedies. The selected industries span airlines, hotels, restaurants, retail, and ride hailing and delivery, covering a broad range of service processes, customer touch-points, and resolution requirements. Within each industry, we select representative brands with com-parable service processes and customer support models, allowing complaints from multiple brands to be organized under a unified industry level cus-tomer journey map. Although the raw source cor-pus is naturally imbalanced across industries, it is used only for complaint mining and taxonomy construction. The final benchmark is constructed through balanced sampling across industries and complaint categories. Table 16 reports the selected customer care accounts and the number of raw com-plaint dialogues collected for each industry.

C.4 Customer Journey Map Construction 

We construct customer journey maps through an iterative process that combines topic discovery, subtopic exploration, and expert-guided coding. BERTopic and K-means are used to surface recur-ring complaint patterns in the cleaned dialogue corpus. Based on these issue patterns, we develop an interpretable taxonomy that organizes service failures by industry-specific journey stages. The re-sulting customer journey maps provide the category structure for balanced scenario generation. • BERTopic-based topic exploration. We first apply BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022) to iden-tify broad recurring complaint themes from the cleaned dialogues. BERTopic combines semantic representation with density-based topic discovery and does not require the num-ber of topics to be specified in advance. We en-code each dialogue with NV-Embed-v2 embed-dings (Lee et al., 2025). We then project the embeddings into a lower-dimensional space using UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018), with 

n_components =10 and n_neighbors =15. Fi-nally, we cluster the reduced representations with HDBSCAN (Malzer and Baum, 2020). Since complaint issues vary in both frequency and density, we tune HDBSCAN by search-ing min_cluster_size over {25 , 35 , 50 , 80 }

and min_samples over {3, 5, 8, 10 }. For each topic, we review representative dialogues and topic keywords to identify the dominant com-plaint pattern and potential issue boundaries. • K-means-based subtopic exploration. Be-cause broad BERTopic clusters can contain multiple issue types, we further apply K-means clustering (McQueen, 1967) within ma-jor topic clusters. We vary k from 5 to 25 and inspect representative samples from the result-ing subclusters. This step helps uncover finer-grained distinctions within broad complaint themes. • Expert-guided coding and taxonomy devel-

18 opment. Using the BERTopic themes and K-means subclusters as analytic materials, we develop the final taxonomy through an expert-guided coding process. For each industry, the research team and hospitality and service domain professionals proceed in four steps. They define the major customer journey stages based on the industry service process and observed complaint contexts, review repre-sentative dialogues from the discovered topic groups and assign provisional complaint la-bels, compare labels within and across groups to merge overlapping labels and refine cat-egory boundaries, and retain recurring and interpretable categories relevant to complaint handling while removing or merging overly narrow and low-frequency issues. Each com-plaint category is then mapped to the corre-sponding journey stage. • LLM-based taxonomy assignment. After fi-nalizing the taxonomy, we use GPT-4o mini to classify the cleaned complaint dialogues into the finalized categories. Dialogues that cannot be assigned to any category are discarded. 

C.5 Scenario Data Provenance 

ServiceBench combines real complaint data with reconstructed context to support realistic multi-turn interactions. The source dialogues provide the orig-inal complaint facts, from which additional sce-nario components are reconstructed or generated. The selected scenarios are then manually reviewed and refined to improve realism and internal consis-tency while preserving the original facts. Table 18 summarizes the provenance of each component. 

C.6 Complaint Case Quality 

We first filter the 14,373 candidate complaint cases using LLM-based judges. Each candidate is scored from 1 to 5 on coherence, plausibility, specificity, and category fit, and we retain only cases that re-ceive at least 4 on all four dimensions. The can-didates achieve average scores of 4.429 for coher-ence, 4.439 for plausibility, 4.441 for specificity, and 4.629 for category fit, with 86.68% meeting the filtering threshold. From this qualified pool, we manually select 2,500 scenarios that most closely reflect realistic service situations and preserve the complaint facts and contextual information con-tained in the original dialogues. We then manually refine the selected cases to improve realism, com-pleteness, and internal consistency before including them in the final benchmark. • Coherence assesses whether the complaint case is internally consistent without contradic-tions or unsupported event transitions. • Plausibility assesses whether the complaint case is credible for the corresponding industry and realistic for complaint handling. • Specificity assesses whether the case contains concrete details needed for complaint han-dling rather than generic descriptions. • Category fit assesses whether the central ser-vice failure matches the assigned complaint category and customer journey stage.

D.2 Post-Training Details 

We fine-tune Qwen3-8B using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by direct preference opti-mization (DPO). We first split the scenarios into separate training and evaluation sets, balanced across service industries and customer journey stages. The SFT data are constructed from ser-vice interaction rollouts generated by the seven models evaluated in our main experiments. We select high quality rollouts with scores of at least 3 on each justice dimension. Each rollout is con-verted into turn level examples using the preceding dialogue context and the corresponding service re-sponse. We then manually screen these examples and retain only high quality training instances, re-sulting in 5,125 SFT examples.
