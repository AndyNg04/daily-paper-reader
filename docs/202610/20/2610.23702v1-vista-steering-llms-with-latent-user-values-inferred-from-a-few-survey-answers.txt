## VISTA: Steering LLMs with Latent User Values Inferred from a Few Survey Answers 

Yinuo Xu 1, Jadie Adams 2, Brian Hu 2, Shivani Kumar 1, David Jurgens 1, and Arslan Basharat 21University of Michigan, 2Kitware Inc. 

{yinuoxu, kshivan, jurgens}@umich.edu ,

{jadie.adams, brian.hu, arslan.basharat}@kitware.com 

Abstract 

Personalizing a language model requires know-ing what a person values, which stays largely consistent across tasks. Yet most methods ei-ther learn task-specific preferences or place the user’s profile in the prompt, where mod-els struggle to act on it. We introduce VISTA (Value-Informed Steering and Targeted Ac-quisition), which infers a latent profile from users’ (full or partial) survey answers, and maps the profile through a hypernetwork to a user-specific steering intervention in frozen LLM to select answer choices or generate free-text responses. Given the same evidence, VISTA matches or beats in-context profiles on syn-thetic preferences (Food2D), the World Values Survey (WVS), and PrimeX, without adding prompt tokens. To reduce the cognitive bur-den of completing full surveys, VISTA also chooses which questions to ask and when to stop: on WVS it stops after 6% of the question-naire while retaining 94% of the full-history gain, where standard adaptive testing retains at most 42%. The dominant steering direction is an interpretable value axis on which VISTA places unseen users accurately. Our results point toward pluralistic alignment that adapts to individuals at deployable cost. 

1 Introduction 

Aligning a language model to an individual requires knowing what that person values, not merely which of two candidate responses they happened to prefer (Kirk et al., 2024). Most personalization work takes the latter route, inferring a latent user representa-tion from pairwise judgments on a task’s responses (Poddar et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Bose et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), so the signal is tied to the task it was collected on. Values are organized dif-ferently: their structure is largely shared across people and cultures, and individuals differ in the priority they give each dimension (Schwartz, 1992). Demographic personas capture group tenden-cies but not individuals (Santurkar et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024), and placing the user’s description in 1arXiv:2610.23702v1 [cs.CL] 16 Oct 2026 context helps (Joshi et al., 2025; Li and Conrad, 2026; Moon et al., 2026) but does not guarantee the model acts on it (Kumar and Jurgens, 2025), while the prompt grows with every answer it carries. Additionally, long surveys impose a cognitive load that respondents manage by satisficing, answering with less care and attention as the task wears on, which lowers the quality of the data (Barge and Gehlbach, 2012). A practical system should ask few questions, choosing which ones matter and stopping when it has asked enough. We introduce VISTA (Value-Informed Steering and Targeted Acquisition) (Figure 1), a framework for aligning LLM responses with individual user values. VISTA infers a posterior over a user’s value latent from full or partial survey answers and maps it through a hypernetwork to a user-specific steer-ing intervention in a frozen LLM. It uses the same posterior to select questions and decide when to stop. VISTA needs no per-user weight update and adds a small, fixed cost per query, unlike best-of-

N reranking or long user prompts. Prior work addresses these pieces separately: preference mod-els personalize by reranking or guiding sampled outputs (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026; P˘ adurean et al., 2025), per-user interventions cap-ture writing style(Zhang et al., 2025; Popp et al., 2026) or use point estimate from randomly cho-sen answers(Li et al., 2024a), and adaptive elicita-tion uses fixed question budgets (Choudhury et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025), or repeat the process for every new request(P˘ adurean et al., 2025). VISTA is the first to infer a posterior over a user’s values from a few survey answers, gener-ate an activation-level intervention from them, and decide which questions to ask and when to stop. The value encoder also transfers: trained on the World Values Survey, it can be reused without re-training on a different population and questionnaire (PrimeX). Our contributions are: (1) A learned latent represents a user better than a written profile. Given identical evidence, VISTA matches or beats placing the user’s answers in the prompt on all three datasets, and on PrimeX it is the only system whose personalized free text yields a sig-nificant net gain. (2) Personalization at constant cost and under realistic question budgets Each query adds one steering vector or 16 soft tokens to a frozen model, where prompting re-reads the user’s answers every time. VISTA decides which questions to ask and when to stop: on WVS it stops after 6% of the questionnaire while retain-ing 94% of the full-history gain. (3) An inter-pretable representation. The dominant steering direction is a human-readable value axis, and steer-ing places unseen respondents at their own position on it ( r = 0.73 on WVS, 0.59 on PrimeX). 

2 Related Work 

Preference and user modeling. Preference mod-els learn shared structures from pairwise compar-isons: weighted basis rewards (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Bose et al., 2025), Gaussian bases (Lee et al., 2026), prototype mixtures (Chen et al., 2024), vari-ational latents (Poddar et al., 2024), or sociolinguis-tic and value dimensions for communities (Li et al., 2025). These methods rely on reranking (Lee et al., 2026; P˘ adurean et al., 2025) or reward-guided de-coding (Shenfeld et al., 2025), requiring multiple candidates or per-step evaluation. 

Activation steering. Steering vectors are typi-cally shared across users (Cao et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026), with prompting outperforming vector injection for per-sonality traits (Banayeeanzade et al., 2026). Per-user methods derive writing-style vectors from past texts (Zhang et al., 2025) or three samples (Popp et al., 2026), or adjust fixed-vector strengths (Bo et al., 2026). Closest to VISTA, Li et al. (2024b) learn point-estimate respondent embed-dings mapped to prefix tokens from random an-swers, while Tan et al. (2026) generate query-specific LoRA weights from writing histories. 

Representing a user’s values. Demographic per-sonas poorly represent individuals (Liu et al., 2024), while personal rationales (Joshi et al., 2025) and narratives (Li and Conrad, 2026) help. Sorensen et al. (2025) compress demonstrations into inter-pretable but lossy value profiles, without signifi-cant gains in their free-form text generation. Even structured moral profiles can yield near-chance per-formance (Kumar and Jurgens, 2025). 

Adaptive elicitation. Computerized adaptive testing selects informative items and stops at a standard-error threshold (Xu et al., 2023; van der Linden and Glas, 2010). LLM-based methods max-imize information gain about natural-language hy-potheses (Choudhury et al., 2026), held-out an-swers (Wang et al., 2025), or textual preference criteria (Bose et al., 2026), under fixed budgets. VPL uses fixed queries (Poddar et al., 2024), PReF adapts within a fixed budget (Shenfeld et al., 2025), and UserAlign stops by rule but re-elicits per 2prompt (P˘ adurean et al., 2025). VISTA infers a posterior over individual values from a few survey answers, maps it to reusable interventions in a frozen LLM with an interpretable value axis, and adaptively selects questions and stops by expected predictive information gain. 

3 Data 

We evaluate VISTA on three datasets: syn-thetic food preferences (Food2D), real-world so-cial values (WVS), and opinions and world be-liefs (PrimeX), Together, they test personalization across synthetic and real-world preferences, with PrimeX additionally evaluating transfer to a new population and survey instrument. We describe each dataset in detail next: 

Food2D (P˘ adurean et al., 2025) is a synthetic dataset with 10 food questions (e.g., “What should I eat tonight?”), each with 20 candidate responses annotated on two features, spiciness and vegetarian-ness ( φ). An item is a pair of responses to the same question (1,900 pairs). Each simulated user has a two-dimensional taste vector θ and chooses be-tween two responses noisily, in proportion to how well each matches their taste; this noise caps ac-curacy at 66% even for a method given the true θ.We sample 2,000 training users answering 40 pairs each and hold out 300 unseen users. Our models see only the response text, never φ or θ.

World Values Survey. We use US and UK respon-dents from WVS Wave 7 (Haerpfer et al., 2024): 5,652 people (3,956/848/848 train/val/test) answer-ing about 130 of 226 value statements across 15 topics, including religion, politics, economics, im-migration, ethics, trust, science, and security. This gives 73,288 held-out (person, item) pairs. It gives us a real testbed for personalized value inference as it captures naturally occurring preferences on socially consequential topics. We converted the WVS into binary choice-questions following Jiang et al. (2025). There are 226 items, each rewritten as first-person statements (e.g., "religion is important in my life" vs "religion is not important in my life"), resulting in 217 items having two sides and 9 hav-ing three. Multi-point scales are split at midpoint so each answer maps to one statement. 

PrimeX (Koncel-Kedziorski et al., 2025) serves as a transfer test with a new population, item bank, and domain. It has 858 US respondents, each an-swering about 46 of 48 binary items (30 opinions on medicine, science, and risk; 18 primals , or ab-stract world beliefs), along with demographics and free-text explanations. We converted PrimeX into the same format as WVS: each item is rewritten as a pair of first-person statements, one per side. Multi-point scales are split into two sides: 4-point opinion scales at split. At midpoint, and 6-point Primals scales into agree vs. disagree. 

4 Methods 

4.1 Inferring Latent Values 

Human values are heterogeneous and cannot be fully inferred from limited enrollment survey re-sponses. We therefore adopt the Variational Infor-mation Bottleneck (VIB) framework (Alemi et al., 2017) to learn probabilistic latent representations that capture individual preferences and uncertainty while leveraging shared structure across a popula-tion. Our approach,VISTA-VI (Value inference component of VISTA), builds upon Variational Preference Learning (VPL) (Poddar et al., 2024), which models heterogeneous preferences through user-specific latent variables in personalized re-ward learning. We adapt this approach to value elicitation from enrollment surveys, incorporating swap-guided regularization to mitigate encoder col-lapse and semi-amortized inference to improve user-specific representations. We represent each user’s values through a la-tent variable z ∈ R32 , which captures unobserved factors influencing their survey responses. We set the latent dimension to 32, chosen on a vali-dation split from K ∈ 16, 32, 64 : it is the only size at which every search trial converged (Ap-pendix 9.2). Training and refinement. The model is trained to predict held-out survey responses with a KL penalty toward a standard Gaussian prior. To pre-vent posterior collapse, a known failure of VPL in which the encoder ignores the user, we add swap-guided regularization (Kim and Kim, 2026). Be-cause one encoder shared across the population can be suboptimal for an individual, we use semi-amortized inference (Kim et al., 2018): the en-coder’s posterior mean is refined for 100 gradient steps on the user’s observed answers, with a penalty that keeps it close to the encoder’s estimate. Objec-tives and training details are in Appendix 9.4. 

4.2 Activation Steering from Inferred Values 

A hypernetwork hψ, inspired by HyperSteer (Sun et al., 2025), maps the inferred representation ˆzu

to an intervention in a frozen Llama-3-8B-Instruct. Steering vectors are usually extracted from con-trastive examples on a predefined concept (Pan-ickssery et al., 2024), which requires choosing con-cepts and building contrast sets for each. A hyper-network instead learns a mapping from the value latent to the LLM intervention. The resulting di-rections carry no imposed meaning, but are still interpretable (cf. Section ). The hypernetwork is a small self-attention network (four blocks, eight heads), and it is the only component trained, as gradients reach it through the frozen model. 

Steering for forced choice. hψ (2.4M parameters) outputs a single steering vector ∆u, normalized to unit norm so that only its direction varies across users, which is added to the residual stream at layer 10 at every token position. The layer is chosen by a validation search (Appendix 9.3). Training minimizes cross-entropy between the model’s dis-tribution over the answer letters and the user’s own answer (Appendix 9.5). 

Steering for free-text generation. hψ (15M pa-rameters) outputs a 16-token soft prefix inserted after the BOS token. It is trained in two stages. We first distill the value model into the prefix by maxi-mizing the likelihood of a target response the value model selects for that user: the highest-scoring of 16 candidate responses on Food2D, and an ex-planation written by another training respondent who chose the option the value model predicts on PrimeX. WVS is excluded because it contains no user-written free-text responses. We then refine it with personalized DPO (Li et al., 2024c) on length-matched pairs of explanations that take opposing positions. A control with randomly assigned targets checks that gains come from the user representation (Appendix 9.5). 

4.3 Adaptive Elicitation Framework 

We use the probabilistic value representation to adaptively select enrollment questions that are most informative for predicting downstream preferences. Following expected predictive information gain (EPIG) (Smith et al., 2023), we select questions based on their expected information about target-item responses rather than simply reducing uncer-tainty in the latent representation. For each unasked question, we estimate its mutual information with the target-item responses under these samples and select the highest-scoring question. The posterior is updated through a single encoder forward pass after each new answer. 

Adaptive stopping. Rather than imposing a fixed question budget, we stop when the maximum re-maining EPIG score falls below a fraction ε of the user’s initial maximum score. This allows elic-itation to terminate at different question counts depending on the information gained from each user’s responses. At stopping, we infer the final user representation from the collected responses and evaluate it using the trained steering module. We compare adaptive stopping with fixed-budget elicitation on the same users and frozen hypernet-work, measuring the tradeoff between enrollment burden and personalized prediction accuracy. 

5 Experiments 

Our research questions are: RQ1 : Can a latent value or preference profile inferred from an indi-vidual’s survey responses steer an LLM’s choices 4toward that individual’s judgments, both in-domain and on a new survey instrument, and how does it compare to existing inference and steering meth-ods? RQ2 : Can we steer the model to generate free-text user opinions on these survey questions? 

RQ3 : Can the inferred profile select which ques-tions to ask and decide when enough answers have been collected to steer the model’s choices? 

5.1 Latent value inference and steering (RQ1) RQ1 (a) Can we infer latent user profiles? We split each respondent’s items into an observed con-text and a held-out set, and report held-out accuracy against two controls that remove person informa-tion: population (¯z over training users on Food2D, the per-item training-fold average on PRIMEX, 

z = 0 on WVS) and shuffled (another user’s rep-resentation). Gains are paired per person with 95% person-clustered bootstrap intervals. Base-lines are a majority predictor and two alternative user-representation models: LoRe writes a user’s reward as a weighted sum of shared basis rewards (Bose et al., 2025), and PAL places the user at a convex combination of learned prototype ideal points and scores a response by its distance to that point (Chen et al., 2024). VISTA-VI (Value infer-ence component of VISTA) (Ours) has the largest user-specific accuracy lift on Food2D ( +11.4 vs. PAL’s +9.3 ) and WVS ( +8.7 vs. +6.6 ), and ties PAL on PrimeX, where the person effect is small for every model (Table 4). The gain also transfers across question types: given only the 18 abstract Primals items, VPL still lifts opinion prediction by +2.0 points, tied with PAL and double LoRe’s. We build on VPL because question selection and stopping both need a posterior, which PAL’s point estimate does not provide; PAL is carried forward as an ablation. 

RQ1 (b) Can we steer for answer choice selec-tion from inferred values? We evaluate VISTA with other baselines across Food2d, WVS, and PrimeX. On PrimeX, we do transfer learning from WVS: A frozen VPL encoder, trained on WVS (UK and US), maps the user’s Primals answers to a 32-dimensional value embedding z (the pos-terior mean). A hypernetwork maps z to a steer-ing vector added to the residual stream of Llama-3-8B-Instruct at layer 10; it is trained on the training folds’ PrimeX answers. At test time z

is shrunk toward the mean training embedding, 

¯z + s(z − ¯z), with s chosen on a separate calibra-tion fold. We compare VISTA with these models: (1) Zero-shot LLM. Llama-3-8B-Instruct answers each held-out item as a multiple-choice question over its option statements, with no information about the user. We take the highest-probability option, averaging over all cyclic orderings of the options. (2) In-context profile (LLM) (Jiang et al., 2025) We include all (130 on WVS) of the user’s answers into the prompt as the statements they chose. Because raw LLM option probabilities are poorly calibrated, we recalibrate each item’s logits with a per-item slope and bias fitted on the training folds. (3) Per-item base rate. This is the no- per-sonalization comparison, where each held-out item is answered with its modal class across users, fit-ted leave-one-fold-out so that no evaluation person contributes to the baseline scoring them. 

Setup and metrics. We frame each held-out item as a forced choice, selecting the highest-probability option averaged over all cyclic orderings, and com-paring it with the respondent’s answer. The model conditions on the respondent’s complete recorded responses and is scored on a disjoint set of their unseen items. On WVS, we split 3,956 train / 848 val / 848 test respondents. There are about 130 context answers each, with n=73,288 scored rows. Hypersteer turns z into a steering vector added to the residual stream of Llama-3-8B-Instruct at layer 10. On PrimeX, the encoder is frozen and trans-ferred from WVS, mapping a respondent’s Primals answers (context B) to z, shrunk toward the mean training embedding by a factor set on a separate cal-ibration fold. There are 858 people total, too small for a train/test split, so we instead rotate 5 disjoint cross-validation folds ( n=12,141 ). Food2D has 300 users with 24 observed and 16 disjoint held-out comparisons each ( n=4,800 ). The main metric is accuracy on the held-out set. We measure personal-ization gain in two ways: population conditions on the mean training representation, and shuffled on another respondent’s. The population gain (steered 

− population) is what knowing this person adds over knowing the population; the user-specific gain 

(steered − shuffled) additionally controls for condi-tioning on somebody at all. Contested items have train-label entropy above 0.6 nats (WVS, Food2D) or 0.65 nats on the other folds (PrimeX). All arms within a dataset are scored on identical rows, with 95% person-clustered bootstrap intervals. 

Results. (1) VISTA’s user representation matches or beats prompt-based profile steer-ing from the same evidence (Table 5). Given every observed answer, either to the encoder or 5to the prompt, VISTA ties in-context conditioning on PrimeX ( +0.41 points) and on contested WVS items ( +0.32 ), and wins on WVS overall ( +2.61 )and by nearly ten points on Food2D. (2) VISTA’s user-specific gain is a multiple of in-context’s on two of the three datasets (Table 5). Measured against another person’s representation, VISTA is twice in-context’s on PrimeX and about five times on Food2D. On WVS, the two are equivalent. (3) VISTA has the largest net gain across minority-and majority-view rows (Tables 6, 7). VISTA’s net gain is the highest on both PrimeX and WVS, where in-context gets -3.5 net gain. 

RQ1 (c) Are VISTA steering vectors inter-pretable? We extract the leading principle direc-tion ( ∆-PC1) of the per-respondent shift Hyper-Steer applies, and see what it corresponds to in the respondents’ own answer space and whether the steered model places each held-out respondent correctly along it. We find that: (1) A single inter-pretable steering axis spans both leading dimen-sions of answer variation (Fig 2, 7). ∆-PC1 aligns with neither leading answer component alone but cuts across the plane they span ( ρ = −0.72 and 

+0.46 on WVS; 0.54 and 0.33 on PrimeX). It is religious traditionalism on WVS (religious values/ ethics, against immigration / science / technology) and safety versus danger on PrimeX. (2) VISTA places unseen respondents on that axis where they actually sit. The unsteered model puts ev-eryone in the same place. The steered model re-covers each held-out respondent’s own position at r = 0.73 on WVS and r = 0.59 on PrimeX . UK respondents in WVS also completed a Moral Foundations questionnaire. We take their bind-ing score (the average of the loyalty, authority and purity foundations), find the direction in answer space that best tracks it among training respon-dents, and ask where the steered model places each held-out respondent along that direction. This is a demanding test because the value model is trained only to predict survey answers and never sees the moral-foundations questionnaire. The model re-covers each respondent’s own position at r = 0.73 .VISTA has learned where this individual sits, not where people sit on average. 

5.2 Steering to Generate Free Text (RQ2) Setup and metrics. WVS has no user-written re-sponses, so we evaluate on Food2D and PrimeX. Llama-3-8B-Instruct writes a free-text response to a question with no answer options, and a judge from a different model family (Qwen3.5-9B) reads it. Every system is scored against its own user-agnostic control (no persona for prompting, the population mean ¯z for VISTA), so the reported gain isolates what the user representation adds. On Food2D (23 simulated users) the prompting base-lines are oracles: a persona sentence written from the user’s true taste vector θ, optionally with its numeric coordinates. We report how often the sim-ulated user would prefer the generated text to the model’s base response (50% means no better). On PrimeX (686 unseen respondents) the prompting baselines receive the person’s 18 Primals answers and/or demographics as text, and VISTA a soft pre-fix distilled from the same 18 answers. Accuracy is agreement between the side the judge reads in the text and the respondent’s own survey answer. See Appendix 9.6 for more details.

(1) CAT (Xu et al., 2023) is the standard method for adaptive questionnaires: a two-parameter item-response model places each respon-dent on a single latent trait and selects the item with the highest Fisher information. 6 Conclusion 

We presented VISTA, which infers a posterior over a user’s values from a few survey answers, maps it through a hypernetwork to an intervention in a frozen LLM, and uses the same posterior to choose questions and decide when to stop. Across syn-thetic preferences, the World Values Survey and PrimeX, the learned latent matches or beats plac-ing the same answers in the prompt, at a fixed cost per query. On WVS, eight questions recover 94% of the gain from a respondent’s full history, and the dominant steering direction is a recognizable value axis on which unseen respondents are placed accurately. Together these results suggest that a compact value representation, elicited once and reused, is a practical route to aligning a model with an individual. We will release our code on github. 87 Limitations 

Eliciting informative questions for free-text gen-eration . Our current elicitation results are for forced choice only. Which questions are most in-formative for what a user would write, and when enough have been asked for generation, remains an open question. 

Using uncertainty for steering. VISTA carries two uncertainty signals that we only use partially. The first is the posterior variance over the value la-tent, which does enter question selection and stop-ping, but the steering intervention is generated from the posterior mean alone. The second is coherence, or how well a user’s answers fit the value structure learned from the population, which identifies users with unusual profiles. Future work could use them to scale steering strength, or to ask more questions of users the model represents poorly. 

Mitigating misalignment . Personalization does not help on every item. When a user holds the majority view, steering toward their inferred values sometimes pulls the model away from an answer it already answered correctly: accuracy on majority-view rows falls by 3.7 points on PrimeX forced choice and by 2.1 points on free text, although the net effect is positive. Future work could detect when steering is likely to hurt and withhold it in those cases. 

Surveys are not behavior. We evaluate on survey answers and on free-text opinions about survey top-ics. Value–behavior correlations are modest and uneven across dimensions (Bardi and Schwartz, 2003), attitudes predict behavior best when both are measured at the same level of specificity(Ajzen and Fishbein, 1977), and general moral values pre-dict everyday behavior more weakly than context-specific ones (van den Berg et al., 2022). Whether a latent inferred from value questions steers a model well on concrete dilemmas remains open. A direct testbed is the UniMoral data (Kumar and Jurgens, 2025), which contains moral dilemmas annotated with labels for action choices, ethical principles, contributing factors, and consequences, alongside annotators’ moral and cultural profiles. 

Generalization to model families . All steering experiments use one frozen base model, Llama-3-8B-Instruct. Two of VISTA’s three modules do not depend on the LLM: value inference embeds state-ments with a frozen sentence encoder and doesn’t use a casual LM, and adaptive elicitation selects and stops using only the posterior over user repre-sentation z learned from the value inference model. The only model-specific component in the hyper-network, whose output lives in one model’s resid-ual stream and needs to be retrained for each base model, with finetuning on the injection layer and steering norm. 

Risks . A model steered toward a user’s values may reinforce their existing views, including harmful ones, and reduce exposure to other perspectives (Kirk et al., 2024). We do not test whether steering weakens the base model’s safety behavior, and per-sonalization should be bounded so that it does not apply to factual or safety-relevant content.

9.2 Value-model search: latent size and training hyperparameters

Semi-Amortized User Refinement. Although amortized inference provides an efficient estimate of each user’s latent preferences, a single encoder shared across the population may produce subopti-mal representations for individual users. To address this amortization gap, we employ semi-amortized inference (Kim et al., 2018), combining population-level initialization with user-specific optimization. At inference time, the encoder provides an initial estimate μu,0 . 9.5 Steering details Steering for forced choice. The hypernetwork maps the inferred representation to a single steer-ing vector ∆u = hψ(ˆ zu ) ∈ Rd, where d is the hidden width of the base model. We constrain

direction of the intervention while its magnitude is held constant across users. At inference and during training we add α∆u to the residual stream at the output of layer 10. We sweep across various lay-ers of the residual stream of the LLM, and choose layer 10, which maximizes the user-specific effect (steered-shuffled) (Appendix Sec 9.3). The steering vector is broadcasted over every non-padding token position, following the convention used by Hyper-Steer and AxBench (Wu et al., 2025), and α is a scalar strength multiplier fixed to 1. Because ∥∆u∥

is fixed by construction, α is exactly the interven-tion magnitude, which makes it the natural place to modulate steering strength per user. The base LLM is frozen, but its activations are not detached: gradi-13 ents propagate through the injection site back into 

hψ , so the hypernetwork trains end-to-end through the frozen model. We train the additive steering module to predict held-out survey responses condi-tioned on each user’s inferred values. For each item, the LLM assigns scores to the valid answer letters, which are normalized into a categorical distribu-tion over the available options. The hypernetwork is trained using cross-entropy against the user’s selected answer, making its performance directly comparable to the value inference model’s held-out prediction accuracy.

Target policies . Training targets depend on the available dataset annotations. Each setting includes a random-policy control with targets drawn from another respondent independently of the user’s la-tent representation. This tests whether apparent gains reflect personalization rather than stylistic or evaluation biases. A distilled model should ap-proach its policy’s pool ceiling and not exceed it by much. 

9.6 Steering to generate free text experiment details (RQ2) Setup and metrics. We exclude WVS from this experiment because it does not contain any user written responses. Both Food2d and PrimeX share the same setup: Llama-3-8B-Instruct writes a free-text response to a question with no answer options, a Qwen3.5-9B judge reads the text. The judge is a different model family from the generator, so no system is rated by a relative of its own writer, and it sees only the question, its options and the generated text. It is validated separately on each dataset: on Food2D, calibrated per axis to GPT-4o on 300 labelled responses, it reproduces GPT-4o’s candidate-vs-baseline winner 81.5% of the time [78.8, 84.1] (tied with Qwen3.5-35B-A3B); on PrimeX, judging side as A / B / unclear with option orders averaged, it labels the survey’s own statements 18/18 and is right 95.4% of the time on held-out respondents’ real explanations when it commits to a side, abstaining on 47%. Every system is scored against its own user-agnostic control, meaning there is no persona for the prompting baselines, the training-population mean ¯z for distilled prefixes (VISTA), so the re-ported gain isolates what the user representation adds. The datasets differ in what personalization is available and in what the judge extracts. On Food2D (23 simulated people) the baselines are oracles: a persona sentence rendered from the per-son’s true taste vector θ (e.g., “this user strongly loves bold heat and somewhat prefers plant-based dishes”), optionally with the raw unit-vector co-ordinates appended (“on a −1 to +1 scale, spici-ness: +0.9 , veginess: +0.4 ”). The judge scores the generated text’s own φ = ( spiciness , veginess ),and we report the simulated user’s BTL probabil-ity of preferring that text to the question’s base response, win = σ(θ · (φtext − φbase )) , where 50% means the text is no better than the model’s base response. VISTA never sees θ. On PrimeX (686 un-seen respondents) no system has an oracle: prompt-ing baselines (persona, demographic persona, per-sona+demographics) receive the person’s 18 world-belief (Primals) answers and/or their demograph-ics as text, VISTA receives a 16-token soft prefix distilled from the same 18 answers, and generic writers (unsteered, population ¯z) receive the ques-tion alone. The judge reads which side of the topic 14 the text takes, and accuracy is agreement with the respondent’s own survey answer. 

9.7 Active elicitation experiment details (RQ3) 

We evaluate our system in a more realistic setting, where users only answer up to 10 questions, instead of the full set of survey questions. We want to see if the inferred profile select which questions to ask and decide when to stop, so that a few answers suffice to steer the model’s answer choices . 

Models for comparison . We compare VISTA with other end to end systems, user beliefs, its own question-selection rule, and its own predictor for the held-out answers. (1) 2PL CAT (Fisher Infor-mation) (Xu et al., 2023) is the standard method for adaptive questionnaires, and the only baseline with a stopping rule. A two-parameter logistic item-response model places each respondent on a single latent trait θ and each item j on a dis-crimination aj and a difficulty bj . The posterior is: P (yij =1 | θi) = σ(aj (θi − bj )) , and we fit 

(a, b) by marginal maximum likelihood. We use fisher information for selection, integrated over the posterior. The predictor is the IRT posterior predic-tive. CAT also has its own stopping rule: stop once the posterior standard error falls below a threshold, which we evaluate at the textbook operating point 

SE < 0.30 (reliability 0.91 ). (2) Prompt-Only (LLM) (Zhang et al., 2024): LLM is shown the answers so far and 15 shuffled candidate items, and names the next question, It then predicts the held-out answers itself, in context, from the answers it collected. The context answers then enter the prompt as the statements the person chose, where each target asked is a letter choice over its options. 

(3) Chain of Thought (CoT, ReAct-Style) (Yao et al., 2023). This is prompt-only with an explicit Thought to Action step before each pick. (4) BED-LLM (Choudhury et al., 2026). We follow their preference-elicitation setup, in which a hypothesis is a paragraph of natural language describing the user. We then sample a pool of K = 1,500 short respondent profiles from Llama-3-8B-Instruct and take the model’s normalized distribution over each bank item’s answer options, given a profile, as the likelihood p(y | θk, x). A respondent’s answers reweight this pool and we select the item maximiz-ing EIG. We use the same reweighted pool as its predictor. (5) Per item modal answer : we pre-dict each item’s most common answer, fitted on respondents who are not scored. 

Setup and metrics . Each system is run for a bud-get of q questions. It selects one question at a time from the respondent’s own candidate pool and is shown their answer before the next pick. Then the system predicts that respondent’s held-out an-swers. We sweep q ∈ 1, 2, 5, 10 . On WVS, 40% of each respondent’s answered items are held out and the remainder forms their candidate pool (131 items), with 8 held-out items scored per respondent. PRIMEX has a 48-item instrument with 33-item askable pool, and we report over 5 disjoint folds with about 14 scored items per person. On Food2d, a unit is (person, held-out question q∗, seed), and the pool is 9 × 190 = 1,710 comparisons from the other nine questions. Each system is scored on 8 held-out comparisons from q∗, which it was never allowed to ask about. This cross-question setup is different from the forced-choice experi-ment on Food2d in Section 5.1, where we condi-tion on a respondent’s complete recorded response set (since our research question there is represen-tational). Here, the object of the study is about budget. So we require pool and scored items to come from disjoint questions. Because Food2d answers are Bradley-Terry draws, accuracy has a noise ceiling of 0.666 over all pairs against a 0.496 

modal floor, so we also report it as a fraction of that range, as Section 5.1 does. The main metric for all three datasets is accuracy on held-out. We also re-port gain over the budget: acc( qmax ) − acc( qmin ),which measures whether a system uses the answers it collects. All intervals are paired and respondent-clustered: every system is scored on the same held-out answers of the same people, and we bootstrap the per-answer difference 10,000 times over the respondents. Stopping. The two rules differ. CAT stops when its posterior SE falls below a threshold; at SE < 0.30 that is 6.72 questions on WVS, trig-gers for 60% of units at 7.36 questions on Food2d, and never triggers within budget on PrimeX (me-dian SE 0.613 ). VISTA stops when acquisition score falls below ε times that respondent’s own first-step score, with ε fit on calibration respondents: it asks 8.1 questions and retains 93.6% of the gain available from the complete response history, with no measurable loss against the full ten-question budget ( +0.32 [ −0.19, +0.85] ). CAT recovers at most 34.8% of that gain at any budget we measured (33.5%, 28.9%, 21.5%, 34.8% at q = 1, 2, 5, 10 ), so no threshold choice can improve it. 15 20 40 60
