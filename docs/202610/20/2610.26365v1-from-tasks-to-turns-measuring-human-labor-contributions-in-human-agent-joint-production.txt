Abstract 

In agent workflows, a brief human instruc-tion can alter subsequent search, reasoning, and tool execution, yet final task outcomes rarely reveal the contribution of an individual instruction to human–agent joint production. We propose a Human Turn-level framework for measuring human contributions, using task quality and token usage to characterize the collaborative production outcomes associated with each human instruction. We compare continuations from adjacent checkpoints un-der matched models, budgets, and termina-tion conditions, and assess stability through re-peated measurements. Across four archived and simulated interaction settings, we conduct 29,124 comparisons between predicted and ob-served directions of quality and computational change at both Human Turn and session levels. On HumanBench, we obtain 506 valid com-parisons across 85 real-user scenarios. Results show that quality and computational changes associated with human guidance depend on task conditions. Expert-style and ordinary-style guidance differ in information provision, correction, and repetition. Even when task quality after continuation is identical, remain-ing computational demands can differ substan-tially across checkpoints. Our framework ad-vances the measurement of human labor con-tributions from tasks and sessions to individ-ual turns, providing a repeatable, fine-grained foundation for studying human contributions and their allocation in human–agent joint pro-duction. 

1 Introduction 

As language-model agents become involved in software development, tool use, and long-horizon tasks, human labor increasingly takes the form of natural-language guidance: identifying errors, adding constraints, supplying information, or de-ciding what should happen next. One brief in-struction may save a lengthy search; another may prompt additional verification and tool calls. Its productive effect is reflected in the subsequent work jointly carried out by the human and agent. Measuring labor embedded in machine execution is therefore a fundamental problem for understand-ing human–agent joint production. Task-based frameworks in labor economics ex-plain technological change through the allocation of tasks between labor and capital (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019). Research on human–agent col-laboration often evaluates final artifacts, task suc-cess, or entire sessions (Chang et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2026b). These units capture team perfor-mance but aggregate human guidance, agent exe-cution, and environmental feedback. Two sessions may pass the same acceptance test while consum-ing different amounts of computation, and individ-ual guidance turns may accompany different forms of progress. Final outcomes alone cannot describe the production outcomes associated with individ-ual human inputs. We address this gap at the level of a single guid-ance turn, which we call a Human Turn . We opera-tionalize “labor contributions” not as hourly wages or monetary value, but as changes in joint exe-cution state and remaining token use associated with human natural-language guidance. We record each human input alongside the subsequent joint work, retaining two separate dimensions: task quality, assessed against explicit acceptance crite-ria, and computational input, measured by logged token usage. Under matched task, model, and bud-get conditions, these dimensions connect guidance to observable outcomes and resources consumed during execution. The framework combines session-level and turn-level records. For a session, we record final 1arXiv:2610.26365v1 [cs.HC] 19 Oct 2026 quality and the total tokens consumed by both exe-cuting and guiding models. For a Human Turn, we align the instruction and subsequent agent activity with adjacent checkpoints, independently resume the task from each checkpoint under matched con-ditions, and compare final quality and remaining tokens. These comparisons describe local collabo-rative state changes; they do not isolate the instruc-tion’s causal effect. We also examine guidance functions, information added beyond the preced-ing history, and task state to understand variation in the measurements. We study five settings involving tool execution, code repair, mathematical reasoning, and human-origin conversations. Four archived and simulated settings yield 29,124 evaluation judgments com-paring predicted and observed directions of quality and computational change. We compare expert-style and ordinary-style simulated guidance, as-sess perceived naturalness through blind human judgments, and examine stability through repeated autonomous continuations. HumanBench adds 506 valid checkpoint comparisons from 85 human-origin source sessions. Of these, 409 achieve iden-tical final quality, while remaining token use varies in both directions. Our contributions are threefold. First, we in-troduce a micro-level measurement framework organized around the Human Turn, linking hu-man inputs to their associated joint execution. Second, we develop a two-dimensional proto-col based on task acceptance and token usage, with matched conditions, complete model-token accounting, and repeated measurements. Third, we apply the framework to simulated guidance and human-origin interactions, exposing relation-ships between guidance behaviors, task states, and quality–computation outcomes that aggregate task results obscure. The framework supplies empiri-cal quantities for studying labor contributions and collaboration capabilities, rather than assigning a monetary price to human labor. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Task Allocation and Human–Agent Evaluation 

Task-based economics studies allocation between labor and capital (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019); mixed-initiative and human–AI design work stud-ies coordination and user control (Horvitz, 1999; Amershi et al., 2019). Collaboration benchmarks emphasize joint outcomes or sessions (Chang et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2026a,b). Interaction logs and meta-analysis further reveal process de-tail and heterogeneous gains (Lee et al., 2022; Vac-caro et al., 2024). We instead examine individual guidance turns and their associated execution. 

2.2 Language Feedback and Human Contributions 

Agents interleave reasoning, actions, and observa-tions (Yao et al., 2023), and MINT studies sim-ulated feedback in tool use (Wang et al., 2024). Model critique and reflection support refinement (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Wad-hwa et al., 2024), yet multi-turn information can be used unreliably (Laban et al., 2026). Unlike these feedback sources, our unit is a human turn. Whereas Xie et al. (2026) estimate human informa-tion in final artifacts, we pair guidance with subse-quent quality and token use. 

2.3 Process Evaluation and Computational Input 

Executable benchmarks measure task completion (Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024a; Jimenez et al., 2024); AgentBoard tracks execution progress (Ma et al., 2024), and step verification supplies interme-diate training supervision (Lightman et al., 2024). Cost-aware evaluation and test-time scaling moti-vate measuring computation (Kapoor et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2025). We separately record full-session and checkpoint-to-finish tokens. 

2.4 Rubric-Based Evaluation and Simulated Users 

Rubrics and specialized evaluators make criteria explicit (Hashemi et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024), but LLM judges have known biases (Zheng et al., 2023). We there-fore compare predictors with execution-derived evidence. Interaction benchmarks provide con-trolled simulated users (Zhou et al., 2024b; Yao et al., 2025), whose reliability requires separate study (Dou et al., 2025). We assess linguistic nat-uralness blindly and execution effects on human-origin interactions.

3.4 Comparable Conditions and Repeated Measurements 

Valid comparisons require compatible task crite-ria, continuation models, and budgets. We record quality validity, token completeness, and technical interruptions separately. Joint analyses use their valid intersection; individual dimensions use their corresponding valid sets. Each checkpoint receives one prespecified to-ken estimate: the arithmetic mean of three com-plete, contract-matched continuations when avail-able, or a previously selected valid single continua-tion otherwise. Comparisons sharing a checkpoint reuse that estimate. Repeated token measurements do not overwrite prespecified quality observations. Repeatability analyses fix quality-tied compar-isons with three complete continuations per end-point. We compare individual runs and endpoint means, clustering bootstrap analyses by root task and checking alternative weighting schemes (Ap-pendix B). 

3.5 Guidance Behavior and Text-Based Evaluation 

We label simulated guidance by one primary lin-guistic function and potentially multiple informa-tion changes relative to its history (Appendix D). We compare four text-based evaluators: Direct, without explicit criteria; CollabSkill-fixed, with eight fixed criteria (Shao et al., 2026b); RaR, with task-specific criteria but without reinforce-ment learning (Gunjal et al., 2026); and JEV, with an independent model and native selection inter-face. 4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Tasks and Datasets 

We use five settings (Table 1): multistep tool ex-ecution in Terminal-Bench (Merrill et al., 2026), code repair in HumanEvalFix (Muennighoff et al., 2024), two mathematical-reasoning settings based on Omni-MATH (Gao et al., 2025), and human-origin code and text sessions in HumanBench. Omni100 and Omni300 overlap in underlying mathematics problems but follow different interac-tion and restoration protocols, so we report them separately. Across four archived and simulated settings, we obtain 29,124 predicted-versus-observed di-rectional judgments, summing four evaluators over quality and computation at adjacent- and first–last-checkpoint levels. Each dimension and level uses its common valid sample. Sepa-rately, 3,400 archived checkpoint pairs yield 3,168 token-complete, contract-matched pairs and 3,043 jointly valid quality–computation pairs. Session ledgers and evaluator outputs retain their own valid sets (Appendices A and C).

5 Results 

5.1 Why Human Turn-Level Measurement? 

Final quality alone does not capture computational demand. Across four archived and simulated set-tings, 2,537 checkpoint comparisons are jointly valid and 2,320 achieve identical final quality. Tie rates range from 80.1% to 97.8%, yet remaining to-kens vary in both directions within these ties (Fig-ure 2). In HumanEvalFix, 62.6% of tied compar-isons use over 20% fewer remaining tokens and 19.6% use over 20% more. The corresponding pro-portions are 11.4% and 55.0% in Omni300, and 32.4% and 32.6% in Terminal. Quality and com-putational input therefore provide complementary observations of collaborative production. Text-based judgments provide proxy estimates. Table 2 compares predictions of remaining-computation direction. At turn level, JEV has the highest average accuracy (53.3%), while RaR has the highest three-class Macro-F1 (36.1%). At ses-sion level, CollabSkill-fixed has the highest ac-curacy (55.7%) and RaR the highest Macro-F1 (35.1%). Different rankings distinguish overall ac-curacy from balanced recognition of all three di-rections. Repeated autonomous continuations under matched archived configurations exhibit consis-tent aggregate patterns (Table 3). On the fixed quality-tied subset, three runs yield proportions of 60.93%, 61.67%, and 61.02% with over 20% fewer tokens, and 20.19%, 20.84%, and 22.60% with over 20% more. 5.2 Comparing Synthetic Guidance Strategies 

The strategies differ in linguistic behavior (Fig-ure 3). Expert-style guidance more often sup-plies new information (15.7% versus 7.6%), re-finements (81.6% versus 75.3%), and correc-tions (41.0% versus 35.6%). Ordinary-style guid-ance more often contains repetition (60.8% ver-sus 56.4%) and clarification (4.6% versus 0.6%). Role-specific prompting thus produces different information-provision and interaction patterns. Guidance-associated outcomes depend on task conditions. Figure 4 compares matched tasks and execution conditions. Among quality-tied Hu-manEvalFix comparisons, the task-weighted pro-portions with over 20% fewer remaining tokens are 76.37% for ordinary-style and 73.56% for expert-style guidance. Terminal yields 25.79% and 32.61%, respectively. Complete-session outcomes provide a comple-mentary view (Table 4). In Terminal, expert-style guidance achieves 81.04% quality versus 70.65% for ordinary-style guidance, with 4.0% fewer to-tal tokens in the point estimates. HumanEval-Fix yields 98.57% versus 100.00% quality, with 7.8% more expert-style tokens. In Omni100, both achieve 100.00% quality, while expert-style guid-ance uses 10.6% fewer tokens. These descrip-tive estimates retain the archived paired-session cohorts. Complete accounting distinguishes execution from guidance tokens. In HumanEvalFix, indepen-dent execution averages 16.77k tokens. Collabo-ration uses 19.53k execution tokens plus 14.63k guidance tokens: 34.16k in total, a 103.7% in-crease. Guidance contributes 42.8% of collabora-tive model tokens. The session ledger therefore captures resources that local checkpoint measure-ments do not represent. In the blinded naturalness study’s subset with-out exact-match exchanges, original human mes-sages, prompt-based simulations, and dialogue-adapted utterances are selected as most human-like in 32.1%, 26.7%, and 41.2% of valid judg-ments. This provides a human assessment of lin-guistic naturalness (Appendix F).

6 Discussion 

6.1 From Task Outcomes to Human Labor Contributions 

Human guidance supplies information, corrects er-rors, and redirects machine execution. Human Turn-level records connect each instruction to the associated joint work, acceptance outcomes, and remaining computation. Alongside session ac-counting, these records expose local production differences without collapsing collaboration into an overall score. 

6.2 Comparable Measurement of Quality and Computation 

Task acceptance and logged tokens make qual-ity and computation observable. Matched exe-cution conditions, complete accounting, and re-peated continuations establish comparability with-out imposing an exchange rate between the two di-mensions. Here, fairness means operational com-parability, rather than a definitive valuation of la-bor or attribution of net contribution to an instruc-tion. 

6.3 Studying Value in Human–Agent Joint Production 

These records supply empirical variables for study-ing guidance skills, division of labor, and resource allocation. Comparing people requires broader shared-task coverage, repeated observations, and controls for effort. Controlled counterfactual con-tinuations, human time, and outcome value could support future studies of attributable contributions and value distribution. 

7 Conclusion 

A human instruction can shape subsequent search, reasoning, tool use, and task execution. We in-troduce a Human Turn-level framework that asso-ciates this joint work with task quality and com-putational input. Across five settings, complete session accounting, adjacent-checkpoint continua-tions, and guidance analysis expose process-level differences obscured by final outcomes. In partic-ular, 506 valid comparisons from 85 human-origin source sessions show that remaining computation can differ despite identical task acceptance. Mov-ing from tasks and sessions to Human Turns es-tablishes a reproducible micro-level empirical ba-sis for studying the productive roles of guidance, comparing collaborative behaviors, and investigat-ing labor contributions in human–agent joint pro-duction. 

Limitations 

Measurement and attribution. Our framework measures collaborative state changes associated with human guidance. Adjacent-checkpoint inter-vals may contain human instructions, agent execu-tion, and environmental feedback, so the observed changes cannot be interpreted as isolated causal ef-fects of individual instructions. Equal native ac-ceptance scores do not necessarily imply identical underlying quality. Data and scope. Expert-style and ordinary-style guidance are simulated strategies. Blind human evaluation does not make their differences directly generalizable to real human populations. Hu-manBench contains genuine instructions from 85 source sessions, not 85 independent participants. Our settings cover software engineering, multistep tool execution, mathematical reasoning, and gen-eral interactions. Applicability to other domains requires further validation. 

Comparability and generalization. Task crite-ria, models, tokenizers, budgets, and termination conditions define the scope of comparison. Re-peated measurements assess stability, but stochas-tic execution can still affect individual pairs. Ob-servations from different tasks and conditions are not inherently comparable. The protocol applies primarily to joint production with explicit objec-tives, verifiable criteria, and observable computa-tion. Comparing real individuals requires broader shared-task coverage, repeated observations, and controls for human effort and collaboration con-ditions. Settings without standardized acceptance criteria, including creative writing, open-ended decisions, and social interaction, require further study. 

Ethical Considerations 

We report anonymized aggregate statistics for human-origin interactions. Original private mes-sages, proprietary code, and identifying informa-tion are not distributed with the paper. Any later re-lease of interactions requires separate verification of source permissions and redistribution rights. This work develops an empirical basis for study-ing human labor contributions, rather than directly determining wages, hiring decisions, or perfor-mance evaluations. Comparisons between people must account for tasks, computational resources, experience, and uncertainty. B Repeatability and Weighting Sensitivity 

We measure repeatability on a fixed subset of equal-quality comparisons. Eligibility requires equal original quality, complete costs at both end-points, and three continuations matching the con-tract at each endpoint. Of 1,119 original equal-quality comparisons, 1,075 meet these require-ments; the remaining 44 lack complete triplicates at both endpoints. Each repeat and the endpoint-mean calculation use this same eligible subset. With equal weight per source session on this subset, savings frequencies range from 65.80%– 68.51% across the three repeats, and increase fre-quencies range from 15.40%–19.14%. With equal weight per root task, the corresponding ranges are 63.83%–64.63% and 17.77%–21.20%. Leaving out one continuation at a time and averaging the remaining two at each endpoint gives savings rates of 60.00%, 60.93%, and 62.79%, and increase rates of 20.19%, 19.63%, and 18.88%. In all three cases, bootstrap intervals over root tasks for the difference between savings and increase frequen-cies remain positive. For variation in single-run tokens at the same checkpoint, 1,797 complete states yield 5,391 pair-wise ratios, with an empirical 90% ratio-coverage scale of approximately 2.977. Thus, 20% serves as a display threshold for magnitude, not a test of sta-tistical significance for an individual comparison. Figure 6 summarizes the comparison-weighted results. Averaging all three token measurements at each endpoint before computing ratios yields 62.51% savings and 19.26% increases. All esti-mates use the same 1,075 comparisons. E HumanBench Data Provenance 

HumanBench contributes 85 human-origin source sessions covering 49 root tasks and 600 adja-cent checkpoint pairs. Of these pairs, 506 have valid task-quality observations, complete to-ken records, and matched continuation contracts. The 85 source sessions are not 85 independent participants. The paper reports only aggregate execution-based measurements and does not dis-tribute original messages, code, screenshots, or model-assisted semantic-category analyses.
