ABSTRACT 

AI agents increasingly interact with external environments to complete complex user tasks. These interactions can lead to unexpected behavior and expose agents to security threats such as indirect prompt injection. Current agent security sys-tems largely protect agents by restricting their actions, which can result in a sub-stantial security–utility trade-off for complex, open-ended tasks. To address this, we propose a quantitative risk-based approach to access control for AI agents that regulates measurable risks accumulated during task execution. As a concrete realization of this approach, we introduce R ATION , a security framework that tracks measurable risks associated with agent actions and enforces quantitative constraints throughout execution. R ATION first constructs a task-specific policy by combining explicit constraints derived from the user request with implicit risks calibrated from historical executions of similar tasks. continuously tracks the accumulated risks and applies different enforcement strategies depending on the type of risk. Extensive experiments on four security benchmarks show that R ATION provides strong security while maintaining high task utility across diverse models and environments. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

The impressive reasoning and planning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have enabled agentic systems that can execute complex tasks on behalf of users. These AI agents are rapidly be-coming part of the everyday computing ecosystem, yet security mechanisms have not kept pace with their growing capabilities. Existing security mechanisms were developed for pre-agent computing environments characterized by tightly scoped and largely deterministic software behavior. Access control mechanisms, in particular, are primarily designed around human users and often grant them broad privileges that are, in principle, sufficient to cause significant harm. This design rests on sev-eral assumptions: most users act in good faith; malicious actors can be deterred through auditing and real-world consequences; and humans take actions, whether correct or erroneous, at a relatively limited rate. AI agents challenge these assumptions. They can exercise privileges and take actions at machine speed, are not themselves deterred by real-world consequences, and may take actions that are technically permitted but that a typical human user would rarely take. As agents become increasingly autonomous, access control mechanisms designed for human users are poorly matched to the needs for controlling agents. We argue that Quantitative Risk-Based Access Control (QRBAC) is a promising paradigm for controlling agent access. The key ideas are to (1) quantify the risk associated with each individual access; (2) track the cumulative risk incurred over time; (3) establish a risk threshold for a task or entity; and (4) make access control decisions by comparing the accumulated risk against this threshold. The concept of risk-based access control was introduced in a JASON report that examined the limitations of traditional access-control models and advocated more flexible approaches that explicitly account for risk (JASON Program Office, 2004). Despite its promise, risk-based access control has seen limited adoption, perhaps because conventional mechanisms, such as Discretionary Access Control and Role-Based Access Control (Sandhu et al., 1996), have provided a reasonable balance between security and utility for human users in the pre-agent era. We argue that this balance 1arXiv:2610.24311v1 [cs.CR] 16 Oct 2026 Preprint changes with the emergence of AI agents. Their autonomy, speed, and ability to perform long sequences of actions make QRBAC particularly well suited to controlling agent access. In this paper, we apply the principle of QRBAC to AI agents, focusing on mitigating the risks caused by indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks (Greshake et al., 2023). Existing agents use tools to interact with external environments and incorporate the resulting observations into subsequent decisions (Yao et al., 2023; Debenedetti et al., 2024). However, such interactions also expose agents to untrusted external content. In IPI, an adversary embeds malicious instructions in external content to manipulate an agent into executing unintended actions. Current system-level defenses for IPI impose external controls around the agent’s interaction with its environment, drawing on the idea of information-flow control (Denning, 1976). These mechanisms improve security by restricting what information may propagate to the agent or which actions it may perform. However, legitimate behavior often depends on information discovered during execution, making necessary adaptations difficult to distinguish from adversarial manipulation. Existing defenses can therefore face a signif-icant security–utility trade-off, particularly for complex tasks. Moreover, existing defenses typically constrain the agent at the level of individual actions: they determine whether each proposed action is permitted, such as which tools the agent may invoke and which data it may access. However, an execution can accumulate substantial security risk even when every action it takes is individually permitted. For example, an injected log entry could repeatedly induce an agent to restart an authorized service. Each restart may satisfy the action-level policy, yet repeated restarts can keep the service unavailable. Action-level policies alone therefore cannot capture security conditions that depend on what accumulates across an execution. Motivated by these limitations, we introduce R ATION , a system-level security framework that real-izes QRBAC for AI agents. R ATION constructs and enforces a task-specific security policy from two complementary sources. Explicit constraints capture requirements stated in the user request, while 

implicit risks use execution histories of similar tasks to establish reference thresholds for behavioral quantities. Before task execution, an LLM-based Provisioner identifies the applicable task category and combines its implicit rules with explicit constraints extracted from the user request. During ex-ecution, a deterministic Risk Monitor tracks the relevant risk quantities and evaluates each proposed tool call against the resulting policy. It blocks actions that violate explicit constraints and intervenes when implicit risks exceed their thresholds, allowing the agent to reconsider subsequent actions. We evaluate R ATION on four agent security benchmarks ( i.e., DTap, AgentDyn, AgentDojo, and ASB) against a broad range of existing defenses. Across these benchmarks, R ATION consistently provides strong security while preserving task utility. On DTap, the most challenging benchmark in our evaluation, R ATION reduces the average attack success rate (ASR) from 38.7% to 16.5%, while preserving or even increasing the task utility. On the other three benchmarks, R ATION re-duces ASR to at most 1.4% while maintaining high task utility. Compared with existing defenses, which often achieve stronger security by substantially restricting legitimate agent behavior, R ATION 

provides a favorable security–utility trade-off. We further evaluate R ATION across different models and agent security threats beyond IPI, demonstrating its effectiveness and broad applicability. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: • We identify QRBAC as a security paradigm particularly well suited to AI agents. • We develop R ATION as a concrete realization of this paradigm. R ATION constructs task-specific risk policies from user-specified constraints and execution histories of similar tasks, and enforces them through deterministic runtime monitoring. • We evaluate R ATION against a wide range of defenses across four agent security benchmarks. The results show that R ATION consistently provides strong security while preserving task utility. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Prompt Injection Attacks. Prompt injection attacks embed malicious instructions either directly in user requests or indirectly in external information ingested by LLM agents. These attacks cause LLMs to confuse untrusted data with instructions and follow attacker-specified objectives. Prompt injection has been listed as the number one security issue for LLM applications (OWASP GenAI Security Project, 2026). For LLM agents, such attacks can manipulate multi-step reasoning and induce unauthorized tool actions, data exfiltration, or other harmful behavior (Zhan et al., 2024). 2Preprint 

Indirect Prompt Injection Defenses. Existing defenses against IPI can be broadly divided into model-level and system-level approaches. Model-level defenses improve the model’s ability to distinguish trusted instructions from injected content through defensive fine-tuning (Chen et al., 2025a;b;c; Peng et al., 2026). System-level defenses instead impose external controls around the agent without modifying its underlying model. These approaches reinforce or distinguish trusted instructions from external content (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Hines et al., 2024), detect and filter suspected injections (Liu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; Chennabasappa et al., 2025), verify whether proposed instructions or actions remain consistent with the user request (Jia et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; He et al., 2026), or constrain execution through tool permissions, tool arguments, workflows, and information-flow policies (An et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Costa et al., 2025; Debenedetti et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Hu et al., 2026). We discuss these defenses in detail in Ap-pendix A and compare R ATION against representative methods from these categories, showing that existing defenses often face a notable security–utility trade-off. 

Quantitative Risk-Based Access Control. Risk-based access control has been explored as a more flexible alternative to conventional authorization models. An early formulation appeared in a 2004 JASON report, produced by an independent scientific advisory group that advises the U.S. govern-ment on science and national-security issues, which proposed explicitly incorporating acceptable risk into access-control decisions (JASON Program Office, 2004). Subsequent work further devel-oped this idea, usually focusing on how to estimate the risks of individual accesses (Cheng et al., 2007; Molloy et al., 2008; Wang & Jin, 2011). However, these efforts have largely remained at the conceptual or design level, with limited concrete implementation and adoption. R ATION builds on this principle by applying quantitative risk-based access control to AI agents.

Threat Model. We consider a tool-using agent that receives a user request and interacts with external systems through a set of tools. We use task execution to refer to the complete sequence of agent interactions from receiving the user request until the task is completed or terminated. Following the threat model for IPI (Greshake et al., 2023), we assume that the user request and the agent’s configuration (e.g., system messages and tool descriptions) are trusted, while external observations, such as webpages, files, and tool outputs, may con-tain untrusted or adversarial content. RATION regulates the agent through a task-specific risk policy , which consists of rules over the agent’s tool calls. The policy com-bines two complementary sources. Explicit constraints capture requirements stated in the user request, such as an email recipient or a spending limit. An implicit risk policy captures the ex-pected tool use and execution behavior of similar tasks, based on their historical executions. These rules may constrain either an individual tool call or quantities accumulated over the execution. Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow of R ATION , which consists of two stages: policy construc-tion and runtime monitoring . During policy construction, R ATION uses historical task executions to construct an implicit risk policy for each task category. When a new user request arrives, an LLM-based Provisioner identifies its task category, selects the corresponding implicit risk policy, and extracts explicit constraints from the request. Together, they form the task-specific risk policy. At runtime, a deterministic Risk Monitor evaluates each proposed tool call against this policy and updates the accumulated risk quantities as the task proceeds. As summarized in Table 1, a task-specific risk policy combines explicit constraints from the user request with an implicit risk policy associated with its task category. We next describe how these two components are constructed for a new task. 

Explicit Constraints. The user request provides the most direct specification of what the agent is authorized to do. R ATION translates these requirements into structured constraints over tool calls. We consider three common forms of explicit constraints. A value constraint restricts a tool argument to user-authorized values, such as a specified email recipient. A per-action limit bounds a numeric quantity in an individual call, while a total limit bounds a count or accumulated quantity across multiple calls. The Provisioner extracts these requirements from the user request and grounds each one to the corresponding tool and, when applicable, tool argument using the declared tool interfaces. 

Implicit Risk Policy. User requests typically specify what an agent should accomplish, but do not fully characterize how the task should be carried out. Our intuition is that tasks with similar goals tend to rely on similar tools and exhibit similar execution patterns. Historical executions of such tasks can therefore provide a useful reference for the expected behavior of a new task. In deployed 4Preprint agentic systems, such histories can be collected from execution logs, providing a practical source for estimating implicit risks. R ATION treats deviations from these expected patterns as implicit risks that may warrant additional intervention. To instantiate this idea, R ATION first groups historical user requests into a small number of task categories according to the task of the user request. The historical executions associated with each category are used to construct its implicit risk policy. Specifically, each implicit risk policy contains two components: a tool allow-list and a set of behavioral thresholds . The behavioral thresholds constrain three execution quantities: total tool calls, write/execute calls, and token usage. For each quantity, R ATION records its distribution across historical benign executions and sets the corre-sponding threshold to a high quantile, using the 95th percentile by default. Following the privilege categories of DRIFT (Li et al., 2025a), we treat a tool as write/execute if it modifies data or triggers an interaction with a third party, and use an LLM to label each tool. For requests that do not match any identified category, R ATION uses a fallback category that permits all available tools and adopts the maximum corresponding behavioral thresholds across the identified categories. 

Policy Provisioning. Before task execution begins, an LLM-based Provisioner constructs the task-specific risk policy for the current user request. It identifies the corresponding task category and selects its implicit risk policy, while also extracting explicit constraints from the user request and grounding them to the relevant tools and arguments. The selected implicit risk policy and extracted explicit constraints together form the task-specific risk policy used throughout execution. 3.3 RUNTIME MONITORING AND ENFORCEMENT 

Risk Monitoring. During execution, a deterministic Risk Monitor intercepts every proposed tool call before it reaches the environment. For rules over an individual tool call, the monitor checks the proposed tool and its arguments against the policy. For cumulative tool-call rules, it evaluates the proposed call together with the current accumulated quantities to determine whether execution would exceed the corresponding limit or threshold. After an allowed call is executed, the monitor updates the relevant accumulated quantities and uses the updated state to evaluate subsequent calls. Each quantity is maintained separately and evaluated against its corresponding limit or threshold. 

Enforcement. RATION applies different enforcement depending on the type of policy rule. Vio-lations of explicit constraints or the tool allow-list are treated as hard policy violations: the call is blocked before execution, and the agent is informed of the violated rule. When an implicit behav-ioral threshold is exceeded or would be exceeded by the proposed call, the monitor holds the call and prompts the agent to reconsider the action. After each intervention, the corresponding threshold is increased to 1.5 times its previous value to avoid repeatedly interrupting the agent at the same level of activity. The agent’s next proposed action is then evaluated through the same process. If no hard policy rule is violated and no behavioral threshold is exceeded, the call is allowed to execute. 3.4 DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS 

RATION is intended as a proof-of-concept realization of QRBAC for AI agents, with a lightweight and extensible design that controls measurable risks while preserving execution flexibility. It uses broadly applicable risk quantities and enforcement mechanisms that apply to tool-using agents across domains. Our experiments show that these quantities provide useful signals for controlling attack-induced behavior. Practitioners can adapt the framework by defining domain-specific risk quantities and corresponding monitoring and enforcement strategies. For example, in interactive deployments, a tool call that violates the risk policy can be escalated to the user for confirmation. We discuss these design choices, their scope, and the limitations of R ATION in Appendix H.

Evaluation Metrics. Following prior work (Debenedetti et al., 2024), we report three metrics: Attack Success Rate (ASR), Utility Under Attack (UA), and Benign Utility (BU). ASR is the fraction of attacked executions in which the agent achieves the attacker’s goal. UA and BU measure the fraction of tasks for which the agent completes the user request under attack and without attack. ASR and UA are measured on the same attacked executions and are not complementary, as both outcomes may occur in a single execution. We report 95% confidence intervals for main results. 

Implementation Details. We use GPT-5.4 on DTap and GPT-4.1 on the remaining benchmarks for the main evaluation. We select these models to ensure sufficient capability for benign task com-pletion while retaining a non-trivial attack signal (Table 14). As described in Section 3.2, R ATION 

constructs its implicit policies from historical executions. In our experiments, these consist of exe-cutions of each benchmark’s user requests by the undefended agent. We use five-fold cross-fitting: for each held-out fold, we rebuild the task categories and their implicit policies using only the user requests and historical executions from the other four folds, then aggregate evaluation results across all five folds. A RELATED WORK ON PROMPT INJECTION DEFENSES 

Model-Level Defenses. Model-level defenses aim to make the underlying LLM more robust to prompt injection through defensive fine-tuning. StruQ (Chen et al., 2025a) combines a structured input format with instruction tuning so that the model learns to distinguish trusted instructions from untrusted data. SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025b) and Meta SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025c) instead use preference optimization to train the model to follow the trusted instruction rather than competing instructions embedded in external content. More recent work explores alternative training signals for the same objective. SecOPD (Peng et al., 2026) uses on-policy distillation to provide token-level feedback, scoring responses generated from injected inputs against the behavior of the reference model on corresponding clean inputs. 

System-Level Defenses. System-level defenses introduce external mechanisms around the agent and can intervene at different stages of task execution. Early defenses reinforce the trusted user 12 Preprint request or explicitly distinguish external content from trusted instructions (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Hines et al., 2024). Other approaches inspect external content before it influences the agent and detect or filter text that is likely to contain injected instructions (Liu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b; Chennabasappa et al., 2025). These methods attempt to prevent malicious instructions from in-fluencing subsequent reasoning, but their effectiveness depends on reliably distinguishing injected instructions from legitimate information that the task may require. Some defenses evaluate the instructions or actions produced during execution rather than filtering external content directly. Task-alignment approaches check whether instructions or proposed tool calls contribute to fulfilling the user request (Jia et al., 2025). Counterfactual approaches instead re-execute the agent with modified contexts to assess the influence of untrusted content on proposed actions (Zhu et al., 2025; He et al., 2026). Other system-level defenses constrain the agent’s ex-ecution through security policies or architectural separation. Tool-level approaches restrict which tools may be invoked, while more expressive policies additionally constrain tool arguments or per-mitted workflows (Debenedetti et al., 2024; An et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). For example, Progent (Shi et al., 2025) represents privileges as symbolic rules over tools and their ar-guments and checks proposed calls against the current policy. DRIFT (Li et al., 2025a) constructs constraints over tool sequences and arguments and dynamically validates deviations from the initial plan. Architectural approaches instead separate trusted execution from the processing of untrusted content and regulate how information crosses this boundary (Wu et al., 2025; Costa et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026). CaMeL (Debenedetti et al., 2026), for example, separates trusted planning from untrusted-data processing and enforces information-flow policies during program execution. These system-level approaches secure AI agents by controlling the information that can influence execution or constraining the actions the agent may perform. However, such controls can introduce a substantial security–utility trade-off by excluding legitimate behaviors required for complex tasks. RATION takes a different approach: rather than relying solely on whether individual actions are permitted, it tracks measurable quantities that accumulate throughout task execution and constrains them according to task-specific quantitative risk limits. 

B RATION IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

This appendix provides additional implementation details for the policy construction and runtime monitoring described in Sections 3.2 and 3.3. We describe the task-category construction, calibration procedure, Provisioner configuration, policy representation, and runtime accounting used in our evaluation. 

Task Categorization. For each domain and held-out fold, R ATION independently constructs a task-category catalog using only user requests from the other four folds. Using Prompt 1, an LLM groups these requests by task type and adds a fallback category for requests that do not match any specific category. The LLM returns the category definitions and request-to-category assignments. The resulting category descriptions define the catalog used by the Provisioner to route new requests. 

Classification of Write/Execute Tools. We adapt the privilege categories of DRIFT (Li et al., 2025a) to identify tools that perform state-changing or externally consequential actions. Us-ing Prompt 2, an LLM assigns each declared tool interface one of three labels: Read, Write, or Execute. Read tools retrieve information without modifying external state, Write tools create, mod-ify, or remove stored data, and Execute tools initiate interactions with external parties. RATION 

combines Write and Execute calls into a single behavioral quantity. 

Implicit Risk Policy Construction. We construct the implicit risk policy from the historical benign executions using the five-fold protocol described in Section 4.1. For each held-out fold, both the task-category catalog and the category-specific policies are constructed using only the other four folds. These historical executions are generated by the corresponding agent without injected attacks and are grouped according to the task-category assignments described above. For each non-fallback category, the tool allow-list includes all tools observed in its calibration ex-ecutions. We also estimate separate behavioral thresholds for total tool calls, Write/Execute calls, and token usage. Each threshold is set to the nearest-rank 95th percentile of the corresponding cal-ibration values. For each behavioral quantity, the fallback threshold is the maximum corresponding threshold across the non-fallback task categories. 

Policy Provisioning. Before each task execution, the Provisioner makes two sequential LLM calls. First, it compares the trusted user request with the current fold’s category descriptions and selects the corresponding task category using Prompt 3. This selects the implicit risk policy that applies to the task. Second, the Provisioner reads the trusted request, the selected task category, and the declared tool interfaces, and extracts explicit constraints using Prompt 4. It grounds each extracted constraint to the relevant declared tool and, when applicable, its argument. The Provisioner extracts only requirements stated in the user request and does not introduce constraints on values that the agent discovers during execution. 

LLM Configuration and Policy Representation. Task categorization and tool classification are offline procedures, whereas category selection and explicit-constraint extraction constitute the two online LLM calls made by the Provisioner. In the main evaluation, all four procedures use the same model as the corresponding acting agent: GPT-5.4 on DTap and GPT-4.1 on AgentDyn, AgentDojo, and ASB. These auxiliary LLM calls use the provider’s default configuration. The prompt templates in Appendix I.1 specify the semantic outputs of these procedures. In the im-plementation, the two Provisioner outputs are serialized as JSON and locally validated before being incorporated into the policy. Category selection must return an identifier from the current catalog, while explicit constraints must refer to declared tools and, when applicable, valid tool arguments. Invalid outputs receive one corrective retry. If category selection remains invalid, R ATION uses the fallback implicit policy and does not extract explicit constraints. If explicit-constraint extraction remains invalid, R ATION retains the selected implicit policy without explicit constraints. The resulting task policy is stored as a structured object containing the selected category, its tool allow-list, numeric rules, and value constraints. A numeric rule records the measured quantity, the applicable tool or tools, the corresponding argument when required, its limit, and whether the rule represents an explicit constraint or an implicit behavioral threshold. For a per-action limit, the numeric value is checked on the current call; for a total limit, the corresponding count or argument value is accumulated across matching calls. A value constraint instead records a tool, argument, and set of permitted values. Table 8 illustrates how the selected implicit policy and constraints extracted from a user request form a task-specific policy. 

Risk Monitoring and Enforcement. The Risk Monitor operates at the tool-call boundary and maintains the state of each provisioned risk quantity separately. Each proposed call is checked before execution against the applicable tool, value, and numeric rules. Tool-call counts and accu-mulated numeric quantities are updated only after an allowed call executes; blocked or held calls do not contribute to these quantities. Token usage is recorded from the acting agent’s reported usage after model generation, and tokens consumed by subsequent agent turns induced by an intervention remain part of the accumulated token usage. The monitor applies the hard blocking and behavioral threshold interventions described in Sec-tion 3.3. A hard violation prevents the proposed call from executing, whereas exceeding an implicit behavioral threshold holds the proposed call and returns the corresponding feedback in Prompt 5. D BASELINE DETAILS 

We describe the evaluated baseline defenses, grouped into system-level and model-level methods. 

System-Level Defenses. 

• Sandwich (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025) reinforces the trusted user request by repeating it or a task reminder after tool responses. • Instructional (Zhang et al., 2025) uses explicit defensive instructions to prioritize the trusted user request over competing instructions in external content. • Spotlighting (Hines et al., 2024) marks the provenance of external content so the agent can dis-tinguish untrusted data from trusted instructions; we use its delimiting variant for evaluation. • PI Detector (Debenedetti et al., 2024) 5 detects prompt injections with a separately trained De-BERTa text classifier applied to tool outputs. • Prompt Guard 2 (Chennabasappa et al., 2025) 6 uses a dedicated text classifier to identify attempts to override developer instructions or user requests. • DataSentinel (Liu et al., 2025) uses known-answer detection, identifying untrusted text that di-verts a separately trained detector from producing a predefined response. • MELON (Zhu et al., 2025) detects tool calls that may be driven by injected instructions by testing their dependence on the trusted user request. Similar calls produced with and without the original user request are treated as evidence of an injection. • AttriGuard (He et al., 2026) uses causal attribution to identify tool calls driven by instructions in untrusted observations. It permits a call only if the call remains supported after the instructional influence of those observations is weakened. • CaMeL (Debenedetti et al., 2026) separates trusted task planning from untrusted content pro-cessing and enforces information-flow policies during program execution. For DTap, we adopt the CaMeL implementation from EvoSafeHarness (Li et al., 2026b), which preserves the privi-leged/quarantined separation and capability enforcement while approximating data flow through taint propagation. On AgentDyn, we adopt the CaMeL integration provided with the bench-mark (Li et al., 2026a), while on AgentDojo, we use the authors’ released implementation. For ASB, we follow Ji et al. (2025) and evaluate CaMeL’s automated components while disabling those that require manual configuration. • Twin Agent (Hu et al., 2026) combines privilege separation with context residual compression to limit information passed from an agent reading untrusted content to an agent executing tools. Only short hints about information missing from the privileged agent’s context are passed across this boundary. • Tool Filter (Debenedetti et al., 2024) limits the agent’s privileges to a fixed set of tools selected from the trusted user request before any interaction with untrusted content. • Progent (Shi et al., 2025) enforces least privilege through programmable policies over tools and arguments, with deterministic checks before execution. We use its variant that generates task-specific policies from the trusted user request with an LLM. • DRIFT (Li et al., 2025a) uses dynamic security policies that adapt constraints on tool calls and arguments during execution according to operation privileges and the user’s original intent. 

Model-Level Defenses. 

• StruQ (Chen et al., 2025a) combines a secure input format with structured instruction tuning to teach the model to follow trusted instructions while treating external content as data. • SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025b) uses preference optimization to train the agent model to favor responses to trusted instructions over responses to instructions injected into data. • Meta SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025c) extends SecAlign’s preference-based defense to general-purpose models designed to retain utility across downstream tasks. 18 Preprint Table 15: Domain-level performance (%) on DTap using GPT-5.4 (Part I of III).

E.2 COMPARISON WITH MODEL -L EVEL DEFENSES 

StruQ (Chen et al., 2025a), SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025b), and Meta SecAlign (Chen et al., 2025c) improve security by modifying the model itself, so they operate at a different layer from R ATION 

and are evaluated separately. Following He et al. (2026), we evaluate their released checkpoints on AgentDojo under both Important Instructions and Tool Knowledge attacks. For each model-level defense, we pair the released checkpoint with its corresponding undefended base model and apply RATION to the same base model, so that the comparison isolates the effect of the defense rather than differences in the underlying model. As shown in Table 22, R ATION substantially reduces ASR over the undefended models while pre-serving strong utility. For example, on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct under Important Instructions, R A-

TION reduces ASR from 25.3% to 3.4% while increasing UA from 41.6% to 51.7%. On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, R ATION achieves higher UA and BU than StruQ and SecAlign. Meta SecAlign achieves higher utility than R ATION on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. We attribute this difference to tool-call exe-cutability: the undefended base model more frequently emits malformed function calls that cannot be parsed, whereas the fine-tuned Meta SecAlign model produces executable calls more reliably, an observation consistent with prior work (He et al., 2026). We further evaluate whether R ATION can complement a model-level defense by applying it on top of Meta SecAlign. Following prior work on layered defenses (Shi et al., 2025), we retain the Meta SecAlign checkpoint and additionally enforce R ATION during execution. This combination further reduces ASR from 1.9% to 0.5% under Important Instructions and from 1.7% to 0.3% under Tool Knowledge. These results show that R ATION can complement model-level defenses as an additional layer to provide stronger security protection. E.3 DOMAIN -L EVEL RESULTS ACROSS AGENT MODELS

F EVALUATING RATION BEYOND PROMPT INJECTION 

Problem and Experimental Setting. Agent Meltdowns (Jha et al., 2026) studies unsafe agent be-havior triggered by benign environmental failures rather than adversarial instructions. We evaluate two meltdown scenarios from the benchmark, each of which compares the same web task under an error-free condition and an error-injected condition. In Named-URL 404 , the task requires ac-cessing information from a specified URL, while the error condition causes the requested resource to return an HTTP 404 response. In 429 rate limiting , the target service instead rejects the agent’s requests with an HTTP 429 response. Such failures can cause the agent to expand its recovery be-havior beyond the original task, for example by probing additional web paths or accessing resources outside the intended scope. We evaluate 12 tasks in the 404 scenario and 18 tasks in the rate-limit scenario using GPT-5.4 with Magentic-One. For each task, we run both the corresponding error-free execution and the error-injected execution. 

Evaluation Metrics. The two conditions measure complementary aspects of defense performance. 

Task success is the fraction of error-free executions in which the agent fulfills the user request, measuring whether a defense preserves normal task utility. Unsafe execution is measured on the error-injected executions. Using the original study’s taxonomy and judging prompt, we employ GPT-5.4-mini with high reasoning to annotate behaviors by category, severity, and execution stage (planned, attempted, or succeeded). The benchmark includes behaviors such as reconnaissance or access beyond the task scope, circumvention of security controls, unauthorized external actions, and misleading reporting. Using these annotations, we define unsafe execution as the fraction of error-injected executions containing at least one medium- or high-severity behavior marked as succeeded. Multiple unsafe behaviors within the same execution are counted once. 

Results. We compare R ATION with the system-level defenses reported in Table 4. Without defense, unsafe execution occurs in 83.3% of the 404 runs and 11.1% of the rate-limit runs. In the 404 sce-nario, these failures primarily arise when the agent continues searching after the requested resource becomes unavailable and probes additional web paths beyond the original user request. The rate-limit scenario produces fewer unsafe executions, but the observed failures similarly involve broader web or local reconnaissance while the agent searches for alternative ways to complete the task. Ex-isting defenses reduce these failures but do not eliminate them across both scenarios. In contrast, RATION reduces unsafe execution to 0% in both scenarios while achieving 100% task success on the corresponding error-free tasks. These results illustrate why cumulative risk control is useful beyond prompt injection. An individual recovery action, such as trying another path after a failed request, may appear reasonable in isolation. However, as recovery continues, the execution can accumulate substantially more tool activity and token usage than historical executions in the same task category. R ATION tracks these quantities throughout execution and can trigger enforcement once the corresponding behavioral thresholds are exceeded, limiting further escalation. This allows R ATION to constrain risky executions without requiring the failure to originate from a malicious instruction. The only error-free task failure among the evaluated defenses occurs with Twin Agent (Hu et al., 2026). In that run, its output-isolation mechanism withholds the original webpage from the task-completing agent and exposes the required facts only through generated hints. Although the hints contain the necessary information, the agent continues seeking direct verification of the withheld source and fails to complete the task. 

G ADAPTIVE ATTACK AND FAILURE ANALYSIS 

G.1 ADAPTIVE ATTACKS 

Prior work has shown that adaptive attacks can substantially weaken prompt injection defenses, but existing evaluations have primarily focused on relatively simpler benchmarks such as Agent-Dojo (Nasr et al., 2026; He et al., 2026). To our knowledge, this is the first adaptive attack evalua-tion of a system-level defense on the more challenging DTap benchmark. Although DTap’s released attacks are generated through autonomous red teaming, they are constructed independently of the deployed R ATION defense. We therefore evaluate whether an attacker with knowledge of R ATION 

and feedback from repeated executions can identify additional failure modes. 24 Preprint 

Adaptive Threat Model. For each task, the attacker is given the trusted user request, the attack objective, the available tools, and a description of R ATION . For iterative attacks, the attacker addi-tionally receives feedback from each attempted execution, including the agent trajectory and defense events, and uses this feedback to refine subsequent payloads. Final attack success is always deter-mined by DTap’s benchmark judge. 

Attack Setup. We evaluate one fixed defense-aware attack and two iterative adaptive attacks. Man-ual uses a single prompt manually tailored to R ATION and does not adapt based on execution feed-back. PAIR (Chao et al., 2025) iteratively revises the payload using GPT-5.4 to generate refinements and score intermediate attempts. Search-based follows the search-based optimization of Nasr et al. (2026) using the implementation released with AttriGuard (He et al., 2026), with GPT-5.4 as the mutator and scorer, two search islands, and an elite archive of 25 payloads. For both iterative at-tacks, we allow up to 200 agent executions per task and terminate early when the DTap judge reports attack success. We optimize each adaptive attack against R ATION . The resulting payload for each task is then re-played directly against each baseline without further adaptation. These baseline results are therefore not defense-specific adaptive evaluations and may underestimate the vulnerability of each baseline to an attacker optimized directly against it. 

Results. As shown in Table 7, the fixed Manual attack achieves a lower ASR against R ATION 

than the Static attack on DTap. This may be because DTap’s released attacks have already been iteratively optimized against a surrogate agent. In contrast, PAIR and Search-based increase ASR to 12.0% and 15.7%, respectively. We next analyze the successful adaptive attacks to understand how they circumvent R ATION .G.2 FAILURE ANALYSIS 

All PAIR and Search-based attack successes against R ATION in the evaluated Telecom domain ex-ploit modifications to tool descriptions. Tool descriptions are part of the trusted agent configuration under our threat model. For example, in the Telecom task finance_fraud/006 , the user asks for the total amount owed on an internet bill, while the attack aims to manipulate the amount re-ported to the user. The search-based attacker appends a fabricated billing rule to the description of 

query_bill , instructing the agent to round the returned amount up to the next $10 bracket. The agent follows the modified description: it calls query_bill with the correct customer identifier, receives $75.89, but reports $80.00 in its final answer. Because R ATION enforces its risk policy at the tool-call interface, it does not verify the semantic correctness of the agent’s free-form outputs. Protecting the integrity of tool descriptions largely eliminates this DTap failure mode. We add an 

Integrity safeguard that restores tool descriptions from a trusted specification after the benchmark applies the injection and before the tool interfaces are presented to the agent. When PAIR and Search-based attacks are re-optimized against this safeguard, their ASRs decrease from 12.0% and 15.7% to 1.2%. These results support our diagnosis that the increased adaptive ASR on DTap is driven by an attack surface outside the threat model considered by R ATION .

H DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS AND LIMITATIONS 

H.1 DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS 

Lightweight Integration and Enforcement. RATION operates at the tool-call interface and can be integrated into existing agentic systems without modifying the agent’s internal reasoning. Al-though LLMs are used during policy construction, runtime monitoring requires only structured rule checks and updates to the quantities defined by the provisioned policy. It therefore avoids additional LLM judgments for individual actions. This design keeps runtime enforcement deterministic and lightweight while allowing flexible policy construction. 

Choice of Risk Dimensions. RATION is the first implementation applying the idea of quantitative risk-based access control to agents. Its current design focuses on risk dimensions that are broadly applicable to tool-using agents and can be measured directly from execution. These dimensions are tracked independently rather than combined into a single risk score. These dimensions provide 25 Preprint a generic basis for risk-based control, and our experiments show that they are already effective at constraining attack behavior. Future systems can extend the policy with additional measurable quan-tities that capture application-specific forms of operational exposure. One can also design specific QRBAC systems for different domains, e.g., finance, health-care, and so on. 

User Oversight and Deployment Trade-offs. The enforcement strategy used in our evaluation is designed for fully autonomous benchmarks. In interactive deployments, when a policy violation is detected, it is expected that users will be asked to choose what actions to take. The triggering rule and measured risk quantity can be included to provide an intuitive explanation to help users assess whether the agent’s behavior remains consistent with their intent and decide whether to authorize continuation or revise the policy. A deployed system can balance the frequency of user interruptions against the degree of agent autonomy by choosing a higher or lower risk threshold ( e.g., the 99th percentile versus the 95th percentile) according to its design preference. 

Comparison with Existing Defenses. RATION differs from existing defenses in both what its policy constrains and how its quantitative bounds are derived. Existing system-level defenses commonly protect agents by restricting information flow, validating individual actions, or constraining the ac-tions and execution structures available to the agent. R ATION additionally constrains quantitative behavior that accumulates over an execution. Moreover, whereas existing defenses typically derive their policies from the current task, R ATION calibrates quantitative bounds using historical execu-tions of similar tasks, providing a reference for the expected scale of legitimate behavior. This allows RATION to constrain unusually high levels of security-relevant activity without prescribing a fixed execution path. H.2 SCOPE AND LIMITATIONS 

Expressiveness of the Risk Policy. RATION implements a generic set of risk controls that can be measured at the tool-call interface, rather than attempting to capture every security-relevant property of an agent execution. Its effectiveness therefore depends on both the risks represented in the policy and the granularity exposed by the tool interface. For example, a generic tool that accepts free-form commands exposes less structure for policy enforcement. Our current implementation also consid-ers only a limited set of broadly applicable behavioral quantities and explicit constraints; specific applications may require additional domain-specific quantities, non-additive constraints, or relation-ships across multiple tools. Deployments can extend the policy with additional measurable risks and corresponding enforcement rules, but identifying and validating such risks remains domain-specific and requires application-specific specification. 

Dependence on Historical Executions. Constructing the implicit risk policy requires historical executions that provide a representative reference for each task category. In practice, such execution logs can be collected from deployed agentic systems. We also study synthetic user requests as an alternative source of calibration data in Appendix E.4.
