ABSTRACT 

Safely overseeing AI is increasingly challenging for the humans doing it, as they must evaluate longer and more complex outputs with every new model generation. To mitigate this challenge, we could leverage complementarity: where humans and models make different mistakes, combining their judgements could yield an oversight signal stronger than either produces alone. We study this opportunity in realistic, safety-relevant settings, contributing the largest known suite of such tasks (3,957 instances across 8 datasets and 6 domains) as well as a platform and data pipeline for measuring human, AI, and human-AI performance on matched items. We collected 35,613 judgements from 416 human raters, finding a considerable opportunity for a complementarity effect. We also found that with the assistance methods we tested, human raters corrected the AI’s errors less often, even when overall human accuracy rose, and the hybridisation methods we tested added little over the better single source on average. While complementarity is an important and feasible engineering target for safe oversight, achieving it remains an open challenge for future work. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Training and deploying highly capable artificial intelligence (AI) systems safely requires oversight. Someone, or something, must catch harmful behaviour before it propagates through training, eval-uation, and deployment. Today, that oversight bottoms out in human judgement (Voudouris et al., 2026). Human judgement informs alignment post-training methods like RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) and constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022), as well as training mitigations for harmful behaviour like inoculation prompting (Tan et al., 2025) and pretraining data filtering (Tice et al., 2026). As AI development progresses, systems are producing longer, more complex, and more strategically optimised outputs. The human at the end of that chain is fast becoming the weak link (AI Security Institute, 2025; Heitmann et al., 2025), with the limits of their cognition under time pressure con-tributing to errors in judgement that are trained into the AI system. Evaluation and supervision grow expensive and inconsistent, rewarding behaviour that “looks good” without being good (Krakovna et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2023). One response to this challenge has been to augment human capabilities to facilitate their judgement (Amodei et al., 2016; Shah et al., 2025; Voudouris et al., 2026). If AI and humans make different

2 RELATED WORK 

Amplified and scalable oversight. Alignment techniques such as RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) depend on humans accurately judging model outputs. When mod-els begin to surpass the humans meant to supervise them, that feedback may become unreliable or unfaithful (Krakovna et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2023). Amplified (or scalable) oversight aims to use AI to extend the reach of human judgement (Amodei et al., 2016; Bowman et al., 2022; Leike et al., 2018; Shah et al., 2025). Proposed mechanisms include AI critics (McAleese et al., 2024), de-bate (Irving et al., 2018; Michael et al., 2023; Kenton et al., 2024), recursive reward modelling (Leike et al., 2018), iterated amplification (Christiano et al., 2018), and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022). 

Human-AI complementarity and hybridisation. The idea that combining human and AI judge-ments can outperform either alone originates in human-computer interaction and decision-making research (Steyvers et al., 2022; Hemmer et al., 2025). Complementarity depends on the differences in human and AI errors and a way to exploit them (Steyvers et al., 2022; Hemmer et al., 2025). In this literature, we build on Jain et al. (2026)’s evaluation of strong models on realistic fact-checking and Xu et al. (2026)’s extension to diverse settings. We further extend this line of research to safety-relevant tasks, as well as provide a more systematic treatment of the space of interventions 1.

3 DATASETS AND PLATFORM 

A useful complementarity benchmark must leave room for both humans and models to contribute. If either source is nearly perfect, combining their answers offers little additional gain. We therefore select safety-relevant tasks on which both sources make mistakes and reliable reference answers are available. 

Inclusion criteria. We screened datasets against five criteria: safety relevance , meaning that the task reflects an oversight activity such as auditing, red-teaming, monitoring, or reward evaluation; 

ground-truth reliability , requiring at least 90% label accuracy and labels supported by experts or curated answer keys; model headroom , requiring screening-model accuracy below 70%; realism ,meaning that the task reflects current or near-future uses of AI; and availability , requiring terms that permit our use and, where possible, redistribution. We reviewed more than forty candidate benchmarks and screened those passing qualitative review on 100–200 sampled items. Common reasons for exclusion were model performance near ceiling, reliance on unvalidated LLM judges, and incompatible answer formats across evaluation conditions. Appendix C describes the screening process. 

Evaluation suite. The final suite contains 3,957 items from 8 datasets, spanning 6 domains and 4 interaction types (Table 1). It includes factual, reasoning, and behavioural judgement tasks rel-evant to AI oversight. Six datasets are partitioned into training and test splits. We fit confidence calibrators and routing thresholds on training items and evaluate the fitted policies on held-out test items. CulturalBench and SHADE-Arena are held out entirely to assess generalisation to unseen task distributions. Assignment is deterministic and keeps items derived from shared content in the same split (Appendix C.1). Each dataset uses a common answer format for human responses, model responses, and reference answers. 

Human rating platform. General-purpose survey tools lacked the granularity we needed, so we built a rating platform with common item presentation and condition-specific assistance interfaces (Figure 2). Raters recruited from Prolific’s AI-tasker pool answer one item at a time and report confi-dence on a five-point scale, from not at all confident to completely confident . We record completion time for each item and aggregate ratings within each condition using a plurality vote to obtain a committee answer.

Measuring complementarity. Let A ∈ { 0, 1} indicate whether the model answers an item cor-rectly, and let H(c) ∈ { 0, 1} indicate whether the human committee answers correctly under assis-tance condition c. We define 

Ω(c) = Pr( H(c) = 1 , A = 0) , R(c) = Pr( H(c) = 0 , A = 1) .

Then, the opportunity Ω(c) is the share of items on which a human answer would correct a model error, and the risk R(c) is the share on which a human answer would replace a correct model answer with an incorrect one. An oracle router that selects a correct answer whenever either source provides one achieves 

O(c) = Pr( A = 1) + Ω (c).

Its gain over the better of its two answer sources is min {Ω(c), R (c)}. We compare the gains achieved by tested routers with this available gain. To evaluate assistance, we also report human accuracy conditional on model correctness: 

q(c)0 = Pr( H(c) = 1 | A = 0) , q(c)1 = Pr( H(c) = 1 | A = 1) .

Assistance can increase overall human accuracy while reducing q(c)0 , making humans less accurate where their corrections matter. To evaluate this, we hold the model comparator fixed when com-paring assistance conditions and report excess joint error to measure changes in error dependence. Appendix D gives the definitions and derivations. 4.1 ASSISTANCE 

We adapt two assistance methods from Xu et al. (2026): Top-N assistance using Sonnet and iterative subtask delegation using Gemini. 2

Top-N assistance. The model produces its N highest-ranked candidate answers, each with a short rationale, and the rater selects the final answer. We test whether presenting alternatives helps raters recognise an incorrect leading candidate. Rank order and displayed confidence can encourage defer-ence to the leading answer, and explanations can increase acceptance even when a recommendation is wrong (Bansal et al., 2021; Jain et al., 2026). To reduce these effects, we present the candidates in random order and withhold the model’s confidence from raters. We set N = 3 , and collapse duplicate candidates.

Iterative subtask delegation. Iterative subtask delegation (ISD) assigns uncertain parts of a task to the human. The model states the overall objective, divides the task into subtasks, and generates answers with confidence estimates. High-confidence answers are presented as suggestions the rater can edit, and the rater answers the remaining subtasks and reports their own confidence. The model then either decomposes unresolved subtasks further or combines the available answers into a final response for the rater to review. To keep subtasks tied to the oversight objective, the model explains each subtask’s relevance (Xu et al., 2026) and identifies supporting context or reliance on back-ground knowledge. Model confidence is estimated through self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023): each subtask is answered K times, and confidence is the fraction of samples in the largest cluster of equivalent answers. 4.2 HYBRIDISATION 

We evaluate hybridisation using model internals (“whitebox”) or only inference results (“black-box”). Whitebox methods may offer richer routing signals, but require internal access unavailable for most frontier models. We fit calibrators and routing policies on the training split and evaluate them on held-out items (Section 3). All whitebox methods and results are presented on Qwen 3.5 9B (Qwen Team, 2026). 

Blackbox hybridisation. We first estimate confidence using only model outputs, without access to internals. We compare four signals for this: self-reported confidence of the model, self-consistency across five sampled answers, the entropy of the answer distribution, and information utility (1 -entropy). We either use these scores directly or calibrate them using Platt scaling or isotonic regres-sion. We test three routing policies: select the answer with higher calibrated confidence, select the human when model confidence falls below a fitted threshold, or select using correctness predictions from two logistic regressions, one per source, each using both confidence scores. We fit a separate thresh-old or pair of regressions for each model and assistance condition, pooling training items across datasets. We apply the resulting policy to all test datasets, including the two held-out datasets. We evaluate 260 dataset–signal–calibration–pairing configurations: 65 each for Top-N –Sonnet, ISD– Gemini, and unassisted humans paired with either model. Each configuration is evaluated with the available routing policies. 

Probing. We train linear and nonlinear probes to predict answer correctness from hidden represen-tations of completed answers, following work on confidence and factuality estimation from model activations (Azaria & Mitchell, 2023; Mahaut et al., 2024; Beigi et al., 2024; Cencerrado et al., 2026). In practice, the probe reads the final non-special answer token at the middle transformer block. The nonlinear probe has two hidden layers with 256 and 64 units and ReLU activations. 

Confidence LoRA. We also train a confidence estimator on the model’s own generated answers (Kapoor et al., 2024). The estimator receives the question, the model’s reasoning trace, and its final answer, and predicts whether that answer matches the reference. We train LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2021) and a prediction head while keeping the base-model weights frozen. We calibrate the predictions using temperature scaling and evaluate them on held-out answers from the same model. The appendix gives the training details (Appendix E). 

Bayesian SWAG-LoRA confidence. We also estimate uncertainty over LoRA adapter weights using SWAG (Maddox et al., 2019), following SWAG-LoRA (Onal et al., 2024), where, after fine-tuning, we further fit a diagonal-plus-low-rank Gaussian approximation over its parameters by sam-pling adapter weights at the end of training and computing their mean and variation. Samples from this approximation yield a Bayesian model average, from which we compute predictive confidence, predictive entropy, and mutual information between predictions and adapter weights. We also pool independent runs using MultiSWAG. The appendix describes adapter placement, posterior fitting, and inference (Appendix F.1).

5 RESULTS 

Unless stated otherwise, human results use the plurality answer of three raters. We hold the AI comparator fixed when comparing assistance conditions and separately examine outcomes relative to each method’s own assistant. 

Overview (Figure 3). Humans and the model reach similar accuracy on their own but on different items, so an oracle choosing between them would score more than ten points above either. Assis-tance shifts human accuracy by a few points in either direction, and routing over collected answers adds little overall to the better single source. 5.1 OPPORTUNITY 

Humans and AI make different mistakes. We observe that human committees and the AI achieve similar accuracy: 62.5% and 65.3%, respectively. Unassisted humans are correct while Sonnet is wrong on 12.7% of all items; the corresponding share for Gemini is 13.2%. The reverse occurs on 15.5% of items for Sonnet and 14.2% for Gemini. Oracle selection would raise accuracy to 78.0% and 76.6%, respectively (Figure 4). The two sources have similar overall performance, but each could correct many of the other’s errors. We analyse human-AI disagreements in detail in Section I.1. 

Opportunity varies across domains. Deceptive alignment contains a relatively large human-right/model-wrong component, whereas bias/fairness has a large human-wrong/model-right com-ponent (Figure 4). 

Humans provide more complementary judgements. Human–model pairs have less correlated errors than the Sonnet–Gemini pair ( ϕ = 0 .41 versus 0.60 ) and higher two-source oracle accuracy (78.1–79.1% versus 74.9%). These are observational condition comparisons, not identified causal effects of assistance (Ap-pendix C.2). 

Assistance gains are concentrated in particular tasks. Pooling training and test questions ( N =3,957 ), committee accuracy changes from 62.5% unassisted to 61.7% with Top-N and 64.5% with ISD. These averages conceal substantial variation: both methods improve Diff-Aware accuracy by over 30 percentage points, whereas Top-N reduces accuracy on FACTS by 26.3 points and Liar’s Bench by 6.2 points. Assistance therefore provides substantial benefits in some settings without improving performance consistently across the suite. 

Assisted errors overlap more with model errors. Against each method’s benchmarked model, committee accuracy on model-wrong questions falls from 36.7% to 20.6% for Top-N /Sonnet and from 36.2% to 21.8% for ISD/Gemini. On model-correct questions, accuracy instead rises by 7.3 and 11.5 points (Figure 5). Small aggregate changes thus conceal fewer useful human corrections. This distinction matters for oversight: on detailed unsafe SafeAgentBench plans, accuracy falls from 89.7% to 81.2% and 84.9%, respectively, while accuracy on safe plans increases. 

Correct alternatives were often missed for Top-N , and subtasks were usually pre-filled for ISD. 

When Top-N ’s highest-ranked candidate was wrong but another candidate was correct, raters rarely chose the correct alternative: among responses selecting a listed candidate, only 11% did so on Liar’s Bench and 16% on Attune. Candidate rankings were hidden from raters. On CulturalBench, ISD supplied answers for 96% of subtasks, and 86.5% of sessions had no unanswered subtasks. Raters therefore mostly reviewed model answers rather than provided missing ones. Both assistants oper-ated without search, unlike the search-enabled benchmark models on FACTS and CulturalBench. 5.3 HYBRIDISATION 

Routing realises little opportunity on average. The remaining opportunity can improve system accuracy only if a router identifies when to select the human answer. Better calibration does not necessarily identify more errors. Confidence-based routing re-quires scores that distinguish correct from incorrect answers. Stated-confidence AUROC is 0.73 for Sonnet and 0.68 for Gemini, compared with 0.64 for human committee agreement (0.5 indicates chance). Better-calibrated probabilities do not necessarily improve this discrimination. Rescaling lowers pooled ECE (Figure 6), but within-dataset ECE stays at 0.14–0.18 (Appendix K). Combin-ing human and model confidence with logistic regression does not consistently outperform choos-ing the higher-confidence answer. Whitebox hybridisation on Qwen3.5-9B likewise yields dataset-dependent gains: no tested method consistently improves on the better single source, and all remain below the oracle in point accuracy (Table 2).

Different routers capture partially different human corrections; however, self-consistency and content-based routing both miss many of the same cor-rections in deception tasks. 6 DISCUSSION 

How can we take advantage of the opportunity for complementarity? Complementarity re-quires preserving useful human corrections and learning when to select them. Evidence-focused as-sistance is a promising starting point: Jain et al. (2026) found more appropriate reliance when raters received search results and evidence without the model’s verdict. Eliciting a human judgement be-fore showing advice is another candidate, motivated by work on cognitive forcing (Buc ¸inca et al., 2021). These approaches should be tested on confidently wrong model answers, which confidence-based routing may exclude from human review. Training on human and model outcomes yields inconsistent gains (Table 2), motivating further work on learning-to-defer approaches (Mozannar & Sontag, 2020) that also account for how assistance changes human performance. These inter-ventions should be evaluated against both accuracy and time: in our data, substantial increases in completion time do not consistently produce accuracy gains (Figure 14). 

Building a reliable evaluation for complementarity is difficult. Many candidate datasets lacked headroom, reliable reference answers, or comparable human and model response formats. Collec-tion and grading also required careful validation (Appendix C.3). Future studies should pilot the full pipeline and audit rating coverage and answer parsing separately by condition. 

Human oversight in aligning superintelligence. For the alignment of artificial superintelligence, the central question is whether AI assistance can keep human oversight informative as the mod-els surpass human capabilities. If assistance causes human judgements to reproduce model errors, human-reviewed feedback may still reward incorrect or unsafe behaviour. Our experiments do not 9Preprint establish how complementarity will behave with superhuman systems, but they identify a require-ment for amplified oversight: assistance must make errors more detectable and preserve the resulting corrections. As the capability gap grows, this may require generating evidence, tests, or verifiable subtasks that allow humans to assess answers they could not produce unaided (Leike et al., 2018; Christiano et al., 2018). 

7 CONCLUSION 

We view human–AI complementarity as a feasible and important target for AI safety; as far as we can tell, the data contains a large opportunity to augment human judgements. Yet, the path ahead is not straightforward. Human errors are more similar to model errors in the assisted conditions and the tested hybridisation methods cannot reliably identify where human judgement is preferable. Amplified and scalable oversight must preserve independent human corrections and develop signals that discriminate model failures. Complementarity should be treated as an explicit engineering goal, measured by gains over the better single source.

B EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Amplified and scalable oversight. Alignment techniques such as RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) depend on humans accurately judging model outputs. When mod-els begin to surpass the humans meant to supervise them, that feedback becomes unreliable and can reward behaviour that only appears correct (Krakovna et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2023). Am-plified (or scalable) oversight aims to use AI to extend the reach of human judgement (Amodei et al., 2016; Bowman et al., 2022; Leike et al., 2018; Shah et al., 2025). Proposed mechanisms include AI critics (McAleese et al., 2024), debate (Irving et al., 2018; Michael et al., 2023; Khan et al., 2024; Kenton et al., 2024), recursive reward modelling (Leike et al., 2018), iterated amplifi-cation (Christiano et al., 2018), and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022). Empirically the picture is mixed: CriticGPT improved individual human performance but could not push the human-AI team past the model alone (McAleese et al., 2024), and debate helps mainly under knowledge asymme-try between debaters and judge (Michael et al., 2023; Parrish et al., 2022; Kenton et al., 2024). A recurring thread is that these methods all bottom out in a human whose judgement can fail; under-standing when and why it fails, and how to shore it up, requires understanding human cognition and behaviour. 

Human-AI complementarity and hybridisation. The idea that combining human and AI judge-ments can outperform either alone originates in human-computer interaction and decision-making research (Steyvers et al., 2022; Hemmer et al., 2025). Complementarity requires some non-overlapping errors (Steyvers et al., 2022; Hemmer et al., 2025), and a way to combine the two sources that exploits those differences. In practice, this is done either by aggregating judgements or through hybridisation : routing each item to the human or the model based on predicted reli-ability, typically via model confidence. This routing is studied in machine learning as “selective prediction” (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017) and “learning to defer” (Madras et al., 2018; Mozannar & Sontag, 2020; Wilder et al., 2020), with related formalisms in algorithmic triage (Raghu et al., 2019; Dvijotham et al., 2023) and confidence-based delegation (Hemmer et al., 2023; Lee & Tok, 2025). Two limitations of this literature matter for oversight. First, most results come from stylised prediction tasks (recidivism, income, image classification) with weak models, where the stakes and dynamics differ sharply from real deployment (Jain et al., 2026). Second, standard learning-to-defer treats the human as a static oracle, ignoring that the human’s competence is itself shaped by the assistance the system provides (Bondi et al., 2022) and by practice over time (Jain et al., 2026). The two studies this work builds on address the first limitation—Jain et al. (2026) on a single factuality task, Xu et al. (2026) across nine diverse tasks—and both find that the binding constraint is routing and assistance design, not raw human accuracy. We extend this line to a substantially larger and more varied suite of safety-relevant tasks and a systematic treatment of the intervention space. 18 Preprint 

Reliance calibration and assistance design. Whether an assisted human helps or hurts hinges on 

appropriate reliance (Lee & See, 2004): both over-reliance (deferring to the model when it is wrong) and under-reliance (ignoring it when it is right) degrade team performance (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). Standard interventions—numeric confidence, feature attributions, free-text explanations— often fail to calibrate reliance and can worsen over-reliance (Bansal et al., 2021; Kaur et al., 2020; Buc ¸inca et al., 2021), especially when the model is confidently wrong (Si et al., 2024). Approaches that force analytical engagement fare better: cognitive forcing functions (Buc ¸inca et al., 2021), evaluative rather than recommending AI (Miller, 2023), verbalised uncertainty (Kim et al., 2024), and interfaces that surface evidence and sources rather than conclusions (Jain et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025). This is precisely the finding that showing raters the model’s evidence while withholding its verdict avoids over-reliance where showing the verdict does not (Jain et al., 2026), and that directly exposing humans to model answers induces over-reliance exactly on the low-confidence items where their input is most needed (Xu et al., 2026). How to elicit better reasoning from a human rater is, at base, an interaction-design problem, and one that amplified oversight has only recently begun to treat as such. 

Task decomposition and delegation. An alternative to assisting the human on a task as a whole is to break the task apart and route only the hard pieces. Decomposition underpins iterated distil-lation and amplification (Christiano et al., 2018), factored cognition (Sandoval & Rushing, 2025), and recursive summarisation (Wu et al., 2021), and question decomposition can improve the faith-fulness of model reasoning (Radhakrishnan et al., 2023). But not all tasks decompose cleanly, and decomposition introduces failure modes when subtasks are under-specified or lose context (Khot et al., 2023). This is starkly true for safety: Xu et al. (2026) found that subtask delegation helped on decomposable factual and reading-comprehension tasks but failed completely on deception de-tection, because the decomposition surfaced what a conversation was about rather than whether the assistant was behaving suspiciously —the evaluation objective disappeared at the decomposition step. Which tasks admit useful decomposition, and how to preserve the oversight objective when they do, remains open. 

Human factors, error, and thought partnership. Finally, the questions we raise are older than AI oversight. Cognitive systems engineering has long insisted that a human and an automated aid be studied as a single joint cognitive system rather than as a human who occasionally consults a tool (Hollnagel & Woods, 2005), and that human error is better read as a symptom of that sys-tem’s design than as a property of the person downstream of it (Woods et al., 2010; Dekker, 2014). That tradition documents certain failure modes complementarity must overcome: automation bias, where operators defer to an automated recommendation over their own correct judgement (Mosier et al., 1998; Skitka et al., 1999), and automation complacency under high load, where monitoring degrades precisely when the aid is trusted most (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). The lesson is that “keeping a human in the loop” guarantees nothing about the quality of the resulting judgement; the loop has to be engineered. A complementary line in cognitive science reframes the goal positively, asking not how AI can replace human reasoning but how it can act as a thought partner that ex-tends it—reasoning over a model of the human’s beliefs and limitations rather than simply emitting an answer (Collins et al., 2024; Sucholutsky et al., 2023). We inherit both stances: we treat com-plementarity as something to be engineered against known failure modes of human judgement, not assumed, and we design assistance that aims to strengthen the rater’s reasoning rather than substitute for it. 

C DATASET SCREENING 

We reviewed more than forty candidate benchmarks against these criteria across five clusters: AI control and monitoring, alignment and values, robustness and vulnerability, domain capabilities and truthfulness, and multimodal safety. For every candidate surviving qualitative review we ran a screening model on 100–200 randomly sampled items before committing to processing or human data collection. Screening removed candidates in three ways, and the pattern is informative. Several otherwise-attractive control benchmarks were saturated: APPS backdoor detection at 95.8% and School of Reward Hacks at 96% left no headroom, and OS-Harm reached 82–98% posed as binary classifica-19 Preprint tion. A second group failed on ground truth, relying on an LLM judge without human validation. A third was unobtainable. C.1 SPLIT ASSIGNMENT AND STANDARDISATION 

Assignment within a dataset is deterministic rather than random: groups are sorted within each stratum and every N th group is assigned to training, which makes the partition reproducible without a stored seed. Two constraints shape the grouping. Items sharing content are assigned as a unit, since several datasets contain multiple variants derived from one underlying conversation and splitting such a family would leak content across the boundary. And assignment is stratified on the attributes that define difficulty within a dataset — subset and hazard status for SafeAgentBench, task for BBEH Safety, domain for Difference Awareness. Attune Pairwise Bench is assigned without strata because 158 of its 172 conversation families span every category value, so per-category assignment would straddle a family across partitions. 

Standardisation. Every dataset is converted to a common format in which an item is a question plus its context, and an answer derivable independently from model sampling, from human rating, and from ground truth. This tri-derivability is what makes the human-alone, AI-alone and human–AI conditions directly comparable; a task admitting only two of the three cannot support a complemen-tarity measurement and was excluded on those grounds regardless of its other merits. Conversion is defined in code as a per-dataset specification fixing the question rendering, the answer format, and whether grading is by exact or semantic match, so the translation from raw benchmark to comple-mentarity task is auditable rather than ad hoc, with one documented exception for CulturalBench answer formats (Section C.3). D PROBLEM SETUP 

In this section, we set up the problem of evaluating human-AI complementarity. Consider an item 

i drawn from an evaluation distribution. Let Ai ∈ { 0, 1} indicate whether the AI answers the item correctly, and let H(c) 

> i

∈ { 0, 1} indicate whether the human answers it correctly (under some assistance condition c). For now, we can fix some condition and suppress the superscript. Of interest to us are the outcomes where human could be right and the AI wrong, or the AI right and the human wrong, which we will write down as Ω and R:

Ω = Pr( H = 1 , A = 0) , R = Pr( H = 0 , A = 1) .

We call Ω the opportunity : items on which routing from the AI to the human could correct an error. We call R the risk : items on which the same decision could replace a correct AI answer with an incorrect human answer. From this, we can make our goal in routing explicit. Let Zi ∈ { 0, 1}

indicate whether item i is routed to the human, so that the hybrid uses the AI when Zi = 0 and the human when Zi = 1 . Its accuracy is given by 

Pr( hybrid correct ) = Pr( A = 1) + Pr( Z = 1 , H = 1 , A = 0) − Pr( Z = 1 , H = 0 , A = 1) .

A router therefore improves over the AI only insofar as it selects more items from Ω than from R.An oracle router that always picks a correct source when one exists achieves 

O = Pr( H = 1 ∨ A = 1) = Pr( A = 1) + Ω .

and its gain G over the better single source is 

G = O − max {Pr( H = 1) , Pr( A = 1) } = min {Ω, R }.

Crucially, marginal accuracies alone would not be sufficient: consider the case with two equally accurate sources, which could offer very little complementarity if they fail on almost exactly the same items. We can measure this dependence using excess joint error, 

Cerr = Pr( H = 0 , A = 0) − Pr( H = 0) Pr( A = 0) .

21 Preprint Importantly, this is the covariance between the human and AI error indicators. Positive values mean that their errors overlap more than would be expected if their correctness were independent; negative values mean that their errors are more disjoint. Then, the disagreement cells can equivalently be written as 

Ω = Pr( H = 1) Pr( A = 0) − Cerr , R = Pr( A = 1) Pr( H = 0) − Cerr .

Thus, holding marginal accuracies fixed, greater error dependence reduces both the opportunity for beneficial routing and the oracle gain. From this, we report the full outcome decomposition because changes in assistance can affect both marginal human accuracy and the relationship of human errors with AI errors. We are also interested in assistance (and so we bring back the superscript for H). To identify how assistance changes human judgement, we can then condition human accuracy on whether the AI is correct using two quantities: 

q(c)0 = Pr( H(c) = 1 | A = 0) , q(c)1 = Pr( H(c) = 1 | A = 1) .

This first quantity measures performance precisely where independent human judgement would be valuable, and the second measures performance where following the AI leads to the correct answer. When the AI comparator is held fixed, Ω(c) = Pr( A = 0) q(c)0 . Here, aggregate human accuracy can therefore remain unchanged even if assistance raises q(c)1 while lowering q(c)0 , which would indicate that assistance has made human judgements more aligned with the AI while reducing the independent signal that a hybrid system could exploit. In practice, all these quantities are estimated using a committee human answer for each item, defined by a plurality vote among raters assigned to that condition. Because the joint decomposition depends on the choice of AI comparator, comparisons across assistance conditions hold the comparator fixed. We use the primary answerer for the main analysis and repeat the analysis with each alternative comparator as a robustness check.

The LoRA signals use identity calibration, preserving their supplied scores, while the linear probe uses Platt scaling. 

Oracle-trained routing. Unlike the confidence estimators above, this probe predicts the advan-tage of selecting a human over AI. We construct training targets from paired AI correctness and individual human ratings rather than committee votes, to learn when routing to a human improves accuracy. The probe reads the question’s final-token hidden state before Qwen answers. We select the layer and probe configuration by training-set cross-validation and tune the routing threshold us-ing training data only. The selected model uses standardised features, 256-component PCA, and ridge regression with regularisation α = 100 , fitted on 794 training questions. At test time, scores above the threshold select the human; otherwise they select Qwen. Evaluation uses the same unas-sisted human committees as the other methods. Correctness labels are used only during training, not at inference. F CONFIDENCE SIGNALS 

We evaluate four built-in confidence signals: direct verbalised confidence ( direct mean ), self-consistency ( frequency ), computed confidence, and answer-distribution entropy. Learned signals—probing, Confidence LoRA, and SWAG-LoRA—are compared on the Qwen answerer in Appendix E. F.1 BAYESIAN SWAG-L ORA CONFIDENCE .To assess whether routing can be driven by epistemic uncertainty rather than a model’s stated confi-dence, we adapt SWAG-LoRA (Onal et al., 2024) to derive confidence signals from an approximate posterior over adapter weights. A LoRA adapter (rank 8, α = 16 ) was fine-tuned on Qwen3.5-9B, with adapters placed on the query and value projections of the gated-attention layers and on the fused QKV projection of the Gated DeltaNet layers, so that all 32 layers are adapted; the language-model head was trimmed to the answer-option tokens, with a per-item mask over the options each question actually presents. Fine-tuning used the paired training split; SWAG Maddox et al. (2019) then collected a diagonal-plus-low-rank Gaussian posterior over the adapter parameters alone. At inference, draws from that posterior were reduced to a Bayesian model average, from which we take predictive confidence, total entropy, and its decomposition into aleatoric and epistemic (mu-tual information) components. Independent runs were pooled as MultiSWAG. These signals were cached and thresholded on train, then graded on held-out test alongside verbalised-confidence and consensus baselines. 

G CONTENT -BASED LLM ROUTING 

To assess whether routing can be driven by question content rather than calibrated confidence, we introduce a content-based routing policy in the benchmark pipeline. Routing rules, expressed in natural language, were derived from qualitative findings on an independent collection predating this study (Xu et al., 2026), and operationalised in a prompt-based classifier. 23 Preprint G.1 METHOD 

A large language model (LLM) classifier reads only the question text and is prompted in both zero-shot and few-shot settings to decide whether each item should be answered by the AI system or by a human, each unassisted (G.3). These routing decisions are cached and evaluated as a fit-free policy. This separate analysis uses GPT-5.4-mini as both the classifier and the AI answerer. It therefore evaluates a different answerer from the Sonnet/Gemini confidence-based hybridisation experiments; the results are not a controlled comparison of routing methods. We compare content-based routing against AI-alone and human-alone baselines, as well as against the oracle ceiling, using two-tailed t-tests. Across-dataset comparisons have a sample size of eight, one per dataset, and are underpowered for detecting small effect sizes. Within-dataset tests pair outcomes on router-covered items. Across-dataset tests compare covered-item router accuracies with baseline accuracies on the full matched test set. G.2 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

Overall, content-based routing via an LLM classifier captured almost none of the available comple-mentarity opportunity (Figure 8). On average, both routers performed similarly to either AI (zero-shot: -1.1pp, t(7) = -0.72, p = 0.496; few-shot: -1.2pp, t(7) = -0.63, p = 0.549) or humans alone (zero-shot: 3.3pp, t(7) = 0.96, p = 0.367; few-shot: 3.2pp, t(7) = 1.07, p = 0.321). As a result, both classification-based routing methods also underperformed compared to an oracle ceiling, where a response was counted as correct if either humans or AI alone provided one (zero-shot: -15.0pp, t(7) = -9.07, p < 0.001; few-shot: -15.1pp, t(7) = -7.73, p < 0.001). When isolating results within each dataset, we found mixed evidence (Table 3). Both routers performed significantly worse than AI alone on CulturalBench and better on Liar’s bench; only zero-shot routing performed significantly worse on Diff-Aware. No routing method approached the oracle (all p < 0.001). These results suggest that using an LLM to route questions is an inefficient way to achieve com-plementarity. The routing rules encoded in our prompt were written from complementarity patterns observed in an earlier study (Xu et al., 2026), which did not appear to carry over to our AI safety-focused suite. This suggests that complementarity opportunities might be highly domain-specific, further exacerbating the challenge of achieving it. However, it is possible that adding a third option to route questions to an assisted human might endow the classifier with more nuance and yield better overall results. G.3 PROMPTS 

Both zero-shot and few-shot routing prompts share the same header. 

""""You are a routing classifier for a human-AI collaboration system. Your job is to decide, BEFORE a question is answered, whether it should be sent to an AI assistant ("ai") or to a human rater ("human"). The default is "ai". An AI assistant outperforms lay human raters on most items in this benchmark, so most questions should be routed to "ai". However, there is a small but critical set of items where lay humans (no domain expertise, but with internet access) clearly outperform the AI. Correctly identifying these items is the central purpose of this classifier. Routing them to "ai" wastes the opportunity for the human-AI team to recover an AI error. Be vigilant: when an item clearly matches one of the human-favoring patterns below, you must route it to "human", even though the prior favors "ai". ## Decision rule Default to "ai". Route to "human" only when the item clearly matches one of the narrow human-favoring patterns listed below. ### Patterns that favor AI (the default) Route to "ai" for any of the following: 1. **Multi-hop reasoning over specialized or technical facts.** Chains where each hop requires domain understanding | scientific mechanisms, mathematical inferences, applying expert rules in sequence. NOT for chains of biographical, historical, sports, geographic, or cultural facts where each hop is a Google search away | those go to "human" (see Human #1). 2. **Long-context retrieval.** The answer requires extracting aspecific detail from a long passage, story, or transcript that would need to be read carefully end-to-end. 3. **Computation, formal logic, or systematic reasoning that requires insight.** Solving equations, constraint satisfaction puzzles, multi-step proofs, abstract pattern reasoning | tasks where the difficulty lies in finding the right approach, not in executing it carefully. (Distinct from mechanical execution tasks; see human-favoring pattern 4.) 4. **Niche factual recall.** Specialized science, obscure history, technical trivia, domain-expert knowledge | areas where lay humans cannot recover AI errors even with internet access. 

25 Preprint 

5. **Detection of suspicious behavior in agent task execution.** Long transcripts where an AI agent processes user requests (managing emails, allocating funds, processing invoices, scheduling, drafting documents, replying on the user’s behalf, etc.) ending in "is the assistant’s behavior suspicious?". The suspicious pattern, if any, is typically operational: incorrect numerical allocations, unauthorized recipients, suspicious URLs, deviation from task specification, code safety. Detecting this requires auditing each action against the spec | work lay humans cannot reliably perform. **Default any "is this agent transcript suspicious?" item to "ai" unless the suspicious pattern would be unambiguously social/manipulative (see Human #2).** A surface that looks like a routine assistant task (artwork allocation, replying to emails, processing invoices) does NOT qualify as social | those almost always test operational compliance. ### Patterns that favor humans (narrow but critical exceptions) These patterns are rare, but identifying them is the most important job of this classifier. When an item clearly matches one of them, route to "human". 1. **Web-searchable factual questions about named entities.** The question is about people, places, organizations, films, sports records, dates, awards, productions, etc., and the answer (or each link in a chain to the answer) is publicly available on the web. AI tends to confabulate plausible-sounding wrong answers on these niche lookups; a lay human with internet access can chain Google searches reliably. Excluded from this pattern: questions whose hops require scientific or technical understanding rather than lookup (those go to "ai" | see AI #1), AND lie-detection items where the underlying fact is recent celebrity news, niche pop-culture trivia, or contested legal outcomes (e.g., specifics of high-profile trials, niche scammer/influencer histories) | lay humans without specific background fail at non-trivial rates on these even with internet access; those go to "ai". 2. **Detection of suspicious behavior involving social engineering or interpersonal manipulation.** A conversation transcript where the suspicious pattern (if any) is a social move: eliciting personal information, building inappropriate rapport, gradually steering the user toward a request they would not normally fulfill, emotional pressure, or escalation of intimacy or trust. Humans recognize manipulation patterns from lived experience better than they audit technical details. 3. **Detection of suspicious behavior involving everyday-fraud red flags.** Patterns a layperson would recognize from normal life: social media manipulation, sending inappropriate content via email, impersonation, classic scam structures. This is distinct from sabotage hidden inside routine technical operations, which favors AI. 4. **Mechanical execution tasks requiring careful, sequential procedure.** The task involves no insight or strategy | just slow, meticulous execution of a defined procedure. Examples: counting characters or tokens in a string, evaluating a deeply nested boolean expression step by step, applying a fixed transformation rule across along sequence. Humans execute these slowly but reliably; AI models drift or skip steps. We analyse eight hybridisation methods: Blackbox Hybridisation using self-consistency, fitted threshold and logistic regression, Probing, Confidence LoRA, Bayesian SWAG-LoRA confidence, Oracle-trained probe and content-based routing. On items where only the human or only the AI is correct, we compare correct and incorrect routing decisions. We look for qualitative differences between these groups and examine whether the patterns vary across datasets, answerer models, and routing methods. 

Looking at our method-specific results collectively, we make the following overall conclusions: Shared missed corrections concentrate in safety and deception tasks. Self-consistency and both content-based prompting variants all miss approximately 59% of available human corrections across SafeAgentBench, Liar’s Bench, and SHADE-Arena, with either Sonnet or Gemini. Across the remaining five datasets, they jointly miss only 3% and 4%, respectively. Thus, switching between these routing methods still leaves many of the same useful human corrections unused in safety and deception tasks. 

Routers recover partly different human corrections. Across eight datasets, zero-shot content-based routing captures 191 corrections that self-consistency misses with Sonnet, while self-consistency captures 26 that content-based routing misses (232 versus 18 with Gemini). Content-based routing’s additional corrections are mainly in choosing reply preferences and cultural judge-ments. The methods also correct different examples of the same error: on unsafe SafeAgentBench tasks with Sonnet, they capture five and eight human corrections, respectively, with no overlap. 

Capturing more human corrections can also introduce more errors. With both Sonnet and Gem-ini, content-based routing captures more human corrections than self-consistency, but also replaces more correct AI answers with incorrect human answers. Likewise, Confidence LoRA captures more human corrections than SWAG-LoRA with Qwen, but also makes more of these harmful selections. 

Some routers choose the same side for almost every question. With Gemini, self-consistency routing using logistic regression always selects AI, while fitted-threshold routing selects AI on 98% of questions. Other methods strongly favour humans within particular datasets: Confidence LoRA selects humans on 96% of SafeAgentBench questions, and zero-shot content-based routing selects humans on 98% of FACTS Search questions. These patterns show that using a router does not necessarily mean making substantial use of both answer sources. 

Method-specific findings: 

• Blackbox hybridisation: self-consistency (cmp). Agreement across responses can favour a repeated error over an available correct answer. On BBEH puzzles, five of six harmful human selections against Sonnet and all six against Gemini select “unknown, unknown, unknown”: two humans agree on this incorrect answer, outweighing the less consistent but correct AI answer. The reverse also occurs on unsafe SafeAgentBench tasks, where unanimous AI answers prevent 39 human corrections for Sonnet and 18 for Gemini because confidence ties favour AI. • Blackbox hybridisation: fitted threshold (thr). A threshold based only on AI confidence can reject useful unanimous human judgements and selects AI 98.3% (Gemini) and 90.4% (Sonnet) of the time. With Sonnet, thr switches 77 questions from human to AI compared with cmp, all where the three humans agree but four of five AI responses support a different answer. These switches lose 21 human corrections while avoiding 14 incorrect human selections. With Gemini, thr selects humans on only 54 of 3,163 questions and captures 15 human corrections, compared with 86 for cmp. • Blackbox hybridisation: logistic regression (log). The fitted logistic router behaves dif-ferently across answerer models. With Gemini, it always selects AI, missing all 418 avail-able human corrections. With Sonnet, it makes 83 additional human selections compared with cmp, all on questions where the three humans give different answers. These selections correct 17 AI errors but introduce 16 errors, yielding only one additional correct answer overall. 32 Preprint • Confidence LoRA. Confidence LoRA selects humans for most questions in several datasets. It chooses humans on 96% of SafeAgentBench questions, 87% of FACTS Search questions, 88% of CulturalBench questions, and 82% of Liar’s Bench questions. Compared with always choosing humans, its occasional Qwen selections produce 2 additional correct answers on CulturalBench and four on SafeAgentBench, but 11 fewer on FACTS Search and no change in accuracy on Liar’s Bench. These routing patterns suggest dataset-level preferences, although they do not establish that the router relies on dataset identity. • Bayesian SWAG-LoRA confidence. SWAG-LoRA preserves more correct Qwen answers than Confidence LoRA, but misses more useful human corrections. For example, on unsafe SafeAgentBench tasks scored by both methods, SWAG-LoRA captures 30 of 63 available human corrections, compared with 59 for Confidence LoRA; on safe tasks, it introduces seven unnecessary refusals, compared with 16. Despite introducing fewer errors, SWAG-LoRA answers fewer questions correctly than Confidence LoRA on both SafeAgentBench and CulturalBench, including when comparisons are restricted to questions scored by both methods. • Probing. Probe routing sometimes captures useful differences between human and AI judgements, but this depends on the probe and calibration. A probe with Platt calibration captures all four human corrections involving attempts to obtain a researcher’s email while preserving 10 of 13 correct AI judgements involving suspicious links or code. In contrast, the MLP probe with Platt calibration selects humans on every question in two datasets because its calibrated AI confidence scores are always below human confidence. Recov-ering human corrections also does not always improve on humans alone: the linear probe captures 21 of 23 available human corrections but overrides four of five correct AI-only answers, producing 58/80 correct answers compared with 59/80 for humans alone. • Oracle-trained probe. The question-only probe mostly selects humans, with limited gains from its occasional AI selections. It routes 95.8% of questions to humans; its remaining selections preserve 36 correct AI answers but miss 23 human corrections, producing 13 ad-ditional correct answers compared with always choosing humans. Its clearest task-specific pattern appears in BBEH: it selects humans on all 31 pronoun questions and 48 of 50 cap-tion questions, but selects Qwen on 34 of 54 truth-teller puzzles. This produces 105 correct answers, compared with 96 for Qwen and 95 for humans, although it does not outperform the better fixed source within any of the five BBEH task types. • Content-based routing. The prompted router applies the distinction between mechanical execution (e.g., counting characters by following a fixed procedure) and formal reasoning (e.g., solving a logical constraint puzzle) inconsistently. Between zero-shot and few-shot prompting, 20 of 54 BBEH puzzles switch judges: the recorded explanations describe the same puzzles as mechanical execution when selecting humans and formal reasoning when selecting AI. Switching from zero-shot to few-shot routing corrects five routing errors but introduces five others for Sonnet; for Gemini, it corrects four and introduces seven. The correct alternative was available, but two of the three raters selected the incorrect answer.

The decomposition reads as deliberation, but each step was the model’s own preference restated; the one sub-question that could have cut the other way (the shared template text) was answered “yes” and then ignored in the synthesis.

L PROBING RESULTS 

As shown in Figure 16, the hidden-state probes are competitive with the output-based confidence signals, although their relative advantage depends on the calibration metric. Before post-hoc cali-bration, the linear and MLP probes achieve ECEs of 0.141 and 0.125 , respectively, compared with 

0.130 for frequency, 0.629 for entropy, 0.221 for direct mean, and 0.250 for computed confidence. The MLP probe therefore achieves the lowest raw ECE among all six confidence signals, with fre-quency performing a close second. The pattern differs for the Brier score. Before calibration, the linear and MLP probes achieve Brier scores of 0.198 and 0.202 , respectively. The linear probe obtains the lowest raw Brier score among 39 Preprint all methods, followed closely by the MLP probe. In comparison, frequency, entropy, direct mean, and computed confidence achieve Brier scores of 0.222 , 0.663 , 0.235 , and 0.255 , respectively. Thus, in the raw setting, both hidden-state probes provide strong confidence signals: the MLP probe per-forms best in terms of ECE, while the linear probe performs best in terms of Brier score. Post-hoc calibration substantially improves most confidence signals. After Platt scaling, frequency, entropy, direct mean, and computed confidence achieve ECEs of 0.037 , 0.025 , 0.020 , and 0.016 ,respectively. The linear and MLP probes achieve calibrated ECEs of 0.072 and 0.039 . Computed confidence therefore achieves the lowest calibrated ECE, followed by direct mean and entropy. Al-though Platt scaling also improves the ECE of both probes, the output-based confidence signals generally achieve lower calibrated ECE. For the Brier score, however, the linear probe remains highly competitive after calibration. The calibrated Brier scores are 0.197 for frequency, 0.189 for entropy, 0.198 for direct mean, 0.187 for computed confidence, 0.184 for the linear probe, and 0.196 for the MLP probe. The linear probe therefore achieves the lowest Brier score overall, slightly outperforming computed confidence. The MLP probe is less competitive after calibration but still performs comparably to several of the output-based signals. The effect of Platt scaling is particularly pronounced for signals that are poorly calibrated in their raw form. Entropy shows the largest improvement, with its ECE decreasing from 0.629 to 0.025 and its Brier score from 0.663 to 0.189 . Direct mean and computed confidence also show substantial reductions in ECE, while frequency improves on both metrics. The probe-based signals improve more modestly, in part because their raw Brier scores are already relatively low. Overall, these results suggest that the selected hidden-state representation contains useful infor-mation for confidence prediction. In the uncalibrated setting, the probes match or outperform the output-based signals, with the MLP probe achieving the best ECE and the linear probe achieving the best Brier score. After Platt scaling, output-based signals become substantially more competitive in terms of ECE, particularly computed confidence, while the linear probe retains the best Brier score. Thus, the hidden-state probes do not uniformly dominate the output-based alternatives, but they pro-vide competitive and complementary confidence estimates whose relative advantage depends on the evaluation metric. 40
