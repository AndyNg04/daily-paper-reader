## Abstract 

Mixed reality interfaces must continuously adapt to a user’s chang-ing task and environment, yet adaptation policies are fixed in ad-vance, either preset by the system or configured by the user. Natural language offers a way to state changing needs mid-task, but whether users can express interface behavior this way, and what it gains over direct manipulation, is unknown. We first elicited how users verbalize adaptation needs (N=19). We then built ConverseMR, which translates a spoken request into adaptation objectives and constraints and satisfies them continuously as the user’s context changes. In a within-subjects study (N=24), conversational and direct control proved complementary: direct manipulation for pre-cision, ConverseMR for reach and expressive breadth, with their combination rated highest. Spoken commands were rarely final and users frequently refined and replaced them upon experienc-ing the result, characteristic of, what we call, user-led adaptation, where users communicate adaption needs and the system identifies a solution.

## 1 Introduction 

Mixed Reality (MR) interfaces need to be adaptive to be usable across changing contexts, tasks, individual user preferences or con-straints [25]. In recent years, research has proposed a range of ap-proaches to adapt MR interfaces, in particular the spatial placement of UI elements: view management techniques that keep content visible and unoccluded [2, 15, 38], world- and egocentric anchoring strategies[18, 29, 31, 32], and optimization-based methods that place elements with respect to multiple objectives such as ergonomics, semantic relevance, or the user’s task context [4, 6, 26]. Typically, arXiv:2610.24640v1 [cs.HC] 17 Oct 2026 Elahimanesh et. al. 

adaptation criteria (e.g. to avoid occlusion of task-relevant things, to follow the users movement, or to ensure semantic similarity, etc.) are pre-defined by researchers or designers. However, a user’s con-text and personal preferences are rich and change continuously [3], and determining which adaptation criteria apply to a specific user in a specific moment is error-prone. Recent work has therefore recognized the importance of letting end-users shape the spatial organization [19, 24, 36] and adaptive behavior [3] of MR inter-faces themselves through direct manipulation and selecting from pre-configured configurations [3, 17, 31]. To illustrate this, consider Alice cooking a meal, following an MR recipe with video instructions and a timer (Figure 1). While she prepares ingredients at the counter, these elements are best anchored where they do not occlude her hands. When she walks to the fridge, she needs the ingredient list to come with her, while the video and timer are momentarily irrelevant. Detecting such a subtle shift in task, inferring the task-relevance of each element, and choosing a suitable adaptive behavior for each is difficult to do automatically in a domain-independent way without occasionally inferring the wrong intent. For decades, research on adaptive GUIs has shown that the accuracy and the predictability of an adaptation determine whether users benefit from it at all [11, 13, 14], and that users may prefer an interface they control themselves over a more efficient one that adapts on their behalf [10]. In MR the stakes are higher. Adaptation is a precondition for usable interfaces rather than a convenience, since content has no fixed screen to occupy and must be placed continually with respect to a user moving through a changing physical world [25]. A poor placement can occlude the real-world task the user is performing, or force them to interrupt it. What is needed is a way for users to state their changing needs without interrupting their activity, and a computational approach that turns those needs into interface adaptations. Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a promising way to translate intent expressed through natural language into system behavior and interface configurations [5, 24], offering func-tional flexibility [20, 21] and more broadly a new way to access and personalize computing [33] . We therefore see natural-language interaction and LLMs as a promising technique for users to commu-nicate changing interaction needs during MR use. Recent work has shown the feasibility of this for generating static MR layouts [24]. Whether users can also control the adaptive behavior of an interface this way, and how they express their interaction needs when they do, is unclear though. In this paper, we aim to understand whether natural-language based control of adaptive MR interfaces is usable and useful. Specif-ically, we want to understand whether users are able to verbalize their adaptation needs, and how natural-language-based control compares to direct manipulation in terms of customization fre-quency, breadth of expressible adaptations, and usability. We first conducted a formative study (N=19) in which participants per-formed an MR task under different mobility settings and were asked how they would verbally instruct the interface to behave. A the-matic analysis of 57 elicited responses yields a variety of adaptation needs, from which we derive five design requirements for conver-sational MR adaptation. Guided by these, we develop ConverseMR, a system that lets users control the adaptive behavior of MR in-terfaces through speech. ConverseMR uses a structured, few-shot prompt to extract a user’s intent from a spoken utterance into an intermediate representation of adaptation objectives, constraints, and parameters, asking for clarification where the interpretation is uncertain and otherwise adapting the interface through multi-objective optimization based on AUIT [4]. It outputs a summary of the adaption intent as feedback to the user, who can refine, replace, or revert their command, after experiencing the result. In a within-subjects study with 24 participants, we let users engage in a representative multi-tasking scenario, systematically varying interaction requirements and movement demands to elicit changing adaptation needs. We explore how participants use and rate ConverseMRcompared to a direct manipulation interface, and a combination of both, to customize the adaptive behavior of three UI elements to the changes in their environment and task. . Interest-ingly, Combined condition is rated highest throughout. Rather than one method dominating, participants identified trade-offs between them. Direct manipulation was preferred for precise, close-range placement, for its determinism, and for its discoverability of func-tionalities. ConverseMR on the other hand was preferred for its expressive breadth, for leaving the hands free, and for reach with-out walking. manipulation wherever speaking aloud is undesirable. Despite these trade-offs, voice absorbed the majority of adaptation activity once it was available, and over half of our participants never used the graphical menu in the Combined condition. We contribute (i) a characterization of how users verbalize MR adaptation needs established through a formative study and ex-tended through observations during live interaction, (ii) ConverseMR, a conversational system enabling intent-driven control of UI adap-tation in mixed reality, and (iii) an empirical understanding of the trade-offs between conversational and direct control of interface adaptation, showing that they are complementary rather than com-peting. More broadly, our work points to a shift in how adaptive interfaces might be built. Rather than choosing between systems that adapt on the user’s behalf and systems users configure by hand, ConverseMR lets users state their adaptation needs and leave it to the system to carry it out. This form of user-led adaptation is a promising direction for adaptive interfaces beyond MR. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Adaptive Mixed Reality Interfaces 

The adaptation of Mixed Reality interfaces has been an active topic of research since its early days, as changes in the user’s context such as movement and physical constraints require UI modifications to continue to be usable [25]. Pioneering work from Bell et al. focused on view management techniques to maintain virtual content in the user’s field of view, prioritizing the avoidance of occlusion by updating the position, size, and transparency of the virtual content [2]. Grasset et al. later extended this with an image-based method that analyses the environment and adapts UI positions in the 2D view plane of an AR browser [15], and Tatzgern et al. moved the approach into three dimensions [37]. Beyond position, researchers have adapted other UI properties: Tatzgern et al. adapted level of detail to information density [38], and Lindlbauer et al. adapted both position and level of detail to the user’s context using com-binatorial optimization [26]. Subsequent work broadened the set of factors an MR interface can adapt to, including environment Say It or Grab It? Conversational Control of UI Adaptations in Mixed Reality 

semantics [6], ergonomics [7, 8], and the user’s surroundings or social situation [23]. Most of these systems formulate adaptation as an optimization problem. Belo et al. consolidate these objectives in the AUIT toolkit, separating adaptation objectives, optimization method, and the mechanism by which the UI changes [4]. Johns et al. build on this with ParetoAdapt, which presents multiple adaptation proposals from which the user selects [19]. More recently, symbolic optimiza-tion has been extended with LLM-based reasoning, for example to interpret the scene [23] or the user’s preferences [24]. Across this literature, the objectives that govern adaptation are authored by the designer at development time. The user’s role is, at most, to select among system-generated proposals [19]; they cannot state what the interface should optimize for, nor revise it as their task changes. An exception is the recent work by Li et al. [24] which proposes a similar architecture to our ConverseMR system, trans-lating natural language into a symbolic layout model and using the AUIT toolkit [4] to solve and execute the resulting model in XR. Rather than optimizing static UI layouts, we focus on enabling users to (re-)specify the adaptive behavior of UI elements as their task and interaction needs change over time. 

## 2.2 Spatial Reference Frames for UI anchoring 

Early view-management techniques proposed UI positioning strate-gies to improve the usability of XR systems, but only recently have researchers investigated how the choice of frame of reference it-self affects usability. The question is particularly relevant under locomotion, where content can be anchored to the physical envi-ronment, to the user’s body, or to physical objects [3, 18, 29, 31, 32]. 

World-anchored interfaces hold a stable position relative to the en-vironment, and Manakhov et al. [29] show that target acquisition becomes faster and more precise as a target’s stability relative to the environment increases. Egocentric frames, in contrast, move with the user: head-anchoring keeps content within the field of view for quick glances, torso-anchoring offers a more stable egocentric reference, and hand-anchoring supports on-demand access to small elements [3, 18, 32]. These trade-offs suggest that no frame of reference is universally optimal. Belo et al. [3] found that usability of and preference for an anchoring strategy depends on the user’s real-world activity and locomotion, the virtual and real-world tasks, the interaction modality and other properties of the UI element, and individual factors such as the user’s mental model of where interfaces live. The same study reports that participants configured their anchoring once at the start of a condition and revised it in only 6 of 57 cases during the trials that followed, which suggests that the cost of reconfiguring was high relative to the benefit users expected from it. Anchoring is therefore a decision that a designer cannot make in advance and that a user is unlikely to revisit through manual controls. Whether speech can serve as a more seamless mechanism for making and revising this decision during an ongoing interaction has not been examined. 

## 2.3 Adaptive and Adaptable Interfaces 

Who decides how an interface changes is a long-standing question in HCI, and the two established answers are the system and the user. Adaptive interfaces automatically infer what to change and when. Studying adaptive menus, Gajos et al. found their perceived benefit and users’ performance with such systems to largely depend on the accuracy of the inference [13] and the predictability of the adaptation [14]. Findlater and McGrenere [10] compared static, adaptive, and adaptable menus and found that adaptable menus were faster than adaptive ones and preferred by most participants. Findlater and McGrenere [11] further showed that these effects depend on the interaction context, with adaptation becoming more valuable as the cost of accessing an element grows. In adaptable interfaces the user configures the behaviour directly, which removes the accuracy problem but introduces an interac-tion cost. Early work by Mackay [28] showed that customization is episodic rather than continuous: users change their software in response to specific triggers, and are held back by the time and attention the change demands. Banovic et al. [1] demonstrated that this is partly a visibility problem, as surfacing customization oppor-tunities in context substantially increases how often people take them up. The same pattern holds in MR. Direct manipulation tech-niques give users fine-grained, precise control over UI placement and anchoring [3, 17, 31], but Lu and Xu [27] found that users prefer semi-automated transition mechanisms over either fully manual or fully automatic ones, and the low reconfiguration rates reported by Belo et al. [3] indicate that the cost of manual customization is perceived as particularly high when users are engaged in time-critical tasks. Our work explores a third approach. Instead of the user config-uring a static setting or the system continuously reassessing and inferring the user’s needs from context, ConverseMR allows the user to iteratively refine through natural language how the inter-face should behave, and the system translates the user’s intent into an adaptation policy which it refines or replaces as the user’s needs change. The user decides what the interface should do, while the system carries out that specification continuously. Whether users prefer this to configuring the interface themselves, and whether it lowers the effort of adjusting an interface during an ongoing task, is what we examine in this paper. 

## 2.4 Conversational Interaction with User Interfaces 

Natural language has been used to operate graphical interfaces for some time, most often to issue a single action in place of a manual one. Pasupat et al. [30] ground commands such as “click on the second article” to elements on a web page, and Li et al. [22] map multi-step instructions to action sequences on mobile UIs. Large language models have widened what such systems can do: Wang et al. [41] enable conversational interaction with mobile UIs, includ-ing question answering over a screen and mapping instructions to actions. Language has also been used to interrogate interfaces rather than operate them, as by Todi et al. [39], who let designers and developers query large GUI datasets conversationally. Closer to our setting, recent work uses language to produce the interface itself. Cao et al. [5] let end-users generate and iteratively tailor GUIs through natural language over an evolving task-driven data model. In XR, Li et al. [24] allow users to open applications and specify their spatial layout through natural language commands, Elahimanesh et. al. 

with clarification questions where the request is ambiguous. In all of these cases the utterance resolves into a single outcome: an action is performed, an answer is returned, or a layout is produced. The specification is complete once it is satisfied. Our focus is different. An anchoring decision is not a state to be reached but a rule about how the interface should behave from now on, and its appropriateness changes as the user moves, switches task, or is interrupted. Speech is a promising interaction method for continuously refining the adaptive behavior of UI elements in MR because the hands and gaze are typically committed to the task that motivated the change in the first place. We therefore study conversational interaction as a means of specifying and revising the ongoing anchoring and placement behaviour of MR UI elements, and how such interaction performs against and alongside direct manipulation during an ongoing task. 

## 3 Formative Study 

To better understand how users naturally describe spatial anchor-ing behavior in Mixed Reality and what a conversational system for controlling adaptive MR interfaces would need to support, we first conducted a formative qualitative study. Participants (N=19) interacted with an initial version of the study interface described in detail in Section 5 (see Figure 4). Participant’s primary task was to search for a specific key hidden in boxes placed throughout the room (simulated in an MR interface for better study control). The configuration of boxes changed throughout trials to enforce differ-ent locomotion conditions (stationary, semi-stationary, and mobile). While doing the task, participants interacted with three UI ele-ments that differed in interaction modality, interaction frequency, and task-relevance and were encouraged to manually configure their preferred anchoring modes for each UI element using hand input and dragging the element (see more details below 5.2). After completing the tasks, we asked participants the following open-ended question and recorded their responses: 

“Instead of moving and anchoring the interface, imag-ine you could talk to it. How would you tell the inter-face to behave?” 

We collected 57 responses across the three locomotion condi-tions. Each response described how participants would request adaptations for each of the three UI elements using natural lan-guage, specifying the different interaction needs and preferences arising from the corresponding locomotion condition and the dif-ferent interaction requirements of each UI element. The format of responses was mixed with some formulating actual commands (e.g., "Attach to my left arm") while others were more descriptive (e.g. "I’d like to find some way to call each interface"). The responses were coded inductively in a bottom-up process using ten thematic labels by three researchers from this project with the goal to cap-ture the requirements for such an interface. Since a single utterance could express multiple adaption intents, multiple labels could be assigned to the same response. In total, the coding produced 245 label assignments across the 57 responses. The remaining themes reveal additional expectations, like ergonomic considerations (#6, 𝑛 = 13), such as reachability and avoiding visual obstruction, and occasional rule-based specifica-tions formulated by participants (#5, 𝑛 = 12), such as displaying a specific UI element only when receiving a call. A smaller number of responses expressed constraints on preserving previous placements or limiting larger deviations (#10, 𝑛 = 5). Overall, our themes show that natural language commands for MR adaptation are compositional and can combine several criteria and targets in a single utterance. We note that participants answered in an interview, with time to think and without seeing the resulting interface behavior, so the responses are one-shot descriptions rather than the turns of an ongoing interaction. In practice, users might not specify all desired adaptations within a single utterance but take multiple turns to refine their adaptation intent. Moreover, a user’s message might be ambiguous and underspecified, for example, not specifying parameters (e.g. how far away something should be placed), leaving trade-offs unresolved (e.g. is the visibility of the UI or the real-world task more important if both cannot be fulfilled) or not mentioning relevant criteria (e.g. the UI element should be visible and reachable). A user might forget to specify them or not be aware of it before experiencing the resulting UI behavior. Importantly, adaptation needs will change as the user’s task or environment change, requiring users to periodically revise or adjust previously specified behaviors. Finally, since intent extraction is inherently uncertain, the system may misinterpret a command or realize it in a way the user did not anticipate. Users therefore need to understand what was applied and be able to revert it. From these findings and our reflections, we define five design requirements for a conversational system for MR adaptation that guide our design of ConverseMR which we develop in Section 4: 

DR1 Compositional commands. Interpret messages that jointly specify a target interface, spatial references, desired behav-iors and constraints within a single utterance. 

DR2 Grounded reference resolution. Allow explicit and im-plicit references to interface elements, body parts, physical surroundings, virtual objects, or previous adaptations. 

DR3 Persistent and conditional behavior. Treat a command as a standing policy. Continuously re-evaluate it as the user’s pose, task, and environment change, and trigger adap-tations on specified conditions.

## 4 System Design 

We next develop ConverseMR, a conversational interface for con-trolling MR interface adaptations through natural language. Con-verseMR allows a user to speak a request, interprets the user’s utterance against the current adaptation policies and environment, and translates it into a new adaptation policy. In the following, we describe the four steps of this pipeline, visualized in Figure 2, and how they address our design requirements. 

## 4.1 Speech Input 

The user activates ConverseMR through a double pinch and then speaks an instruction describing the desired adaptation. We use an explicit activation gesture rather than continuous listening to avoid unintended activation, since our study tasks also required participants to speak. The utterance is transcribed with a speech-to-text module (Meta Voice SDK). We treat the resulting transcript as noisy rather than attempting to improve recognition itself and handle recovery in the next steps. . 

## 4.2 Intent Extraction 

The goal of this step is to turn the transcript of the user’s utterance into a specification for UI adaptation that can be executed. Com-mands collected in the formative study show that such utterances can be expected to be compositional. They will jointly name a target, a spatial reference, a desired behavior, or constraints (DR1). They might also refer to entities in the world and to earlier commands (DR2). We therefore pass the transcript to an LLM (OpenAI Responses API, GPT 5.2) and require a JSON output following a fixed schema we devised. Along with the transcript, we also pass a structured scnapshot of the current scene, containing the available interface el-ements, relevant objects and surfaces in the environment (through MRUK), and their spatial information such as positions and bounds, as well as the user’s current position as a structured textual repre-sentation of the current context. We are also passing the recent com-mands, conversational turns, and current adaptation state, which allows the model to resolve pronouns and contextual expressions such as “move it higher” or “make that follow me”, and revert or adjust the previous adaptations. The JSON representation specifies the target interface, the adap-tation objectives, spatial references and relations, body-relative placement, an interpretation confidence, clarification question (if applicable - for ambiguous commands), and whether the system should apply, clarify, or undo an adaptation. Specifically, adapta-tions are expressed through predefined objectives, based on those provided by AUIT [4], including visibility, anchoring, positioning, keeping interface elements in view, and spatial movement. In addi-tion, we support distance constraints, reference points, and relative movement commands. It can also represent multiple objectives for multiple interface elements. The prompt template was developed from the command set identified in Section 3 and refined through iterative testing with variations of those commands, checking whether the model could robustly interpret paraphrases, incomplete utterances, follow-up instructions, and noisy speech-recognition outputs. It is described in detail in Appendix C. Two parts of it are specific to controlling adaptive behavior. ## 4.3 Intent Validation 

Both the transcription of the users utterance and the interpretation of it can fail or contain mistakes, and an adaptation the user did not ask for is costly in MR because it can obstruct the real-world task. Thus, rather than requiring a confirmation or correction for each command, we make the system’s interpretation visible and ask clarification questions only if the system’s confidence is low (DR4, DR5). To this end, the intent validation steps inspects the confidence value reported in the intermediate representation to determine whether and how to proceed with the interaction. If confidence is high (above 80%), the adaptation is applied and ConverseMR reports a summary of the detected adaptation through text-to-speech (Meta Voice SDK / Wit.ai) and as written text, so that users can relate the observed interface change to what the system understood. This summary is purely informational. Users can undo the adaptation at any point. If confidence is low or multiple interpretations are possible, the system instead asks a follow-up question, which is indicated in the clarification options in the intermediate represen-tation, before applying anything, and the user’s answer re-enters the pipeline as a new turn. If the command falls outside the sup-ported adaptation domain or is unrelated to interface adaptation, the system outputs feedback to that effect and makes no change. 

## 4.4 Execution and Refinement 

The adapation needs verbalized by users have to be turned into standing adaptation policies that have to hold as the user’s pose, task, and environment change (DR3), and that the user must be able to revise once they have seen its effect (DR5). The final step therefore maps the structured representation to adaptation constraints aligned with objective categories defined by AUIT and translates it into executable adaptation rules applied to the respective UI element, to update its spatial position in real time while the user continues their task. ConverseMR then performs multi-objective optimization using AUIT’s weighted objective costs. We update objective weights from the recent conversational his-tory through a recency-based deterioration factor, so that recently requested adaptations receive higher priority at runtime and users can override earlier requests as their needs change. Concretely, for each incoming command, the weights of previously stored com-mands are multiplied by a deterioration factor of 0.95, gradually reducing the influence of older commands over time. We keep two objectives always active, independently of what the user requests to avoid occlusion of the user’s view and to keep interfaces oriented toward the user (same for the direct manipulation baseline, see Section 5.1). Upon seeing and hearing about the effect of their command, users can engage in a refinement or replacement loop, which uses the same mechanism. They can modify, extend, undo, or clarify previous adaptations in additional turns, grounded in the memory module described in Section 4.2, so that the interface can be ad-justed iteratively and does not need to be configured in one shot or reconfigured from scratch. 

## 5 User Study 

We conducted a mixed-methods user study to investigate how con-versational interaction can support interface adaptation in mixed reality. In a within-subject study, participants went through three Say It or Grab It? Conversational Control of UI Adaptations in Mixed Reality 

blocks of trials where they used, ConverseMR, a direct manipu-lation (DM) baseline and a combination of both (Combined) to customize the adaptive behavior of three UI elements with different interaction properties and in response to changes in their task and environment. In particular, we were interested in exploring the following research questions: 

RQ1 How does conversational control of MR interface adap-tation compare to direct manipulation in terms of usability and preference? 

RQ2 Which factors lead users to prefer one interaction method over the other, and what trade-offs do they perceive be-tween them? 

RQ3 How does the availability of conversational control change what and how often users customize MR adaptations? 

RQ4 How do the adaptation needs expressed in live interac-tion compare to those elicited in the formative study, and how well does ConverseMR support them? In the following, we first describe the DM baseline and experi-mental design, followed by the study task, procedure, participants, and apparatus. We then describe the data we collected and our analysis approach. 

## 5.1 Direct Manipulation Baseline 

We implemented a Direct Manipulation (DM) baseline, which com-bines spatial placement through hand-based dragging with a graphi-cal control panel for selecting a predefined set of adaptive behaviors, that allowed users to perform almost the same types of interface adaptations supported by ConverseMR through hand input manu-ally or using a menu with a set of configurations for each interface. Figure 3 illustrates the interaction flow for conversational control and the two forms of direct manipulation available in the DM base-line. Users can directly grab and reposition interface elements to make continuous spatial adjustments. For configuring adaptation behaviors, DM provides a compact, head-anchored menu containing separate controls for the three adaptable interfaces in the environ-ment. Users first click on a small access button to open the menu, select an interface they want to adapt and can then modify its adap-tation properties by choosing a frame of reference (head, torso, hand, or world-anchored) and an interaction distance (near, mid, or far). All UI elements automatically avoid occlusions and maintain orientation toward the user.Changes selected through the panel are applied immediately and can subsequently be refined either through further interface selections or by directly grabbing and repositioning the interface. The DM menu was intentionally kept simple and exposed only anchoring and distance controls, more than most prior work (e.g., [3]). Earlier iterations also included options such as grid-based position-ing relative to anchors, visibility settings, and occlusion manage-ment. However, pilot testing showed that adding these controls quickly increased menu complexity and interaction time.We thus made them accessible through manual dragging. 

## 5.2 Study Design 

We used a within-subjects design with one independent variable, Interaction Method, comprising three conditions: DM, Con-verseMR, and Combined. In the DM condition, participants adapted the interfaces using the graphical panel and direct spatial manip-ulation described above. In the ConverseMR condition, partici-pants performed adaptations through spoken natural-language commands as descibred in section 4. In the Combined condition, both interaction methods were available. Participants conducted 3 blocks of 6 trials each, one for each condition. The order of DM and ConverseMR was counterbalanced across participants, with 12 participants completing DM first and 12 completing ConverseMR first. Combined was always presented as the third block, ensuring that participants had prior experience with both individual interaction methods before deciding how to use them together. 

## 5.3 Experimental Tasks 

Our experiment leverages a dual-task paradigm with one primary task and two secondary tasks with three UI elements overall, closely following previous work [3]. This reflects a common use of MR interfaces which allow people to seamlessly switch between real-world tasks and digital content [32]. The three UI elements corre-sponding to each task require different interaction modalities and frequencies and the set up of the primary task demands different levels of movement throughout the world. Together, these mimic frequent changes in a user’s task and environment and participants were asked to adapt the spatial placement and adaptation behavior of UI elements to prioritize their comfortability over speed and accuracy, allowing them to interact with the UIs while busy with the tasks comfortably. To make spatial references easier during con-versational interaction, we added visible labels above all boxes and other virtual objects, and displayed the name above each interface, allowing participants to refer to them directly in their commands (e.g., “put the Key Task UI above Drop Box 1”). The primary task in each trial was the Key task : participants searched for the correct key among five alternatives in boxes placed throughout the room, took it out of the box and put it into one of two delivery boxes. An instruction interface (see Figure 4) dis-played the correct handle and tip of the key, as well as the intended delivery box, which participants needed to toggle through using buttons. The Key task UI thus required both visual attention and manual interaction. The spatial arrangement of the boxes was used to induce different mobility levels. In the first set, the boxes were positioned around the participant and could be reached without sub-stantial movement. In the second one, boxes were grouped across multiple locations in the room, separated by virtual walls, requir-ing participants to move between areas. In the third one, all boxes were distributed around the room, requiring continuous movement through the environment. Each arrangement was repeated for two trials with the order randomized within each Interaction Method block. At random intervals (5-15 seconds after the start of the key task, and again 5-15 seconds after completing the first secondary task)„ participants were prompted to do one of two secondary tasks. At random intervals, they were prompted to report verbally how many faces matched a specified target color. ## 5.4 Procedure 

Each session began with informed consent and a background ques-tionnaire. The experimenter then introduced the purpose of the study and the concept of MR interface adaptation using a cooking scenario as a motivating example. Participants were introduced to the three tasks, the DM panel and grabbing interaction, and how to address ConverseMR. To familiarize participants with the capabilities of the potential adaptations without prescribing partic-ular commands, the experimenter provided a high-level description of the supported adaptation categories without giving example utterances. Participants first completed a Key-Task-only familiarization round to become comfortable with basic MR interaction. They then com-pleted three practice trials, one with each mobility level. Both DM and ConverseMR were available during practice, and participants were encouraged to try direct grabbing, the graphical panel, and conversational interaction. After familiarization, they conducted 3 blocks of 6 trials each, as described above. Before each trial started, participants were given the chance to familiarize with the current placement of boxes and to configure their intended adaptation over the three interfaces. When ready, they could press a button to start the trial. This mechanism ensured that they were not in a rush by the task, and they had enough time to prioritize their interaction comfort over task execution time and to apply adaptations based on the mobility level, task characteristics and personal preference. After each block, participants completed a questionnaire followed by a semi-structured interview about their experience with that In-teraction Method. After completing all three blocks, participants completed a final questionnaire and closing interview, followed by a debriefing. The study lasted 90 minutes per participant and was rewarded with 20€. An experimenter was present throughout the session. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the university’s ethics committee.

## 5.7 Data Collection and Analysis 

5.7.1 Data Collection. We collected quantitative, qualitative, and behavioral data throughout the study. Quantitative data on user ex-perience, ergonomics, and usability of each Interaction Method was collected through structured questionnaires after each trial block containing eight Likert-scale items. Preferences towards each method were collected through two bipolar comparison items in the post-study questionnaire (see items in Table 2 and Appendix A). Qualitative explanations and preferences were collected through two open-ended questions after each Interaction Method, and questions about perceived trade-offs and situations in which par-ticipants would prefer each interaction method at the end of the study. Questionnaire items were adapted from validated instru-ments where a close match existed and written as study-specific items otherwise; the complete item wording and provenance are provided in A. Open-ended questions were administered as semi-structured interviews rather than as typed responses. The exper-imenter asked each question and followed up conversationally where appropriate. During the sessions, the experimenter also recorded voice from the sessions to write transcripts, and also kept observational notes about participants’ interactions, comments, difficulties, and other events relevant to their experience. These notes were used alongside participants’ interview responses in the subsequent qualitative analysis. The application additionally logged participants’ interactions throughout each session. The logs captured session and trial events; the conversational pipeline, including speech transcription, the LLM-generated intermediate representation, and the applied adap-tation; DM and adaptation-history events, including undo states; and performance on the three secondary tasks. These logs enabled us to reconstruct participants’ adaptation behavior and analyze the conversational pipeline from spoken input to executed interface change. 

5.7.2 Data Analysis. 

Questionnaire Data. To answer RQ1, We analyzed quantitative data from questionnaires to examine participants’ perceived usabil-ity and experience across the interaction methods. We compared Likert-scale responses across interaction methods using Friedman’s test, followed by Holm-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pairwise comparisons where appropriate. Because Likert responses are ordinal and our inferential analyses are rank-based, we use the median as the primary measure of central tendency and re-port means alongside it to facilitate comparison with prior work. Categorical responses are reported as counts and percentages. 

Qualitative Data. To answer RQ2, We conducted an inductive thematic analysis of the qualitative data. One researcher, who also conducted the study sessions, performed the coding. The analysis drew on participants’ responses from the Post-Method and Final interviews together with the researcher’s observational notes and notes from discussions during the sessions. The researcher reviewed each participant’s qualitative data holistically and developed codes inductively from recurring patterns in the data rather than using a predefined coding scheme. Codes were assigned based on the con-tent of participants’ responses rather than the specific question that elicited them, as participants frequently raised issues relevant to multiple or adjacent questions. The codes were iteratively reviewed Elahimanesh et. al. 

and grouped into higher-level themes capturing recurring experi-ences, preferences, and trade-offs across the interaction methods. We used an LLM (Claude) as an assistant during this process to help organize codes and suggest potential groupings and themes; all resulting themes were reviewed, refined, and finalized by the researchers. 

Log data. We analyzed the interaction logs to answer RQ3 and RQ4. For RQ3, we computed customization frequency per partici-pant and condition, counting each issued voice command or DM menu selection as one adaptation action. Hand-based grabbing and dragging were not logged as discrete actions and are therefore ex-cluded from this measure, making DM and Combined frequencies a lower bound on manual activity. For RQ4, we analyzed conversa-tional commands at the individual-command level to characterize participants’ adaptation needs, compare them with the themes from the formative study, examine how commands were clarified, refined, replaced, repeated, or reverted over time, and evaluate the different stages of the conversational pipeline.

Median adaptation actions per participant were 18.0 in Con-verseMR, 11.0 in Combined, and 7.0 in DM ( Figure 6 6, left). 6.3.2 Communication of Adaptation Needs in Natural Language.

## 7 Discussion & Conclusion 

We set out to understand whether users can control the adaptive behavior of MR interfaces through natural language, and what such control offers over direct manipulation. Participants expressed a broad range of adaptation needs in speech, and used ConverseMR for most of their adaptations whenever it was available. ConverseMR alone, however, was never rated highest on any measure. It was statistically indistinguishable from DM on seven of eight usabil-ity items and worse on the time spent correcting mistakes, while the combination of both methods was preferred throughout. We discuss below what this pattern means for the design of conversa-tional adaptation, and for the role users can take in adapting MR interfaces. 

## 7.1 Language Replaces the Menu, Not the Hand 

The Combined condition revealed that participants did not take a choice between conversational control and direct manipulation. Rather, they split their adaptations by the kind of work involved. One kind is deciding how an interface should behave, such as which anchor it should follow, which element a request applies to, and under which condition the behavior should hold. The other is putting an element at a particular spot, “a little higher”, “slightly closer”, “just above the box”. Language covers the first kind well. Anchors, interfaces, objects in the room and task conditions all have names, so a participant could state a complete behavior in one utterance, as in “Anchor the control panel to my head” (P5). Language covers the second kind poorly. A position in space has no name, and the vocabulary avail-able for it is too coarse to carry the value. Participants who wanted an element slightly elsewhere said “Move key task a little further” 

(P6), then looked at the result and said it again. The hand does not have this problem, since the movement is itself the specification [ ?

]. The DM menu offered only the first kind of work. It let partic-ipants select an anchor and a coarse distance, which is precisely what they could also say out loud, and saying it required no panel, no search for the right entry, and no selection from drop-down menus. Given both, most participants stopped using it. Thirteen of 24 never opened the menu once voice was available, and anchoring requests rose from 54% of commands in ConverseMR to 67% in Combined. Dragging was not replaced in the same way, and Adjust-ments fell from 35% to 20% of voice commands once participants could reach for an interface by hand instead. Choosing between ConverseMR and DM is therefore note just a choice between coarse and fine adaptations, which is how par-ticipants described it, but between what can be expressed through language and what is better shown or gestures through hands. ## 7.2 Designing for Predictable Conversational Adaptation 

Prior experience and failures shape interaction expectations. Par-ticipants’ expectations of ConverseMR were shaped both by their experience during the study and by prior interactions with speech-based assistants. Several spontaneously compared ConverseMR with Siri or Alexa, bringing existing assumptions about what voice interaction can understand and how reliably it should respond. These expectations then changed through use in both directions. Successful commands helped participants predict how to phrase later requests, and some revised an initially negative view once the method paid off: “At first I thought I didn’t like voice... then I saw it’s much easier, and I preferred voice” (P21). A single failure could be enough though to move a participant the other way, as P16 described: “I lose trust with one failure - I just do it myself [after that].” A failed interaction therefore affected not only the immediate recovery but the participant’s choice of modality for the rest of the session, and their developing model of what the system could do. Early interactions and clear, predictable responses are thus particularly important for helping users learn when conversational adaptation will work and when another method is preferable. 

Communication gap between commands and expected outcomes. 

Some unexpected outcomes were not caused by incorrect system interpretation or incorrect user input, but by a gap between what a command specified and what the participant expected to hap-pen. For example, P13 asked ConverseMR to follow them while moving. The system interpreted this as a conditional behavior and followed the participant only while they were in motion (a condi-tional trigger). While this interpretation was consistent with the command, P13 expected the interface to start following immedi-ately and continue doing so, even when they were standing, like a head-anchored interface. Similar cases occurred when the system applied a command as specified, but the resulting behavior still differed from what the participant had expected. This suggests a communication gap between the literal meaning of a command and the behavior users have in mind. Addressing such cases requires more accurate intent recognition, better ambiguity detection, and a more responsive system that communicates its interpretation clearly, allowing users to identify and correct mismatches during interaction. 

Feedback mechanisms. Conversational adaptation needs light-weight feedback that makes the system’s current state and recent actions visible without interrupting the interaction. While Con-verseMR provided these, participants’ suggested improvements across different stages of interaction: when the system starts lis-tening, when an object is being changed, and when the system is uncertain. For example, P13 preferred an auditory cue for listening because it would be easier to notice than text, and also suggested highlighting or spatialized sound to indicate which object was mov-ing and in which direction. P15 similarly viewed clarification as useful when the system was uncertain. These comments suggest that feedback should not only confirm that a command was re-ceived, but also communicate what the system understood and what changed as a result. This becomes particularly important for recovery. If users cannot easily tell what a command changed, de-ciding whether to undo and knowing what will be undone also becomes difficult. A lightweight history of recent adaptations, com-bined with clear multimodal confirmation and an explicit undo action, could therefore make conversational adaptation more pre-dictable and easier to recover from without requiring users to ask the system for its current state. 

## 7.3 Toward User-Led MR Adaptation and Beyond 

Our log data shows that participants did not simply state an adapta-tion intent, receive a solution, and stop. Requests sometimes needed clarification before a solution could be formed, and applied solu-tions were then refined, replaced, repeated, or reverted. Participants only produced these once they could see an applied change, which suggests that the loop closes not at the command but at the adapta-tion the user observes. We read this as evidence that adaptation of MR interfaces need not be either something a system does to a user or something a user configures once. Users can instead state what they want the interface to do and leave the system to work out how, revising the policy as their situation changes. User-led adaptation thus sits between automatic adaptation and manual customization. It is an iterative process between a user and a system, aligning the user’s intent with the system’s solution. Clarification, refinement, replacement, and recovery are therefore not exceptional error cases but central parts of that loop, which means designing for user-led adaptation is designing for the loop rather than for the command. The same structure may apply beyond MR adaptation to tasks such as scheduling, trip planning, resource allocation, or personal con-figuration, where users begin with an incomplete or implicit intent and progressively shape a solution provided by the system [42]. Our transition categories offer a first empirical vocabulary for describ-ing and evaluating such intent-driven systems, with ConverseMR as one concrete realization. This iterative behavior also gives customization frequency two possible interpretations. More actions can be positive, indicating that participants used the available flexibility to make more adap-tations, but they can also reflect difficulty reaching the intended result, requiring users to refine or replace earlier changes. This distinction is visible across conditions. ConverseMR-only had the highest frequency ( 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 18 .5), but also lower subjective ratings and more replacements, suggesting that at least some of these addi-tional actions reflected correction or refinement. Combined had a lower frequency ( 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 11 .0) despite offering greater flexibility, suggesting that participants could make complex adaptations with fewer steps. DM had the lowest frequency ( 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 7.0), which may instead reflect its more constrained adaptation space. Thus, higher customization frequency is not inherently better or worse, but needs to be interpreted alongside how and why participants continued adapting. 

## 7.4 Limitations and Future Work 

Our study lasted only 90 minutes, which means our results describe early rather than habituated use. Participant’s comments showed that they were still learning what ConverseMR could understand. In some cases, a single failure was enough to move some of them to Say It or Grab It? Conversational Control of UI Adaptations in Mixed Reality 

the manual alternative for the rest of the session. Since Combined always came third in our study design, its high ratings might be partially conflated with longer experience. Long-term studies are needed to explore how the use of conversational systems changes users’ preferences and interactive behavior as people gain more experience with them. The study room was compact (5 × 3.5 m), so walking to an inter-face cost participants little. ConverseMR’s reach advantage is likely understated here and might grow in larger spaces or mobile out-door conditions. The same holds for the environments themselves. Our scene was sparse and its objects were labelled to make spatial references easy, whereas real spaces offer many more anchors and no labels, and resolving a reference such as “next to the shelf” be-comes harder as the number of candidates grows. Further studies in larger more varied settings might reveal different considerations and preferences. The DM baseline is one of several defensible choices. We com-bined the menu with manual spatial movement to give participants the full range of manual control, but the two could have been separated into conditions of their own, and the menu-only and dragging-only ratings in Section 6.1.1 suggest they are experienced differently. The adaptation space we supported with either method also bounds what participants could ask for. In some cases, participants requested anchors we did not offer, in particular objects and sur-faces in the environment and further locations on the body, as well as layouts they could save and recall. Several suggested gesture shortcuts for frequent adaptations such as anchoring, which would sit between speaking and navigating a menu. A system that recog-nises which adaptations a user requests in which situation could offer them again when the situation recurs, which would spare users from restating the same policy and would still leave them in charge of what the interface should do. Beyond these extensions, our results point to three important directions for future work: handling domain-specific terminology and ambiguity in language, making both the scope and the outcome of an adaptation legible in the interface, and closing the latency gap between stating an adaptation and seeing it applied, which participants experienced as a frustration distinct from being mis-understood.

## B System Validation 

Across the full corpus, we examined accuracy along the intent →

transcript → structured representation → executed adaptation →

perceived outcome pipeline. For speech recognition, we aligned logged transcripts with the notes taken by the experimenter on what participants actually said as the ground truth utterances. We found that of these, only 49.1% were transcribed correctly by the Meta SDK, 37.4% were partially preserved, and 13.5% were totally incorrect. After the LLM-based interpretation stage, most of this inaccuracy could be resolved though. For high-confidence com-mands that did not require clarification, the objective extracted into the intermediate representation matched the ground truth ob-jective in 96.0% of cases, suggesting that the second stage of the pipeline could recover from many speech-recognition errors in-troduced in the first stage. It was consistent with high confidence (𝑀 = 0.845, 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 0.86) and infrequent clarification (11.2% of 916 decisions). Execution was similarly stable: only 13.4% of 543 consec-utive applied-command pairs immediately replaced a property just set on the same target, an upper bound that also includes normal refinement, and only 15 explicit reverts occurred. Finally, participants rated whether the system behaved as ex-pected at 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = 5.0/7, statistically indistinguishable from DM (5.5) and below Combined (6.0). Overall, most observed errors arose during speech recognition, while the intent extraction stage was tolerant to such problems, and it could resolve the noisy speech recognition transcripts. Particularly for study-specific terminology, while interpretation and execution were considerably more reliable once usable input reached the system.
