# Ego2Contact: Predicting Touch from Egocentric Human Video for Contact-Rich Robot Manipulation 

Qianqian Yang 1, Dayou Li 2, Bangya Liu 3, Zihao Zhu 2, Nuo Chen 2, Siyuan Xu 2, Hezhen Hu 4, Chen Yu 4,Rebecca Friesen 2, Minghui Zheng 2, Cynthia Hipwell 2, Xiaoning Qian 2, Kenji Shimada 1, and Zhiwen Fan 2

Abstract —Contact locations and interaction forces are critical for contact-rich robot manipulation but are often ambiguous from vision alone. Measuring them on robots requires specialized tactile hardware and platform-specific data collection, which keeps robot tactile data scarce. Human egocentric video is far easier to scale but lacks touch. Recent predictors trained on egocentric recordings paired with tactile-glove measurements can predict this missing signal from video alone. These predictors, however, remain confined to human hands, which differ from robot hands in appearance and tactile layout. We present Ego2Contact, which learns a visual-tactile prior from human interactions and transfers it to robot hands. Its predictor fuses temporal scene context with hand-local visual features to infer bimanual tactile fields from the RGB video. Anatomical region correspondences align varying robot tactile layouts with the predictor’s canonical grid, turning robot measurements into fine-tuning targets and predictions into policy inputs. Ego2Contact outperforms prior tactile predictors on human data, including unseen objects, and on robot data after adaptation. The adapted predictor provides tactile inputs for both simulated reinforce-ment learning and real-robot policy post-training. In simulation, policies using predicted touch outperform non-tactile baselines and recover part of the benefit of ground-truth tactile sensing. These results show that human-learned tactile priors can supply touch when direct tactile sensing is limited. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Reliable contact-rich robot manipulation depends on un-derstanding where physical contact occurs and how interac-tion forces evolve. Dexterous behaviors, from regulating grip force against slip to detecting contact onset under occlusion, rely on contact information that is often ambiguous from visual observations alone [1], [2]. Tactile sensors provide direct measurements of these interactions, yet collecting large-scale visual-tactile data on robots remains challenging. Tactile skins and optical sensors require specialized hardware that is difficult to integrate and tightly coupled to specific end-effector geometries [3], [4]. Consequently, tactile recordings are costly to collect and difficult to reuse across platforms, leaving tactile measurements largely absent from large-scale cross-embodiment datasets [5]. Human egocentric video, by contrast, is far easier to collect at scale [6]–[8] but lacks touch. Recent datasets recorded with sensing gloves pair egocentric RGB video with dense full-hand pressure measurements [9]–[11]. Training a predictor on such

However, existing visual-tactile predictors are developed and evaluated exclusively within the human domain [10]–[12], and they do not show that inferred touch can drive robot policies. In contrast, robot-centric approaches that imagine or predict tactile signals learn entirely within a single robot’s native sensor space and do not draw on human interaction data [13], [14]. Methods that do transfer human tactile priors to robots entangle tactile estimation with a specific policy ar-chitecture or action space [15], [16]. What remains missing is a unified mechanism that extracts a generalizable visual-tactile prior from human interactions and delivers it to heterogeneous robots as explicit tactile observations that their policies can consume. Establishing this human-to-robot bridge involves two funda-mental challenges. The first is to resolve the visual ambiguity during contact inference. Since the interacting surfaces are routinely occluded by the hand, a static image or an estimated 3D hand pose does not uniquely determine the underlying pressure distribution. Identical hand configurations can exert vastly different forces depending on the task phase. Therefore, reliable inference demands integrating the temporal dynamics of the scene with fine-grained local hand appearance. The sec-ond challenge is to overcome the morphological and sensory discrepancy between human and robot hands. Robot hands differ visibly from gloved human hands, which shifts the visual input away from the human training distribution. A predictor trained on human gloves also produces pressure on a human-centric layout, whereas the downstream robots may rely on sparse rigid-link force readings in simulation or high-density taxel arrays in the real world. Without a structured interface, predictions in human space cannot be supervised by robot measurements or consumed by robot control policies. We introduce Ego2Contact , a framework that learns a visual-tactile prior from human egocentric video and transfers it to robot hands, where it provides tactile observations for robot learning (Figure 1). Ego2Contact formulates tactile esti-mation as conditional flow-matching generation over a canon-ical 2D hand-grid representation [10]. To address the visual ambiguity, our diffusion transformer is conditioned on a cross-attentive fusion of temporally aggregated scene tokens and hand-local visual features. The canonical grid organizes tactile signals into explicit anatomical regions, so it naturally serves as a cross-embodiment interface. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Vision-Based Contact and Pressure Estimation 

Predicting physical interaction from images has progressed from localizing contact to estimating continuous pressure. Early datasets capture where contact occurs on object or hand surfaces [17], [18], and dense predictors estimate the binary hand-surface contact [19]. A parallel line regresses hand pres-sure from RGB, from planar touchpads [12], [20] to egocentric observations with pose and mesh-space pressure [21], [22]. Recent work scales this to full-hand tactile fields by pairing egocentric video with wearable-glove measurements [9]–[11], [23], [24], and estimates contact and 3D force jointly from monocular input [25]–[27]. Among these, TouchAnything in-troduces the EgoTouch dataset and a bimanual predictor whose 

21 × 21 canonical layout we adopt [10]. These methods show that dense touch is visually predictable, but they operate and are evaluated within the human domain. 

B. Tactile Sensing and Imagination for Robot Manipulation 

Tactile feedback improves contact-rich control when vision is ambiguous. Policies combine visual and tactile observations to select regrasping actions [2] and to react to force feedback during execution [28]. Multimodal visual-tactile representa-tions support contact-rich tasks [29], and tactile sensing has enabled dexterous skills such as in-hand object rotation from vision and touch [30], [31]. Since tactile hardware is not always available at deployment, a line of work predicts tactile signals within the robot’s own domain. TacImag learns a vision-to-touch model from paired robot demonstrations and supplies imagined tactile to sensor-free policies [13], while ViTacWorld learns an action-conditioned visuo-tactile world model from robot trajectories for rollout generation and policy learning [14]. C. Transferring Human Priors to Robot Tactile Learning 

Beyond extracting visual representations [32] or motion tra-jectories [33]–[35] from egocentric datasets [36]–[38], recent efforts have begun transferring human tactile priors to robots. VTDexManip [15] pretrains visual-tactile representations from human demonstrations to accelerate dexterous reinforcement learning, whereas H-Tac [16] learns a joint tactile-action latent space that transfers to robots via post-training. In particular, both paradigms entangle tactile estimation with a specific policy architecture or action space. Ego2Contact instead decouples perception from control, learning a dense pressure predictor from human video and bridging robot sensor layouts via anatomical correspondences. The predictions are evaluated as observations for both simulated and real-world policy training. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Problem Formulation 

Ego2Contact learns to infer touch from human interaction videos and adapts this visual-tactile prior to robot learning. Given an RGB clip Vt = {It−(T −1)∆ , . . . , I t} of T =8 

frames with sampling interval ∆=2 , the model predicts a synchronized bimanual tactile field Pt ∈ [0 , 1] 2×21 ×21 . Each hand’s tactile field uses the 21 × 21 layout introduced by TouchAnything [10], with 217 valid sensor cells. We first train the predictor fθH on paired egocentric human video and tactile measurements, with human tactile targets normalized per sequence. For robot domain R, we initialize θR from θH

and fine-tune the predictor on robot tactile targets mapped to this grid through anatomical region correspondences. 

B. Learning a Visual-Tactile Prior 

Visual conditioning. Figure 2 summarizes the predictor. Predicting touch requires scene context and local evidence of hand-object interaction. A frozen DINOv2 ViT-B/14 en-coder [39] extracts 256 patch tokens of dimension 768 from each RGB frame, which a three-layer temporal transformer aggregates across the clip. To complement this scene represen-tation, a frozen WiLoR ViT-H backbone [40] extracts spatial features from per-hand crops, with left-hand crops mirrored to match WiLoR’s right-hand convention. Spatial pooling retains 16 tokens per hand, and a learned projection maps the 1280-dimensional WiLoR features to the 768-dimensional scene-token space. These hand tokens query the temporally aggregated scene features through two cross-attention layers, producing conditions ct that associate local hand appearance with its scene context over time. To improve appearance robustness, we augment the input online before the frozen backbones. HSV hue shifts with Gaussian noise are applied separately to the hand region and to the background, and the hand crop is rotated in plane by up to 20 ◦. Recoloring the near-black glove to varied bright colors discourages reliance on glove color. We also drop the entire WiLoR conditioning with probability 0.2 during training, to encourage use of scene features. Tactile supervision trains the temporal and fusion modules together with the generator, while the visual backbones remain frozen. 

Conditional grid generation. Conditioned on ct, a six-block grid-based Diffusion Transformer [41] generates the tactile field. We partition each noisy 21 × 21 hand grid into 3 × 3 patches and embed them as 49 tokens. Within each block, self-attention exchanges information among grid locations while cross-attention incorporates ct. Zero-initialized adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN-Zero) further modulates the grid features using the flow-time embedding and the mean conditioning token, after which the shared output head reconstructs a 21 × 21 velocity grid for each hand.

C. Robot Adaptation via Anatomical Correspondence 

Figure 3 summarizes our adaptation procedure. The pre-dictor’s 217 valid grid cells per hand are grouped into 17 anatomical regions, namely a distal, middle, and proximal region for each non-thumb finger, three thumb segments, the palm, and the little-finger metacarpal. These regions define correspondences across robot tactile sensors with different numbers, placements, and scales of sensing elements. The predictor retains the same output layout across embodiments. Forward mapping for supervision. In simulation, the engine reports contact forces on the hand’s rigid bodies (Fig-ure 4a). We spread each force over the cells of its anatomical region with Gaussian weights that sum to one, conserving the total force, then divide by the cell sensing area to obtain pressure. On the real robot, which wears a dense tactile glove of 880 taxels per hand, each taxel is assigned to a region and the mean of the taxels falling in a cell gives that cell’s pressure target. For both robot domains, the resulting grid targets are normalized using a per-mask maximum and clipped to [0 , 1] .

IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate tactile prediction on human interactions, adap-tation to robot sensing layouts, and the utility of predicted touch for robot learning. EgoTouch [10] provides the human training data and seen- and unseen-object test sets containing 179 and 85 episodes, respectively. EgoTactile [11] provides a second human domain with an object-held-out test set of 55 episodes. Robot prediction uses held-out episodes separate from predictor fine-tuning. We report contact IoU (C.IoU), temporal contact accuracy (T.Acc), volumetric IoU (V.IoU), and normalized pressure MAE on sensor cells. C.IoU measures thresholded contact overlap, T.Acc measures agreement in frame-level contact presence, and V.IoU measures the ratio of summed pressure intersection to union. MAE is computed on the valid sensor cells. For robot prediction we additionally report link-level contact IoU and center-of-pressure error (Link C.IoU, Link CoP), obtained by pooling grid cells into the robot’s sensing links. We evaluate simulated control by task success rate. 

Simulated benchmarks. We study four contact-rich dex-terous tasks in NVIDIA Isaac Gym [43]. Pen Cap Removal is built on Bi-DexHands [44], while Lever Sliding, Bottle Cap Turning, and Bimanual Handover are adapted from the VTDexManip simulator [15]. All tasks use a simulated dex-terous hand with full-state proprioceptive observations, and every policy is trained with PPO for 2,500 to 12,000 iterations depending on the task. We run 200 parallel environments, reducing this for the predicted-tactile condition because it queries the predictor online at every step. 

Policy comparisons. The simulated comparison varies the tactile channel of the PPO observation. Base observations contain the benchmark’s robot state and task-required state. The matched GT and Pred conditions append 17 pressure values and 17 contact indicators per hand. In the real-robot study, N0-VTLA receives head- and wrist-camera images, robot state, a task instruction, and a tactile image stack. Its post-training datasets share the original observations and action targets, with measured, predicted, or zero-valued tactile inputs defining the comparison. 

B. Human Tactile Prediction 

Table I compares the human-trained predictor with our TouchAnything reproduction and a pose-conditioned variant. Ego2Contact achieves the highest contact overlap and tem-poral accuracy and the lowest pressure MAE on both object splits. Relative to TouchAnything, the gains are larger on seen objects, while performance on unseen objects remains ahead on these three metrics. Unseen-object V.IoU is slightly lower at 0.3417 versus 0.3457, despite the improvement in MAE. We examine the role of hand-local visual conditioning in the model analysis below. To test cross-dataset adaptation, we fine-tune both Ego2Contact (pose) and Ego2Contact on EgoTactile, each initialized from its human-trained checkpoint. Hand-local con-ditioning improves all four metrics on the object-held-out split (Table II), extending its advantage to a second tactile dataset. Figure 5 shows qualitative comparisons of ground-truth, TouchAnything, and Ego2Contact tactile maps on rep-resentative interactions. 

C. Model Analysis 

Table I also isolates the effect of the conditioning signal. Conditioning the generator on 3D hand pose raises contact overlap on seen objects but degrades it on unseen objects, where it falls below the TouchAnything reproduction. Re-placing pose with hand-local visual features recovers this loss and improves every metric on the unseen split, so the conditioning choice matters most when the object is new. The pose variant overfits to seen-object configurations, whereas hand-local appearance generalizes to new objects and drives the gains of our full model. 

D. Robot Adaptation and Simulated Control 

To adapt the predictor to the robot domain, we roll out ground-truth-tactile policies in the Isaac Gym benchmarks and keep successful episodes as paired RGB and rigid-body contact forces. The collected episode lengths differ widely across tasks, thus we select a balanced subset of fixed-length clips so that each task contributes a comparable number of frames rather than a fixed number of episodes. TouchAnything and Ego2Contact are fine-tuned on 8,741 clips (69,928 frames) from 2,258 episodes and evaluated on 949 clips from 249 held-out episodes, each starting from its human-trained checkpoint. 6

V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented Ego2Contact, a framework for learning visual-tactile prediction from human egocentric interactions and adapting it to robotic hands. Temporal scene context and hand-local visual features condition a flow-matching tactile generator, while anatomical region correspondences connect the predictor to heterogeneous robot sensing layouts. The pre-dictor outperforms regression and pose-conditioned baselines on human data and, after target-domain adaptation, on robot tactile prediction in simulation. Used as a virtual tactile sensor, it supplies online observations for simulated reinforcement learning and offline tactile annotations for real-robot policy post-training. In both settings, policies with predicted touch improve over non-tactile baselines and recover part of the benefit of ground-truth tactile sensing. Future work will ex-amine how much robot data adaptation requires and develop a more universal tactile representation and data format that connects diverse sensor layouts and embodiments without hand-designed per-robot correspondences.
