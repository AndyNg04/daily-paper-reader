# MIND: Instruction-Conditioned Incremental Grounding for Robots 

Mohamed Abouagour 1, Junhyun Nam 2, Wonse Jo 2, and Byung-Cheol Min 1

Abstract —Robots following persistent language instructions must decide which cached room judgments remain valid as their environment changes. We present MIND (Maintenance via INstruction-conditioned Dependencies), a framework for main-taining target-room decisions without re-evaluating every room after each event. A language model compiles each instruction into dependencies for semantic qualification and target selection. The controller intersects semantic dependencies with event footprints, refreshes affected judgments, and updates reachability and se-lection separately. For fixed deterministic evaluation, planning, and selection, conservative dependency and footprint coverage preserves the decisions of full recomputation. On 240 five-event ProcTHOR sequences with an exact benchmark predicate, MIND reduces mean semantic evaluations from 30 .6 to 11 .2

while preserving all 1,200 post-event decisions. Frozen-evaluator experiments with Phi-4 and Qwen3-14B also preserve all 1,200 

decisions, without improving the evaluators’ underlying task accuracy. In 32 closed-loop AI2-THOR episodes using exact-predicate qualification and metadata-derived footprints, MIND matches the final target decision of full recomputation in every episode. Unitree G1 experiments demonstrate target reuse and reselection across room transitions without additional semantic evaluations under MIND, and abstention after a barrier and an object removal at 4 evaluations against 15 to 18 . Additional materials are available at https://mind-icra27.github.io/. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A robot may continue following the same instruction while its environment changes. For “bring me the nearest mug,” 

entering another room, closing a doorway, or removing an object can alter the appropriate target in different ways. The robot has already evaluated which rooms satisfy the instruction, yet those cached judgments need not all become invalid together. Re-evaluating every room discards reusable computation; retaining every judgment can preserve a stale target. Each room judgment is a language-model call, which on a robot shares the GPU with perception and on a hosted model is billed, so the calls avoided are the resource at stake. We ask: after a world change, which room judgments must the robot check again? 

The event alone does not determine the required update. For the nearest chair , robot motion can change the selected room without changing which rooms contain chairs. For achair in the room I am in , the same motion changes which rooms qualify. The same variable thus enters qualification or selection depending on the instruction, and its stage decides whether semantic re-evaluation is needed. Dependency-based reuse is well established in incremental computation, where dependencies may be declared in program or query structure or discovered during execution [1–4]. In our setting, the state dependencies relevant to maintenance are implicit in a language instruction and differ between qualifi-cation and selection. MIND infers these instruction-specific dependencies from language, separately for qualification and selection, to determine which cached judgments require updat-ing. We therefore study decision-preserving maintenance of a given grounding pipeline, rather than replacing its semantic evaluator or replanning the entire task after every event. We present MIND (Maintenance via INstruction-conditioned Dependencies), a framework for instruction-conditioned incremental grounding . Before execution, alanguage model compiles the instruction into separate dependency sets for room qualification and target selection. After an event, the controller intersects the semantic dependencies with a footprint of reported state changes to identify which cached judgments to refresh. Reachability and deterministic selection are maintained separately, allowing a target to change without unnecessary semantic evaluation. Under the fixed-evaluator and conservative-coverage assumptions of Section IV, MIND preserves the decisions of full recomputation. Over-approximation may increase work, whereas missing coverage can leave stale judgments; the guarantee does not certify the compiler’s predictions or the evaluator’s accuracy. Grounded planning and dynamic revision. Language-conditioned robots ground instructions in affordances and scene graphs [5–7], revise plans from feedback [8–10], and abstain or relax infeasible goals [11–13]. Other systems main-tain contextual or task memory for replanning [14–17]; Event-Grounding Graph represents spatial structure and events [18], while LINGO-Space updates location distributions as referring expressions arrive [19]. These approaches motivate main-taining useful world knowledge, but stored information can become stale [20]. MIND addresses the validity of cached room-qualification judgments under a persistent instruction: which judgments a world change requires the controller to refresh, and when selection alone must be reconsidered. 

Incremental computation and embodied reuse. Self-adjusting computation, view maintenance, build systems, and truth maintenance reuse outputs through dependencies on changed inputs [1–4]. Dependencies may be declared in for-mal descriptions or tracked during execution. KEEP manages model-state reuse, whereas AgenticCache caches and validates planning transitions [21, 22]. MIND shares the dependency-based reuse principle but caches regional semantic verdicts rather than model states or plan transitions: instruction-conditioned signatures separate qualification inputs from se-lection inputs, regional footprints scope the invalidation, and the rule preserves a fixed pipeline’s decisions when both conservatively cover the required updates. 

Dependencies derived from language. LiP-LLM, DeCom-plex, and DART-LLM infer precedence among subtasks [23– 25]; incremental parsing repairs grammar or lexical knowledge after parsing or planning failures [26]. LG-TAP constructs temporally compact world models through backward observa-tion search and uses efficient graph updates to reuse ground-ing computations by selectively recomputing world-dependent factor features [27]. MIND applies dependency-based reuse to a different update unit: cached room-qualification ver-dicts supporting a persistent target decision. It infers separate qualification and selection dependencies and combines them with event footprints, yielding explicit conditions for decision-preserving regional updates under conservative estimates. V = {inventory , region label , position , history , topology }.

(1) We instantiate V with five state categories common to indoor grounding. Concretely, inventory is the multiset of object types present in a region, so counts matter and removing one of several chairs is a change, region label is its semantic name, position is the region the robot currently occupies rather than a metric pose, history is the set of regions already used to satisfy the instruction, and topology is the region-adjacency graph with per-doorway passability. History is therefore region-level: a request such as another chair asks for a chair in a region not yet used, not for a different chair instance within a used region, which the state does not represent. Grounding remains region-level and does not represent object coordinates or instance identities. In the graph benchmarks, nearest means fewest doorway hops in Gt; the closed-loop AI2-THOR experiment instead uses navigation-grid path length. The rule requires only a variable vocabulary shared by compiler, evaluator, and detector; further categories can be added without changing it. We assume their current values are available to the controller; perception is outside the present formulation. The six-family dependency and request benchmarks use automatically checked manifests frozen by hash before model evaluation. A dependency manifest is refused if a sequence has no verdict-changing event or abstains throughout, a history sequence is not moved by its use event, a move or door event changes a verdict for a nearest request, or no event exposes the inventory-only rule; a request manifest is refused if families are unbalanced or share a signature, a minimal pair adds an edge other than the one claimed, a held-out template leaks into development, or a signature disagrees with the event instrument. These are consistency checks, not independent cer-tification of natural-language semantics. Generators, prompts, and model configurations are in the repository 1.

Dependency benchmark. The primary benchmark contains 

240 five-event sequences over houses from the ProcTHOR [28] 

test split, using 11 distinct instructions across six request fam-ilies. Inventory-only reads inventory only; named-region adds a semantic region label; current-region adds robot position to qualification; history-dependent adds the set of previously used regions; nearest-candidate places position and topology in selection; and nearest+history combines the last two. Events shut doorways, empty or relocate objects, move the robot, and consume a referent. After each event, the manifest records the reference target and the regional verdicts that actually change value. The latter define invalidation recall and precision, dis-tinguishing missed changed verdicts from conservative extra refreshes. The 240 sequences span 57 houses, with 55 , 9, 27 ,

54 , 40 , and 55 sequences for the six families in the order listed. The 11 instructions are held-out-template phrasings from the request benchmark and are disjoint from the six compiler examples; two also appear among the minimal pairs.

Unseen-placement benchmark (Adjacent). A seventh family tests a dependency placement absent from all six compiler demonstrations. The benchmark interprets a chair in a room next to the one I am in as a chair in a room connected to the robot’s current room by one currently passable doorway, so closing that doorway changes qualification although the rooms remain physically adjacent. The semantic dependencies are inventory, position, and topology. The judge receives passability as a state field; the compiler sees only the phrasing and the variable definitions, in which topology is reachability given doors. The benchmark contains 80 sequences in 80 

houses, six held-out phrasings, three of which name a door or connection explicitly, and all five event types. 

Backbones. We evaluate Phi-4, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B (microsoft/phi-4, Qwen/Qwen3-8B, Qwen/Qwen3-14B) as lo-cally deployable models through HuggingFace Transformers, and gpt-oss-120b through a hosted OpenAI-compatible end-point. Decoding is greedy (temperature 0 at the endpoint), Qwen3 thinking mode is disabled, compiler outputs are capped at 512 new tokens, and judge verdicts at 8. Local weights load in bfloat16 on one L40S GPU (Transformers 5.8, PyTorch 2.10), and judge prompts run in chunks of four per forward pass. The fallible-evaluator experiments use frozen Phi-4 and Qwen3-14B verdicts; the timing study uses Phi-4. 

Policies. Within each evaluator-interface condition, policies share the semantic evaluator, planner, deterministic selector, and composition rule (3). They differ in the maintenance schedule, including semantic refreshes and reselection triggers. Table I compares full recomputation, a static inventory-only signature, a universal semantic signature, reference variables with and without stage placement, and the compiled signa-ture. Reference signatures are oracle controls, not inferred baselines. The exact predicate, the affordance sets it scores, and the selector are benchmark components fixed per family: the selector returns the fewest-hop feasible region for nearest-candidate requests, breaking ties on the display name, and the alphabetically first feasible region otherwise. Every policy reruns this selector after every event, so the inventory-only row fails only through stale semantic verdicts and never through suppressed reselection. Maintenance policies receive the compiled signature and the footprint, not the family label. 

Semantic evaluators and prompt interfaces. An ex-act benchmark predicate isolates maintenance errors from semantic-model errors. Fallible-judge experiments use a frozen verdict tape : each distinct regional question is evaluated once per interface, and the resulting verdict is replayed to every policy; an unparsable generation aborts the run. E. Physical-robot demonstration 

Fig. 4 shows MIND on a Unitree G1 humanoid in a four-zone laboratory: ROS 2 with lidar localization on a frozen map, Nav2 planning, a YOLO-World detector, and a 4-bit on-board judge (Qwen3-8B in scenarios 1 and 2, Phi-4 in 3 and 4). Signatures came from the deployed Phi-4 compiler, declared to the controller. Provided: zone polygons, candidate regions, and a scoring-only ground-truth inventory; observed: the robot’s region, planner reachability, and the detector’s inventory. Four scenarios were run, 26 scored runs and 8 excluded with reasons. (1) Something to sit on , chairs in A and C, a backpack in the start room D: three evaluations selected A, and the walk through C, a transient loss and recovery of the route to A, and the arrival re-judged no room. MIND used 3 semantic evaluations and approximately 0.33 s of judge time per run over five runs, compared with 9 evaluations and approximately 

1.0 s for full recomputation over three. (2) The nearest thing to sit on , same layout and move: the same move re-ranked the target from C to A without additional semantic evaluation under MIND, 3 against 9; in both scenarios all scored runs reached the expected target. (3) A chair in the room I am in , chairs in all three rooms: position is semantic, so every move re-judged every room under both policies and nothing was saved, as (6) predicts for a global refresh. The judge read the inventory line but not the position line, so every decision in the six runs inherited the same error while navigation and perception passed; the two policies agreed on every decision, as a fixed evaluator requires. (4) Something to sit on , then a barrier closing the route to A and the chair removed from C: the barrier refreshed reachability only and the removal re-judged one room, 4 evaluations against 15 to 18 , and both policies abstained; one floor span was declared as wall so the barrier closed the only route. Evaluation counts per event were identical across repetitions; one full-recomputation run was excluded for a detector miss, a perception failure scored apart from the decision. The evaluations separate savings from spatial scope, request-specific variables, and stage placement, and expose the con-sequences of missed dependencies and footprints. The controlled benchmarks assume populated semantic maps and a restricted state vocabulary; dependency labels are not independently annotated. Metadata-derived closed-loop updates and known-removal image localization test different components. Frozen verdict tapes isolate maintenance fidelity, not task correctness or online determinism.
