ABSTRACT 

Proactive agents can support group collaboration by surfacing useful information without waiting to be asked, but even relevant contributions can be disruptive if poorly timed. In this work, we isolate the timing question: given a fixed candidate contribution such as a clarification or relevant fact, when should an AI agent chime in? We introduce P ITCH (Proactive Intervention Timing in CH at) 1, to our knowl-edge the first benchmark with large-scale human judgments of proactive agent timing collected from participants in the same unscripted multi-party conversa-tions they took part in. To collect high-quality judgments, we design a novel in-terface in which 2-4 people chat synchronously, author candidate AI contribution messages, place their own and others’ contributions into the chat history, and then deliberate as a group about the best placement for each message. P ITCH contains 384 candidate contributions and 1,091 judgments, each marking an ideal turn, an acceptable window, or that a contribution should not appear, from 89 participants engaged in 35 conversations. Evaluating 15 models across 28 configurations, we find that while models can place contributions well when given the entire conver-sation history, they systematically chime in too early when asked to decide live as the conversation unfolds : best-turn accuracy falls from 57.8% to 19.0%, with nearly half of placements occurring before every participant’s acceptable window. In comparison, lay humans deciding live are more accurate (23.7% vs 19.0%), and fewer of their placements are premature (39.7% vs 46.4%). Model reasoning traces show that 93% of intervention justifications cite relevance, while almost none consider waiting. By providing a human-grounded benchmark, our work reveals gaps in agents’ ability to integrate into group settings and points towards proactive agents that can reason explicitly about appropriate timing. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Achieving proactivity in AI agents requires both having useful information to contribute and know-ing when to chime in with it. Agents are increasingly capable of providing contextualized factual information such as clarifications, examples, or relevant facts that can benefit users Wang et al. (2025), but less attention has focused on timing. Getting the timing right has particularly high stakes in group settings where an agent interjection can negatively disrupt the flow of conversation (Yeo et al., 2026). For instance, in a group chat about travel planning, an agent might have useful informa-tion about hotel pricing—but bringing it up after the group has settled on a hotel would be too late, while introducing it before they begin discussing accommodations might distract from the current topic. Appropriate timing therefore involves weighing the benefit of contributing now against the possibility that waiting would produce a better moment (Horvitz, 1999). Evaluating how well agents perform on timing requires knowing when the participants in a conver-sation would have actually welcomed a particular contribution (e.g., a message containing useful clarifications). For each contribution, the interface presents the message alongside the transcript and asks each group mem-ber to identify an ideal turn and an acceptable window, or indicate that the contribution should not appear at all. However, when models must commit without knowing what follows (the (A) turn-by-turn replay 

setup), the prediction accuracy for ideal turns falls from 57.8% to 19.0%, and 46.4% of predic-tions occur before any annotator’s acceptable window opens, compared to 1.0% after every window closes. We find that while non-participant lay annotators also find the online task challenging, they still perform better than models (23.7% vs 19.0%) and are less likely to make premature placements (39.7% vs 46.4%). Collectively, these results show that recognizing relevance is insufficient for well-timed proactive assistance, and that evaluating models with full conversational hindsight can substantially overstate their ability to intervene appropriately in live collaboration. We contribute (i) PITCH , a participant-grounded benchmark of proactive intervention timings 

containing human-authored contributions and large-scale timing preferences from participants in unscripted multi-party conversations (Section 2.4); (ii) an interactive collection protocol that elicits ideal placements, acceptable alternatives, and persistent differences in participant preferences for fixed contributions (Section 2); (iii) a formulation and set of evaluation measures for participant-aligned timing that preserve both individual preferences and shared acceptable regions (Sections 2.2 and 3); and (iv) empirical findings on model timing alignment , showing a substantial gap between post hoc and online intervention decisions and models’ tendency to treat relevance as sufficient reason to speak even when the timing is not appropriate (Sections 4 and 5). 

2 EVALUATING PROACTIVE AGENT TIMING WITH HUMAN CONVERSATIONS AND PARTICIPANT -G ROUNDED TIMING JUDGMENTS 

PITCH evaluates whether models contribute messages containing helpful factual knowledge at times preferred by the actual conversation participants . Because these preferences are participant-specific and cannot be reliably inferred from transcripts or simulations, we designed an interactive protocol that jointly collects real conversations and participants’ retrospective timing preferences. 2.1 FOUR PHASES OF THE INTERACTIVE DATA COLLECTION PROTOCOL 

Our data collection protocol captures two key pieces of information unavailable from conversation transcripts alone: what information an AI could have proactively contributed and when participants would have wanted to receive it . Participants first take part in a real group discussion, then author candidate AI contribution messages containing factual information that could have proactively sup-ported the group, such as clarifying a concept, providing evidence, or offering relevant logistical details. Using real discussions also preserves conversational dynamics that synthetic interactions may smooth away (Zhou et al., 2024; Bian et al., 2025; Kamoi et al., 2026). Concretely, the data collection protocol consists of four phases (shown in Figure 1): • Phase 1: Conversation. Groups hold real-time, unscripted typed conversations in two settings: 

open research discussion , where research literature and scientific evidence support expert infor-mation needs, and collaborative planning of trips or events, where prices, logistics, and options support everyday information needs. See example topics in Appendix A. • Phase 2: Message contribution. Participants review the conversation and author factual mes-sages that could have helped the group, with optional search and AI support. • Phase 3: Timing judgments. Group members independently judge others’ contributions, select-ing an ideal placement, an acceptable timing window, or that the message should not appear. 3Preprint • Phase 4: Deliberation. Participants view peers’ judgments and may revise their own, letting us measure whether timing preferences persist after reconsideration without requiring consensus. This design offers two benefits. First, participant-authored contributions reflect information needs that arose in the actual discussion, making them more likely to be genuinely useful. Second, it avoids restricting the benchmark to information that current models can already identify, retrieve, synthesize, and contextualize from an ongoing group conversation. 2.2 TASK DEFINITION : P ROACTIVE AGENT CONTRIBUTION TIMING 

We define a contribution as a message that introduces factual information intended to help the conversation, such as by clarifying a concept, providing evidence or an example, or offering relevant logistical information. In a deployed proactive-agent setting, this message would be generated by the AI agent. Given a fixed contribution, P ITCH evaluates when, if ever, it should be introduced .Fixing the message isolates timing from upstream challenges such as identifying information needs, retrieving relevant information, and generating the contribution itself. Formally, a conversation C = ( u1, . . . , u T ) is a sequence of turns from an unscripted multi-party human discussion, and m is a participant-authored candidate contribution. The model chooses when, if ever, to insert m into this fixed trace. For each contribution, participant i provides a timing judgment consisting of an ideal turn ti, the best turn after which to insert the message, and an acceptable window [si, e i], spanning the earliest point at which the message becomes relevant to the latest point at which it remains useful. Partici-pants may instead select do-not-surface if they believe the message should not appear anywhere in the conversation. Candidate contributions were reviewed for coherence and factual plausibility. 2.3 PARTICIPANT RECRUITMENT AND STUDY PROCEDURES 

Participant recruitment. We recruited 89 participants through social media, personal networks, and online and physical advertisements at several large research universities. Participants received a $35 gift card for a 90-minute session. They reported topic interests in open research discussion and 

collaborative planning in advance and were grouped by shared preferences. 

Study procedure. Participants first held a 15–25 minute typed conversation in groups of 2–4 us-ing our study platform (Appendix C). Afterward, they reviewed the transcript and authored factual messages they believed could have helped the group, with optional search and AI drafting support. Participants were instructed to write messages that an ideal participant or agent could plausibly have contributed during the conversation. Each contribution was then evaluated by all group members, who independently selected an ideal turn and acceptable timing window or marked it do-not-surface .This yielded 1,091 timing judgments: 384 from message authors and 707 from their 1–3 peers. Fi-nally, participants could revisit their judgments after seeing peers’ placements and optionally revise them. Of the preferences revisited during peer deliberation, 85% retain their original ideal turn. 2.4 PITCH BENCHMARK COMPOSITION

To measure how well model preferences align with those of human participants from the conversa-tion group, we define the following metrics and task setups. 

Ground truth of human preferences. For a candidate message judged by N participants, each annotator either names a placement (ti, [si, e i]) or marks do-not-surface (DNS). Let P be the partic-ipants who place the message. The ground truth is the pair B = { ti : i ∈ P } and W = S 

> i∈P

[si, e i].where B is the set of ideal turns they named, and W is the set of turns inside any of their acceptable windows. We define the ground truth as sets of turns instead of a single point in the conversation because of the inherent ambiguity of timing judgment. For a given message, appropriate turn-taking timing could vary with participants’ knowledge, goals, and interpretation of the discussion Cox et al. (2025); Templeton et al. (2023), so reducing these preferences to one turn would discard valid timing preferences. 

Task setups. We evaluated 15 models from five providers, using standard and, where supported, maximum-reasoning configurations, for 28 configurations in total. Each configuration was evaluated across three task setups that vary in how much of the conversation is revealed before the model must commit, and consequently in how closely they reflect realistic deployment settings. 

(A) Turn-by-turn replay (replay ): This is the most realistic deployment setting. The conversa-tion is revealed one turn at a time, and the model decides at each turn whether to intervene. Once it answers “yes,” that turn becomes its final placement and cannot be revised. 

(B) Replay with five-turn lookahead (delayed replay ): This is a less constrained replay setting in which the model can see the next five turns at each yes-or-no decision. 

(C) Selection from the full transcript (pick ): This serves as an upper bound rather than a real-istic deployment scenario. The agent sees the entire conversation and selects one turn. 

Metrics. We quantify performance across task setups using metrics defined against human timing preferences.

An annotator’s chosen turn falls within others’ acceptable windows 76.1% of the time, but exactly matches their ideal turn only 40.6%. 

Placements are spread through the conversation. Participants’ placements of candidate messages were evenly spanned across the conversation. Across 1,047 placements, the mean relative position is 0.493, and the median is 0.486, with the middle half between 0.27 and 0.73. This broad distribution indicates that there is no universally good time to place a candidate message. This distribution also helps explain some annotator disagreement. A message can both reasonably reopen a thread the group dropped or support a claim after the group returns to the topic. We therefore treat disagreement in annotation data as a signal about the range of recipient preferences and define the model’s target as a set of acceptable placements, rather than forcing the model to match a single averaged placement that may reflect no annotator’s judgment.

6 DISCUSSION , L IMITATIONS , AND FUTURE WORK 

The same proactive message can be appropriate at multiple distinct points in a conversation. 

Half the messages in P ITCH have ideal placements more than two turns apart even after deliberation, and some cluster around multiple distinct points in a conversation. For example, a snack suggestion was placed when food was first raised and again when the topic returned much later. This motivates metrics and ground truth that preserve multiple valid timings rather than force a single gold turn. We found that participants tend to agree when a message has a clear conversational anchor, such as when a participant directly asks about the cost of a bus fare. They are more likely to disagree when the same message could be useful at multiple points in the conversation. For example, a comment about medical evaluation could either revive an earlier thread or support a related claim made twenty turns later. These cases suggest that some disagreements that remain after deliberation reflect different valid uses of the same message, rather than disagreement about the facts. 

What does the retrospective reference measure? The reference in P ITCH is retrospective: par-ticipants see the full conversation and may revise placements after peers’ choices, whereas replay models observe only prior turns. Alignment therefore measures how well models anticipate partici-pants’ timing preferences, rather than how they compare with humans making the same online deci-sion. These retrospective preferences still provide a meaningful target for when recipients consider 8Preprint an intervention appropriate, but they cannot establish whether a live intervention would improve the discussion, since it may alter what follows. Our data also captures preferences for only a single proactive interjection, and thus cannot model how prior interventions or intervention frequency shape later timing preferences. Future work could study these sequential dynamics through richer human data collection or simulation-based bench-marks as models become better at reproducing realistic group conversations. Under replay , model placements receive 34.5% endorsement versus 76.1% for participants’ retro-spective placements. Together with frequent premature interventions, this gap motivates policies that explicitly weigh acting now against waiting. Prompt ablations and an online human baseline could further separate model-specific limitations from decision framing and task difficulty. Simi-lar challenges arise in proactive coaching, where whether, when, and how to intervene are distinct decisions Srinivas et al. (2025). 

Current recruitment limitations and opportunities for broader participation. Our current par-ticipant pool reflects the practical demands of synchronous group sessions, with many participants recruited from university communities and through word of mouth. Future work could broaden the demographic range and examine how timing preferences relate to participants’ roles and percep-tions within a conversation, such as topic expertise, relative influence, or personality, as well as whether models align differently with different participants. The research discussions also center largely on AI, CS, HCI, and related computational fields; extending the protocol to natural sciences, humanities, and other domains could reveal additional conversational dynamics and timing patterns. 

Human baselines and the potential for “superhuman” proactive agents. The considerable gap between retrospective participant judgments and the layperson online baseline suggests opportu-nities to go beyond matching online human decisions. With richer participant context and better models of how the conversation may unfold, an agent could potentially anticipate preferred in-tervention points more accurately than a layperson deciding in real time. Another direction is to explicitly trade off placement quality against latency by allowing the agent to wait briefly before intervening. In threaded text chat, interface design allows for this tradeoff by enabling an agent to insert a contribution into the recent conversational history, making slightly delayed but better-placed interventions feasible. Indeed, our delayed replay task setup probes this possibility, showing some limited improvements in placement accuracy with up to a 5-turn delay in timing.

Proactive AI agents. Recent proactive agents increasingly model timing explicitly, often with a gate that decides whether the agent should speak (Mao et al., 2024; Eckhaus et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026). prediction (Patel et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Nama et al., 2026), but typically designate a single correct intervention point, often in synthetic or semi-synthetic conversations. In contrast, P ITCH 

uses unscripted human conversations and timing judgments from the actual participants, allowing multiple acceptable placements and treating persistent disagreement as meaningful variation (Aroyo & Welty, 2015; Plank, 2022; Abdulhai et al., 2026). 

Human–AI collaborative agents. A parallel body of work studies AI agents in human conver-sations and collaborative group settings (Do et al., 2022; Houde et al., 2025; Yeo et al., 2026). In groups, proactive timing is especially consequential because an intervention enters a shared space and draws multiple participants’ attention (Yeo et al., 2026). Prior work often evaluates downstream outcomes or predefined intervention policies rather than whether timing aligns with participants’ preferences. P ITCH complements this literature by isolating the timing of a fixed contribution and evaluating it against participant-grounded judgments. 9Preprint 

8 CONCLUSION 

We introduce P ITCH , a benchmark for evaluating when proactive agents should intervene in multi-party conversations. By jointly collecting unscripted human conversations, participant-authored messages, and participants’ timing judgments, P ITCH provides a human-grounded testbed for study-ing proactive timing. Our results show that current models often intervene too early and that appro-priate timing can vary across participants and conversational contexts. We hope P ITCH enables future work on agents that better understand not only what to contribute, but when to contribute it. 10 Preprint ETHICS STATEMENT

We developed a web-based data collection platform to collect conversations while supporting both candidate knowledge message creation and cross-annotation. It hosts the full study workflow. 

Conversation and contribution. Participants first use the platform to engage in a synchronous group conversation. After the conversation, they review the transcript and create candidate knowl-edge items they believe could have been useful during the discussion. The message contribution interface (Figure 6) requires participants to identify where in the conversation the item would be relevant, including ideal turn and a range of acceptable turns. Integrated tools help with resource search and retrieval as well as message authoring. 

Figure 6: Message contribution interface. The full transcript sits in a scrollable pane on the left and the candidate message and placement controls on the right. 

Timing judgments. Post-construction, participants play the role of participants, reviewing the neutralized (Appendix G.3) contributed messages created by each other. Deliberation. A subsequent deliberation stage presents each item from the set of created knowl-edge messages with every participant’s placement, allowing participants to review and revise their own annotations (Figure 8). Researchers manage study group formation, review knowledge items, configure annotation assign-ments, and export the resulting data through an admin interface. D HUMAN BASELINE FOR INTERVENTION TIMING 

To contextualize model performance, we conducted a human baseline study measuring how lay users perform the same replay intervention-timing task. The goal was to determine whether models’ difficulty in selecting an appropriate intervention point and tendency to speak early reflects the difficulty of the task or a gap between model behavior and human preferences. D.1 STUDY DESIGN 

Participants were recruited through Prolific and completed the replay task (Section 3) on a web-based annotation platform. We collected data across both scholar and trip conversation scenarios which allows us to measure human baseline performance across the full range of contexts used in model evaluation. Our ground truth is derived from the data collected for PITCH. For each candidate contribution, we considered two measures: ideal turn (the set of turns selected as ideal intervention points in our ground truth) and union windows (span of earliest to latest acceptable intervention points across participants). These measures allow direct comparison with model performance. D.2 ANNOTATION PLATFORM AND PROTOCOL 

The annotation interface presented participants with a conversation transcript alongside three corre-sponding candidate contributions, de-anonymized and pulled from the PITCH dataset. The interface simulates a natural flow of conversation by allowing participants to progress turn by turn, deciding at each point whether any of the three candidate contributions should be sent. This reflects the replay task we used for model evaluation. 17 Preprint 

Figure 8: Deliberation interface. Users are presented with the conversation transcript once again, alongside timing judgments made on a contribution message by all other participants. The right pane allows users to optionally revise and update their judgments. At each turn in the conversation, participants could see: • The conversation history up to that point • The three candidate contributions • A “Send” button for each contribution that had not yet been placed • A “Continue” button to advance to the next turn without placing any contribution If participants reached the end of the conversation with contributions still unplaced, they were given the option to mark those remaining contributions as “not relevant”–indicating the message did not fit anywhere in the conversation. Each session consisted of placing three candidate contributions within a single conversation and participants could complete multiple sessions across different conversations. Participants received a base compensation of $1.00 USD, with up to an additional $2.00 bonus for placing contributions within the ground truth union windows. This incentive structure encouraged accuracy while acknowledging that reasonable people may disagree on exact timing. D.3 PARTICIPANTS AND QUALITY CONTROL
