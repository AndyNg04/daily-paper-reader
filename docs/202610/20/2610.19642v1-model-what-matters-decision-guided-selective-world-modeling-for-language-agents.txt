Abstract 

Reinforcement learning provides a direct mech-anism for optimizing task objectives in large language model-driven agents, but task rewards are typically sparse trajectory-level signals that only evaluate task success, without directly su-pervising action-conditioned environment tran-sitions, leaving the model with limited ex-plicit understanding of action consequences and environment dynamics. Recent work in-troduces auxiliary supervision through policy and world modeling co-training, yet typically treats complete environmental observations uni-formly as world modeling targets, overlooking the varying decision relevance of observation content. We propose DGS-WM (Decision-

Guided Selective World Modeling), a decision-guided policy-world modeling co-training ap-proach for language agents. DGS-WM em-ploys a Decision-Guided Observation Selec-tion component that filters observation units with decision value as world modeling targets based on their support for subsequent policy responses, and a Confidence-Adaptive Weight-ing component that adaptively adjusts supervi-sion strength according to the model’s predic-tion confidence on selected targets. Together, these components form a decision-aware aux-iliary world modeling loss, jointly optimized with the RL loss on the same policy model. Across multiple benchmarks covering single-hop/multi-hop question answering and inter-active web tasks, DGS-WM achieves consis-tent improvements under different model scales and RL algorithms, demonstrating more stable and effective performance compared to full-observation world modeling methods. 

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM)-driven agents can complete tasks such as retrieval, question answer-ing, and web operations through multi-turn inter-actions with environments. Reinforcement learn-ing (RL) provides a direct training mechanism for optimizing task objectives for such agents, en-abling models to progressively improve action se-lection and task execution capabilities based on environmental observations and task rewards. In recent years, PPO, GRPO, and related methods have been widely applied to LLM reasoning opti-mization and agent training, achieving significant progress (Schulman et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; DeepSeek-AI, 2025). Multi-turn tasks such as retrieval, question an-swering, and web operations typically exhibit long-horizon dependencies: current actions change en-vironment states, and state changes determine sub-sequent available environmental observations and feasible actions (Feng et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025). To make effective decisions continuously in such tasks, policies need to form usable representations of action consequences and environment dynamics. RL primarily optimizes policy behavior through task rewards; trajectory-level or outcome-level re-wards typically only evaluate task success and do not directly supervise action-conditioned environ-ment transitions (Jin et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025). Therefore, models can learn which behaviors are more likely to obtain high rewards, but may not explicitly learn what environmental observations actions will produce and how these environmen-tal observations affect subsequent interactions; the action-environmental observation relationships al-ready recorded in rollouts are also not fully con-verted into independent training signals. World modeling methods provide explicit envi-ronment dynamics supervision for policies by learn-ing subsequent states or textualized environmen-tal observations conditioned on actions, thereby supplementing training signals that rely solely on task rewards. Recent research further combines this learning objective with on-policy RL: ECHO optimizes the policy objective while using environ-mental observations produced by actions in roll-outs for auxiliary prediction; PaW further filters 1arXiv:2610.19642v1 [cs.AI] 14 Oct 2026 transitions to be included in world modeling train-ing on this basis. For transitions included in joint training, both typically use their complete next ob-servation as the world modeling target (Figure 1 left), enabling the policy model to learn environ-ment dynamics during interactions beyond task re-wards (Shrivastava et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). This raises the question we focus on in this paper: within a single environmental observation, should the complete observation be treated by default as a unified world modeling target? Our core insight is that environmental observa-tions do not inherently possess equal supervision value. An environmental observation may simulta-neously contain key information that can influence future decisions or content unrelated to the current task; if the complete environmental observation is uniformly used as a supervision target, the model must bear the same auxiliary prediction pressure for this content. Therefore, we argue that only observation content valuable for future decisions should be retained as world modeling supervision targets. Based on this insight, we propose DGS-WM (Decision-Guided Selective World Modeling), a decision-guided policy-world modeling co-training method for text agents (Figure 1 right). DGS-WM first filters content with decision value from envi-ronmental observations through Decision-Guided Observation Selection and constructs them as world modeling targets; subsequently through Confidence-Adaptive Weighting, it adjusts aux-iliary supervision strength based on the model’s current prediction confidence for retained targets. Based on selected prediction targets, DGS-WM constructs world modeling objectives to jointly train the policy model together with RL objectives. 2.1 Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents 

Reinforcement learning has become an important paradigm for optimizing LLM policies. Existing methods first revolve around policy updates and advantage estimation: PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) constrains on-policy updates with clipped surrogate objectives, REINFORCE-style methods (Williams, 1992) and RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024) reduce reliance on explicit value models through sequence-level returns and leave-one-out baselines, while GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) performs group-based op-timization using relative returns from multiple sam-ples under the same input. Subsequently, extensive work has improved the training efficiency and sta-bility of LLM RL from aspects such as clipped ob-jectives, sampling strategies, loss aggregation, and importance weights, such as DAPO (Meng et al., 2024) and GMPO (Zhao et al., 2025). For long-sequence and multi-turn agents, research further focuses on how to perform finer-grained credit as-signment using sparse outcome rewards, including GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) based on episode/state grouping, SPO (Li et al., 2024) based on segment-level advantage, and Tree-GRPO (Li et al., 2025) that constructs intermediate process signals using structured rollouts. Compared to outcome-level re-wards, process supervision provides finer-grained training signals for intermediate steps, but typically involves additional annotation, estimation, or data construction costs (Lightman et al., 2023). The method proposed in this paper provides auxiliary supervision for RL training through world model-ing, requiring no additional annotations and can be combined with different on-policy RL algorithms (such as GRPO). 

2.2 World Modeling for LLM Agents 

World modeling methods for language agents mainly revolve around learning action-conditioned environment transitions, i.e., predicting subsequent states or textualized environmental observations based on interaction history and actions. Early work typically places the learned world model out-side the policy optimization process, serving as an environment simulator or planning module for gen-erating imagined rollouts, performing search, or conducting lookahead planning (Ha and Schmid-huber, 2018; Hao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2026). Building on this, the WM-to-RL staged paradigm first uses interaction trajectories to learn environ-ment dynamics, future state prediction, or other environment knowledge, then uses the obtained world model checkpoint or representations for subsequent policy optimization; Early Experience (Zhang et al., 2025) and RWML (Yu et al., 2026) represent the reward-free world model warm-up and the approach of first performing world model learning then task-success RL, respectively. More recent work places world model learning and on-policy policy optimization in the same training pro-cess, forming a policy-world modeling co-training paradigm (Shrivastava et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). Under this paradigm, environmental feedback in-cluded in WM training typically directly uses the complete next observation as the world model-ing target, without further distinguishing content within the feedback, making all content jointly bear auxiliary supervision. DGS-WM continues this co-training paradigm but consciously selects world modeling targets, retaining only feedback content valuable for future decisions for world modeling supervision.

3.2 Decision-Guided Observation Selection 

Complete environmental observations typically mix decision-relevant information with task-irrelevant content. Full-observation world mod-eling (Eq. 2) treats all content uniformly as world modeling targets, forcing the model to exert equal prediction effort on both noise and useful infor-mation. This not only wastes model capacity but may also interfere with the policy’s learning of critical information. Our core thesis is: only envi-ronmental observations that hold value for fu-ture decisions merit serving as world modeling targets . To this end, Decision-Guided Observa-tion Selection uses subsequent policy responses to select supervisory content from observations. We first partition environmental observation ot+1 

into a set of candidate units. The partitioning fol-lows a “minimal semantically complete unit” prin-ciple: for retrieval document feedback, we split at sentence boundaries to obtain single-sentence candidates; for structured webpage observations, we identify semantically complete UI blocks (e.g., product cards, core purchase information, interac-tion feedback) as candidate units. 3.3 Confidence-Adaptive Weighting 

Target selection determines what feedback con-tent to learn. To avoid continued equal-strength auxiliary supervision on already-mastered targets, Confidence-Adaptive Weighting adjusts supervi-sion strength for each target based on the model’s current prediction confidence. Using the target average negative log-likelihood 

ℓt defined in the previous section, we compute pre-diction confidence: 

qt = exp ( −ℓt) . (9) 

qt equals the geometric mean of target content to-ken conditional probabilities, reflecting the model’s mastery of predicting the entire target. We accord-ingly compute the adaptive weight: 

wt = stopgrad [(1 − qt)γ ] . (10) where γ controls confidence weighting decay strength. stopgrad indicates the weight does not participate in gradient backpropagation; gradients flow through the target prediction loss to update the model. Each concatenated world modeling target cor-responds to one qt and one wt; all content tokens of that target share this weight. High-confidence targets receive smaller supervision weights, while lower-confidence targets retain stronger learning signal, thereby adaptively adjusting auxiliary super-vision pressure according to the model’s mastery of the target.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We evaluate on three task categories: (i) Multi-hop QA : HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022), Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023); (ii) Single-hop QA : Natural Questions (NQ) (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023); (iii) Interac-tive Web Shopping : WebShop (Yao et al., 2022). 

Baselines. We compare three method categories: (i) Prompting methods : Direct Inference and Re-Act (Yao et al., 2023); (ii) RL-only methods : Pri-mary experiments use GRPO (Shao et al., 2024); cross-algorithm experiments additionally use PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), RLOO (Ahmadian et al., 2024), and GMPO (Zhao et al., 2025); (iii) RL-augmented world modeling methods : ECHO (Shrivastava et al., 2026) and our proposed DGS-WM, which augment the RL algorithms above with world modeling. ECHO uses complete en-vironmental observations as world modeling tar-gets, serving as a full-observation baseline against DGS-WM’s decision-value-guided observation se-lection. Cross-model and cross-algorithm results compare each RL algorithm alone with its ECHO-and DGS-WM-augmented variants. 

Metrics. Single-hop and multi-hop QA use Ex-act Match (EM, %). Unless otherwise speci-fied, single-hop results are macro-averaged over NQ, TriviaQA, and PopQA; multi-hop results are macro-averaged over HotpotQA, 2WikiMul-tiHopQA, MuSiQue, and Bamboogle. In Table 1, Trivia, 2Wiki, MusiQ, and Bamb abbreviate Triv-iaQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, MuSiQue, and Bam-boogle, respectively. WebShop reports Success rate (%) and Score (0–100 scale). 

Implementation Details. Primary table exper-iments use Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B (Team, 2025); component ablation uses Qwen3-1.7B. Pol-icy models are fine-tuned via LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) with rank 32. For QA tasks, we use the Wiki18 corpus with E5-base-v2 (Wang et al., 2022) embeddings and FAISS (Johnson et al., 2017) for retrieval. The selection standard deviation coeffi-cient κ and confidence decay exponent γ are set to 0.5 and 1.0 respectively; following ECHO (Shri-vastava et al., 2026), we set the world modeling auxiliary loss coefficient λWM = 0 .02 . 6 Limitations 

Despite its gains across benchmarks, DGS-WM still has two limitations. First, observation units depend on environment structure: QA uses retrieval-feedback sentences, while WebShop uses page structure. How to iden-tify meaningful units in complex or unstructured feedback remains open; adaptive segmentation based on semantic boundaries, information entropy, or attention is a promising direction. Second, the response support score estimates an observation’s decision value from the policy’s av-erage token log-likelihood of the true subsequent response. It assumes that greater consistency with the true response implies greater decision support, but this is correlation, not causation. A Implementation Details 

Our experiments cover two types of tasks: Open-Domain Question Answering and Interactive Web Shopping. 

A.1 Datasets A.1.1 Open-Domain QA 

Open-domain QA requires agents to answer ques-tions by interacting with an external retrieval sys-tem without pre-given documents. We train on a mixed NQ + HotpotQA dataset and evaluate on multiple benchmarks. 

Training Data. We mix the following datasets for training: • Natural Questions (NQ) (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019): 79,168 training samples and 8,757 valida-tion samples from real Google search queries • HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018): 90,447 training samples and 7,405 validation samples requiring cross-document reasoning From the mixed dataset (169,615 training sam-ples in total), we perform stratified sampling to obtain 27,648 samples for RL training and 1,024 samples for validation. The sampling strategy uses two-level stratification: (1) at the dataset level, NQ and HotpotQA are sampled with weights 1:4; (2) at the category level, within each dataset, samples are drawn proportionally to question categories (single-hop questions for NQ, type and level for HotpotQA). This ensures that the sampled dataset maintains the same distribution of hop counts and question types as the source dataset. 

Test Datasets. 

• Natural Questions (NQ) : 3,610 questions evalu-ating single-hop fact retrieval • TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017): 11,313 questions on trivia knowledge • PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023): 14,267 questions focusing on popular culture entities • HotpotQA : 7,405 questions requiring multi-hop reasoning • 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020): 12,576 questions requiring comparison of two entities • MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022): 2,417 questions designed to avoid shortcut reasoning • Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023): 125 manually constructed difficult questions 

Task Setup. Each question allows the agent at most 5 turns to complete the task. In each turn, the agent can execute search[query] (retrieve rel-evant documents) or finish[answer] (submit the final answer). The retrieval system is based on the Wiki18 corpus (a 2018 snapshot of approxi-mately 5 million Wikipedia articles), using the E5-base-v2 encoder (Wang et al., 2022) and FAISS index (Johnson et al., 2017) for dense retrieval, re-turning the top-3 documents per search. 

A.1.2 Interactive Web Shopping (WebShop) WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) simulates a realistic e-commerce environment with 1.18M products and 12,087 crowd-sourced shopping instructions. The agent needs to browse product pages and complete purchases according to user requirements (e.g., “buy a waterproof blue backpack under $50”). 

Training Data. We use all 10,587 instructions in the training split; 1,000 instructions are held out for validation and 500 for testing. 

Task Setup. Each shopping task allows at most 

15 steps to complete. The agent can exe-cute search[query] (search for products), 

click[element] (click page elements), or 

buy (purchase item). Success criteria include: (1) purchasing the correct item; (2) item attributes (color, size, etc.) matching requirements; (3) price within budget. 

A.2 Training Details A.2.1 Model and LoRA Configuration Base Models. Our main experiments use Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B (Team, 2025) as base lan-guage models. Cross-model generalization experi-10 ments further test Qwen2.5-1.5B , Llama-3.2-3B ,and Qwen2.5-7B .

LoRA Fine-tuning. To achieve parameter-efficient fine-tuning, we apply LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) to all linear projection matrices in attention layers: • LoRA rank: r = 32 

• LoRA alpha: α = 32 

• Target modules: Q, K, V, O projections and all linear layers ( all-linear )

A.2.2 Reinforcement Learning Hyperparameters 

We use GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as our primary RL algorithm. Key hyperparameters are as follows: 

Batch Size and Sampling. 

• Train batch size: 144 • Rollout group size: 8 (8 independent trajectories per group) • Number of groups per rollout: 18 (144 trajecto-ries total) • PPO mini-batch size: 72 • PPO epochs: 1 

Temperature and Sampling Strategy. 

• Rollout temperature: Trollout = 1 .0

• Validation temperature: Tval = 0 .0 (greedy de-coding) 

Optimizer and Learning Rate. 

• Optimizer: AdamW ( β1 = 0 .9, β 2 = 0 .999 , ϵ =10 −8)• Learning rate: 5 × 10 −6

• LR schedule: constant (QA); cosine decay (Web-Shop, 25% warmup, minimum LR ratio 0.1) • Weight decay: 0.01 • Gradient clipping: maximum norm 1.0 

A.2.3 DGS-WM Hyperparameters 

The core hyperparameters of our method are: 

Decision-Guided Observation Selection. 

• Selection standard deviation coefficient: κ = 0 .5

• Threshold formula: τt = μt + κ · σt, so a candi-date unit’s response support score must exceed the mean by κ standard deviations; μt and σt

are the mean and standard deviation of response support scores within the current observation 

Confidence-Adaptive Weighting. 

• Confidence decay exponent: γ = 1 .0 (linear decay) • Weight formula: wt,j = cγt , where ct is the re-sponse confidence at turn t

World Modeling Loss Coefficient. 

• λWM = 0 .02 (consistent with ECHO) 

A.2.4 Reward Function 

We adopt sparse binary reward signals for all tasks: 

QA Task Rewards. 

• Success (exact answer match): R = 1 

• Failure (answer mismatch or exceeding turn limit): R = 0 

• Invalid action penalty: −0.01 per invalid action (applied to corresponding action tokens in per-action mode) 

WebShop Task Rewards. 

• Success (correct item purchased with all at-tributes fully matched): R = 1 

• Failure (no purchase, wrong item, or attributes not fully matched): R = 0 

• Invalid action penalty: −0.01 per invalid action (applied to corresponding action tokens in per-action mode) 

B Observation Unit Partitioning 

B.1 QA Tasks: Sentence Splitting 

For retrieval document feedback, we split at sen-tence boundaries using forward sentence splitting with the following rules: 

Split pattern: We use the regex pattern 

(?<=[.!?])\s+(?=[A-Z0-9"’\(\[]) ,which splits after sentence-ending punctuation followed by whitespace and a capital letter or digit. 

Abbreviation protection: Common abbrevia-tions (Dr., U.S., Mr., Mrs., Ph.D., etc.) are pro-tected from splits by checking whether the period is preceded by these patterns. 

Single-letter protection: Single capital letters followed by periods (e.g., "A. Smith") are pre-served as part of the same sentence to avoid break-ing names or list markers. Each resulting sentence becomes one candidate observation unit ct,j with metadata tracking its para-graph ID, rank within the paragraph, sentence in-dex, and character positions in the original obser-vation. 

B.2 WebShop: Semantic UI Block Identification 

For webpage observations, we identify semantic blocks based on page type inference: 11 Page type inference: We classify each page as one of: search_results , product_main ,

search_home , or done based on page structure markers (search results list, product details section, search bar, confirmation message). 

Semantic block construction by page type: 

• search_results page: Each product card be-comes one block, containing ASIN, descrip-tion, and price as a unit. • product_main page: Core purchase informa-tion (selected options + title + price + rating + "Buy Now" button) forms one block; product description paragraphs are split into sentences; customer review blocks remain intact. • search_home page: Search bar and category buttons each form separate blocks. • Feedback blocks: System messages (e.g., "Added to cart") remain as complete units. 

Fallback handling: For unrecognized page structures, interactive buttons remain as whole-line units, while plain text content is split into sentences using the QA sentence splitting rules. 

B.3 Granularity Design Rationale 

The "minimal semantically complete unit" princi-ple avoids two failure modes: 

Over-coarse splitting (e.g., entire paragraphs or full product pages): Dilutes response support scores by mixing decision-relevant content with irrelevant context, reducing selection precision. 

Over-fine splitting (e.g., individual words or tokens): Breaks semantic integrity, making isolated fragments uninformative even when the complete unit would support decisions. Our task-aware unit definitions balance these concerns: sentences preserve propositional content in documents, while UI blocks maintain functional coherence in interactive environments. 12 C Case Studies
