ABSTRACT
Virtual Reality (VR) is changing how we interact with complex
spatial datasets. VR has the potential within forestry to help anal-
yse remotely sensed spatial data for inventory management. How-
ever, challenges of scale and legibility of dense forest point clouds
must be addressed. To do so, we present three VR Focus+Context
(F+C) techniques: Wedgelight, Remote Lantern, and Ray Lantern.
These techniques present point cloud data using a constrained high-
detail focus within a broader abstracted context representation, ma-
nipulated using egocentric and exocentric interactions. We con-
ducted two user studies.
The first informed the design of the
F+C techniques.
The results from the first study indicate that
VR significantly outperforms desktop displays in basic analysis of
forestry point clouds, with improvements to both time and accuracy.
The second study evaluated the performance of the resulting F+C
techniques in a task analogous to real-world forestry operations.
Our F+C techniques significantly outperformed current approaches,
confirming their viability for facilitating detailed and effective for-
est assessments. These findings indicate that VR has significant ad-
vantages for spatial data analysis tasks in forestry, making a strong
argument for its adoption in future analytical frameworks. The F+C
visualisation techniques we introduced show considerable promise
for analysis of high density point clouds, opening new avenues for
development in immersive analytics. Supplemental materials (code,
scans, results) are available at https://osf.io/hqc9u.

INTRODUCTION
In this paper, we present a set of Virtual Reality (VR) visualisation
techniques designed to improve the analysis of dense forest point
clouds. Our techniques are based on a Focus+Context (F+C) ap-
proach [22], whereby a user-controlled focus is displayed in detail,
while the data context is displayed at some higher level of abstrac-
tion. Traditional approaches to silviculture are data-informed but
imprecise: tree selection, the process by which specific trees are
marked to be thinned from the stand (a contiguous community of
trees) to maximise the stability and growth of the remaining trees,
relies on snap judgements by operators in the field. Similarly, stand
assessment, the process of taking a measurement of the forest, tra-
ditionally involves measuring a small subset of the trees and extrap-
olating. Both processes would benefit from higher precision.
Forest practitioners are increasingly making use of remote sens-
ing data for forest management purposes. High density LiDAR or
photogrammetry based point clouds are captured to generate high
fidelity 3D depictions of the forest being managed [1]. The ob-
jective is to deploy these datasets towards optimisation of forest
planning and silviculture. Increasingly, forest managers are explor-
ing whether the digital representations of forests can be used to
reduce the need for field inspections, to transfer traditionally field
based tasks to the office, providing a conduit to more precise and
automated operations. For this to be possible forest practitioners
need to be able to interact with the digital forest in an effective and
ergonomically sound manner, including with the raw data that pro-
vide the basis for the construct.
VR presents an opportunity to improve visualisation and inter-
action with dense point clouds. VR displays are inherently immer-
sive [39], and have been shown to enable a higher level of spatial re-
call and understanding of point clouds than 2D representations [37],
suggesting that VR may be ideal for advanced 3D forestry analyt-
ics. However, the rendering requirements of VR bring strict perfor-
mance limitations, and the methodology for comfortable and effi-
cient interaction with dense point clouds is still unclear, so design-

ing suitable implementation methods is essential. To this end, we
seek to answer the following: How do we leverage VR to support
dense point cloud tasks such as is needed in forestry management?
We developed three VR F+C techniques for understanding for-
est point cloud data: Wedgelight, Remote Lantern and Ray Lantern.
The design of these techniques is informed by results of a formative
study and feedback from domain experts. Each technique, named
following an illumination metaphor, is a combination of an egocen-
tric or exocentric control scheme, and a focus shape. This set of
techniques allow users to assess point clouds through direct manip-
ulation of a high-resolution focus, within the context of a simpli-
fied structural visualisation. Our specific implementation uses AI
derived tree features as a context dataset, and the focus dataset is a
high density point cloud produced by LiDAR scans.
In this paper, we present the design of our techniques, beginning
with the results of a formative study to examine how VR visuali-
sation can support understanding of forestry data compared to the
current standard of desktop displays. This initial investigation in-
formed the design of our F+C techniques. We describe the design
of our techniques and the motivating requirements. We conducted
a subsequent user study to evaluate the practical utility of our F+C
techniques in an industry context, assessing their performance in
a task analogous to a tree thinning operation. Assessment of user
performance on a series of success parameters, movement statis-
tics, and participant feedback were utilised to validate the tech-
niques. While our assessment focuses on forestry, these visualisa-
tion techniques have broader applications in other domains dealing
with dense point cloud data.
The key contributions of this paper are the empirical results of
a study showing the advantages of VR over Desktop for a set of
forestry point cloud analysis tasks; the design and implementation
of a set of novel F+C visualisation techniques for analysing dense
forest point clouds in VR; shared scan data and study scripts; and
results of a study assessing the performance of the visualisation
techniques for the analogue of a real world forestry operation.

BACKGROUND
2.1
Immersive Analytics
Immersive Analytics [5] is a research area that explores how human
computer interaction and immersive display technologies can facili-
tate complex data analysis. Part of the field explores how 3D visual-
isations can improve assessment of abstract and 3D data [46]. Such
visualisations enhance depth perception and presence for users, im-
proving comprehension and analysis [23, 35].
Immersive analytics tools have been explored for a variety of in-
dustrial applications. Tasks like analysis of multidimensional volu-
metric datasets for materials analysis [12] see advantages in struc-
ture and pattern identification, with observations noting that 3D vi-
sualisations are highly suited for assessing curved structures, which
are otherwise difficult to track and analyse using a 2D cross section.
Immersive analytics tools have provided an ‘accessible and fully
immersive comprehensive picture’ of physicochemical parameters
in biological datasets [30]. Forestry is not absent in this regard,
with explorations in measurement and annotation of field scans in
VR [20].
2.2
VR in a Forestry Data Context
There have been efforts to explore immersive analytics tools like
VR within forestry operations, such as with the aforementioned
field scan annotation. VR displays are fundamentally well suited
to analysis of complex spatial forestry data: 3D displays can alle-
viate the difficulty of analysing complex 3D datasets [43], and the
3D environment can facilitate rapid identification and selection of
features in such data [10]. Analytical task performance has been
shown to vary with level of immersion; increased immersion offers
substantial benefits for 3D data and cluster detection [24], which
has applications in forestry analytics.
However, dense forest point cloud datasets present challenges;
the volume and area of scans can be on the scale of hectares, pro-
ducing vast datasets. The position of trees is often scattered, and
neighbouring branches commonly overlap, creating occlusions and
identification problems. Accurately assessing trees requires looking
into branch and stem structure, which can be hidden from the out-
side. There are also challenges with utilising VR in forestry to its
full potential. In one recent case, Widjojo et al. [44] explored using
immersive analytics for forest assessment, looking specifically at
inventory assessments of radiata pine data. Following a user study
assessing the tools, they noted that the density of points within
a tree, and the segmentation techniques used on the point cloud
both impacted assessment performance considerably.
A lack of
colour information on points also made tasks more difficult. They
noted that “achieving a higher density of point clouds in certain ar-
eas in a VR-point cloud...is likely to be sufficient to improve task
performance in the VR-point cloud”. A systematic literature re-
view by Perez-Huet [33] further highlights some of these barriers.
The review notes that transitioning visualisations between scales of
forested areas is a challenge, as is designing displays with Level of
Detail (LOD) to maintain performance, while still emphasising that
VR models can be advantageous in creating “flexible, interactive,
and controlled ways to view [forestry data]”. VR has also seen use
as an outreach and engagement tool in the forestry sphere [3, 27], as
well as application in training and planning operations [18, 26, 32].
Performance limitations make rendering high resolution point
clouds in VR a challenge, independent of analytical context. They
can be circumvented by using clouds that do not exceed the up-
per performance limit for the display device: for example, Stets et
al. [40] were able to label the data from a Microsoft Kinect with no
need for pre-processing. For point clouds that go beyond this per-
formance threshold, pre-processing or display techniques are re-
quired to display the data comfortably. Ramirez et al. [36] use a
voxelization algorithm to process their data before displaying it,
reducing display complexity while still providing structural scene
context. However, this approach is not suited to tree data for all
cases, as without data of sufficiently high resolution the voxeliza-
tion process is likely to blur the distinction between branch and
leaf, stem and crown. Another approach that sees use in research
projects [36, 10, 38] is LOD systems: dividing the scan data into
subsets and rendering each at a lower resolution based on its prox-
imity to the focal point. This improves performance, while still
facilitating assessment of the whole dataset. A similar approach
is manual selection of area of interest, such as using a cross sec-
tion to isolate a subset of a point cloud for closer inspection [9].
Improvements can be gained by small optimisations like frustum
culling (unloading the points outside of the users view) which sees
use in point cloud interaction tools [14]. Alternatively, one can sim-
ply compress the dataset by reducing its resolution altogether. This
can be achieved through de-noising and refinement processes that
leverage techniques like octree encoding and surface approximation
[16, 19]. Point cloud editors like CloudCompare [15] offer tools
that can procedurally remove points from a cloud, trading quality
for size and performance, but this comes at the risk of sacrificing
details if processed over-zealously. Addressing performance limi-
tations is necessary to optimise forest point cloud analysis in VR.
2.3
Focus+Context
A technique that may work to remediate some of these issues is the
F+C methodology described by Kosara et al. [22] succinctly as “a
way to zoom in on parts of a large dataset without losing track of
where one is within the whole data”. Early work by Furnas [11]
conceptualised F+C as a focal point, a degree of interest function
and the context, which has since been generalized [17] and seen a


variety of approaches [6]. A simplification of this F+C method is
the Filtering approach, whereby instead of distorting or separating
the point of interest, additional information is provided only in areas
of interest in the dataset. A conceptual example exists in the form of
3D Magic Lenses [41] as early as 1996, however this render is de-
pendent on the users perspective. Kalkofen et al. [21] developed an
immersive F+C visualisation frame work for an Augmented Reality
(AR) context. Their work demonstrated how the ability to focus on
important information while still perceiving contextual information
in the periphery can reduce clutter while improving spatial compre-
hension. As they put it, F+C techniques “are highly successful in
scientific visualisation”, suggesting they may be a suitable solution
to the challenges of utilising VR in a forestry data context.

STUDY 1: POINT CLOUD COMPREHENSION ON DESKTOP
VERSUS VR
To inform the design of our forestry visualisation techniques, we
conducted a formative controlled user study to understand the ef-
fects of different display modalities on comprehension of forestry
point cloud data. To this end, we compared VR to traditional desk-
top displays. To focus on the effects of stereopsis and structure
from parallax independent of display resolution, we controlled for
both the display resolution and system input (as described in Sec-
tion 3.0.5). The study design was approved by the University Hu-
man Research Ethics Committee.
3.0.1
Task
To ensure silvicultural validity, the tasks were designed to be ana-
logues to real-world forestry tasks. Participants were required to
answer a set of comprehension questions about forestry point cloud
scans, repeated for each display condition. To maximise the rele-
vance of study results to industry, questions were based on domain
expert feedback and real world point cloud scans were used. (Sub-
sets of the data described in 4.3 were used for each question task.)
We utilised 6 types of comprehension question, each with 4 unique
variations, totalling 24 tasks:
Counting: the number of trees in a scan comprising 20–30.
Branching: find the tree with the most branches from 3 trees.
Tallest: Identify the tallest tree from 10–25 trees.
Cluster: Identify the tallest group of 3 trees from 3 groups.
Split: Identify the tree with a split stem from 20–25 trees.
Mirror: Identify the two identical trees from 6 trees.
Participants were prompted with the task objective (ie. Identify
the tallest tree), then allowed unlimited time to inspect the asso-
ciated point cloud, and finally prompted to make a selection on an
answer screen. This answer screen allowed users to select an integer
from a list, representing a tree count or specific tree ID, depending
on the task. Participants could use a controller to rotate, scale, and
translate the dataset during the task to change their perspective.
3.0.2
Independent Variables
Independent variables were counterbalanced to avoid effects of fa-
tigue or learning. The two independent variables were as follows:
Display Condition: Desktop or Virtual Reality.
Question Type: The different question type (Counting, Branch-
ing, Tallest, Tallest Cluster, Split, Mirror).
3.0.3
Dependent Variables
The seven study dependent variables were as follows:
Correctness: Binary correct or incorrect indicating if the partic-
ipant correctly answered the question.
Tree Counting Accuracy: Measured for the Counting task, the
distance to the correct answer, calculated as the relative difference
between the users answer and the true number of trees in the task.
Cloud Observation Time: The time (in seconds) the participant
took to assess the point cloud for each task.
Frame-of-Reference Transformation: Cumulative FoR met-
rics (translation, rotation, scale, and resets) recorded every 0.1s.
Mental Effort: Response on a nine point Likert scale [29].
System Usability: Participants SUS [4] questionnaire ratings.
Qualitative Feedback: Preferred Condition for Viewing; Per-
ceived Accuracy, Enjoyment, and Overall, and any additional com-
mentary about the techniques and the study experience.
3.0.4
Hypothesis
The hypotheses for the study are as follows:
H1 VR display will improve user performance over the Desktop.
H2 Users will prefer the VR display over the Desktop display.
H3 The impact of the VR display will vary across task types.
3.0.5
Apparatus
Both study conditions were developed and run through the Unity
editor running version 2021.3.16f1 through use of a Dell Preci-
sion 7760 Laptop (Win 10, 32 GB RAM, 11th Gen Intel Core i7-
11850H, RTX 3080). The VR condition used a Meta Quest Pro
tethered to the laptop via Quest Link. The Desktop condition was
run by extending the laptop display to a 51 cm wide (23 inches di-
agonal) monitor positioned 70 cm in front of the participant. The
display of the monitor was down-sampled to 900 × 600 pixels to
match the effective resolution of the Meta Quest Pro for the equiv-
alent field of view of the monitor (a display width of 51 cm placed
70 cm from the user’s eyes has an equivalent FOV of 40◦; given the
Meta Quest Pro pixels per degree of 22PPD, 40 × 22 ≈900). A
standard Xbox 360 Gamepad was used for interacting with the dis-
played datasets, and for the answer entry system. Participants re-
mained seated for the desktop condition. In the VR condition they
were invited to either sit or stand, as long as they were consistent.
3.0.6
Procedure
After a preliminary introduction and consent form, the study was
explained to participants. Task training was conducted on the desk-
top first, to allow participants to familiarise themselves with the
controls, then repeated in VR. There was no time limit any task, but
users were asked to be as fast and as accurate as possible. For both
conditions, participants completed every task, then a post condi-
tion survey. They concluded with a post study questionnaire. This
resulted in 6 (question types) × 4 (variations) × 2 (conditions).
3.0.7
Participants
We recruited 24 participants from university staff and students, (16
male, 8 female) aged 19–37 (M = 28, SD = 4.6). Each participant
was required to have normal or corrected to normal vision. Eigh-
teen participants were noted as having a background in computer
science. The remainder were split across the fields of Architecture,
Design, Business, Accounting, Psychology and Environmental Sci-
ence. Participants received a $20AUD (≈$13USD) gift card.
3.1

tors on participant performance, as facilitated by R and the package
lme4 [2]. The models included fixed effects for display condition
and question type, applied to measures of time, correctness, and

random effect. Statistical significance was assessed using Type II
Wald Chi-square tests, with a predetermined alpha level of 0.05.

test was employed. Analysis of participant system preference was
conducted using Chi-square goodness-of-fit tests. Lastly, partici-
pant feedback and verbal commentary were recorded and analysed
post-hoc to identify recurring themes and capture feedback. Where
question type was found to be a sole significant factor during anal-
ysis it was disregarded, as the varied requirements of each question

3.2
Discussion of Study 1 Results
VR was found to outperform the Desktop in all three performance
metrics, supporting hypothesis H1. Participants were more likely to
determine the correct answer to tasks, perform faster, and achieve
better accuracy in the counting task. The depth perception afforded
to VR from its stereoscopic view and structure-from-motion out-
performs the manual manipulation required by Desktop displays.
Participants greatly preferred the VR display over the Desktop
display, supporting hypothesis H2. VR was the majority choice for
every feedback preference category, in addition to showing higher
system usability and lower mental effort. This showed that VR was
simply easier and more enjoyable to use for the assigned tasks.
Despite finding that VR improved overall performance, we
found no interaction effect of VR for any of the particular question
types, contradicting hypothesis H3. We had expected that ques-
tion types like split identification would be more strongly effected
by being displayed in 3D. The advantages gained by use of VR is
generally consistent for all task types, leading us to the conclusion
that it is the best choice for dense forest point cloud analysis. Ul-
timately, VR appears to be faster, more accurate, and more user
friendly for assessing forest point cloud data than the equivalent
Desktop display within the parameters of our assessment.
The formative study had some limitations. Our participant gen-
der ratio was not balanced. Using a controller does not leverage the
full utility of VR. One participant commented that “it would feel
more natural in VR to have hand interactions”. Matching the res-
olution of the headset precluded us from testing higher resolution
desktop displays.

FOCUS+CONTEXT
TECHNIQUES
FOR
POINT
CLOUD
FOREST ASSESSMENT
To address the challenges of dense point cloud analysis in VR, we
designed a set of Focus+Context (F+C) visualisation techniques to
support forestry assessment tasks. These techniques are informed
by observations and comments from the formative user study and
forestry domain expert feedback. In this section, we identify the
requirements for a functional F+C technique, provide an overview
of our visualisation design, and describe our implementation.
4.1
Requirements
We conducted a series of interviews with forestry domain experts
to capture appropriate requirements. This involved informal dis-
cussions with four inventory and resource management specialists
to identify shortfalls in 2D point cloud visualisation software, and
inform the needs for the visualisations. The discussions helped out-
line that the techniques need to work with large datasets, on the
scale (R1) of compartmental LiDAR scans. The visualisations need
to be of sufficient quality (R2) that it is possible to perform tree fea-
ture identification. The techniques should be of sufficient perfor-


mance (R3) that they do not cause discomfort to users. Additional
requirements were determined from feedback from the formative
study (Section 3). Users need be able to annotate (R4) the data,
visually marking trees of interest. The technique should provide an
indication or trace (R5) of which trees have already been assessed.
To alleviate obfuscation, users should be able to quickly and re-
sponsively control (R6) how much of the data they can see. The
dataset should have transformation tools, enabling users to change
the scale and rotation of the visualisation frame-of-reference (R7).
4.2
Visualisation Design
We use a Focus+Context approach for our visualisation techniques.
Understanding spatial position within a dataset is important for
forestry analysis, as the condition of nearby trees is as important
as the tree being assessed. F+C is a good fit for such analysis, as it
allows users to view a scan in high detail, while retaining geospatial
context. The techniques work on the basis of a forest point cloud
dataset being represented in a frame-of-reference in two ways: An
abstracted context version of the dataset is displayed; the full reso-
lution dataset is aligned to the context, but only displayed within a
defined focus. In this section we describe the components of a F+C
technique, then describe our specific solutions in Section 4.2.4.
4.2.1
Frame-of-Reference
The Frame-of-Reference (FoR) defines the position and projection
of data in the virtual environment, comprising both the context and
focus. The user can manipulate the visualisation FoR independent
of the user’s position, adjusting the scale and rotation (R7) using
the controller joystick.
4.2.2
Context
Our context is modelled as low-resolution fitted cylinders that rep-
resent estimated stem points at fixed height intervals. These cylin-
drical models provide a simplified dataset that shows the approx-
imate shape, size and location of all the tree stems in the scan
without extraneous detail like small branches or leaf matter. This
model (illustrated in Figure 1b) provides an overview of the full
dataset with minimal performance overhead. The fitted cylinders,
though simple, provide feature cues for analysts, letting them see at
a glance the approximate structure of any tree.
There are a number of fair use tree/forest LiDAR processing
tools available capable of generating such datasets. To create our
context dataset, we leveraged an AI structural segmentation tool:
Forest Structural Complexity Tool (FSCT) by Krisanksi et al. [25].
We scanned a forest using an Emesent Hovermap ST [45] and pro-
cessed the scan using FSCT under default parameters, which pro-
duced the dataset for the context of our techniques. Though the AI
models are still improving, the fitted cylinders approach is sufficient
to provide geospatial continuity and context to users. As these tools
develop, there is utility in F+C techniques for validation.
4.2.3
Focus
The techniques we describe are distinguished by the area of the fo-
cus and how it is controlled. The focus displays a full resolution
point cloud (R2) in an area defined by a control method, point of
focus, shape, and size. The control method defines the position of
the point of focus (R6). We explore two control methods: ego-
centric control, where the focus is controlled relative to the user’s
point-of-view, and exocentric control, where controlling the focus
is relative to the FoR. For example, a typical egocentric control may
be a raycast, whereas exocentric might be a joystick control.
Any shape that can be described in a shader could reasonably be
used for the focus. Users can dynamically adjust the width of the
focus area within defined limits that ensure the number of rendered
points never exceeds the performance threshold (R3). This allows

users to interact with large point clouds (R1) without harming per-
formance in VR. The system tracks which trees have been displayed
in the focus and applies a subtle colour tint to their context repre-
sentations, allowing users to see at a glance which sections of the
data they have not assessed yet (R5). Tree annotation (R4), specif-
ically the selection of individual trees, is supported by applying a
colour multiplier to trees as they are selected.
4.2.4
Techniques
We initially experimented with a ‘flashlight’ technique, in which a
cylindrical focus extended from the users controller, reminiscent of
using a real flashlight to illuminate the data. This suited fine anal-
ysis, but made it challenging to assess tree stems, as users needed
to pan up and down repeatedly to ascertain the full structure. To
alleviate this, we projected the focus from the forest floor to the top
of the canopy, ensuring the full height of the tree is always visible.
Through further experimentation we found that a cylindrical focus
was ideal for stem assessment, as it fit the shape of the trees, and
did not cause obfuscation when assessing trees away from the user.
The Wedgelight (egocentric) technique utilises a wedge shaped
focus projected from the user’s controller and constrained to a two-
dimensional orientation parallel to the forest floor. This results in
‘wedge’ focus that can be pointed wherever the user wants to in-
spect in detail, shown in Figures 1d and 4a. Users can control the
aperture and focal length of the focus using the controller joystick.
The Remote Lantern (exocentric) technique uses a cylindrical
focus at a fixed position, see Figures 1c and 4b. The controller
joystick is used to move the focus relative to the FoR, and users can
independently control its radius with the controller buttons.
The Ray Lantern (egocentric) technique uses the same focus
shape and visualisation as the Remote Lantern but the focus follows
the intersection of an infinite raycast from the user’s controller to a
plane on the forest floor. Users point their controller (casting a ray)
to aim the focus, or press a button to pause it.
Each focus was encapsulated by a transparent mesh as a visual
cue to scaling. This mesh briefly appears visible when the user ad-
justs scale, then gradually fades back to transparent, as a tangible
way to illustrate the focus area to the user. Points are rendered using
an accessible colour scheme [13]. Scale is discrete for the Lantern
techniques, and continuous for the Wedgelight. Quick-reset but-
tons were implemented to reset the position of the Remote Lantern,
and the scale and rotation of the frame-of-reference. These features
were informed by observations and feedback from Study 1.
4.3
Implementation
Our techniques are implemented in Unity game engine 2021.3.16f1.
The techniques utilise forestry point clouds in a .las [28] file con-
taining a high resolution point cloud of the forest plot. For the
purposes of our study, a LiDAR scan of a production Pinus radi-
ata 1 forest in the Green Triangle region of South Australia was
1Data is publicly available from https://osf.io/hqc9u.


capturedthe data from which is shown in Figure 1a.
The context data is processed using FSCT as described in Section
4.2.2. Once the focus and context point clouds are imported into
Unity, Point Cloud Tools [31] is utilised to convert each cloud into
a mesh. A custom material shader is then used to selectively filter
the mesh. The shader takes as input the transformation matrix of
the current focus object. It then calculates if each vertex within the
tree mesh is inside or outside of the focus area, storing this result
in the vertex’s UV coordinates system. The fragment shader then
assesses the values stored in the UVs and discards fragments of the
context mesh or focus mesh accordingly. This results in the full
point cloud being drawn within the focus area, and the synthetic
data being drawn outside of it, as illustrated Figure 1c. A different
version of this material exists for each of the selection states of the
cloud, allowing subsets (in our case, individual trees) to be drawn
in different colours reflecting their state.

STUDY 2: VR F+C TECHNIQUES
Following our formative study (Section 3) and design of our F+C
techniques (Section 4), we conducted a summative user study to
examine the designed techniques. The study design was approved
by the University Human Research Ethics Committee.
5.0.1
Task
The study task was designed as an analogue for a real world forestry
process.
Participants were tasked with assessing forest grids in
the virtual environment and marking the trees that displayed the
‘double leader’ characteristic (trees in which the stem splits into
two trunks near the base) by touching them with the controller and
pressing the trigger, which would mark that tree. Participants were
asked to distinguish between single instances of these trees (indi-
viduals) and adjacent pairs of these trees (clusters) by marking the
latter cases an additional time, and asked to identify as many of
these individuals and clusters as possible within a sixty second time
limit. This task is a simplified version of the tree selection process
described in the introduction (henceforth tree marking for clarity).
5.0.2
Study 2 Dataset Generation
A forest generator tool was created to synthesise appropriate and
realistic datasets for a controlled repeated study of this nature. This
generator used real-world LIDAR scans of 14 single trees: 10 single
leaders and 4 double leaders. The data also included 2 false negative
and 2 false positive versions (where focus did not match context)
to be representative of a real dataset. The generator used spatial
Poisson disk sampling [8] to distribute double leader trees around a
grid of ordinary trees, resulting in 1×10228 possible permutations
for each task. The generator also utilised random scale, rotation and
position jitter to create the illusion of a real forest.
5.0.3
Control
We designed a Control condition to emulate the constraints of us-
ing no F+C technique. In the control, the user views either the full
context data or the full focus data at any one time, toggled with
a button press. In order to function at an acceptable performance
level, the control condition was forced to utilise a lower resolution
source point cloud (averaging 50k vertices per tree instead of 500k).
This concession was deemed necessary as using the full resolution
cloud was impossible and independently segmenting the cloud both
was insufficient. Rendering the entire grid at full resolution caused
the average framerate to drop below acceptable levels. From pilot
benchmarking, we found the number of points that could be dis-
played on our hardware without a drop below 70 fps was 15M, or
a maximum of 33 displayable trees. To mitigate this, the large grid
condition was fixed to 15 × 15 trees across all conditions, and the
source point cloud was subsampled with a minimum distance of
0.06 m between points for the control. This ensured performance
never fell below 70 fps. As an alternative for the control method,
we explored but eventually rejected subdividing the plot into sec-
tions and loading them individually. Loading and rendering the
full-resolution sections would give the participants less than seven
seconds per block of 33 trees for our intended task, and would make
the assessment of adjacent trees between blocks impossible.
5.0.4
Independent Variables
Independent variables were counterbalanced to avoid effects of fa-
tigue or learning. The two independent variables for the study are:
Technique: Wedgelight, Remote Lantern, Ray Lantern, Control.
Grid Size: Alternate forest grid sizes comprised of rows of trees.
5.0.5
Dependent Variables
The following are the eight independent variables for the study:
Tree Identification and Error: The number of correctly identi-
fied double leader trees, with false positives counted as errors.
Cluster Identification: The number of double leaders correctly
identified as being individuals or clusters.
User Movement: Cumulative user movement metrics for the
user controllers (distance, rotation), headset (distance, rotation),
eye movement (rotation), each recorded at intervals of 0.1s.
Focus Movement and Scaling: Cumulative movement metrics
for the focus (distance, scale), recorded at intervals of 0.1s.
Frame-of-reference Transformation:
Translation and scale
metrics of the visualisation frame recorded at intervals of 0.1s.
Effort: Participant rated levels of physical and mental effort af-
ter each condition on respective nine point Likert scales.
System Usability: Participants SUS [4] questionnaire ratings.
Preference: Participant technique preference for a variety of cri-
teria: accuracy, assessment, enjoyment, and overall favorite.
5.0.6
Hypothesis
The hypotheses for the study are as follows:
H1 The Wedgelight, Remote Lantern and Ray Lantern tech-
niques will improve user performance compared to the Control.
H2 Grid size will effect technique performance.
H3 Users will prefer a F+C technique for tree marking.
H4 A ranking of effectiveness will emerge for the techniques.
5.0.7
Apparatus
The study was conducted standing in an unobstructed 3 m2 × 3 m2
area to give users freedom of movement. The VR headset and teth-
ered laptop setup described in Section 3.0.5 were used for this study.
5.0.8
Procedure
The study introduction involved a pre-study questionnaire and ex-
planation of procedure. Participants were acclimatised to the VR
headset through fit adjustment and eye tracking setup, then by in-
duction to the study environment.
For each condition, they were
taught to use the controls, then assessed. Training was conducted on
a forest grid comprised of 5×5 trees with example double leaders,
repeatable until the participant was comfortable with the controls.
The assessment consisted of five large grid tasks (15×15 trees), and
five small grid tasks (10 × 10 trees) for each technique, in random
order, with an option to rest between each task and a post condition
survey. After all four conditions were completed, the participant
filled out a final post-study questionnaire. This resulted in 2 (forest
grid size) × 5 (repetitions) × 4 (techniques).
5.0.9
Participants
A total 24 participants were recruited from university staff and stu-
dents (16 male, 8 female) aged from 20–45 (M = 28, SD = 6.1).
Participants were required to have normal or corrected to normal
vision. Seventeen participants identified as computer scientists, the
remainder were divided across psychology, architecture, aviation,

Study 2 Results

R, with fixed effects of interaction technique and grid size, to mea-
sures collected across each condition. Participant variation was ac-
counted for as a random effect. The statistical significance of the ef-

test was utilised to analyse participant feedback metrics. Analysis
of technique preference was conducted using Chi-square goodness-
of-fit tests. In our analysis, we found no interaction effect between
grid size and any other factor on any of our measures.

5.3
Discussion of Study 2 Results
The F+C techniques were found to outperform the Control in ev-
ery performance metric, supporting hypothesis H1. The techniques
granted users significantly higher rates of successful tree identifi-
cation, tree marking, and overall accuracy. The most significant
impact of the techniques was the lower rate of errors, and hence
increase in overall accuracy rate. This suggests the techniques are
advantageous for preventing false positives in tree identification.
We found no evidence that the size of grid used for the task
effected the performance of any F+C technique, contradicting hy-
pothesis H2. As in real operations, the total area of the forest is less
important for assessment than the proportion of trees being thinned.
Users overwhelmingly preferred the F+C techniques for tree
marking over the Control supporting hypothesis H3. This is unsur-
prising, as reported mental effort required was significantly higher
for the Control, and it was the only condition for which system
usability score fell below the ‘good’ range. The cylindrical focus
shape for Ray Lantern and Remote Lantern made them well suited
to the study task. This is likely why they were ranked first and
second in overall preference. The Wedgelight technique was less
suited to the task, but was rated highest for enjoyment. We expect it
would excel at finer analysis of tree structure. We did not find clear
rankings between the F+C techniques (failing to support H4). Our
findings suggest that F+C in general is a good method for present-
ing complex point cloud data.
Participant comments provided insight to how the techniques
might be improved, such as the option to freely change the shape of
the focus (e.g., square, circle, column), more options for highlight-
ing trees when illuminated (e.g., set a time threshold for the colour
change), and expand on the controls, specifically: raising and low-
ering of the context, individually rotating each tree around its axis,
and dynamically scale up trees in focus.
The techniques are transferable to other fields, though alternate
methods of creating context are needed for other domains. They
could be used in construction geometry quality inspection, as view-
ing the source data circumvents the minimum fidelity problem [42].
They could also be used to validate outputs of data abstraction pro-
cesses, like those applied in archaeological artifact analysis [34]
and agricultural structure detection [7].
Our study was not without limitations. The primary constraint
was the need to use lower resolution data for the Control condition.
When the performance of VR is on par with the fidelity of remote
sensing, the techniques should be compared directly. The gender
ratio of participants was not evenly balanced. To improve relevance
to real world forest management, it should be expanded to other
task analogues, such as tree species and age classes.
6
CONCLUSIONS
Our formative user study showed users were faster and more accu-
rate using VR than desktop for forestry analytics tasks. The depth
perception and freedom of movement offered by VR displays make
them ideal for structural analysis of dense point clouds, even requir-
ing less controller movement than on the desktop. VR has inherent
advantages for forestry data analytics.
Subsequently we described a series of F+C techniques, leverag-
ing these advantages for dense forest point cloud assessment. Our
follow up user study showed these techniques all performed equally
better than the control, leading us to conclude that F+C techniques
are a natural fit for this branch of forestry analytics. We believe
they are viable for other fields with similar spatial data, and we have
shared our study data, point clouds and scripts for future research.
The cylindrical focus methodology described for Lantern tech-
niques best suits comparative analysis of multiple trees, whereas
the directed focus for the Wedgelight technique would likely be
more useful for more detailed structural analytics. This implemen-
tation is not the only way to design F+C techniques for these tasks;
however, future research should explore other methods for gener-
ating context data. The ability to switch between multiple diverse
synthetic datasets would be an interesting way to explore the inter-
action between AI data analysis and human understanding. Virtu-
alised tree analysis in the future would benefit from implementation
of F+C tools for point cloud analysis.
