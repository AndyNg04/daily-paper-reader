ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong potential for human mobility
generation by modeling individual routines, activities, and mobility preferences.
However, existing approaches remain largely resident-centric: generating realis-
tic individual trajectories does not necessarily recover the encounters that emerge
when multiple residents co-occur at the same place and time. To this end, we
study encounter-aware human mobility generation, which jointly preserves indi-
vidual mobility and population-level interaction structure. We propose ENCOUN-
TERMOB, an encounter-driven LLM framework consisting of a coarse-to-fine En-
counter Generator and an Iterative Coupling Framework. The Encounter Gener-
ator first captures city-level encounter rhythms and then instantiates participant–
time–venue encounters, while iterative coordinates these encounters with personal
mobility behavior through successive refinement. We further introduce an en-
counter recovery protocol covering encounter marginal and joint distributions over
residents, times, and venues. Experiments on the Japan and NYC datasets show
that ENCOUNTERMOB outperforms all external baselines on all seven encounter
metrics and all six personal mobility metrics. Compared with ELLMob, it reduces
the participant–time–venue joint error by 40.4%/41.7% and the mean personal
mobility error by 58.8%/32.7% on Japan/NYC, respectively.

INTRODUCTION
City-scale human mobility data capture the spatiotemporal movements of residents across cities and
support the study of individual mobility patterns and urban dynamics, with established applications
in transportation planning (Çolak et al., 2016), public-health decision-making (Chang et al., 2021),
and urban planning and management (Zheng et al., 2014). However, access to real-world mobility
data are often constrained by collection costs, limited coverage, commercial restrictions, and privacy
risks (Kostandova et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2023a). Human mobility generation therefore aims to
synthesize spatiotemporal trajectories that reproduce real-world mobility patterns, alleviating data
scarcity and supporting downstream mobility analysis and urban applications(Yuan et al., 2026).
Recent LLM-based mobility generation methods have substantially improved individual trajectory
synthesis by leveraging rich behavioral information, including historical routines(Wang et al., 2024),
activity semantics(Gong et al., 2024), mobility motivations(Ju et al., 2025), and contextual factors
(Wang et al., 2026). Beyond reproducing spatiotemporal regularities, these methods use LLMs to
interpret the behavioral semantics underlying individual mobility, such as what activities residents
perform, what places they prefer, and how their mobility adapts to different contexts. Such semantic
reasoning enables generated trajectories to better reflect not only where and when each resident
moves, but also the behavioral patterns, preferences, and motivations that shape their movement.
Despite their remarkable success, existing methods primarily adopt a resident-centric perspective,
generating individual trajectories according to personal mobility behavioral. However, mobility
data also capture cross-resident relations, including encounters, i.e., the co-presence of two or more
residents at the same place and time (Sun et al., 2013). Preserving such encounters requires co-
ordination across multiple residents, since the activity of one resident must align with the timing,

bility data, as illustrated in Figure 1(b). This formulation introduces two key challenges. First,
the encounter structures used for coordination need to be established before generating individ-
ual trajectories. We introduce a coarse-to-fine encounter generator that first captures the temporal
and category-level distribution of encounters and then instantiates concrete participant–time–venue
events. Second, these encounter structures need to be coupled with the mobility patterns of in-
dividual residents. We therefore introduce an iterative coupling framework, where a joint critic
evaluates their consistency and guides the refinement of both encounter structures and personal tra-
jectories. Together, these components enable coordinated trajectory generation while preserving
plausible personal behavior. Besides, we introduce an encounter-aware evaluation protocol that as-
sesses encounter recovery across participants, times, and venues, together with their joint structures.
Experiments on the Japan and NYC datasets demonstrate consistent improvements in both encounter
and personal mobility fidelity. Compared with ELLMob, ENCOUNTERMOB achieves lower scores
on all seven encounter metrics, reducing the joint encounter score by 40.4%/41.7%(Japan/NYC),
and on all six personal mobility metrics, with mean reductions of 58.8%/32.7%, respectively. Ab-
lation studies show that removing either city-level rhythms or Encounter Instantiation increases the
full group–time–venue JSD by 33.6% on average across the two ablations and both datasets. Remov-
ing iterative coupling increases this score by 74.4% on average across the two datasets, supporting
the contributions of encounter generation and iterative refinement to encounter recovery. Our con-
tributions are summarized as follows:
1. To the best of our knowledge, we present the first city-scale human mobility generation
study aimed at recovering the encounter structures observed in real mobility data.
2. We propose ENCOUNTERMOB, an encounter-driven LLM framework that generates en-
counter structures and coordinates them with personal mobility behavior.

RELATED WORK
City-Scale Human Mobility Generation
Deep learning has been widely studied for human mo-
bility prediction and generation (Luca et al., 2023). Early neural methods use recurrent architec-
tures to capture temporal dependencies in trajectories (Kulkarni & Garbinato, 2017; Berke et al.,
2022), while later studies employ variational models, GANs, adversarial imitation learning, and dif-
fusion models for trajectory generation (Huang et al., 2019; Feng et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2021; Zhu
et al., 2023b; Zhang et al., 2025). More recently, LLM-based methods incorporate behavioral and
contextual information into individual trajectory generation, including activity patterns and motiva-
tions (Wang et al., 2024), persona-conditioned activity planning (Ju et al., 2025), and event-specific
context (Wang et al., 2026). These advances establish LLMs as a promising direction for human
mobility generation.
LLMs for Human Mobility Modeling
LLM-based approaches to human mobility use mobility
histories and behavioral context to reason about individual movement (Wang et al., 2023; Gong
et al., 2024). Recent frameworks further incorporate demographics, activities, motivations, prefer-
ences, and external events to support interpretable trajectory generation and behavioral adaptation
(Zhang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Ju et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). Beyond individual mobil-
ity, household mobility work uses LLMs to infer activity dependencies among household members
and coordinate their schedules (Liu et al., 2025). These studies extend LLM-based mobility mod-
eling from individual behavior to coordinated and population-level mobility across multiple scales.
However, existing city-scale mobility generation primarily treats resident independently, with en-
counter structures arising only from spatiotemporal overlap rather than cross-resident coordination.

PROBLEM FORMULATION
Given historical mobility records and the set of active residents Ract for the target day, we aim to
generate a daily trajectory bτr for each resident r ∈Ract. Each trajectory records a time-ordered
sequence of venue visits. When these trajectories place two or more residents at the same venue
during the same time interval, they form an encounter, denoted by e = (te, ve, Re), where te, ve,
and Re specify the time, venue, and complete set of co-present residents, respectively. For a set of
trajectories T , let E(T ) denote all encounters induced by T . Their organization across participants,
times, and venues defines the encounter structure of T . Formally, our task is to generate the joint
trajectory set bT = {bτr | r ∈Ract} such that each trajectory preserves the mobility regularities of
its resident, while E(bT ) reproduces the encounter structure observed in real mobility data.
4
EVALUATION PROTOCOL FOR ENCOUNTER RECOVERY
Existing evaluation of human mobility generation mainly assesses mobility patterns at the individual
or population level (Wang et al., 2024; 2026; Zhang et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). However, these
two levels capture different aspects of mobility fidelity and do not evaluate the cross-resident struc-
ture formed by encounters. For example, generated trajectories may reproduce individual mobility
patterns and aggregate time–venue distributions, while different groups of residents meet at those
times and venues. To this end, we propose a evaluation protocol to evaluate encounter recovery
across participants, times, and venues, including their marginal and joint distributions.
Specifically, each encounter is jointly characterized by its complete participant group G, time T,
and venue V . We evaluate encounter recovery using seven distributions, as summarized in Table 1.
The marginal distributions of G, T, and V assess which residents participate in encounters, when
encounters occur, and where they occur. The joint distributions G×T, G×V , and T×V evaluate
the associations between these dimensions, while G×T×V evaluates their participant–time–venue
structure. Together, these dimensions provide a comprehensive evaluation of encounter recovery.

METHODOLOGY
Figure 2 presents ENCOUNTERMOB, an encounter-driven LLM framework for city-scale human
mobility generation. It consists of a coarse-to-fine Encounter Generator and an Iterative Coupling
Framework. The Encounter Generator derives concrete participant–time–venue encounters from
city-level encounter rhythms, while the Iterative Coupling Framework iteratively adjusts encounter
proposals and personal mobility behaviors. Together, the two components guide resident trajectories
toward realistic encounter structures while preserving individual mobility regularities.
5.1
COARSE-TO-FINE ENCOUNTER GENERATOR
To guide mobility generation with encounters, we need to identify plausible encounters for the active
residents. We introduce a coarse-to-fine Encounter Generator that first infers city-level encounter
rhythms and then instantiates them into concrete participant–time–venue encounter proposals.
City-Level Encounter Rhythms
Encounters arise as residents carry out their daily activities
across the city. Since these activities follow recurring temporal and spatial routines, the resulting
encounters also exhibit regularities in when and where they occur (Leng et al., 2021). For exam-
ple, commuting brings many residents to stations during the morning and evening rush hours, while
meal periods concentrate residents in restaurants, creating more opportunities for encounters at these
times and places. Based on this, we characterize the overall encounter activity of the target day as
city-level encounter rhythms, providing coarse guidance on the times and venue categories in which
encounters are likely to occur.
To infer these rhythms for a specific target day, we consider both historical encounter activity and
the behavioral context of the active residents. Historical encounter activity is summarized from sev-
eral aspects, including daily encounter volume, temporal distribution, venue-category composition,
and weekday variation. For each active resident, we further summarize historical mobility into a
behavioral profile describing recurring activities together with their typical timing and venue con-
ditions. The LLM combines these sources to determine how the historical encounter regularities
should be reflected for the current population, producing the target-day encounter volume and its
coarse distribution over time and venue categories.
Encounter Instantiation
Given the city-level encounter rhythms, the next step is to instantiate
specific encounters for the target day. In addition to the resident profiles used above, we summa-
rize historical encounters through their participants, meeting times, venues, and activity contexts,
providing more specific evidence for how residents have interacted in the past. The LLM com-
bines this historical evidence with the coarse temporal and venue-category guidance to determine
plausible participant–time–venue configurations. Historical relations support previously observed
combinations, while the behavioral context of the residents also allows the LLM to consider new
combinations when they are consistent with their activities. The resulting instantiated encounters
form the initial encounter proposals for the subsequent Iterative Coupling Framework.

5.2
ITERATIVE COUPLING FRAMEWORK
The initial encounter proposals need to be further coordinated with the mobility behavior of the
involved residents on the target day. A proposed encounter may require a resident to change an oth-
erwise plausible activity, while established personal mobility patterns may make the proposed time,
venue, or participant combination difficult to realize. We therefore treat both encounter proposals
and personal mobility behavior as revisable. Then an Iterative Coupling Framework is introduced to
enable them to couple each other through iterative feedback (Madaan et al., 2023; Gou et al., 2024).
First, we prompt the LLM to generate a complete daily trajectory for each resident based on histori-
cal personal mobility patterns, and then extract the encounters formed by these trajectories. Second,
the LLM compares these extracted encounters with the encounter proposals and examines the histor-
ical evidence supporting both sides. For personal behavior, such evidence includes recurring activi-
ties and their typical timing and venue conditions; for encounter proposals, it includes the historical
encounter contexts and resident activities used during instantiation. Based on this comparison, the
Joint Critic either accepts the current coordination or identifies the remaining inconsistencies and
provides feedback for refinement.
At each refinement round, the Critic feedback is passed to the personal mobility generator to revise
the affected trajectories while preserving personal behavior consistent with the supported encounter
proposals. The revised trajectories are then fed back to the coarse-to-fine Encounter Generator,
which updates the city-level encounter rhythms and subsequently regenerates the encounter propos-
als conditioned on the updated rhythms and current personal trajectories. The revised trajectories
and encounter proposals are evaluated again by the Joint Critic in the next round. Through this pro-
cess, personal behavior can adapt to supported encounter proposals, while encounter proposals can
also be revised according to well-supported personal behavior. The refinement proceeds for at most
K rounds and terminates earlier once the Joint Critic accepts the current coordination.

EXPERIMENTS
6.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets.
We use two city-scale mobility datasets constructed from the global Foursquare check-
in resource associated with LBSN2Vec (Yang et al., 2019; 2021), covering Japan and the New York
metropolitan area. The Japan and NYC datasets use fixed cohorts of 1,049 and 226 residents con-
structed solely from historical-period encounters, with 60/30 and 123/61 historical/test days, re-
spectively. Both datasets are discretized into 10-minute intervals(Wang et al., 2024; 2026) and use
Foursquare venue categories. More detailed information is provided in Appendix C.1.
Metrics.
We evaluate both encounter and personal mobility fidelity. For encounters, we use the
metrics defined in Section 4: TD, VD, GroupD, T×V , G×T, G×V , and G×T×V . For personal
mobility, we use six metrics from LLMob and ELLMob (Wang et al., 2024; 2026): step distance
(SD) and step interval (SI) measure consecutive-stop distances and time gaps; daily activity routine
distribution (DARD) and spatio-temporal visit distribution (STVD) capture time–activity and time–
location patterns; category distribution (CD) and spatial grid distribution (SGD) capture activity
composition and spatial visit distributions. All scores compare normalized observed and generated
histograms using Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) (Lin, 1991); lower is better. Detailed definitions
and calculation settings are provided in Appendix C.2.
Baselines.
We compare ENCOUNTERMOB with two types of baselines: (1) deep-learning-based
methods, including predictive models LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997), GETNext (Yang
et al., 2022), and MHSA (Hong et al., 2023), and generative models TrajGAIL (Choi et al., 2021) and
DiffTraj (Zhu et al., 2023b); and (2) LLM-based methods, including LLMob (Wang et al., 2024) and
ELLMob (Wang et al., 2026). Within each dataset, all methods use the same settings, information,
and evaluation metrics; the LLM-based methods use the same backbone unless otherwise specified.
Implementation and generation details are provided in Appendix C.3.
6.2
MAIN RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
Main results.
Tables 2 and 3 evaluate encounter and personal mobility fidelity on Japan and NYC.
ENCOUNTERMOB outperforms all external baselines on all seven encounter metrics and all six
personal metrics. Several baseline scores reach the maximum JSD value of 0.69315, indicating
no overlap between the observed and generated distributions. ELLMob is competitive on personal
mobility, leading all six baseline metrics in Japan and five in NYC, yet its encounter errors remain
substantially higher than those of ENCOUNTERMOB. This shows that personal fidelity alone is not
enough to assess generated mobility, and encounter recovery provides a complementary view.
The encounter improvements extend from temporal and venue distributions to complete participant
groups and their joint configurations. Compared to ELLMob, ENCOUNTERMOB reduces GroupD
by 46.2% in Japan and 66.2% in NYC, and reduces G×T×V by 40.4% and 41.7%, respectively.
These results support encounter modeling for recovering which residents meet, when, and where.

Ablation study.
We conduct ablation studies to examine three key designs in ENCOUNTERMOB:
city-level encounter rhythms (w/o Rhythm), Encounter Instantiation (w/o Instantiation), and the
Iterative Coupling Framework (w/o Coupling). As can be seen in Table 2, removing city-level
encounter rhythms consistently degrades encounter generation performance on both datasets. The
improvement on TD, VD, and T × V is the most significant, showcasing the importance of city-
level rhythms in recovering the temporal and venue distributions of encounters. We also observe
improvements in GroupD on both datasets. This effectiveness is likely attributed to more accurate
temporal and venue guidance, which reduces inconsistency in the resulting group distribution.
The importance of Encounter Instantiation is particularly evident in GroupD and the group-related
joint metrics, where its removal causes consistent performance degradation on both datasets. This
highlights its critical role in determining which residents participate in encounters and how they are
associated with time and venue. These results also indicate that city-level encounter rhythms alone
are insufficient to recover concrete participant–time–venue configurations.
By comparing ENCOUNTERMOB with w/o Coupling, we observe clear improvements in GroupD
and the group-related joint metrics on both datasets, highlighting the role of iterative coupling in
coordinating encounter requirements with personal mobility behavior. Meanwhile, the mean of the
six personal mobility errors decreases by 53.1% in Japan and 35.4% in NYC, showing that improved
encounter fidelity is accompanied by better individual mobility fidelity.
6.3
RECOVERY OF CITY-LEVEL ENCOUNTER RHYTHMS DISTRIBUTIONS
Figure 3 compares the encounter distributions in ground truth, ELLMob, our city-level encounter
rhythms, and the final generated trajectories. The heatmaps show that ELLMob deviates from
ground truth in how encounters are distributed across hours and venue categories, whereas our
city-level encounter rhythms more closely capture the observed distribution. The hourly counts
in Figure 3(f) reveal a similar difference in encounter volume: ELLMob produces an overly concen-
trated peak, while our city-level encounter rhythms better follow the temporal variation in ground
truth. The encounters extracted from the final trajectories largely preserve this distribution, while
the adjustments introduced during coupling generally bring both the category allocation and hourly
counts closer to ground truth. These results show that city-level encounter rhythms provide effective
guidance for recovering the encounter distribution while allowing adaptation to personal mobility.

6.4
RECOVERY OF PARTICIPANT IDENTITIES
Beyond predicting when and where encounters occur, Encounter Instantiation must also identify the
full set of residents involved in each encounter. The group-based distributional metrics used in the
main evaluation evaluate the distributions of participant groups, but they do not directly measure
whether individual groups are recovered exactly. We therefore also report group precision, recall,
and F1 under exact matching. A predicted group is counted as correct only when its full participant
set exactly matches a group in the ground-truth data. As shown in Table 4, Encounter Instantiation
improves participant-group recovery across both datasets. These results highlight its role in recov-
ering the complete sets of residents involved in encounters. In NYC, w/o Rhythm achieves higher
Recall and F1, but only by generating more encounters.
6.5
EFFECT OF ITERATIVE COUPLING
We investigate the effect of refinement rounds in the Iterative Coupling Framework. At each round,
we evaluate encounter fidelity using G×T×V JSD, participant-group recovery using group F1 in
Table 4, and the mean of the six personal JSD metrics. As shown in Figure 4, encounter JSD
decreases while group F1 increases across rounds, indicating improved joint encounter fidelity and
participant-group recovery. Encounter JSD decreases and group F1 increases across refinement
rounds, while personal mean JSD remains low and decreases slightly throughout the process. These
trends indicate that iterative refinement improves both distribution-level encounter fidelity and exact
participant-group recovery without degrading personal mobility.

7
CONCLUSION
This work investigates how encounter information can improve LLM-based human mobility gen-
eration by explicitly modeling interactions among residents. Specifically, a LLM based framework
ENCOUNTERMOB is proposed to incorporate two coupled mobility semantic representation. More-
over, a critic and a recovery protocol are introduced to enable the reliable harnessing of LLMs for
human mobility generation. In experimental studies, we construct encounter-based datasets from
the open-source mobility datasets to evaluate the performance of our framework. Future work will
extend the framework to more diverse mobility settings and incorporate richer social and behavioral
priors, exploring more realistic and generalizable mobility generation.

B.3
ENCOUNTER PATTERN
Initialization and refinement. Prompt 3 generates the initial encounter plan from the City Pattern
and the groups’ meeting histories. Refinement applies the same priorities to the revised City Pat-
tern and the previous plan. Each previous event is accompanied by its meeting history, whether it
is currently realized, any stated member conflicts, and the members’ nearby planned visits. Addi-
tional candidates include unplanned co-presence with meeting history and habitual groups not yet
scheduled.
When to revise or retain. A previous event is retained unless there is a concrete reason to remove it:
a stated member conflict, weak historical support for the target day, or a more plausible alternative
involving the same residents or venue and time. Realization or non-realization alone is insufficient
to retain or remove an event. Plausible co-presence and habitual meetings may be added where
City allows room; co-presence retained by the joint review provides additional support. Meeting
times remain grounded in each group’s history. Venue selection prioritizes repeated meeting venues,
then members’ nearby planned visits, then the group’s most recent venue. All retained and added
events must satisfy the same resident and group consistency constraints. Unsupported City cells
may remain unfilled, with the discrepancy reported.
Revision output. Initialization returns the full event list. Refinement instead returns the patch be-

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
C.1
DATASET CONSTRUCTION AND PREPROCESSING
We construct the Japan and NYC datasets from the global Foursquare check-in resource associated
with LBSN2Vec (Yang et al., 2019; 2021). The check-in records and venue information provide
resident identifiers, visiting times, venue identifiers, geographical coordinates, and venue categories.
Starting from this resource, we construct each dataset in four steps: (1) specify the study region and
historical/test periods; (2) preprocess timestamps and venue categories; (3) extract encounters from
the processed check-ins; and (4) select residents using historical encounters and assemble their
trajectories and encounters for both periods.
1. Study regions and periods.
We construct one dataset for Japan and one for the New York
metropolitan area. The Japan window spans May 2–July 30, 2012, with the first 60 days used as the
historical period and the last 30 days reserved for generation and evaluation. For NYC, we retain
check-ins within the New York metropolitan area (CBSA 35620), using the boundary from the 2024
TIGER/Line CBSA files (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024). Its historical period is July 1–October 31,
2012 (123 days), and its test period is November 1–December 31, 2012 (61 days).
2. Temporal and semantic preprocessing.
We organize check-ins by resident and local date,
converting NYC timestamps to local time using the offsets supplied with the records. For both
datasets, we discretize time into 10-minute intervals, following LLMob (Wang et al., 2024). Each
activity stop retains its venue identifier, and the Foursquare taxonomy provides the venue’s activity
category. These records form the daily trajectories used for encounter extraction.
3. Encounter extraction.
For each day d, ten-minute time slot t ∈{0, . . . , 143}, and venue
identifier v, we collect the distinct residents with a recorded stop in that cell:
Rd,t,v = {r : resident r has a recorded stop at (d, t, v)}.
(1)
Whenever |Rd,t,v| ≥2, we extract one encounter e = (t, v, Rd,t,v), following the definition in
Section 3, and retain its date d. All residents in the cell form the complete participant set, with each
resident counted once. Encounters in consecutive occupied slots are recorded as separate events. We
first apply this procedure to historical records to support resident selection in the next step.
4. Resident selection and dataset assembly.
For each resident pair, we count the historical en-
counters whose participant set contains both residents. A pair qualifies when this count is at least
two. Each distinct day–time-slot–venue cell contributes one occurrence, with time slots defined at
ten-minute resolution; the occurrences may be on the same or different days. We then select every
historical encounter containing at least one qualifying pair and include its complete participant set
in the resident cohort. The repetition threshold applies to the qualifying pair: other residents in such
an encounter are included even if they participate in only one qualifying encounter.
For example, suppose two historical encounters in distinct cells have participant sets {A, B} and
{A, B, C}. The pair (A, B) occurs twice and therefore qualifies. Both encounters are selected, so
residents A, B, and C all enter the cohort, even if C appears only in the second encounter.
The union of these participant sets defines the fixed cohort, yielding 1,049 residents for Japan and
226 for NYC. We retain the selected residents’ trajectories in both the historical and test periods and
apply the same extraction procedure to obtain the encounters in each final dataset. Cohort member-
ship is determined entirely from the historical period and remains fixed throughout evaluation.
Table 5 summarizes the resulting datasets. The Japan test period contains 1,950 encounter partici-
pation records, and NYC contains 154. The NYC historical period contains 5,963 activity stops and
258 encounters.

Example: Realizing an encounter with a local revision
Context: Japan, July 1, 2012. The encounter module proposes A and B at 14:00, Café v18324.

target day.
