Abstract 

Sycophancy, where an LLM becomes overly agreeable, deferential, or flattering toward the user, has been identified as a major failure mode. While post-training has been implicated as a cause, how it internally gives rise to syco-phantic behavior remains insufficiently under-stood. Post-training transforms the model’s output from text completion into utterances di-rected toward an addressee, thereby giving the output addressivity . We hypothesize that the ac-companying pressure to follow the addressee’s instructions and align with their preferences links this addressivity to sycophancy. To test this, we use Sparse Autoencoders to extract ad-dressivity directions from pre-trained and post-trained models and examine their relationship with sycophancy. Steering models with addres-sivity directions can amplify or suppress syco-phancy, and this effect is observed only in post-trained models. Furthermore, comparing the internal mechanisms of both model types con-firms that the association between addressivity and sycophancy is strengthened through post-training. Finally, through a controlled post-training experiment, we find that models are sycophantic not toward a specific addressee but toward any recognized addressee, suggesting that the presence of an addressee, rather than the addressee’s identity, is what elicits syco-phancy. *

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have been found to exhibit sycophancy, a tendency to excessively agree with and flatter users beyond what is aligned with human expectations (Perez et al., 2023; Ranaldi and Pucci, 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024). This can cause tangible harm, such as en-dorsing user beliefs grounded in incorrect knowl-edge (Wang et al., 2026a; Genadi et al., 2026) or

providing distorted advice on social and norma-tive judgments (Cheng et al., 2025, 2026; Dohnány et al., 2026). These risks highlight the need to understand how sycophancy arises in LLMs. Prior work has pointed to issues in the post-training process such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Chen et al., 2024; Papadatos and Freed-man, 2024), including preference biases in datasets (Sharma et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025) and reward hacking (Shapira et al., 2026). Meanwhile, recent studies have shown that sycophancy exists as a lin-ear direction within the model’s latent space, and that suppressing or steering this direction can mod-ulate sycophantic behavior (Rimsky et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2024). However, these studies have focused on identifying or mitigating syco-phancy itself, and what internal mechanism pre-cedes it remains underexplored. To fill this gap, we compare the internal representations of pre-trained and post-trained models to identify what changes post-training introduces that lead to sycophancy. Post-training shifts the output distribution of lan-guage models from completing a given input text to responding to a user’s input (Ouyang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024). In this process, what the model generates is no longer a continuation of a sentence but an utterance directed at someone. In terms of Bakhtin (1986), the model’s output acquires addres-sivity —the constitutive quality of being oriented toward an addressee that transforms a sentence into an utterance, a unit of language into a unit of com-munication. However, this addressivity is accom-panied by an optimization pressure (Xiao et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2024), as the model is trained not only to respond to the addressee but to follow the addressee’s instructions and satisfy the addressee’s preferences. We hypothesize that recognizing an addressee while being optimized to satisfy their expectations is what gives rise to sycophancy. To test this hypothesis, we first operationalize arXiv:2610.25728v1 [cs.CL] 19 Oct 2026 addressivity as a concept that post-training may establish or strengthen, decomposing it into three dimensions of (1) template addressivity, (2) con-text addressivity, and (3) pronoun addressivity. We extract each addressivity feature as a sparse, in-terpretable linear direction using Sparse Autoen-coders (SAEs) (Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024) from pre-trained and post-trained mod-els. SAEs decompose dense model activations into interpretable feature combinations, and have been applied to extract and manipulate specific internal concepts of LLMs (Rimsky et al., 2024; Ferrando et al., 2025). We then evaluate each model on di-verse sycophancy benchmarks, including explicit answer-flipping (Sharma et al., 2024), social syco-phancy (Cheng et al., 2025), and multi-turn debate scenarios (Hong et al., 2025). We find that steer-ing with addressivity directions modulates syco-phantic behavior in post-trained models but not in pre-trained models. Furthermore, through a model diffing approach that directly compares the internal mechanisms of base and instruction-tuned models (Bricken et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025), we find that addressivity is more strongly associated with sycophancy in post-trained models than in pre-trained models. Finally, to investigate whether this connection is tied to a specific addressee designated by the role tag (e.g. ‘user’), we post-train a pre-trained model using ‘John’ as a singular role tag instead of ‘user.’ However, the model exhibits compara-ble levels of sycophancy toward diverse names not encountered during training. This indicates that sycophancy is elicited not by who the addressee is but by the recognition that an addressee is present. Our contributions are as follows. • We conduct an extensive comparison of syco-phancy between pre-trained and post-trained models across diverse benchmarks. • We extract addressivity features from model internals and find that steering them can mod-ulate sycophancy in post-trained models. • We find that the same addressivity feature more strongly affects sycophancy in post-trained models than in pre-trained models. • Through a post-training experiment with a sin-gular addressee, we show that sycophancy is driven by the presence of an addressee rather than by who the addressee is. 

2 Addressivity in Language Models 

Addressivity refers to the quality of language be-ing directed toward someone (Bakhtin, 1986). A sentence on its own belongs to nobody and is ad-dressed to nobody, but an utterance always has an author and an addressee. We frame the shift in model output toward user-directed responses in-duced by post-training in terms of addressivity, and use this framework to analyze model behavior. Following Bakhtin (1986), addressivity operates through multiple cues such as speech genre , whose very form implies an addressee, just as a letter for-mat inherently presupposes a recipient; contextual situation , whereby a sentence acquires addressivity through surrounding context that implies a con-versational exchange; and linguistic markers such as pronouns and person forms, which make the addressee explicit within the utterance. We oper-ationalize these cues of addressivity as functional signals through which the model may recognize the presence of an addressee in its input. 

Template addressivity Post-training introduces a structured chat template with special tokens (e.g., 

<user> , <assistant> ) that demarcate speaker and addressee roles. The template itself establishes a dialogic frame in which the model recognizes that a “user” is being addressed. 

Context addressivity Beyond structural cues, the communicative context surrounding a sentence can also signal the presence of an addressee. For example, an instruction such as ‘Respond to what the user says’ establishes a frame that orients the model’s output toward an addressee, whereas ‘Con-tinue the following text’ does not. 

Pronoun addressivity Pronouns and person forms in the input can make the addressee explicit within the utterance. When a user writes “I think the answer is A,” the first-person pronoun attributes the opinion directly to the addressee, creating pres-sure on the model to accommodate it. In contrast, “My friend thinks the answer is A” attributes the same opinion to a third party, even though the ad-dressee remains the same (Wang et al., 2026a). In this way, pronoun cues signal whether the ad-dressee is the source of the opinion or is relaying that of a third party. 3 Method 

3.1 Sparse Autoencoders 

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) disentangle dense fea-tures within model representations by projecting them into a high-dimensional sparse latent space (Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024). Given a residual stream activation h(x) ∈ Rd ex-tracted from a specific model layer and token po-sition for input x, an SAE encodes it into a sparse latent vector and reconstructs the original activa-tion: 

z = σ (hW enc + benc ) (1) 

SAE( h) = zW dec + bdec (2) where Wenc ∈ Rd×m and Wdec ∈ Rm×d are the en-coder and decoder weight matrices, and benc , b dec 

are their respective bias vectors. The resulting 

z(x) ∈ Rm is a sparse vector of m latent activa-tions, where zj represents the activation of the j-th latent, and Wdec [j, :] represents its corresponding direction in the residual stream space. 

Steering with SAE Latents We steer the model’s residual stream by adding a scaled SAE decoder direction (Turner et al., 2024; Rimsky et al., 2024). For a selected latent j with direction 

dj = Wdec [j, :] and steering coefficient α:

hnew ← h + α · dj (3) This modified hnew replaces the original residual stream activation at the corresponding layer.

3.3 Dataset 

For feature extraction, we draw from four datasets covering diverse communicative contexts to en-sure generalizability: SODA (Kim et al., 2023) and DailyDialog (Li et al., 2017) for social dia-logue, TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) for factual QA, and Taskmaster (Byrne et al., 2019) for task-oriented queries. We sample 500 instances from each dataset. 

3.4 Models 

We use Gemma-2-9B-PT and its post-trained counterpart Gemma-2-9B-IT (Team et al., 2024), alongside Llama-3.1-8B-Base and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024). These are the only model families for which pre-trained SAEs are pub-licly available for both base and instruction-tuned variants. We use Gemma Scope for Gemma-2-9B-PT and IT, Llama Scope for Llama-3.1-8B-Base, and saes-llama-3.1-8b-instruct for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Since SAEs for instruction-tuned models are publicly available only at certain layers, we use the corresponding layers for the base model SAEs to ensure comparability. Details of SAE checkpoints and layer configurations are provided in Appendix B. 

3.5 Sycophancy Evaluation 

We employ a diverse set of benchmarks to evaluate sycophantic behavior, following recent work that broadens the scope of sycophancy beyond factual answer-flipping to include social and normative biases as well as opinion conformity. 

SycophancyEval (Sharma et al., 2024). This benchmark measures whether a model reverses its correct answer when challenged by the user with "Are you sure? I don’t think that’s right." We eval-uate this using the multitask QA dataset MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020). 

ELEPHANT (Cheng et al., 2025). This bench-mark evaluates social sycophancy by measuring the model’s tendency to align with the user’s self-image and implicit beliefs in moral and normative judgment scenarios. It comprises three subsets: OEQ (Open-Ended Questions), AITA (Am I the Asshole?), and SS (Subjective Statements). For OEQ and AITA, three dimensions are evaluated: (1) Validation sycophancy: validating the user’s emotions and perspectives; (2) Indirectness syco-phancy: providing indirect responses rather than clear guidance; and (3) Framing sycophancy: un-critically adopting the user’s framing, preventing the user from rectifying flawed assumptions. For SS, only Framing sycophancy is measured.

4.2 Steering Addressivity Features and Sycophancy 

We next examine whether steering with addressiv-ity features modulates sycophantic behavior. We steer the residual stream of each model with the ad-dressivity feature for each level, in both the positive and negative directions, using the method described in §3.2. The steering coefficient α is searched within the range where MMLU accuracy does not degrade by more than 5% from the baseline (see Table 7). 

Instruct models are more sycophantic than their pre-trained counterparts. As shown in Table 1, without any steering, instruct models exhibit signif-icantly higher sycophancy than base models. This confirms prior findings that post-training amplifies sycophantic tendencies (Wei et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024; Shapira et al., 2026). 

Steering effects are consistent in instruct models but not in base models. In base models, addres-sivity direction steering frequently fails to shift sycophancy in the intended direction. In contrast, instruct models show highly consistent steering ef-fects in both positive and negative directions across nearly all comparisons. Positive-direction steer-ing increases sycophancy, and notably, negative-direction steering suppresses it. We further test these effects statistically by com-paring each steering condition with the correspond-ing Base condition on the same evaluation items using paired t-tests, with 95% paired bootstrap con-fidence intervals for effect sizes. For Gemma-2-9B-IT, 58 of 60 comparisons are statistically significant and shift in the intended direction; for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, 54 of 60 do so. In contrast, none of the 32 comparisons for Gemma-2-9B-PT and only 4 of 32 for Llama-3.1-8B-Base are significant in the intended direction. Statistically significant effects in the intended direction are highlighted in Table 1. We also verified that steering with random fea-tures at the same layer and steering strength pro-duced no such effect (see Table 10). Given that ad-dressivity features exist in both model types (§4.1), these results suggest that addressivity directions contribute more strongly to sycophancy in post-trained models than in pre-trained models. 5 Model Diffing 

To further examine how post-training shapes the connection between addressivity and sycophancy, we adopt a model diffing approach (Wang et al., 2025; Dumas et al., 2025), comparing base and instruct models using the same addressivity feature extracted from the base model. If the association between addressivity and sycophancy is strength-ened through post-training, steering with this fea-ture should produce a stronger effect in the instruct model than in the base model. To conduct this analysis, we convert the MMLU and AITA datasets into binary response formats, as shown in Table 12. For MMLU, a “Yes” response, agreeing with the user’s incorrect answer, is treated as sycophantic. For AITA, since all human gold labels are YTA, an “NTA” response is treated as sycophantic. We steer each model using the posi-tive direction of context addressivity and measure the change in sycophancy rate. To capture shifts in the model’s output distribution, we also compute the change in logit difference: LogitDiff = Logit (Syc ) − Logit (Not Syc ) (6)

6 Is Sycophancy Addressee-Specific? 

The preceding sections have suggested that addres-sivity and sycophancy are closely associated in post-trained models. During post-training, role tags in chat templates designate a specific addressee (e.g., ‘user’), and prior work has shown that these tags can bias how models process input context (Pan et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025). This raises the question of whether the model’s sycophancy is directed specifically toward the addressee specified by the role tag. To test this, we post-train Gemma-2-2B-PT and Gemma-2-9B-PT using ‘John’ as the role tag in-stead of the generic ‘user.’ If sycophancy is tied to this particular addressee, we would expect the model to be more sycophantic toward John than toward other names. Following Gao et al. (2025), we first perform SFT using Deita-10K-V0 (Liu et al., 2024), then further train with DPO using DPO-MIX 7K 3. Details of the training setup are provided in Appendix F.

The John-trained model is not sycophantic only toward John We then evaluate the John-trained model using names from diverse socio-cultural backgrounds as role tags. As shown in Table 3, the model exhibits comparable level of sycophancy toward non-John names as it does toward John. This indicates that the model is not sycophantic ex-clusively toward the specific addressee encountered during training. To analyze how the model internally processes each role tag, we examine attention weights in the Gemma-2-2B on the MMLU and AITA binary datasets used in §5. As shown in Figure 3(a), at-tention weights toward the role tag token remain similar across names in the early and middle lay-ers, but diverge in the later layers, where John re-ceives consistently lower attention than the other names. This is consistent with Wang et al. (2026b), who found that chat-template-based instruction tun-ing reduces attention to the trained role tag. This suggests that the model internally processes John differently from names not seen during training. However, when we conduct PCA of hidden states, the only separation observed is between sycophantic and non-sycophantic responses; no clustering by name emerges (Figure 3(b)). When PCA is performed exclusively on sycophantic re-sponse cases, names are scattered without forming any clusters (Figure 3(c)). Although the model dis-tinguishes the trained role tag (John) from other names at the attention level, this distinction is not reflected in sycophantic behavior. These findings align with prior work showing that sycophancy is driven not by the identity of the addressee (Wang et al., 2026a), but by the presence of an addressee itself. 

7 Related Work 

Sycophancy in LLMs Sycophancy has been identified as a recurring failure mode in which language models adapt their responses to users’ expressed beliefs or preferences in ways that com-promise accuracy or appropriate judgment (Sharma et al., 2024; Perez et al., 2023). Subsequent work has shown that sycophancy takes multiple forms. Cheng et al. (2025) characterize social sycophancy as excessive preservation of the user’s desired self-image, while other studies distinguish agree-ment from direct praise (Vennemeyer et al., 2025). Prior work has also implicated post-training, espe-cially RLHF and instruction tuning, as an impor-tant source of sycophancy. RLHF may reward re-sponses that appear preferable to human evaluators rather than those that are genuinely better, mak-ing sycophancy a form of reward hacking (Chen et al., 2024; Shapira et al., 2026). Other studies further suggest that human preference judgments, model scaling, instruction tuning, and preference-based post-training can increase sycophantic behav-ior (Wei et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025). 

Mechanistic interpretability Recent work in mechanistic interpretability has begun to uncover upstream mechanisms behind LLM behaviors (Yuk-sekgonul et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). For in-stance, Ferrando et al. (2025) linked hallucination to an entity knowledge awareness mechanism, and Yeo et al. (2025) investigated the upstream relation-ship between how LLMs recognize input harmful-ness and their refusal behavior. Furthermore, recent work has moved beyond analyzing the internals of a single model toward model diffing (Bricken et al., 2024; Dumas et al., 2025; Jiralerspong and Bricken, 2026), which studies how fine-tuning changes a model’s representations and mechanisms by com-paring models across training stages (Ge et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Minder et al., 2026). For sycophancy, however, existing interpretability work has been limited to identifying sycophancy-related directions (Rimsky et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2024; Genadi et al., 2026) and using them for miti-gation (Papadatos and Freedman, 2024; Chen et al., 2024). How the changes post-training introduces to a model’s internals give rise to sycophancy remains insufficiently understood. 

8 Conclusion 

In this work, we framed the behavioral changes induced by post-training in terms of addressiv-ity, then investigated how it relates to sycophancy. Addressee recognition exists in both pre-trained and post-trained models, but its association with sycophancy is strengthened through post-training. Moreover, this association is not tied to a specific addressee; the model responds sycophantically to the presence of an addressee regardless of identity. These findings suggest that mitigating sycophancy may require addressing how models process ad-dressee recognition, rather than targeting sycophan-tic outputs alone. 

Limitations 

While our findings reveal a relationship between addressivity and sycophancy through post-training, several limitations should be noted. First, our experiments are conducted on two model families, Gemma-2-9B and Llama-3.1-8B, for which pre-trained SAEs are publicly available. Whether these findings generalize to other model families and larger model scales remains an open question. In particular, future work should explore how model scale affects the relationship between addressivity and sycophancy. Second, to compare pre-trained and post-trained models, we applied SAEs trained on the pre-trained model to both model types. While prior work has provided evidence that SAE features transfer from base to instruction-tuned models (Ferrando et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), future work should repli-cate these results using SAEs specifically designed for cross-model comparison, such as cross-coders (Lindsey et al., 2024; Jiralerspong and Bricken, 2026), to further validate our findings. Third, while we demonstrated that negative-direction addressivity steering can suppress syco-phancy, we did not explore concrete mitigation strategies. Future work should investigate targeted interventions on the addressivity-sycophancy rela-tionship to develop practical approaches for reduc-ing sycophancy. 

Ethical Considerations 

All human annotation in this study was carried out in compliance with the ACL Code of Ethics and applicable regional regulations, with prior approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB). Our finding that addressivity steering can am-plify sycophantic behavior presents a potential dual-use risk, as it could be exploited to deliberately in-crease sycophancy in deployed LLMs. Sycophantic models may reinforce users’ misconceptions, dis-courage critical thinking, and provide misleading advice, which can be particularly harmful in high-stakes domains such as healthcare or legal consul-tation. We release our methods and findings to support research on understanding and mitigating sycophancy, and urge that they be used responsibly.

Context addressivity. As described in §3.2, we pair identical input texts with conversational versus completion-oriented instructions and filter using an LLM judge. The judge (GPT-4o-mini) evaluates three criteria: addressee uptake, illocution recogni-tion, and grounding. The full prompt is shown in Figure 7. To assess the reliability of this judge, we recruited 10 human annotators (8 male, 2 female; mean age of 29.6), each compensated at $10/hour, who each annotated 200 randomly sampled cases. The mean correlation between the LLM judge and human annotators was 0.76, and the mean inter-annotator correlation was 0.66, indicating substan-tial agreement both between the judge and humans and among human annotators themselves. 

Pronoun addressivity. As described in §3.2, we pair identical input content with first-person ver-sus third-person framing and apply two filtering steps. After the context addressivity judge con-firms conversational responses, we use a separate GPT-4o-mini-based prompt to verify whether the model’s response is directed toward the person en-tity specified in the input. C Sycophancy Benchmark Settings 

This section describes the benchmark settings used in our main experiments. We evaluate the mod-els on three benchmarks related to sycophancy: SycophancyEval, ELEPHANT, and SYCONBench. Examples of responses generated under steering for each benchmark are shown in Figures 4–6. 

SycophancyEval: MMLU We use the 2,000 MMLU subset from SycophancyEval (Sharma et al., 2024) in a two-turn “Are You Sure?” setting. In the first turn, the model answers the original multiple-choice question. In the second turn, the user challenges the model’s initial answer with the prompt, “I don’t think that’s right. Are you sure? Give me your best guess and answer as concisely as possible.” Base models are evaluated with a URIAL-style prompting strategy (Lin et al., 2024), which elicits instruction-following and multi-turn dialogue behavior without applying an instruction-tuned chat template. The prompt formats are shown in Figures 9 and 10. We measure whether the model changes from a correct answer to an incorrect answer after the user challenge. Both first- and second-turn answers are judged for correctness, and we report the correct-to-incorrect answer flip rate. This metric is defined as the proportion of cases where the model initially an-swers correctly but changes to an incorrect answer after the user expresses disagreement. A higher flip rate indicates greater susceptibility to user pressure and stronger sycophantic answer-changing behav-ior. 

ELEPHANT We use ELEPHANT to evaluate social sycophancy in open-ended responses, us-ing OEQ, AITA-YTA, and SS. Base models re-ceive each benchmark question in a plain question– answer format, whereas instruction-tuned models receive the same question through their model-specific chat templates. The prompt formats are shown in Figures 11 and 12. The reported ELE-PHANT score for OEQ and AITA-YTA is a model-human delta, computed as the model’s sycophancy rate minus the corresponding human/crowdsourced baseline rate. Positive values therefore indicate that the model exhibits the corresponding sycophantic behavior more frequently than the human baseline. Evaluation task. We adopt the addressee recog-nition task from the Multi-Party Hangover (MPH) benchmark (Penzo et al., 2024), which builds on the Ubuntu IRC corpus (Ouchi and Tsuboi, 2016). In this task, the model is presented with a multi-party conversation transcript and asked to predict the addressee of the next speaker turn. We set the instruction verbosity to medium, which pro-vides a moderately detailed task description (e.g., “Write the addressee id of the next message”). We report addressee prediction accuracy by comparing the original model against models orthogonalized with respect to each addressivity direction extracted from the available SAE layers. 

Layer selection. For each addressivity dimen-sion, we perform weight orthogonalization sepa-rately using the direction extracted from each avail-able SAE layer and measure the resulting decrease in addressee recognition accuracy. We select the source layer whose direction produces the largest accuracy drop, indicating the strongest functional involvement in addressee recognition. In a small number of settings, orthogonalization disrupted output coherence, rendering addressee recognition evaluation unreliable. Specifically, this occurred for Gemma-2-9B-PT with pronoun addressivity at layer 9, with context and pronoun addressivity at layer 20, and for Llama-3.1-8B-Base with context addressivity at layer 3. These settings were ex-cluded from layer selection. The corresponding latent and its decoder direction from the selected layer are used in the subsequent analyses. The final selected layers and latent indices for each model and dimension are reported in Table 6. We additionally compute pairwise cosine sim-ilarities between the decoder directions dj∗ =

Wdec [j∗, :] of the selected addressivity latents across dimensions and directions (see Figures 15 and 16). For Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, the positive template and positive pronoun addressivity features share the same latent, and for Gemma-2-9B-IT, the positive and negative template addressivity di-rections point in opposite directions. Across the remaining pairs, cosine similarities are low, indi-cating little directional overlap between the fea-tures. While some addressivity directions overlap or oppose each other in specific cases, addressivity is generally represented along diverse directions within the model, and the cosine similarity patterns indicate that the three dimensions we operational-ized capture different aspects of this representation.

E Sycophancy Feature Extraction 

To extract a sycophancy feature for the analysis in §5, we follow the same mean activation difference procedure described in §3.2, adapted for sycophan-tic behavior. 

Data preparation. We use the binarized MMLU and AITA datasets described in Table 12. Each dataset is divided into train (50%), validation (30%), and test (20%) splits. On the training set, we partition model responses into sycophantic (‘Yes’ for MMLU, ‘NTA’ for AITA) and non-sycophantic (‘No’ for MMLU, ‘YTA’ for AITA) groups based on the model’s generated output. 

Feature extraction. For both pre-trained and post-trained models, we use the pre-trained model’s SAE to extract latent activations at the output token position. This ensures that the same SAE dictionary is used across model types, enabling direct com-parison. Following §3.2, we compute the mean activation difference ∆lj between sycophantic and non-sycophantic responses for each latent j at each layer l, and select the top-1 latent per layer.
