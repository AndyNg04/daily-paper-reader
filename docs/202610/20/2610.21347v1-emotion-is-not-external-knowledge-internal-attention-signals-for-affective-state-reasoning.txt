Abstract 

Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly operate in multi-turn interactions that require maintaining latent conversational states, not only explicit facts. We study affective state as an implicit state inferred from sparse contex-tual cues that remain relevant after topic shifts, and ask whether LLMs expose internal atten-tion signals for routing such evidence at infer-ence time. Prior attention interventions rely on head profiles derived from labeled exam-ples across multiple profiling tasks and exter-nally specified target spans, limiting transfer across affective-evidence distributions. We pro-pose Self-targeted Internal Attention Boosting (SIAB), which localizes sparse affective heads across transformer layers in each model via causal localization (in detail activation patch-ing) on synthetic minimal pairs and boosts the content tokens these heads already attend to. Both edited heads and target tokens are derived from the model’s own behavior, enabling the same head set to be reused across tasks. We evaluate SIAB on the ECC and GoEmotions benchmarks across three LLMs and multiple prompting settings. Relative to the correspond-ing settings without SIAB, macro-F1 improves by up to 7.67 points on ECC and 3.28 points on GoEmotions. Combining SIAB with PASTA further raises ECC macro-F1 by 12.51 points over the Gemma 3 zero-shot baseline. 

1 Introduction 

Conversation is inherently stateful; What a user means at any point depends on what came before. As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increas-ingly deployed in such multi-turn interaction, suc-cessful responses must not only retain explicit fac-tual context but also maintain latent conversational states, such as a user’s frustration, uncertainty, or trust (Fazzi et al., 2025, Følstad et al., 2024). Af-fective state is a challenging case. It is rarely given as an explicit variable. Instead, it must be inferred 

Figure 1: Long dialogues can bury early affective cues, turning affective prediction into a routing problem. 

from sparse contextual cues whose relevance can persist across topic shifts. As illustrated in Fig-ure 1, a model may therefore need to use an earlier affective cue when making a later prediction. Unlike knowledge-intensive failures, where re-trieval or tool use can supply a missing fact, affec-tive continuity does not require adding new infor-mation. Since the relevant evidence is already in the dialogue, the difficulty is whether the model routes it to the final decision point. Emotion recog-nition therefore provides a controlled proxy for studying this routing problem at inference time. Inference-time attention intervention is a natural way to probe this problem, since it reroutes exist-ing contextual evidence during the forward pass, without parameter updates or newly supplied facts. Existing attention intervention methods, however, rely on external control signals. Post-hoc Attention STeering Approach (PASTA) (Zhang et al., 2024a) profiles which heads to edit from labeled in-domain data and steers attention toward externally speci-fied spans. Later variants reduce but do not remove this reliance on external signals. These methods raise two questions. RQ1: Can internal attention signals route affective evidence without external arXiv:2610.21347v1 [cs.CL] 15 Oct 2026 spans? and RQ2: Are internal and external signals redundant or complementary? We address these questions with Self-targeted Internal Attention Boosting (SIAB), an attention intervention proposed for affective state reasoning. It first localizes a sparse set of emotion-sensitive attention heads in different Transformer layers through causal localization via activation patching. It then uses these heads themselves to select target tokens. At inference time, SIAB adds a scalar bias to the pre-softmax attention logits of the localized heads, boosting the content tokens they already at-tend to. Both the edited heads and the target tokens thus derive from the model’s own behavior. We evaluate on two Emotion Recognition in Con-versation (ERC) settings with complementary dis-tributions of affective evidence. In the Emotion-Cause Conversation benchmark (ECC; He et al., 2025), cues are dispersed across earlier turns and must survive later topic shifts. Meanwhile, in the GoEmotions benchmark (Demszky et al., 2020), a single dense cue must instead be disambiguated among many fine-grained categories. The same head set is reused across both. Since our goal is not only to improve recogni-tion but to understand the internal signal we ex-ploit, we analyze when and why the localized heads work. The gains come from a sparse set of heads; A few heads are sufficient, and they attend more to affective words than random heads from early, middle, and late layers. Whether SIAB succeeds further depends on logit geometry and on whether the boosted tokens align with the final label de-cision. Finally, the effects of SIAB and PASTA complement each other depending on their layer-band proximity. Our contributions are threefold: (i) We pro-pose SIAB, a self-targeted inference-time atten-tion intervention that requires no parameter up-date or annotated span and composes with prompt-ing and PASTA-style external supervision, (ii) We show consistent gains across models, datasets, and prompting settings, and (iii) We characterize its mechanism and boundary conditions. 

2 Related Work 

Existing attention-level methods externalize con-trol to varying degrees, relying on heads selected by testing each head’s steering effect on develop-ment examples with target tokens that are user-annotated (Zhang et al., 2024a), heuristically ex-tracted (Zhang et al., 2024b, Yu et al., 2024), or applied at the head output rather than its attention (Li et al., 2023). Residual-stream steering operates at a coarser granularity (Turner et al., 2024, Rim-sky et al., 2024, Zou et al., 2023). These methods assume that the relevant evidence can be externally identified, an assumption less natural for affective state, whose cues spread across turns, often lack lexical salience, and shift as the conversation un-folds. We instead derive both signals internally — heads from activation patching, target tokens from each head’s own attention— and study how this internal signal composes with external ones. Mechanistic interpretability provides tools for discovering internal structures that mediate model behavior. Activation patching (Wang et al., 2023, Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2023) has been used to localize concrete circuit components, such as name-mover and S-inhibition heads, through clean– corrupted activation contrasts, while direct logit attribution (Belrose et al., 2023) links intermediate contributions to final-token logits. These tools fit our goal of identifying internal signals for affective-evidence routing. Rather than specifying emotion-bearing spans externally, we localize the attention heads that internally route affective contrast. Emotion recognition in conversation naturally probes affective state across dialogue turns, yet existing mechanistic work operates at a coarser granularity. It studies sentiment directions (Tigges et al., 2024), mid-layer multi-head self-attention units (Tak et al., 2025), or residual-stream circuits for emotion generation (Wang et al., 2025), leaving head-level recognition unaddressed. Other work improves accuracy through retrieval, speaker mod-eling, or curriculum learning (Lei et al., 2023, Xue et al., 2024, Fu et al., 2025, Li et al., 2026), but treats the model as a black box and ignores its in-ternal attention signal.

3.1 Head Selection via Activation Patching 

Activation patching localizes the attention heads in different Transformer layers that causally affect the original prediction using prompt pairs that dif-fer only in the feature driving that prediction. In our case, the difference in affective state is driven by the affective cues, so we build clean–corrupted pairs (Xclean , X corr ), e.g., Xclean = “I received a delicious cake. I feel” and Xcorr = “I received a rotten cake. I feel” (Appendix A shows the de-tail of pairs construction). Two properties make these pairs suitable for causal attribution. First, every prompt ends with I feel , so the model is induced to predict an emotion word at the final position, where we read off the effect. Second, the two prompts share the same syntactic structure and differ only in the affective cue, so any differ-ence in the final-position emotion prediction can be attributed to that cue. We run the model on Xcorr as a baseline. Then, for one head h at a time, we replace only that head’s final-token output with the output it produced on the Xclean and complete the forward pass. Every-thing else stays at its corrupted value. If h encodes the cue, restoring its clean output pulls the emotion prediction back toward the clean answer. Other-wise, the model’s prediction should remain close to the corrupted-run baseline. We quantify this as the normalized logit-difference recovery formulated as: 

m(h) = L(patch h) − L (Xcorr )

L(Xclean ) − L (Xcorr ) , (1) 

L(·) = logit( eclean ) − logit( ecorr ).

Here, eclean and ecorr are the top-ranked final-position emotion words on Xclean and Xcorr , re-spectively, and patch h denotes the patched run de-scribed above. Thus m(h) = 1 when the swap fully restores the clean prediction and m(h) = 0 when it has no effect, following Belrose et al. (2023) and Tigges et al. (2023). We apply the test to every head independently and retain those with 

m(h) > τ head , and their union forms the candidate set Hcand .

3.2 Label-free Refinement on Dialogues 

Having obtained the candidate heads Hcand , we do not directly treat all of them as suitable interven-tion targets. A head may obtain a high patching score m(h) through generic mechanisms, such as positional tracking or induction-like copying (Ols-son et al., 2022), rather than by routing affective cues. In natural dialogues, affective evidence is usually carried by lexical content. We therefore use a lightweight content-token filter that removes punctuation, special tokens, pronouns, interroga-tives, and prepositions, while retaining negators and intensifiers. We then refine the candidates on dialogues by measuring their attention and value mass on content-word positions before the emo-tion prediction, using value mass because attention alone does not determine the information written to the residual stream (Kobayashi et al., 2020). For this refinement stage, we use 200 dialogues sampled from the DailyDialog dataset (Li et al., 2017). For each dialogue, we append a probe ques-tion, e.g., “What is the emotion of the last utter-ance?” , and inspect each candidate head immedi-ately before the model generates the answer. Al-though this dataset provides emotion annotations, we use only the dialogue text during this stage and do not access any gold emotion label. 4 Experimental Evaluation 

4.1 Datasets 

We evaluate on two benchmarks that stress differ-ent cue regimes. ECC (He et al., 2025) labels multi-turn dialogues (max. 831 tokens) with 10 

fine-grained emotion classes, where affective evi-dence can appear early and must remain available across many turns. GoEmotions (Demszky et al., 2020) labels short Reddit comments 1 (max. 52 to-kens) with 27 multi-label emotion categories plus a Neutral class, where cues are local and information-dense but must be separated among many fine-grained labels. We exclude samples labeled solely 

as Neutral, since they contain no non-neutral af-fective cue for cue-targeting heads to act on. For multi-label samples, we retain the non-Neutral la-bels. This leaves 3,821 test examples for evaluation. Neutral results are reported in Appendix D. 

4.2 Evaluation Metrics 

We report macro-F1 and Accuracy. For the ECC benchmark, we take the argmax over 10 labels. For the GoEmotions benchmark, we follow the multi-label setting of Demszky et al. (2020). The temperature T and threshold t are tuned on the GoEmotions dev split (The specific hyperparameter values are provided in Appendix C). and Top-1

accuracy counts the argmax label as correct if it belongs to the gold label set. We additionally quantify finite-sample uncer-tainty using paired bootstrap resampling ( B =10 ,000 ). Full pointwise 95% percentile confidence intervals and implementation details are reported in Appendix G. 

4.3 Models 

We evaluate three open, small-to-mid-sized instruction-tuned LLMs from different model families: Gemma3-4B (Gemma Team, 2025), Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025), and LLaMA3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024).

4.5 Results 

Table 1 reports results on two datasets. The re-ported changes in macro-F1 and full accuracy ( ∆)are computed relative to the ZS baseline. 

SIAB generalized across models, datasets, and prompts. Across all 18 model–prompt–dataset settings, the proposed SIAB improved over the corresponding prompting baseline using the same head set, with gains up to +10 .27 macro-F1 on the ECC benchmark and +5 .47 on the GoEmotions benchmark. The effect is not limited to ZS, but under 10-shot ECC, SIAB still added +1 .21 to +4 .25 macro-F1 across models. On GoEmotions, it also reduced harmful few-shot effects. It turned the Gemma3 model’s Mid / High Top-1 drops into gains and weakened the LLaMA3 model’s Macro-F1 drops, while flipping its High-setting Top-1 change from negative to positive. 

PASTA was strong in-domain but transferred poorly. In-domain PASTA profiles were com-petitive with, and often stronger than, ZS+SIAB. However, this strength was task-specific. Apply-ing PASTA-ECC heads to GoEmotions dropped LLaMA3 by −7.19 macro-F1, and transfer in the reverse direction was similarly unstable. PASTA-

∩ also lost in-domain performance without recov-ering cross-task gains. In contrast, SIAB uses the same causally selected head set across both datasets and gave positive ∆ in all 18 settings. Thus, PASTA reached a stronger in-domain peak when its profil-ing task matches the test task, whereas SIAB was weaker but more stable across tasks, motivating their composition. 

The two signals composed additively in five of six (model, dataset) configurations. Across five configurations, PASTA+SIAB recovered 79 –

116% of the naive sum of the two individual ZS gains, and yielded the best macro-F1 on 4 of them, indicating the two signals operate largely orthog-onally. The single exception, LLaMA3 on ECC, recovered only 54% . We attribute this to layer-band disjointness between the PASTA-profiled and SIAB head sets and quantify the relationship in §5.4. 

Effect of head selection: Evidence from Ran-dom. Across all models, datasets, and metrics, ZS+SIAB outperformed the mean score of 20 layer-matched random head subsets. Since Ran-dom uses the same layers, head-set size, and tuning protocol, this argues against a simple accumulation of layer-level effects. It also shows that same-layer heads are not interchangeable for this intervention, but the gain depends on the specific heads selected by SIAB. 

5 Mechanistic Analysis 

To analyze when and why the intervention suc-ceeded, we partition test samples into four groups based on the joint behavior of ZS and the proposed SIAB predictions: POS : ZS incorrect, SIAB cor-rect, NEG : ZS correct, SIAB incorrect, EASY :both ZS and SIAB correct, and HARD : both ZS and SIAB incorrect. 

5.1 Sparsity of the Affective Head Set 

To test whether our selected head set is sufficiently sparse, we vary K and select the top-K heads ac-cording to the ranking determined during refine-ment in §3.2. Figure 3 shows the same trend across all models and datasets, that F1 rose quickly with a few heads, saturated near a small K∗, and dropped as more heads were added. This suggests that af-fective signal is concentrated in a small head set, while lower-ranked heads mostly add noise. Since the same K∗ appears on both the ECC and GoE-motions benchmarks for each model, the sparsity seems more model-dependent than dataset-specific.

The Results section identified the LLaMA3 model on the ECC benchmark as the single non-additive outlier. Here we test whether this outlier was caused by layer disjointness between the two head sets. 

Quantifying additivity. For each PASTA head set, we compare the actual Combo gain to the sum of the two individual gains as: Additivity = ∆Combo 

∆PASTA + ∆ SIAB 

, (6) where ∆X = X − ZS. A value of 100% means the two interventions are exactly additive; < 100% in-dicates redundancy or interference between the two head sets and > 100% indicates super-additivity. 

Layer-disjointness hypothesis. We hypothesize that PASTA and the proposed SIAB compose ad-ditively only when the two head sets occupy over-lapping or adjacent Transformer layers, and breaks down when the two sets sit too many layers apart for their signals to combine. LLaMA3 on ECC was the natural witness. Its scan-selected PASTA Top-10 lay entirely on L≤11 , and Combo provided es-sentially nothing on top of PASTA alone. 6 Conclusion 

Affective state in dialogue depends on contextual cues that are already present but easily lost at pre-diction time. We showed that the proposed SIAB could route this evidence through internal model signals, without relying on externally marked spans or task-specific training. A fixed causally local-ized head set transferred across two contrasting attentional demands, whereas PASTA-style exter-nal profiles were competitive in-domain but trans-ferred poorly. Their additive gains further sug-gested that internal and external signals capture complementary aspects of affective evidence. Fu-ture work should move from a fixed global interven-tion to sample-adaptive control, where intervention strength and target tokens vary with each example’s uncertainty and attention pattern, and test whether the same interface extends to other latent conversa-tional states such as uncertainty, trust, or intent. 

Limitations 

Model scale. We evaluated three open instruction-tuned LLMs spanning 4–8B pa-rameters from distinct families (Gemma3, Qwen3, and LLaMA3). Whether the internal signal contin-ues to generalize at much larger scales (e.g., 70 B+)is an open question. Exhaustive activation patching at such scales is computationally prohibitive in our setting. 

Language coverage. Both benchmarks (ECC and GoEmotions) were in English. Whether the recovered head set transfers across languages, or whether language-specific recognition circuits would emerge under multilingual evaluation, is untested. 

Dataset breadth. The two benchmarks were se-lected to span complementary attentional demands (long-context dialogue integration vs. wide-label distribution shift over short inputs), and we reuse the same head set across them without re-derivation. Generalization to non-emotion classification tasks is left to future work. 

Boundary on HARD samples. A subset of test samples (HARD) remained unrescued even under stronger interventions. As analyzed in §5.3, Both scaling β and suppressing attention sink were in-sufficient. Whether these samples can be rescued by a more selective, sample-adaptive intervention is left to future work.

A Pair Construction for Causal Patching 

Following the minimal-pair design of Wang et al. (2023), each pair (Xclean , X corr ) shares syntactic skeleton and token length and differs only in affec-tive content. 

Coverage. The set spans the six basic emotions of Ekman (1992): Anger, Disgust, Fear, Happi-ness, Sadness, and Surprise, 100 pairs each (600 total), covering both valences and arousal levels to avoid a polarity confound. Example (Anger): clean “During class, you blamed me for the result, and I feel very” (Target angry ) vs. corrupted “. . . you praised me. . . ” (Target 

happy ). Within each category, pairs further vary the syntactic locus of the cue —verb, predicate-adjective, prenominal-adjective, or purpose-clause replacement— and distribute it across multiple con-tent words, so the discovered head set is not tied to a single syntactic position. 

Verification. We constructed these datasets from approximately 25 templates authored by human annotators. Each template contains several replace-able slots, including names, times, locations, ob-jects, and an emotion-bearing word or phrase. The emotion-bearing slots are shown in bold in the full template list. Example templates include: • “[time PP], [Name] [verb-past] me in front of [audience NP], which makes me feel” • “I received a [adj] [noun] from [person NP], and I feel” • “The [adj] [noun] appeared [time/place PP], which makes me feel” • “The results of my [noun] came back [adj] 

[time PP], and I feel” • “[Name] stayed up all night to [VP] , and I feel” Mistral-Small-3.2-24B-Instruct-2506 (Mistral AI, 2025), which is distinct from all downstream models evaluated in this work, instantiated these templates with concrete names, times, locations, objects, and emotion-bearing expressions. 

Generalization beyond localization categories. 

Only 4 of 10 labels in the ECC benchmark and 6 of 27 labels in the GoEmotions benchmark overlap with the pair-set categories. The rest are unseen during localization. Strong performance on these unseen labels as shown in §4.5, indicates that the head set generalizes beyond the specific cues used to identify it.
