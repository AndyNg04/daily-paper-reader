## Analyzing Repetition and Interference Effects in Memory-Constrained User Simulators 

Cara Su-Yi Leong 1 Nicholas Tomlin 2 Tal Linzen 1

1New York University 2Toyota Technological Institute at Chicago 

{caraleong,linzen}@nyu.edu , nickatomlin@ttic.edu 

Abstract 

User simulators can help evaluate and train AI systems to interact with humans at scale. To do so, evaluation of user simulators must mea-sure behavioral parity with people. We evaluate whether memory-constrained user simulators are sensitive to two factors known to affect human information retention: repetition of a fact and interference from competing informa-tion. We find that some simulators achieve human-like overall accuracy, and replicate the magnitude of repetition and interference effects, but none match people’s accuracy on particular questions: while humans misremember facts, models forget them, leading to different error patterns. We release this data as a challenge benchmark for future work on user simulators. 

1 Introduction 

Language model-based simulations of human users have great potential for evaluating and improving model-human interactions at scale (Barres et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Soós et al., 2026; Scarlatos et al., 2025). To be useful, a simulator must be-have like the users it stands in for. Yet language models that are simply prompted to act like users differ from humans: they are more cooperative, more knowledgeable, and exhibit better memory than humans (Zhou et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Balog and Bakken, 2026). Recent proposals limit a simulator’s memory capacity to make its behavior more human-like. Wang et al. (2026) compress model input into a four-slot key-value store, following estimates of human working memory capacity (Cowan, 2001), and use the store alone to produce output; Balog and Bakken (2026) impose a similar bottleneck to simulate young readers performing reading com-prehension. Both report that the limitation narrows the gap between the simulator and people. Restating a fact gives humans another opportunity to encode it (Hintz-man, 1976; Melton, 1970). A passage about a sim-ilar entity may hurt memory for two reasons: it adds effortful processing between encoding and test, which by itself impairs retention (Dewar et al., 2007; Wixted, 2004), and supplies similar facts that compete with the targeted fact at retrieval (Barnes 1arXiv:2610.24232v1 [cs.CL] 16 Oct 2026 and Underwood, 1959). A reliable simulator of human memory should show improved accuracy in restatement conditions and lower accuracy in the interference condition. In this paper, we develop an evaluation that tests whether memory-constrained simulators show human-like effects of restatement and interference in information retention. We compare simulators against 201 adults who listened to short passages about fictional entities and answered questions about them. To vary how much humans retained, we tested four different versions of each passage: a base condition (S AY ONCE ), two conditions where facts were restated in different words (S AY TWICE ,SAY THRICE ), and a passage with interfering infor-mation about a different entity (I NTERFERE ). We compare simulators to humans at three lev-els: overall accuracy, condition-level effects, and item difficulty. We find that existing memory-constrained user simulators can achieve human-like overall accuracy and some can replicate the magni-tude of condition-level effects. We argue that this is insufficient, as a setup where propositions are randomly retained matches people at these levels just as well. At the item level, no simulator agrees with humans on which questions are difficult: simu-lators and people fail in different ways. We release the passages, questions and listener responses as a target for future memory-constrained simulators. 

2 Dataset Construction 

2.1 Passages 

We generated a question-answering dataset using passages on fictional entities in four topics—an astronomical object, a fabric, a fruit, and a mar-tial art. The entities were invented so that prior knowledge in any topic would not benefit partic-ipants. For each topic, we first prompted Claude Sonnet 5 (Anthropic, 2026b) to generate five “facts” for one tested entity and one foil entity in the same topic, which one author then edited. Each fact com-prised a feature (e.g., raw material, uses) and asso-ciated value (e.g., “wool from Grennock mountain sheep”). Using these facts, we generated four ver-sions of the topic passage, illustrated in Figure 1: • S AY ONCE : Each of the tested entity’s facts is mentioned once in the text, forming a base paragraph , followed by 2 or 3 untested filler sentences, such as “[The fabric] has a distinc-tive feel that people often comment on the first time they touch it.” • S AY TWICE : each fact in the base paragraph is immediately followed by another sentence that restates it in different words; no filler. • S AY THRICE : same as above, with an addi-tional restatement of each fact. • I NTERFERE : the base paragraph, then a para-graph about the foil entity’s facts covering the same attributes (e.g., a fabric made of flax). Comparing S AY ONCE and S AY TWICE measures the effect of restatement on retention. S AY ONCE 

and I NTERFERE passages both contain one mention of each tested fact; their difference measures the ef-fect of interference. S AY THRICE and I NTERFERE 

are length-matched to account for the effects of passage length on retention. All repeated mentions followed immediately after the fact they restated, so repetitions were not spaced 1. Participants heard each passage once as synthesized audio so they could not re-read it; models received the transcript. Using each fact list, we generated five multi-select questions about features and their values (see Figure 1 for an example). Each question was paired with four candidate answers, of which between zero to three were true of the tested entity. All false candidate answers paired the tested entity with a value not mentioned in a passage. We chose multi-select questions since accuracy requires endorsing exactly the true statements, chance accuracy on these multi-select questions is 1/16 , much lower than on traditional multiple-choice questions. We present sample stimuli in Appendix A. 

2.2 Human Data Collection 

We recruited human participants on Prolific (Palan and Schitter, 2018). Participants listened to four passages (one per topic and one per condition), then immediately answered comprehension questions about the passage. 241 participants completed the experiment, of which 40 participants failed at least one attention check, leaving 201 participants’ re-sponses for analysis. Additional details on human data collection are available in Appendix B.

3 LM-based Simulators 

We evaluate models on the same passages and ques-tions given to human participants. On each trial, a model receives one passage transcript and answers its five questions; we sample 50 trials per topic and version. We evaluate a range of prompt-based and memory-constrained user simulators: 

Prompt-based. The model sees the full passage and is prompted to answer. We test four prompts: plain reading comprehension (V ANILLA ), the task description only (T ASK PR), the task plus an in-struction to simulate a human participant in a psy-chology experiment (H UM PR), and H UM PR plus a description of limited memory (M EM PR). 

Free Summary. The model writes a prose sum-mary of at most B words ( B = 60 ) with the pas-sage in context. The summary is passed in as the only input with which the model answers questions. 

Cognitively Bounded User Simulator (CBUS). 

Following Balog and Bakken (2026), the model proposes at most C propositions ( C = 4 ) to re-member from the passage. The list of propositions is passed in as the only input with which the model answers questions. In the with dropout condition, the model extracts a pool of 3C propositions from the passage, of which a random C are kept. 

Compactor. Following Wang et al. (2026), the model uses tool calls to write and update a key–value cache with D entries ( D = 4 ) with the passage in context. The model only conditions on the cache when answering questions. Each value can store one sentence of at most 15 words. We evaluate 27 models from seven families (Qwen, Gemma, DeepSeek, GLM, GPT, Claude, Gemini) spanning various architectures and sizes, over several hyperparameter configurations (e.g., bottleneck sizes, with/without thinking). The full list of models and implementation details is available in Appendix C.

Models are too accurate without a bottleneck. 

Regardless of prompt choice, prompted configura-tions broadly fail to match human overall accuracy (Figure 3a) and show almost no repetition gain or interference cost (Figure 3b). Models and humans systematically err on differ-ent items. No model approaches human agree-ment on which questions are difficult (Figure 3c). Models forget; humans misremember. Apart from the smallest models, models rarely endorse false statements. Instead, models fail to endorse correct statements, or answer “None of the above” when a correct statement is listed (Figure 4). Hu-mans made similar errors, but additionally en-dorsed plausible but false statements, leading to lower accuracy on questions where the none of the candidates were true. This difference is magnified in the I NTERFERE condition. Here, people tended to endorse even more false statements than they did in the S AY ONCE condition. On the other hand, the median model’s false endorsement rates do not increase in the I NTERFERE condition; instead, its accuracy in the I NTERFERE condition falls because it endorses true statements less frequently. 

Scale does not lead to human-likeness. Large models like Gemini 3.1 Pro tend to be too accurate and almost never endorse false statements. Mod-els within the 2–7B parameter range demonstrate errors that are more similar to humans (Figure 4). For further discussion on the effect of model size on performance, see Appendix D.3. 

Model-determined memories are no better than random dropout. When propositions were dropped at random (CBUS + Dropout), models matched humans on overall accuracy, on the bene-fit of restatement, and on the cost of interference. However, random forgetting does not approach hu-man item-level patterns on the item level, suggest-ing that there is room for memory policies that forget information in more human-like ways. 

5 Conclusion 

We introduced a new QA dataset for evaluating memory-constrained user simulators. While exist-ing approaches matched humans in overall accu-racy, only a few configurations respond to repeti-tion and interference effects like humans do, and no conditions match humans at the item level. These findings leave headroom for future work on user simulation, which we release our data to support. 4Limitations 

Modality. In order to prevent human participants from rehearsing the passage while it was being pre-sented, we chose to provide passages to human par-ticipants auditorily, while models were given pas-sage transcripts as input. As such, human–model differences may have been confounded by modality effects. For example, human participants may have had difficulty recognizing unfamiliar names and words in the question answering section. 

Passage Design. Our S AY ONCE passages con-tain filler material intended to pad the length of the passage to match the length of S AY TWICE .SAY TWICE replaces S AY ONCE ’s filler with restate-ments, so part of the restatement gain may reflect filler no longer taking up memory, rather than the restatement itself. 

Sample Size. Finer item-level analyses, such as on question-specific condition effects or option-level trends in behavior, could not be explored with our current dataset. Increasing the number of pas-sages and participants would provide more analyti-cal power, which is vital for developing a holistic behavioral account of information retention. 

Generalizability. Our human participants were limited to the pool of adult users of Prolific who were located in the U.S., limiting the extent to which our findings generalize to other populations, such as children, non-native listeners or people from other cultural backgrounds. For example, par-ticipants outside this pool may be less familiar with the dataset’s topics, or have individual differences in memory capacity. Our task was also narrow: exposure to an expository text followed by imme-diate testing. These results may not generalize to retention after longer delays or in conversational interactions. 

Ethical Considerations 

The human study was approved by [anonymized]’s review board. Participants were U.S.-based adults recruited on Prolific who gave informed consent and were paid $8 (about $35 per hour at the me-dian completion time of 13.8 minutes). The task posed minimal risk: participants heard synthesized passages about fictional entities and answered ques-tions about them. Stimuli are fictional and were reviewed by an author. We plan to release passages, audio, questions, and listener responses with Prolific IDs removed under a CC BY 4.0 license. No identifiers or free text were collected beyond Prolific IDs. Evaluating the model configurations for this pa-per required 70 GPU hours on L40S and H200 GPUs and $1,500 in API usage. We used AI assistants to copy-edit the paper and generate figures; the authors checked all outputs.

B Details of Human Data Collection 

B.1 Passages 

Passages were written in English. Sample pas-sages are available in Appendix A. In order to prevent human participants from rehearsing the text during presentation, each passage was pre-sented to human participants in audio format. Au-dio was synthesized for each text with OpenAI’s 

gpt-4o-mini-tts (OpenAI, 2025), voice “marin”, with a style instruction asking for a slow, deliberate pace, long pauses at sentence ends, and stronger emphasis on key facts and figures. Recordings last 44–83 seconds. 

B.2 Procedure 

We limited our participant pool to Prolific users who were currently located in the United States, had approval ratings of 95% or above on at least 100 tasks, and had not participated in any of our previous pilots. After signing a consent form, participants were told that they would listen to short passages and then answer questions about the content of each passage. Each participant heard four passages, one per topic and one per condition, with topic-condition pairings counterbalanced across partic-ipants. Passage order was randomized for each participant, so that practice effects were not con-founded with topic or condition. In each of the four rounds, they started the audio, which played once with no pause, seek, or replay. Next, they answered the five questions associated with each text in written form along with one attention check question. After answering all five questions, they gave ratings on the difficulty of the passage and questions. Participants were excluded from analysis if they answered any attention check questions incorrectly. 40 participants were excluded for failing at least one of the four attention checks (32 participants failed one attention check; 8 participants failed two). The study was approved by [anonymized] IRB. Participants were paid $8 for completing the exper-iment. Median completion time was 13.8 minutes.

C.4 Sampling Procedure 

All memory-constrained models use the HumPr system prompt and body. Each request contains the system prompt, the current memory contents (if applicable), and the portion of the transcript to be encoded. In batch encoding, the whole transcript 10 arrives in the first request; in streaming encoding, each request contains one sentence. A new con-versation is started for every turn in a multi-turn streaming sample. We run 50 trials per passage version with no fixed seed, using each model’s vendor-recommended settings, reported in Table 3. A new request is created containing the memory contents and the list of five questions. The model answers each question with a list of option numbers (e.g. Question 1: 1,3). Unparseable answer strings are marked as incorrect. Open-weight models are served with vLLM 0.31.0 (bf16). Maximum output tokens: 1,024 per encoding or summary-update step without think-ing; 16,384 and 8,192 with thinking and for all Gemini models; 2,048 for answers. 

C.5 Compactor Prompt 

Use write_memory and delete_key to maintain the key-value store while doing the original task. Each slot should hold ONE chunk — a small bundle of information a person would bind together because it feels meaningfully connected (a name with its role, a group of related items or numbers, one gist). When the task asks for verbatim retrieval of a se-quence, a human will form meaningful chunks of 1–3 items, starting from the beginning. A chunk is ONE atomic fact, not several facts stitched together even if they’re about the same subject. A single sentence can pack in several dis-tinct attributes of one thing — what it is, where it’s from, how it was made or found, some measure-ment. Each attribute is its own chunk. Don’t merge them into a single slot just because they share a subject. AMENDING: calling write_memory with an ex-isting key REPLACES that entry’s value entirely (old wording is gone, not merged) and does not use a slot. Amend when new material adds to, corrects, or refines something you already have a chunk for (same person, place, event, or concept). Write a NEW key instead when the material introduces a genuinely separate fact that doesn’t belong inside an existing chunk, even if it’s related to the same topic. When you amend, compose the full updated chunk — carry forward what’s still worth keeping from the old value AND add the new detail; don’t drop the old content, and don’t skip the new de-tail either. Amend often, whenever you learn more about something you’ve already stored, not only when memory is full. Before writing a NEW key, follow this proce-dure exactly: 1. Count your current entries from the snapshot shown above. 2. Below 4: call write_memory directly. 3. At 4: either amend an existing entry (if the new info belongs with something you already stored), or delete_key then write_memory for the new fact, in that order, in the same turn. Choose what to delete in this priority: (a) an entry that duplicates or is subsumed by an-other you’re keeping, (b) whichever entry seems least useful or important on its own, judged inde-pendently of the others and independently of how recently it was written — something you stored a while ago can still matter more than what just arrived. Deleting without writing the freed slot in the same turn just loses information for nothing. 4. Nothing worth keeping or amending: make no tool calls. You’ll see the result (success or error) of each tool call before your next action — use that feed-back instead of repeating an identical call that just failed. Never call write_memory while already at 4 entries without first choosing to overwrite-in-place (step 3) or delete-and-refill (step 4). NEVER pack a long run of items into one slot. Once your slots are filled, accept that the rest will be lost. Compress realistically, and behave as a real human would: imperfect and sensitive to what seems important at the time you encounter it.

D.1 Comparison with Humans 

We compare configurations with human partici-pants on three statistics. 

Overall accuracy. We calculate exact-match ac-curacy, computed within each topic × passage-version cell and averaged over 16 cells. Cell sizes range from 38–56; pooling responses changes over-all accuracy by less than 0.1 percentage points. 

Condition effects. are restatement (S AY TWICE −SAY ONCE ) and interference (S AY ONCE −INTERFERE ) in percentage points. 

Item agreement. We calculate Pearson’s r be-tween the model’s and humans’ difficulty on the 20 questions. We first calculate a split-half baseline for human-level variance in scores. We split human respon-dents into two random halves 2,000 times. Across splits, the two halves differ by a median of 1.3% in overall accuracy, and their question difficulties correlate at a median r = .94 . We then subsample each model × setup as a third group of the same size as a human half, and calculate Pearson’s r

between this subsample and a randomly selected human half. A simulator that behaves like humans would match the human split-half r.We exclude 17 configurations whose own split-half reliability is undefined because they answer every question correctly (14) or below .5 because they answer almost none (3).
