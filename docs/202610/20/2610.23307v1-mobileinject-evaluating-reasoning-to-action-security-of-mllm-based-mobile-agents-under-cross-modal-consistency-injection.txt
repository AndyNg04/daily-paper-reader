Abstract 

Multimodal large language model (MLLM)-based mobile agents are rapidly evolving from conversational assistants into autonomous systems that perceive, reason over, and act on smartphone environments. This capability also exposes a new security risk: adversaries can embed malicious objectives in cross-application mobile content and steer agent behav-ior through indirect multimodal injection. Although prompt injection has been studied in desktop and web settings, its cross-modal properties in mobile ecosystems remain poorly understood, and existing security analyses are still limited. To close this gap, we introduce MobileInject, the first benchmark for evaluating mobile agents against multimodal injection attacks and reasoning-to-action security risks. MobileInject provides a high-fidelity interactive mobile evaluation frame-work covering 11 scenarios, 9 security-risk categories, and 108 executable risk instances. It further introduces Cross-Modal Consistency Injection to exploit corroboration across modalities, two enhanced attack methods, Multi-Role Collab-orative Deception and Reasoning-Camouflage Injection, to intensify trust manipulation and reasoning hijacking, and a Reasoning Security Diagnosis Framework for fine-grained failure analysis. Systematic experiments show that mobile agents are broadly vulnerable to multimodal injection, and that many failures stem from reasoning hijacking at early stages. These findings establish MobileInject as a system-atic cross-modal benchmark for studying and improving the security of mobile agents.

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are increas-ingly evolving from passive conversational interfaces into autonomous mobile agents capable of operating in real-world smartphone environments [19, 25, 38]. Unlike conventional

They can observe mobile screenshots, parse interface structures, switch across applications, click buttons, scroll interfaces, enter text, and complete multi-step user tasks. This transition from mul-timodal understanding to mobile task execution substantially expands the capability boundary of foundation models, en-abling personalized agents to act as autonomous operators for communication, scheduling, information retrieval, payment, file management, and other daily mobile workflows [1, 24]. However, this leap in execution capability also significantly expands the attack surface and amplifies the consequences of security risks [8, 21, 37]. In traditional LLM settings, ad-versarial instructions are usually delivered through explicit prompts [20, 23, 40]; in mobile-agent settings, however, ma-licious objectives can be embedded into the broader mo-bile ecosystem and encountered during normal task execu-tion [8, 37]. For example, adversarial content may be hidden in SMS messages, emails, calendar events, webpages, social media content, or other cross-application content. Once per-ceived by the agent, such content may enter its decision con-text through visual or textual observations, thereby influencing its reasoning, planning, and action execution. Therefore, at-tacks against mobile agents are no longer limited to inducing unsafe text generation, but may lead to more threatening con-sequences, such as privacy leakage, unauthorized operations, financial misconduct, data disclosure, or persistent changes to system and application states. Mobile-agent security is particularly challenging because the mobile ecosystem exposes a broad, temporally distributed, and multimodal attack surface. First, a mobile agent operates in a continuous environment jointly formed by notifications, messages, emails, calendars, webpages, social media feeds, and third-party application states. Because mobile agents commonly ground decisions on both rendered and structured views of the same interface state, attacker-authored content can naturally surface in synchronized visual and textual channels during normal execution. This creates a joint attack surface that single-channel evaluations cannot characterize. More fundamentally, attacker-authored content can enter and manipulate the agent’s reasoning through these observations, displace the trusted user goal, and thereby hijack subsequent mobile actions. Third, because mobile devices are tightly coupled with private data, identity credentials, social relationships, payment systems, and local files, a lack of risk awareness or failures in the reasoning process may lead to privacy leakage, unauthorized operations, system-state dis-ruption, and further cascading propagation through persistent carriers such as calendars, email, and shared cross-application content. Despite growing attention to agent security, existing evalua-tion methods remain insufficient for characterizing this threat model [5, 37], as summarized in Table 1. First, many bench-marks rely on unrealistic sandbox environments, making it difficult to capture risks that emerge in realistic, dynamic, and cross-application mobile environments [19, 33]. Second, existing evaluations lack systematic benchmarks for multi-modal indirect injection in mobile agents. They typically focus on a single textual channel or a single injection carrier, whereas real mobile agents jointly consume various types of cross-application and cross-modal information [10,35]. Third, current evaluations often adopt coarse-grained binary assess-ment schemes that only superficially determine whether an attack succeeds [6, 21]. Such outcome-only evaluation cannot reveal whether the agent recognizes the risk, when its rea-soning begins to follow the adversarial objective, or whether the compromised reasoning further propagates into executed actions. These limitations further raise three critical questions: First, can multimodal injection exploit the cross-modal attack sur-face to amplify the effectiveness of single-modal injection? Second, how can stronger attack strategies be designed to in-duce misplaced trust in malicious content and deeply hijack agent reasoning? Third, how can a fine-grained Reasoning Security Diagnosis Framework move beyond outcome-level success rates to expose when, how, and how deeply agent reasoning is compromised, and whether such compromise propagates to downstream actions?

2 Related Work 

Evolution of Personalized Agents. Personalized agents have evolved from language-only assistants into multimodal opera-tors capable of planning, tool use, and interaction with stateful digital environments. Early methods such as ReAct coupled reasoning with external actions, showing that language mod-els can support sequential decision-making beyond static re-sponse generation [34]. Tool-augmented and function-calling agents extended this paradigm by enabling API and applica-tion use for user goals [5,15,37]. Recent work has increasingly moved toward graphical and mobile interfaces: AppAgent, Mobile-Agent, and Mobile-Agent-v2 study smartphone in-teraction [24, 25, 38]; OSWorld and AndroidWorld provide dynamic computer and Android environments [19, 33]; and DigiRL explores training device-control agents in the wild [1]. Collectively, these advances shift agents from prompt-centric assistants to operators over private, multimodal, and stateful environments, where untrusted external content may appear in both rendered and structured observations and directly in-fluence actions. Security therefore depends not only on output harmlessness, but also on whether untrusted observations are treated as actionable instructions and whether their influence propagates from perception and reasoning to execution. 

From Large-Model Security to Agent Security. Progress 3in multimodal understanding has been accompanied by growing attention to adversarial robustness and model secu-rity [26–32]. Large-model security initially focused on harm-ful generation, bias, privacy leakage, jailbreaks, and prompt robustness [23, 40]. As models became integrated with tools, applications, and external data, risks shifted from text genera-tion to observation consumption, tool invocation, state tran-sitions, and multi-step execution. Indirect prompt injection exemplifies this shift: malicious instructions embedded in third-party content can be retrieved by an application or tool and induce attacker-preferred actions [8, 35, 37]. AgentDojo and ToolEmu evaluate tool-using or sandboxed agents [5, 21]; later studies expand evaluation to privacy leakage, financial loss, unsafe tool use, over-trust in tool outputs, and insufficient risk awareness [11, 39]. In GUI and mobile settings, GhostEI-Bench and OS-Sentinel study environmental injection and trajectory-level safety detection [3, 22], while AgentDoG and DTAP emphasize realistic environments, red-teaming, and fine-grained risk attribution [4, 12]. Nevertheless, existing evaluations still lack a systematic benchmark for multimodal indirect injection in realistic mo-bile ecosystems. They neither test whether attacker-authored content in synchronized visual and textual views of the same mobile state reinforces the adversarial objective beyond either channel alone, nor diagnose when such observations redirect reasoning and whether that deviation propagates into unsafe actions and verified outcomes. Consequently, current evalua-tions cannot fully characterize the practical risk of multimodal injection or the security value of agents and defenses beyond aggregate success rates. These limitations motivate MobileIn-ject, a high-fidelity benchmark for multimodal injection and fine-grained reasoning-security diagnosis in mobile agents. 

3 MobileInject 

In this section, we present MobileInject for evaluating Visual– Textual Observation injection and reasoning-to-action secu-rity in MLLM-based mobile agents. Fig. 2 exposes the bench-mark’s complete input–construction–execution–verification chain; the following subsections define its threat model, con-crete contents, CMCI protocol, and reinforced attack tem-plates. The design of MobileInject is organized around the first two research questions: Q.1 motivates a controlled injec-tion protocol that keeps the adversarial objective fixed while varying its visual and textual carriers, and Q.2 motivates rein-forced methods designed to increase trust-manipulation and reasoning-hijacking pressure. 

3.1 Design Principles and Threat Model 

Design Principles. MobileInject is guided by four principles. 

First, ecological realism : attacks are embedded in executable, interactive, and stateful mobile workflows, thereby preserving cross-app interactions, dynamic interface changes, and action consequences. Second, systematic coverage : the benchmark encompasses diverse scenarios, workflows, injection vectors, attack goals, and security outcomes. Third, controllable and extensible attack design : injections are defined as config-urable templates that can be composed along dimensions such as modality, vector, and complexity. Fourth, trajectory-level observability : MobileInject records step-by-step obser-vations, reasoning, actions, goals, and states, thereby enabling fine-grained diagnosis beyond binary safe/unsafe labels. 

Threat Model. MobileInject adopts a trusted-instruction, untrusted-observation threat model. System instructions and explicit user task objectives define the trusted root, while all environmental observations during task execution are con-sidered potentially untrusted. These observations include vi-sual inputs (e.g., screenshots, images, webpages, emails, mes-sages, social-media content, calendar events, and notifications) and textual inputs (e.g., XML hierarchies, accessibility trees, UI layouts, element attributes, application names, and other UI metadata). Attacker can inject malicious payloads only through these observable surfaces, but cannot alter the system prompt, model parameters, operating system, or genuine user instructions. Payloads may appear in visual channel, textual channel, or both, forming cross-modal injections. This formu-lation treats cross-application content uniformly as part of the observation space and enables evaluation of realistic indirect attacks, including both unimodal and multimodal injections. 

3.2 Benchmark Construction 

High-Fidelity Environment. MobileInject executes each in-stance in a resettable Android emulator rather than as a static prompt–response sample. The agent observes the rendered screen and UI/XML structure, reasons over them, and interacts through taps, long presses, swipes, and text entry. Application and device states evolve after each action. 

Benchmark Contents. MobileInject consists of aworkflow-grounded core and a systematic payload stress layer. The core includes nine benign workflows—messaging, contact creation, email, social posting, note writing, file management, calendar, paper trading, and message-to-web navigation—covering 11 application scenarios. A workflow is included only if it satisfies five criteria: a common or safety-relevant user goal, a natural third-party-content carrier, feasi-ble benign and malicious execution paths, a resettable initial state, and an objective state oracle. The stress layer defines 12 registered adversarial objectives spanning nine consequence categories: content misuse, privacy exposure, financial mis-conduct, privilege abuse, data-integrity compromise, resource abuse, hazardous-content access, discrimination, and phish-ing/social engineering. In total, MobileInject comprises 108 executable instances with persistent and realistically evolv-ing states, each grounded in a concrete mobile workflow and equipped with resettable initialization and verifiable outcome oracles. Q.1 Can multimodal injection exploit the cross-modal at-tack surface to amplify the effectiveness of single-modal injection? 

3.3 Cross-Modal Consistency Injection 

The tested agents jointly receive a Visual Observation and a Textual Observation. In the dual-channel condition, identical injected content appears in rendered application content (e.g., a message, email, webpage, or post) and in the corresponding textual interface representation. Q.2 How can stronger attack strategies be designed to induce misplaced trust in malicious content and deeply hijack agent reasoning? 

3.4 Reinforced Injection Methods 

For Q.2, MobileInject introduces two complementary tem-plate families under a fixed adversarial objective: MRCD supplies role-like authority, priority, and consent cues, while RCI supplies externally visible pseudo-rationale shaped to the current task context. The ablations and matched controls verify their complementary contributions. 

Multi-Role Collaborative Deception. This method con-structs multiple role-like external signals to jointly support the same adversarial objective. Unlike presenting a single ma-licious instruction, the injected content frames the objective as a high-priority, apparently user-initiated, consent-confirmed, urgent, and rationalized task. Specifically, this method simu-lates role fragments that correspond to authority-like priority, user goal declaration, consent confirmation, and action ratio-nale, respectively. These fragments do not truly grant system authority or represent user consent; instead, they test whether the agent incorrectly treats role-like text in the environment as trusted control information. This method therefore stresses whether the agent can preserve the boundary between trusted instructions and untrusted observations when environmental content imitates authority, consent, and task justification.

RCI exposes the adver-sarial objective through three visible components: current app context, a bridge from the benign task state, and the registered adversarial instruction. 4 Reasoning Security Diagnosis Framework 

Q.3 How can a fine-grained Reasoning Security Diagnosis Framework move beyond outcome-level success rates to expose when, how, and how deeply agent reasoning is compromised, and whether such compromise propagates to downstream actions? 

RSDF complements state-verified endpoints with evidence-grounded diagnosis of the externally recorded execution trace. As summarized in Fig. 2, it contains five modules with distinct responsibilities: (1) Trajectory Collector records the post-exposure observation, emitted rationale, executed action and target, and resulting device state; (2) State Verifier evaluates benign and adversarial completion using deterministic An-droid/application oracles; (3) Reasoning Diagnoser assigns evidence-localized awareness, rationale-hijack, and action-hijack labels; (4) Profile Aggregator converts finalized labels and oracle outcomes into episode- and model-level metrics; and (5) Visual Reporter presents the metric profile together with representative evidence-bearing trajectories. 5.3 Additional Diagnostic Analysis 

RATM Analysis. Fig. 10 uses the formally defined Safe Alignment (SA), Action Drift (AD), Reasoning Constraint (RCO), and Complete Hijack (FH) labels. FH accounts for a large fraction of observed post-exposure states for most models, while SA remains limited. By jointly labeling the emitted rationale and executed action at each step, RATM localizes how malicious-goal adoption propagates into the unsafe action path. 

OCM Analysis. Table 6 decomposes the recorded episode endpoints by benign and adversarial oracle values. Safe-complete endpoints are rare for most models, whereas unsafe-fail endpoints are frequent. OCM complements the aggregate UA and AGR measures by showing which of the four end-point combinations each model reaches. 

Analysis of Risk Consequences. Table 7 reports objective-family results. These families map to 9 top-level risk conse-quences, including the merger of content tampering and data destruction under data-integrity compromise. Low UA and high AGR/RHR/AHR across several families demonstrate broad attack transfer and reasoning-to-action failures across distinct consequence categories. 

Analysis across Different Normal Tasks. Table 8 shows failures across all implemented benign workflows, demonstrat-ing that the observed vulnerabilities transfer across MobileIn-ject’s diverse application and task contexts. MobileInject orga-nizes its 108 executable instances around workflow-grounded mobile scenarios and systematic adversarial-payload cover-age. Workflow realism comes from stateful tasks with natural untrusted-content carriers; the complete construction probes whether registered payloads transfer across workflow con-12 texts. 

5.4 Performance of Different Defense Methods under MobileInject 

Table 9 shows that the evaluated prompt-level defenses pro-vide only limited protection under MobileInject. Although SCoT raises OSS from 7.96 to 23.52 and lowers AGR from 83.33% to 70.37%, attack rates remain high; RHR and AHR still yield AGRs of 84.26% and 80.56%, and Safety Sandwich raises UA only to 9.26%. These results indicate that prompt-level guidance alone is insufficient to reliably preserve the trusted task under cross-modal injection pressure. More effec-tive multimodal defenses are therefore needed, particularly mechanisms for source-aware observation filtering, cross-modal consistency checking, and state-grounded action verifi-cation. The archived B1–A10 pair illustrates this limitation. Without defense, the agent adopts the injected priority and executes the registered harmful query; with SCoT, it detects the risk at the first post-exposure decision and refuses, but the defended run also fails the benign oracle, revealing a clear safety–utility trade-off. Across the paired endpoint analysis, SCoT reduces AGR by 12.96 points, yet its cluster interval crosses zero ([ −3.70, 33.33]) and its Holm-adjusted value re-mains non-significant ( pHolm = 0.486). Taken together, these results show that existing prompt-level defenses do not pro-vide reliable protection under MobileInject and underscore the need for stronger multimodal safeguards for secure de-ployment of mobile agents. 

5.5 RSDF and MobileInject Variance Analysis 

Fig. 5 reports empirical repeated-run variances of at most 2.15 for the displayed RSDF metrics and 3.85 for the displayed endpoint metrics on the 0–100 scale, demonstrating stable measurements across the sampled reruns. For the three GLM-4.5V MobAgent endpoint matrices, the embedded seed field gives AGR values of 81.5% (2026; 88/108), 80.6% (2027; 87/108), and 83.3% (2028; 90/108), a 2.7-point range. The di-rectory labels for the 2026 and 2028 files are swapped, so we use the embedded seed rather than the folder name. These end-point matrices confirm within-condition repeatability; RSDF repeatability is reported from the archived diagnostic outputs shown in Fig. 5. 

6 Conclusion 

This paper presents MobileInject, an executable benchmark for evaluating multimodal injection and reasoning-to-action security in MLLM-based mobile agents. MobileInject mod-els mobile agents as stateful closed-loop systems in realistic Android workflows and instantiates attacks through CMCI, which aligns visual and textual observations to reinforce mali-cious objectives, MRCD, which intensifies trust manipulation through coordinated multi-role deception, and RCI, which camouflages adversarial objectives as locally coherent task reasoning. It further introduces RSDF for trajectory-level diagnosis of risk awareness, reasoning hijacking, action hi-jacking, and their propagation. Experiments on 2 × 9 mobile agents show that current systems are broadly vulnerable to injections through untrusted visual and textual observations. Cross-modal alignment reinforces attacks, compromised rea-soning frequently propagates into unsafe actions, and exist-ing prompt-level defenses offer only limited protection. To-gether, these findings establish MobileInject and RSDF as a benchmark and diagnostic foundation for developing safer autonomous mobile agents.
