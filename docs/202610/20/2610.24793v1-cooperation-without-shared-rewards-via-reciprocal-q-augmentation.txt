## Abstract 

General-sum games are more challenging than fully cooperative or adversarial set-tings because agents may deceive or exploit one another. Achieving high social welfare requires sustained cooperation, while a single defection can drive agents to-ward a welfare suboptimal Nash equilibrium. Moreover, assuming access to other agents’ rewards or internal states is often unrealistic and implicitly introduces ad-ditional cooperation into the setting. To address this issue, we introduce Reciprocal Q-Augmentation (RQA), a method that uses a latent-variable opponent model to augment the Q-function with estimates of how one’s actions affects other agents. RQA allows agents to learn both cooperative and defective policies using only their own Q-function. We show that, in social dilemmas, agents that all follow the cooper-ative policy of RQA learn to cooperate faster and reach higher levels of cooperation than fully cooperative algorithms. Moreover, a single RQA agent that switches appropriately between its cooperative and defecting policies can induce coopera-tion in agents trained with standard RL algorithms, which would otherwise end up defecting. We evaluate our approach on the Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma, Iterated Traveler’s Dilemma, Iterated Stag Hunt, and CleanUp-Lite . Our experiments show that the algorithms implementing RQA outperforms fully cooperative methods with shared rewards and enables cooperation with algorithms that otherwise fail to reach high social welfare. 

## 1 Introduction 

Cooperation between multi-agent reinforcement learning agents is a critical problem, especially if the end goal is to deploy such agents in realistic and complex settings (Dafoe et al., 2020). This prob-lem becomes particularly challenging when full cooperation is not necessarily unilaterally optimal from the perspective of each individual agent, despite Pareto dominating the Nash equilibrium the agents would otherwise reach, and thus making every player better off. These settings fall into the category of general-sum games, which generalize both common-reward (fully cooperative) games, where objectives are fully aligned, and two-player zero-sum games, where they are strictly opposed. Falling at neither extreme introduces an additional difficulty: an agent must account not only for how its actions affect its own return, but also for whether the behavior of the other agents makes cooperation beneficial or exploitable. 1arXiv:2610.24793v1 [cs.GT] 17 Oct 2026 The specific settings under which we study this problem are sequential social dilemmas (Leibo et al., 2017). Social dilemmas capture situations in which individually rational behavior can lead to collectively inefficient outcomes: exploiting a cooperative player can be more rewarding than mutual cooperation, while mutual defection can be preferable to being exploited. At the same time, mutual cooperation yields the highest collective welfare. Sequential social dilemmas extend this tension to temporally extended interactions, where cooperation and defection correspond to policies or behavioral patterns rather than isolated one-shot actions (Leibo et al., 2017). An agent must therefore reason about whether the behavior of the other player warrants cooperation over an extended interaction, while remaining able to react when that cooperation is not reciprocated. The tension between cooperation and exploitation is then at the core of the problem we consider: 

How can an agent learning only from its own reward sustain cooperation, resist exploitation, and induce cooperation in a co-player that would otherwise defect? Limiting our agents to observe only their own reward makes this question particularly challenging and relevant to realistic applications. 

Mechanisms for Cooperation Several mechanisms have been proposed to support the emer-gence of cooperation in social dilemmas. One possibility is to allow agents to select whom they interact with (Anastassacos et al., 2020; Leung et al., 2026). In Anastassacos et al. (2020), agents can observe the recent actions of other players and select partners based on their interaction history, while Leung et al. (2026) studies a setting in which direct experience is supplemented by more limited third-party observations. In both cases, partner selection allows agents to preferentially interact with cooperative players and avoid or retaliate against defectors, effectively learning a tit-for-tat strat-egy (Axelrod & Hamilton, 1981). A different direction modifies the incentives under which agents learn. For example, Wang et al. (2019) uses an evolutionary process to learn intrinsic motivations that encourage altruistic behavior, modifying the reward signal on which individual agents learn. More recently, Demir et al. (2026) studies cooperation under asymmetric incentives and adapts fairness-based intrinsic motivations to account for differences between agents. For a broader review of methods for promoting cooperation in multi-agent reinforcement learning and social dilemmas, we refer to Du et al. (2023). In this work, we are interested in a complementary setting in which cooperation must emerge without selecting interaction partners or explicitly modifying the agents’ rewards, and to do so, we have our agent model its opponent. We restrict ourselves to two-player games, the setting of the classical social dilemmas we consider and the one in which reciprocity is unambiguous, since a defection can be attributed to a single opponent and the punishment directed at it. 

Opponent Modeling Deciding whether cooperation should be maintained naturally requires rea-soning about the behavior of the other agent. Opponent modeling provides a general framework for incorporating such information into an agent’s decision making. Previous work has, for example, learned latent representations of other agents from behavioral observations (Rabinowitz et al., 2018), explicitly incorporated learned opponent models into reinforcement learning (Tian et al., 2019), and used variational latent-variable models to represent different opponent behaviors (Papoudakis & Al-brecht, 2020). Related approaches use latent opponent representations to adapt online to unknown or non-stationary opponents (Fu et al., 2022). Opponent modeling is a means rather than an end in our work: we do not introduce a new general opponent-modeling methodology. Instead, we use a latent-variable model to learn cooperative and adversarial strategies, which can then be used to induce cooperation. Given the current state, a latent variable encoding information about the other player, and a candidate action of the controlled agent, the model predicts the action of the other player. This differs from simply identifying a fixed opponent policy: the conjecture is intended to model how the other player’s behavior responds to our own actions. 

Reciprocity and learning-aware adaptation Most closely related to our approach are methods that use reciprocity or learning-aware behavior to cooperate while limiting vulnerability to exploita-tion. Lerer & Peysakhovich (2018) generalize ideas from tit-for-tat to sequential social dilemmas by learning cooperative and defective behaviors and switching between them according to the behavior 2of the other player. We introduce a switching mechanism inspired by this idea, but that does not re-quire learning policies for both players in advance. Instead, we train the cooperative and adversarial policies concurrently, and choose between them based on the predicted response of the other agent. Moreover, our alternative to cooperation is an explicitly adversarial policy, rather than a standard self-interested policy trained independently. Eccles et al. (2019) build on reciprocity, with learning agents that adapt their social behavior to that of their co-players. Their approach does so through an intrinsic motivation mechanism that modifies the learner’s effective reward according to an estimate of the other player’s sociality. In contrast, our approach does not modify the reward function, but explicitly chooses between cooperative and adversarial behaviors based on a model of the opponent. Another closely related objective is that of “foolproof” cooperation (Jacq et al., 2020), which seeks cooperative strategies that cannot profitably be exploited by selfish agents. Agents can fully com-municate and access each others actions and rewards, while using a jointly obtained cooperative Q-function to assess the level of cooperation. The resulting algorithm mostly prevents exploitation when used against a standard RL algorithm, being able to induce cooperation only when multiple instances are deployed in the same game. Finally, Meulemans et al. (2025) study how learning-aware agents can achieve cooperation by ac-counting for and shaping the learning dynamics of other agents, including in sequential social dilem-mas. This provides a complementary mechanism for adapting cooperation to other learners. Our approach does not explicitly model the opponent’s learning rule or its parameter updates; instead, it uses the opponent’s observed behavior to infer a latent behavioral state and predict its response to the agent’s own actions. 

Contributions Taken together, these approaches demonstrate that cooperation can be supported through reciprocity, incentive modification, opponent adaptation, or reasoning about other learn-ers. Our goal is to retain the ability to cooperate conditionally, cooperating when cooperation is reciprocated and responding adversarially otherwise, while requiring neither shared nor modified rewards, explicit communication, nor privileged access to the other agent’s reward or policy. At the same time, both behaviors and the mechanism used to select between them can be learned dur-ing interaction. To achieve these goals, we introduce the following contribution: (1) An innovative method for learning cooperative behavior in general-sum games using only individual rewards, with-out requiring shared rewards, explicit communication, or access to the other agents’ policies. (2) We show that an agent trained with our method can be frozen and used as a cooperation teacher ,inducing cooperative behavior in independently learning agents that would otherwise converge to non-cooperative behavior; (3) We further show that this teaching effect does not require a separately converged self-play phase: cooperation can be induced while the proposed agent and its co-player are still learning concurrently. In the considered sequential social dilemmas, the resulting agents achieve social welfare and cooperation levels comparable to, and in some settings better than, fully cooperative baselines.

## 3 Reciprocal Q-Augmentation 

With our approach we set to achieve two main goals: i) develop an agent that is a very good cooperator, in that it pushes towards cooperation; ii) the same agent should not be easily exploited by free-riders. To achieve this goal, we propose a method called reciprocal Q-augmentation (RQA). We present it incrementally: we first show how an agent can learn to cooperate while observing only its own reward, and then introduce a mechanism that prevents it from being exploited while still promoting cooperation. Our core hypothesis is that maintaining an internal model of the other player is fundamental for learning when to cooperate and when to defect, especially when agents do not have access to each other’s rewards. Building on this idea, our method starts by learning an opponent model, which is then used to augment the agent’s Q-function. This augmentation encourages cooperation while also preventing the agent from being exploited. In Section 3.1, we describe how we model the other players in practice. To do so, we import into multi-agent reinforcement learning the game-theoretic notion of a conjecture : rather than predicting the opponent’s behavior alone, we predict how the opponent responds to the agent’s own action. In Section 3.2, we then show how this model is incorporated into the Q-function, in Section 3.3 we introduce how the agent can avoid exploitation and improve cooperation, finally in Section 3.4. In the following sections, we adopt the perspective of agent i facing its opponent j.

We do not claim a novel opponent-modeling approach: the opponent 4model is only a tool to augment the Q-function, and other models could be used instead. Our approach only requires the model to predict the response of player j to a candidate action of player i,rather than the behavior of j alone. 3.2 Cooperative Q-Augmentation 

Our main contribution is on how to use the opponent model to augment the Q-function. This is loosely inspired by the idea of perspective taking , which is a phenomenon mostly studied and observed in behavioral economics and psychology, showing how considering the perspective of your opponent (or teammate) can alter your decision, both towards a more cooperative action (Galinsky et al., 2008), or a more egotistical one (Epley et al., 2006). Following this idea we introduce a cooperative augmentation for the Q-function: 

QCi (s, a i, a j ) = Qi(s, a i, a j ) + αCi Qi(s, γ ji (ai, s, z i,j ), a i). (6) The second term, weighted by the hyper-parameter αCi ∈ [0 , ∞], is what pushes the agent towards cooperation. Agent i is effectively trying to maximize the reward from the perspective of agent j,in addition to its own. In this sense, QCi can be seen as a crude approximation of the cumulative expected reward of the two agents, which we consider to be the social welfare of the system, and agent i can be seen as maximizing this approximated social welfare. 

Remark 1. From Eq. (6) , one might expect that a perfectly symmetric game is needed to obtain a useful policy. In practice, we only require the two agents to have the same available actions and the same game logic. For example, in the Prisoner’s Dilemma, it is sufficient that the payoff order exploiting > mutual cooperation > mutual defection > exploited holds for both players, even with different numerical rewards. In Section 4, we validate this on asymmetric games. 

The benefit of Eq. (6) is that it does not require introducing a second Q-function, sharing rewards between agents, or accessing the other agent’s policy. Moreover, because the modification is applied directly to the Q-function, independently of the policy learning algorithm, it can be incorporated into any actor-critic method that learns a Q-function, such as Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) (Lowe et al., 2017) or Multi-Agent Soft Actor Critic (MASAC) (Haarnoja 6et al., 2018). Finally, the modification is agnostic to the action-space type and can therefore be applied to both discrete and continuous environments. The augmented Q-function is represented in the yellow box ( final policy ) in Fig. 1, schematically showing how the environment and the opponent model connect to it. 

3.3 Enabling Reciprocal Cooperation via Cooperative and Defective Q-Functions 

As mentioned in the previous section, considering the opponent’s perspective can also lead to adver-sarial and/or selfish behavior. Using this intuition we can easily obtain an augmented Q-function that can be used to learn a defective policy: 

QDi (s, a i, a j ) = Qi(s, a i, a j ) − αDi Qi(s, γ ji (ai, s, z i,j ), a i). (7) The only difference between Eq. (6) and Eq. (7) is the sign in front of the modified Q-function. We also introduce a second parameter αDi , since we may want to give different weights to the cooperative and defective case. We represent schematically how all the modules of our algorithm fit together in Fig. 1. With Eqs. (6)-(7), our agent can learn concurrently two policies, πC , π D (see the yellow box in Fig. 1). The goal for the agent is to understand when to switch between the cooperative and defective policy, timing it so that whoever is the opponent is incentivized to cooperate. The switching logic we introduce is a streamlined version of what Lerer & Peysakhovich (2018) propose, inspired by generalizing the classical tit-for-tat strategy (Axelrod & Hamilton, 1981). In addition our technique does not require a pre-training session to obtain the policies for both players, or sharing the rewards, and the defective policy is not just a standard single agent RL policy. The agent tracks a one-sided CUSUM statistics (Page, 1954) of the cooperation level Gt, updated at each step as

If Gt exceeds a threshold ˆG, the agent switches to its defecting policy for K steps, sets Gt to 0 and does not evaluate δ. After the K steps, the agent returns to the cooperative policy. Since agent i has no access to πj to compute δt, it uses πCi as a proxy for the ideal cooperative opponent behavior, consistent with the perspective-taking assumption of Eq. (6). Further, we explicitly sum over all actions, which is doable only on discrete action spaces. In the case of continuous actions, we would replace this sum by Monte Carlo sampling. As we mentioned, the intuition for this switching logic is to follow a tit-for-tat like behavior: start cooperating, switch to defection if opponent is observed defecting, return to cooperation after a punishing period.

In particular QMIX is used as a cooperative baseline, since it explicitly considers the cumulative reward of both agents. For the different algo-rithms, we consider their implementations in BenchMARL (Bettini et al., 2024), adding our modi-fication on top of that for MASAC, while the different environments we test are implemented using PettingZoo (Terry et al., 2021). All algorithms were fine-tuned on each environment separately, searching for parameters maximizing the cumulative reward. The full code to run the experiments with specific details about the implementation and the hardware used can be found in the online repository https://anonymous.4open.science/r/reciprocal-Q-augmentation-9251/ .

4.3 Analysis: Inducing Cooperation 

When an agent using RQA with only the cooperative augmentation faces an opponent trained with a standard RL algorithm, it is easily exploited. This behavior is illustrated with the Prisoner’s Dilemma, Traveler’s Dilemma and Stag Hunt in Table 2 and Fig. 4, where our agent faces a non-cooperative PPO agent. This is because our agent prioritizes the opponent’s welfare at the expense of its own, which a self-interested opponent can take advantage of. This motivates the need of the switching mechanism. To show that switching between the cooperative and defecting policies can induce cooperation, we evaluate an RQA-MASAC agent against a PPO agent. We choose PPO because both IPPO and MAPPO yielded the lowest social welfare in the previous experiments, which makes it the most challenging opponent to steer toward cooperation. We compare two ways of implementing the switch: • Frozen model: we pre-train RQA-MASAC in self-play twice, once with Eq. (6) to obtain a cooperative policy and Q-function, and once with Eq. (7) to obtain their defecting counter-parts. These frozen policies are then combined through the switching mechanism against a PPO opponent that keeps learning. • Learning model: both policies are learned online while playing against the PPO opponent, and the switching mechanism is applied directly during training. ## 5 Conclusions and Future Work 

In this work, we introduced Reciprocal Q-Augmentation (RQA), a method for promoting cooper-ation in general-sum multi-agent reinforcement learning without requiring shared rewards, explicit communication, or access to other agents’ rewards or policies. RQA uses a latent opponent model to estimate how the other player responds to the agent’s actions and incorporates this prediction into the Q-function, allowing the agent to learn both cooperative and defecting behaviors from its own reward. Our experiments show that cooperative RQA consistently promotes high social welfare across differ-ent sequential social dilemmas. In the symmetric and asymmetric matrix games, RQA-MASAC con-verges more consistently to socially optimal solutions than the evaluated baselines. In CleanUp-Lite ,it also achieves high social welfare while maintaining equality between agents and an efficient balance between collecting apples and cleaning dirt. We further showed that RQA can induce cooperation in an independently learning agent. By switching between cooperative and defecting policies according to the opponent’s behavior, RQA-MASAC substantially improves PPO’s social welfare in the matrix games, both with pretrained policies and when learning concurrently with PPO. The latter shows that cooperation can emerge without an initial self-play phase, access to the opponent’s reward, or explicit coordination between learning algorithms. In CleanUp-Lite , however, the cooperative augmentation achieves higher social welfare than switching, suggesting that reciprocity-based defection is less effective when coordination is the main challenge. Several directions remain for future work. First, RQA could be extended to settings with more than two agents, scaling perspective taking to account for multiple co-players simultaneously. Second, the switching logic could be learned, allowing the agent to determine when and for how long to switch between its policies, possibly addressing the difference between exploitation-centered envi-ronment and coordination-centered ones. These extensions would enable evaluation in larger and more complex multi-agent environments.
