# ABC-Align: Prediction-Powered Alignment with Adaptive Bias Control 

## Eric Frankel ∗ Banghua Zhu Sewoong Oh † Lillian J. Ratliff †

## University of Washington, Seattle 

Abstract 

Language model post-training is often bottlenecked by the need for human-collected preference data, which is expensive and difficult to scale. Reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) style ap-proaches that leverage pseudo labels offer an abundant alternative but introduce systematic biases that degrade downstream alignment. Recent general-purpose semi-supervised methods correct for teacher bias using a small set of human-labeled examples, but suffer from high variance especially when human annotations are scarce. To this end, we propose ABC-Align, leveraging abundant pseudo label signal to minimize variance and applying a lightweight, adaptive correction grounded in the human-labeled subset. The correction strength is tuned automatically during training using plug-in estimates of the relevant bias–variance quantities. On LLM alignment with RLHF, DPO, and GRPO where human feed-back is scarce, we empirically demonstrate that ABC-Align achieves superior performance over prior semi-supervised baselines in a series of experiments on an increasing scale. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in large language model (LLM) post-training have relied heavily on substantial amounts of human labels. In most preference-based approaches like reinforcement learnign from human feedback (RLHF) (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Ziegler et al., 2019), direct preference optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023), and their many variants (Ethayarajh et al., 2024), these labels take the form of pairwise comparisons. Beyond pairwise feedback, outcome-based reinforcement learning (RL) recipes such as group relative preference optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) have become a standard post-training tool for reasoning models, optimizing the policy against scalar reward signals—verifiable outcomes or a learned reward model trained from human-provided ground truths—rather than explicit preference pairs. However, this dependence on human labels is a shared bottleneck: human annotation is expensive, slow, and marked by substantial inter-annotator disagreement (Casper et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). A natural response is to leverage a language model—sometimes, the training model itself—as a source of labels on unlabeled data, potentially increasing the volume of training signal at low cost. This idea has been explored in various forms across the LLM post-training literature, including using a strong LLM as a prefer-ence judge (Bai et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023), training the policy to score its own outputs and iterating (Yuan et al., 2024), having the model discriminate its current generations from human demonstrations (Chen et al., 2024), or distilling preferences from a teacher model directly into the policy (Tunstall et al., 2024). However, each suffers from various failure modes that limit their practical utility on top of human-labeled baselines. For example, LLM judges often display systematic biases towards verbosity and sycophancy (Dubois et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023), while self-play methods plateau or degrade after entropy collapse and reward hacking (Chen et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024). Collectively, these challenges motivate the following question: 

When ground-truth labels are scarce, how can we best leverage abundant but noisy pseudo labels from additional sources to improve LLM post-training?

1arXiv:2609.34374v1 [cs.LG] 28 Sep 2026 Although this problem has been extensively studied in classical semi-supervised learning (Arazo et al., 2019; Lee, 2013; Sohn et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021), typical approaches adhere to a common design principle: the gradient estimate must remain unbiased. Within this family of unbiased estimators, a series of approaches have been proposed to minimize the variance. For example, a recent line of work in prediction-powered inference (PPI) (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a,b) offers a general-purpose framework for debiasing pseudo labels using a small set of trusted labels, providing provable guarantees on bias and variance. PPI first collects pseudo labels on the labeled examples where ground truth is available, which are used to estimate the pseudo labeler’s bias, and de-biases the pseudo labeler’s predictions. For statistical estimation, this yields an estimator that is provably unbiased and has lower variance than using labeled data alone. Recently, PPI has been extended from statistical estimation to model training: PP-SSL (Shoham et al., 2025) constructs an unbiased gradient estimator that tunes a variance-reduction parameter online, and PPI-SVRG (Ao et al., 2026) unifies the PPI correction with stochastic variance reduction. We survey these threads—PPI and its training-time extensions, classical semi-supervised learning, and pseudo-label-based LLM post-training— more comprehensively in Section 6. We observe theoretically in Corollary 2 and empirically in Section 5.1 that, in the covariance decompo-sition of the prediction-powered gradient, enforcing unbiasedness creates a structural consequence: except in a special perfect-correlation regime, the estimator inherits a variance floor of Θ(1 /n ) when there are n

human labeled examples and thus cannot be reduced by adding more pseudo labels. This statement concerns the exact variance of the gradient estimator itself, rather than the looser upper bounds typically used in PP-SSL analyses. To this end, we propose ABC-Align: a structural change that allows one to gracefully trade-off bias and variance. ABC-Align minimizes a bias–variance surrogate by allowing controlled bias in exchange for substantial variance reduction. Crucially, unlike PP-SSL, our method can move much closer to pseudo-label-dominated Θ(1 /N ) scaling when the teacher gradient bias is small, at the cost of an explicit bias term ( N is the number of pseudo-labeled examples). In the ideal perfect-teacher case this removes the labeled-data variance contribution entirely; more generally it yields a controlled bias–variance trade-off. We formalize this estimator and derive its properties in Section 3. Our main contributions are as follows. 1. A biased but lower-variance gradient estimator. For general Semi-Supervised Learning (SSL), we introduce ABC-SSL (Algorithm 1) with two parameters that can flexibly exchange bias and vari-ance, equipped with an adaptive bias controller (ABC) that automatically tunes the two parameters in an online manner based on each mini-batch statistics (Section 3). 2. Oracle characterization and convergence guarantees. The controller is informed by our charac-terization of the oracle per-step bias–variance trade-off of the gradient estimator. Further, we analyze convergence of ABC-SSL for smooth non-convex objectives (Theorem 1). In the regime where the teacher gradient bias is small, this convergence is dominated by the abundant pseudo-labeled set rather than the scarce human-labeled set, improving upon the unbiased methods up to a small controlled bias term (Corollary 1). 3. Empirical validation. We propose ABC-Align, a special instantiation of ABC-SSL under scarce human feedback tailored for each LLM alignment task, including reward model training, DPO-based policy alignment, and GRPO. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to scale up a prediction-powered approach to LLM post-training and demonstrate significant improvement over prior semi-supervised methods in the regimes we study. The largest experiment we run is on full parameter fine-tuning Llama-3.2-3B with DPO on UltraFeedback and with GRPO on GSM8K-style. Our code is available at https://github.com/SewoongLab/abc-align.

# 3 ABC-SSL: Adaptive Bias Control for Semi-Supervised Learning 

Every member of the prediction-powered family {gλt } in Eq. (5) places unit weight on the labeled gradient gnt

in order to achieve unbiasedness, which limits how much variance can be reduced: the only free parameter, 

λt, controls the contribution of the zero-mean correction gN,f t −gn,f t but cannot trade ground-truth signal for pseudo-label signal. We ask whether relaxing this constraint —admitting estimators that can downweight gnt

in exchange for a controlled bias—yields a strictly better bias–variance trade-off. We answer affirmatively by introducing ABC-SSL, built on a two-parameter family of gradient estimators that contains PP-SSL and the doubly robust gradient as special cases (Section 3.1). We characterize its oracle-optimal parameter choices and quantify when the adaptively chosen bias strictly beats the unbiased family’s variance (Section 3.2), including a Θ(1 /n ) variance floor for the unbiased slice as Corollary 2. In a practical setting where we do not have access to an oracle, we give an online controller that learns the two parameters jointly from samples and achieves O(√T ) regret against the best fixed pair. Combining it with a non-convex SGD convergence guarantee gives an end-to-end rate that approaches the oracle (Section 3.3). Notation carries over from Section 2, and proofs are deferred to Appendix A.

# 4 ABC-Align for Prediction-Powered Alignment 

We present three instantiations of ABC-Align, i.e., ABC-SSL algorithm applied to solve LLM alignment problems: reward model training for RLHF, offline preference optimization using DPO, and on-policy RL 8with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The first two are offline preference-learning problems and use standard ABC-SSL with the corrected-norm controller of Section 3.3 (Section 4.2), under which we empirically demonstrate the gain of the improved bias–variance trade-off. The third is on-policy and intro-duces an additional challenge—the gradient distribution shifts with the policy across iterations—which we address by replacing the corrected-norm controller with a finite-sample plug-in for the oracle of Proposition 2 (Section 4.3).

# 5 Experiments 

We empirically validate ABC-Align across four settings of increasing scale and realism: a controlled synthetic preference task (Section 5.1), reward model (RM) training on real preference data (Section 5.2), DPO-based policy alignment (Section 5.3), and on-policy GRPO (Section 5.4). In the synthetic setting, we compare against four baselines: (i) Labeled-only , which trains exclusively on the n human-labeled samples; (ii) Pseudo-label-only , which trains on all N pseudo-labeled samples, ignoring human labels; (iii) Doubly Robust (DR) , corresponding to ( a, b ) = (1 , N/ (N + n)) in Eq. (7), fixed over time; and (iv) PP-SSL (Shoham et al., 2025), the prediction-powered method from Eq. (5). In the LLM experiments, Doubly Robust is replaced with Na¨ ıve combined , which pools human and teacher labels without any correction, a natural baseline used in practice. All methods within each experiment use identical architectures, optimizers, and batch sizes; only the gradient estimator differs. 

## 5.1 Synthetic preference experiment on linear reward model training

# 6 Related Work 

Preference-based post-training. Aligning LLMs to human preferences is standardly done by fitting a Bradley–Terry reward model (Bradley and Terry, 1952) to pairwise comparisons and optimizing a KL-regularized objective against it (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2020; Ziegler et al., 2019), or by collapsing the two stages into a single supervised objective as in DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and its variants (Azar et al., 2023; Ethayarajh et al., 2024). For reasoning models, outcome-based RL with group-relative advantages (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) and its descendants (Liu et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025) have become the dominant recipe. All three families are bottlenecked by the same resource—human-annotated ground truth—which is expensive, slow, and noisy (Casper et al., 2023; 18 Figure 9: On-policy GRPO on GSM8K-style math (Llama-3.2-3B, G = 8 rollouts per prompt). Held-out GSM8K pass@1 across label fractions {0.05 , 0.10 , 0.30 }. ABC-Align dominates at every label fraction; the gap is widest where verifiable labels are scarcest. Gao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). ABC-Align is agnostic to which objective is used: it modifies only the gradient estimator , and we instantiate it for all three (Section 4). 

Pseudo labels for alignment, and why they are not free. The dominant response to label scarcity is to substitute a model for the annotator: RLAIF and constitutional-style pipelines label preferences with a strong LLM (Bai et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023), self-rewarding methods let the policy score its own gen-erations (Wu et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024), synthetic-preference pipelines mine or construct compari-son pairs (Liu et al., 2025a; Pace et al., 2024), and weak-to-strong approaches supervise a strong student with a weak teacher (Burns et al., 2023; Tao and Li, 2025). Such pseudo labels are abundant, but they are systematically—not randomly—wrong. LLM judges exhibit reproducible verbosity, position, and self-preference biases (Dubois et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024; Wataoka et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023), are sycophantic (Wang et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023), and leak preferences when judge and generator share a lineage (Li et al., 2025). Training on them degrades alignment in proportion to the noise rate (Gao et al., 2024), saturates as the generation–verification gap closes (Huang et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025), and collapses without a real-data anchor (Gerstgrasser et al., 2024; Shumailov et al., 2023). The weak-to-strong literature makes the ceiling explicit: the achievable gain is governed by the student’s misfit to the weak labels (Charikar et al., 2024), and refining against a weak labeler alone carries irreducible error (Somerstep et al., 2024). This is exactly the regime our estimator targets: teacher error is a persistent bias , so more pseudo labels do not average it away, and removing it requires an external anchor. 

Prediction-powered inference. PPI (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a) anchors pseudo labels to a small trusted set through a zero-mean rectifier, yielding valid inference regardless of pseudo-label quality; PPI++ (An-gelopoulos et al., 2023b) tunes a scalar power parameter so that the estimator never underperforms the labeled-only baseline, and follow-up work strengthens the design via cross-fitting (Zrnic and Cand` es, 2024a), active labeling (Chen et al., 2025a; Kluger and Bates, 2026; Sfyraki and Wang, 2026; Zrnic and Cand` es, 2024b), stratification (Fisch et al., 2024), bootstrapping (Kluger et al., 2025; Zrnic, 2024), and recalibration (Ji et al., 2025). In particular, the recalibration approach attempts to unify PPI with the classical semipara-metric theory, leading to a series of work at the intersection (Carlson and Dell, 2025; Chen et al., 2025b; Emmenegger et al., 2026; Testa et al., 2025; van der Laan and Van Der Laan, 2026). Song et al. (2026) provide a comprehensive survey of strengths and weaknesses of the recent advances in PPI. 19 Applied to LLMs, this line has so far lived almost entirely at evaluation time, unlike ABC-Align: debi-asing autorater-based benchmarks (Boyeau et al., 2025; Fisch et al., 2024), drawing valid conclusions from unconfident LLM annotations (Gligori´ c et al., 2025), debiasing LLMs’ decisions on moral dilemmas (Broska et al., 2025), auditing LLMs on election-related information (Emmenegger et al., 2026), and unbiased win-rate estimation from mixed human/synthetic feedback (Zhou et al., 2025). Two recent results sharpen the limits of this program and directly motivate ours. Dorner et al. (2025) prove that when the judge is no more accurate than the model being judged, no unbiased debiasing scheme can cut the number of required ground-truth labels by more than a factor of two, however many pseudo labels are available; and Mani et al. (2025) show non-asymptotically that PPI++ can be strictly worse than using the labeled data alone unless pseudo-label correlation clears an n-dependent threshold. Both barriers are statements about the unbiased class; ABC-Align breaks them by leaving it. 

Prediction-powered training. Closest to us is work carrying PPI from estimation to iterative optimiza-tion. PP-SSL (Shoham et al., 2025) forms an unbiased prediction-powered gradient and tunes its single interpolation parameter online; PPI-SVRG (Ao et al., 2026) identifies PPI’s rectifier with the SVRG control variate and inherits an error floor set by prediction uncertainty; and the doubly robust self-training loss of Zhu et al. (2023)—descended from doubly robust estimation in causal inference—is the fixed-λ member of the same family. Each of these estimators is unbiased by construction, which is precisely what pins it to the Θ(1 /n ) variance floor of Corollary 2. Our estimator introduces a second parameter that relaxes unbiasedness, converting that floor into an explicit, adaptively controlled bias–variance trade-off. The semi-parametric strand of alignment theory is complementary rather than competing: Xu et al. (2025a) and Ye et al. (2025) seek consistency under model misspecification and variance reduction from unlabeled responses, not a deliberate bias budget. 

Semi-supervised and noise-robust preference learning. A parallel line keeps the estimator unbiased and instead cleans the labels. Robust DPO (Chowdhury et al., 2024) debiases the DPO loss under a 

known symmetric flip rate; semi-supervised reward modeling (He et al., 2024) and semi-supervised preference optimization (Lee et al., 2025) pseudo-label with confidence thresholds, inheriting the selection heuristics of classical SSL (Lee, 2013; Sohn et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021); and hybrid pipelines route instances between human and LLM annotators to spend a fixed human budget well (Miranda et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025b). These methods either assume a noise model we do not require, or trust pseudo labels after filtering rather than correcting them against ground truth. Our human-labeled set plays a different role: it is not extra training signal to be augmented, but a measuring instrument for the teacher’s gradient bias. 

Biased gradient estimators. That a biased estimator can dominate an unbiased one in mean-squared error is well established in stochastic optimization: biased variance-reduced methods attain lower gradient MSE and faster rates than their unbiased counterparts (Driggs et al., 2022), and convergence under biased oracles is standard for both SGD (Ajalloeian and Stich, 2020; Demidovich et al., 2023) and adaptive meth-ods (Surendran et al., 2024). In RLHF, control variates appear as variance-reducing baselines (Ahmadian et al., 2024), including shrinkage baselines that knowingly trade bias for variance in the GRPO advan-tage (Zeng et al., 2025); GRPO’s group-relative advantage is itself a biased gradient estimator (Liu et al., 2025b; Zhou et al., 2026). What neither literature provides is a bias that is measured against trusted human labels and controlled online , which is what ABC-SSL contributes. 

# 7 Conclusion 

Prediction powered inference (PPI) techniques have emerged as a powerful tool for semi-supervised learning when ( i) we have access to a noisy and biased teacher model and ( ii ) the number of labeled examples n

is smaller than the number of unlabeled examples N . The standard PPI-style model training methods like PP-SSL use a single parameter to control variance while keeping the gradient estimate unbiased . Such unbiasedness forces the resulting estimate to suffer from a variance floor that scales as Θ(1 /n ). To this end, we introduced adaptive bias control for semi-supervised learning (ABC-SSL, Algorithm 1), an iterative algorithm with a two-parameter gradient estimator that improve the bias-variance trade-off beyond the Θ(1 /n ) variance 20 floor by admitting a controlled bias decoupled from variance reduction. Our analysis (Corollary 3) gives a joint regret guarantee in ( at, b t) via the corrected-norm surrogate ℓt and the model parameter θ (Section 3.3), where the parameters ( at, b t) are adaptively learned to control the bias–variance trade-off. This is shown to achieve a strict improvement over the best statically tuned PP-SSL estimator at rate O(T −1/2) for a broad range of parameters. Empirically, our goal is to improve LLM alignment by better utilizing abundant unlabeled text together with easily accessible teacher models. To empirically test the gain of our new approach, we present three instantiations of ABC-Align, i.e., the ABC-SSL algorithm applied to solve LLM alignment problems: reward model training for RLHF, offline preference optimization using DPO, and on-policy RL with GRPO. We show that ABC-Align dominates common baselines of labeled-only, naively combined, and PP-SSL across all three scenarios.

## B.4 Subgroup analysis 

To examine performance on easy versus hard preference comparisons, we split the test set by the magnitude of the true reward difference ∆ i = |r⋆(x1,i ) − r⋆(x2,i )|. Pairs with ∆ i above the test-set median form Group A (easy: clear preference signal), and pairs below form Group B (hard: ambiguous comparisons where annotator noise is most impactful). This split is performed on the test set only and does not affect training. The subgroup analysis reveals that ABC-Align’s advantage is concentrated on hard pairs (Figure 2c). On easy pairs, all methods that use pseudo labels perform similarly, since the teacher’s ranking is usually correct regardless of bias. On hard pairs, however, the labeled-only and unbiased estimators (DR, PP-SSL) are variance-limited because n = 50 provides little signal for near-tied comparisons. ABC-Align’s ability to lean more heavily on pseudo labels while applying a controlled correction yields the largest gains in this regime. 

## B.5 Experimental conditions 

We use n = 50 labeled pairs with N = 5 ,000 unlabeled pairs (100 × ratio). For the main synthetic curve in Figure 2, we sweep μ ∈ { 0.0, 0.2, . . . , 1.0} and average over 25 trials. For the teaser heatmap (Figure 1) and the trajectory and MSE ablations (Figures 3 and 4), we use a coarser μ grid and average over 10 trials. Across all settings, we vary the random seed for w⋆, v, and the sampled comparison pairs. Error bars in Figure 2 show ±1 standard error across trials.

Consequently, no matter how well the controller tracks the oracle, Theorem 1 only guarantees convergence to a neighborhood of stationarity whose radius is controlled by this residual bias floor; the controller cannot remove it without abandoning the oracle (and hence its variance optimality). The two-timescale construction below decouples these concerns: it eliminates the bias floor asymptotically while preserving whatever variance-optimality the controller delivers, since the fast-timescale update modifies the slow gradient without altering ( at, b t). A principled way to remove this floor is to augment the slow parameter update with a fast auxiliary iterate that tracks the unknown bias B(θt) online. This is the classical two-timescale stochastic approximation paradigm of Borkar (1997) and Borkar (2008): two coupled updates run at different rates so that, on the slow timescale, the fast iterate appears to have already converged. The asymptotic theory of such schemes traces back to Polyak and Juditsky (1992), Konda and Tsitsiklis (2003), and Mokkadem and Pelletier (2006). A substantial recent effort has sharpened the finite-time theory: Doan (2021) obtains an O(k−2/3) mean-square rate for nonlinear two-timescale stochastic approximation, Hong et al. (2023) gives matching bilevel rates with explicit dependence on inner-problem conditioning, Han et al. (2024) show decoupled convergence under a nested local-linearity condition, Doan (2026) attains the improved O(1 /k ) rate via Ruppert–Polyak-style averaging of the operators, and Kwon et al. (2025) analyze constant-stepsize variants via Wasserstein-geometric ergodicity. Our debiasing scheme takes the same shape as a Robbins–Monro fixed-point iteration for B(θ) coupled with a slow parameter update on L, and so inherits the TTSA structure. Finally, the idea of debiasing a biased gradient by a running estimate that is itself updated online connects to the STORM-style momentum variance reduction of Cutkosky and Orabona (2019) and the broader debiased-SSL literature (Chen et al., 2022), though the present analysis does not rely on any of those techniques directly.

Three regimes. Theorems 2 and 3 answer different questions and are not directly comparable. 

• Asymptotic consistency (Theorem 2): a classical decaying-step schedule satisfying Eq. (51) drives the 

ηt-weighted average of E ∥∇L (θt)∥2 to zero; for the concrete schedule with ηt = c2t−1 the rate is 

O(1 / log T ), with averaging weights decaying like 1 /t .

• Finite-horizon optimization (Theorem 3): fixing a budget T and tuning the two constant step sizes to 

T explicitly trades off bias, variance, and tracking error, yielding the polynomial rate O(T −1/3) on the uniformly averaged (1 /T -weighted) gradient norm. 

• Truly non-asymptotic / anytime bounds would require step-size choices independent of any future hori-zon and, in the nonconvex setting, typically demand additional structure (e.g., a Polyak–Lojasiewicz condition). We do not establish such a guarantee here. The O(1 / log T ) vs. O(T −1/3) gap reflects these different question regimes—different step-size families, dif-ferent averaging weights, different notions of progress—and is not a deficiency of either result. 

## E.5 Practical considerations 

Memory and compute overhead. Algorithm 2 requires storing the single vector ξt ∈ Rd, identical in size to the model gradient, and performs two scalar-vector operations per step ( ξt-update and subtraction in Eq. (47)). No additional batch gradients are computed beyond those already required by Algorithm 1, so the per-iteration cost is dominated by the same batch-gradient evaluations as vanilla ABC-Align under the corrected-norm controller. 

Initialization. We initialize ξ1 = 0, which corresponds to the prior B(θ1) ≈ 0 (equivalently, an unbiased teacher at initialization). The first-step debiased estimator then reduces to eg1 = ˆ gt, matching Algorithm 1; this is consistent with the first-step choice ( a1, b 1) = (1 , 0) already used in ABC-Align. 

Interaction with the corrected-norm controller. The fast-timescale update Eq. (46) and the joint corrected-norm controller of Section 3.3 operate independently: ξt debiases the slow update, while the controller minimizes the per-step MSE in ( at, b t). They share only the dt stream— ξt consumes it as the bias proxy Et[−dt] = B(θt), while the controller consumes it for the scalar primitives ∥dt∥2 , ⟨dt, c t⟩ ,

Constant-stepsize regime and connections to recent work. A constant slow step size ηt ≡ η and constant fast step γt ≡ γ with η ≪ γ matches the setting analyzed in Kwon et al. (2025), whose Wasserstein-ergodicity machinery implies that ( θt, ξ t) admits a unique joint stationary distribution and gives explicit bounds on the bias and variance of each iterate in terms of the two step sizes. Porting those techniques to our nonlinear nonconvex setting—where L is β-smooth but not strongly monotone in θ—and pairing them with the Ruppert–Polyak averaging of Doan (2026) to reach an O(1 /T ) rate is a natural direction for future work. 46 Caveats.
