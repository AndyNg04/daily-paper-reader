## Abstract 

Large language model-based agents are increasingly deployed to perform domain-specific tasks by interacting with enterprise knowl-edge, tools, and external services. Existing runtime guardrails pri-marily target prompt injection and other attack-specific behaviors under a black-box threat model, but provide limited guarantees that agents operate within their intended functionality. As a result, production agents remain vulnerable to malicious requests and out-of-domain queries that existing defenses often fail to distinguish. We present Persona Guardrail, a production-grade runtime de-fense framework that enforces explicit functional boundaries for customer-facing agentic AI systems through synchronous input and output validation driven by semantic allowlist and blocklist specifi-cations. We also introduce PAGE (Persona-Aware Guardrail Eval-uation), a benchmark for evaluating function-specific guardrails across benign, adversarial, and out-of-domain interactions on both user and agent turns. Compared with a generic LLM-based guard-rail, Persona Guardrail improves overall accuracy from 85.7% to 95.9%, increases out-of-domain detection from 57.3% to 93.5%, and reduces the false-approved rate from 25.0% to 4.7%. Currently de-ployed in production, Persona Guardrail meets its latency budget while sustaining a very low false-block and false-allow rate under realistic production workloads. These results demonstrate that Per-sona Guardrail provides a practical, scalable, and production-ready foundation for securing agentic AI systems.

These advances have en-abled the emergence of what is called agentic AI systems—LLM-powered applications [22] that can autonomously interact with tools, APIs, knowledge bases, and software services to accomplish a broad spectrum of user goals. By combining the capabilities of LLMs with traditional software components, the agentic systems provide a powerful building block for a diverse set of applications, ranging from ride booking and customer support automation to transaction management, information retrieval, and personalized assistance. As agentic AI systems become increasingly integrated into pro-duction environments, ensuring their security has emerged as a crit-ical challenge [12, 16]. Unlike traditional software systems, agentic applications process natural-language inputs while simultaneously possessing the ability to perform privileged actions in production. This combination introduces new attack surfaces that do not exist in conventional applications. Recent research has identified a broad spectrum of attacks against agentic systems, including prompt injec-tion, indirect prompt injection through untrusted external content, tool poisoning, retrieval-augmented generation poisoning, agent goal hijacking etc [6, 8, 11]. However, these attacks assume different threat models and require different defense mechanisms. In this paper, we focus on the practical threat model encoun-tered by many production-facing agents, where a malicious end user has black-box access to the agent through its public interface and cannot manipulate the agent’s internal tools, retrieved doc-uments, memory, or execution environment. In this setting, the adversary attempts to induce the agent to perform actions outside its intended functionality, bypass safety policies, or produce harm-ful responses through carefully crafted user queries. Consequently, indirect prompt injection, tool poisoning, and memory poisoning are outside the evaluated threat model. Our solution may still block a policy-violating final response caused by such attacks, but the evaluated guardrails do not inspect intermediate actions. Existing research on agentic AI security has primarily focused on two complementary directions. The first seeks to develop de-fenses against specific classes of attacks, such as prompt injection attacks [11, 15]. The second evaluates the effectiveness of adversar-ial attack strategies against LLM-based agents and measures their impact on system behavior [1, 6]. While these efforts have significantly advanced our understanding of agentic AI security, they largely focus on attack taxonomies, threat models, and isolated defense mechanisms. Com-paratively little attention has been paid to the practical challenges of deploying comprehensive security solutions in large-scale pro-duction environments, where latency, scalability, reliability, and operational complexity are often as important as security. In this paper, we address this gap by presenting Persona Guard-rail , a production-grade defense framework designed for large-scale agentic systems. Persona Guardrail is based on a simple yet pow-erful principle: every agent should operate within a well-defined functional boundary that specifies its intended capabilities, permis-sible actions, and expected behaviors. The framework continuously enforces these boundaries by validating both inputs to the agent and outputs generated by the agent, ensuring that interactions re-main consistent with the agent’s intended functionality even in the presence of adversarial inputs. Our work differs from prior research in its emphasis on practical deployment. The framework op-erates under real-world constraints, including stringent latency requirements, high request volumes, and evolving adversarial be-havior. ## 2 System Overview and Problem Setting 

In this work, we investigate the design of a comprehensive defense framework for agentic AI systems. We begin by describing the sys-tem architecture, problem setting, design assumptions, and threat model that motivate the proposed defense mechanism. 

## 2.1 System Setting 

We consider a generic architecture representative of modern pro-duction scale agentic AI systems. The system receives user requests in the form of natural-language instructions, structured inputs (e.g., JSON), or a combination of both. In addition to user-provided infor-mation, the frontend application may attach contextual metadata (e.g., user state, session information, or application-specific con-text) that assists the agent in decision making. Before reaching the agent, every incoming request is processed by the input Per-sona Guardrail, which validates the request against the agent’s functional boundaries and security policies. Persona Guardrail can block or send through the request to the agentic system, which comprises one or more large language models (LLMs), enterprise knowledge bases, retrieval components, internal and external tools, APIs, and other software services. During task execution, the agent may perform multiple reasoning steps and interact with these com-ponents to retrieve information, invoke tools, or execute actions before producing a final response. The generated response is subse-quently processed by the output Persona Guardrail, which validates it against the agent’s intended functionality and security policies before it is returned to the user. 

## 2.2 Threat Model 

We consider an adversarial setting in which an external malicious user has unrestricted query access to the agentic system and can observe all responses returned by the system. The adversary pos-sesses general knowledge of the agent’s intended functionality, public interfaces, and expected behavior, but does not have access to the system’s internal state, including system prompts, interme-diate reasoning, memory, tool interactions, orchestration logic, or communication between internal components. We assume the un-derlying LLMs, infrastructure, and trusted software components are uncompromised. Within this threat model, the adversary aims to manipulate the agent through carefully crafted inputs to achieve one or more of the following objectives: 1. Instruction Manipulation and Prompt Extraction. The adversary attempts to extract confidential system prompts, reveal internal instructions or policies, or manipulate the agent into violat-ing its intended behavior through attacks such as prompt injections. 2. Generation of Harmful or Policy-Violating Responses. 

The adversary seeks to induce the agent to produce responses or perform actions that violate organizational policies, safety require-ments, or legal and regulatory constraints, potentially resulting in reputational, financial, or legal harm to the organization. Persona Guardrail: A Production-Grade Defense Framework for Agentic Systems 

3. Functional Boundary Violation. The adversary attempts to cause the agent to operate outside its intended scope by elic-iting responses or executing actions unrelated to its designated functionality. For example, an attacker may attempt to coerce a ride-booking assistant into providing medical advice or revealing internal operational information, thereby violating the agent’s pre-defined functional boundaries. Our objective is to design a defense framework that mitigates these attacks while preserving the utility, responsiveness, and in-tended functionality of the agent under normal user interactions. 

## 2.3 Performance Requirements 

Persona Guardrail is designed to operate under the stringent la-tency requirements of production agentic systems. Both the input and output guardrails make blocking decisions—requests must be validated before agent execution, and responses must be validated before being returned to the end user. Hence, the guardrails cannot be executed asynchronously with the agent. As a result, the pri-mary design objective is to achieve robust security while incurring only a small and predictable latency overhead that preserves the responsiveness required by production-scale deployments. 

## 2.4 Potential Approaches 

From a high-level perspective, an agentic guardrail can be viewed as a reasoning system that determines whether an input or output lies within the functional and security boundaries of an agent. A straightforward approach is to leverage a general-purpose LLM by providing it with a natural-language specification of the agent’s intended functionality, permissible actions, and security policies, and asking it to classify each interaction accordingly. While concep-tually simple, this approach presents several practical challenges for production deployment. First, a comprehensive natural-language specification is difficult to construct and maintain as the underlying agent evolves. Pro-duction agents frequently undergo updates, including changes to prompts, tools, APIs, retrieval sources, and supported capabilities. Consequently, the guardrail specification must evolve alongside the agent to accurately reflect its intended behavior. Second, invoking a medium- or large-scale reasoning model for every request intro-duces significant latency. Since both the input and output guardrails operate synchronously on the critical execution path, their latency directly impacts end-to-end response time and user experience. Finally, a static guardrail is unlikely to remain effective in dynamic production environments where both adversarial attack strategies and legitimate user behavior continuously evolve. These challenges raise several fundamental design questions. How can the functional and security specification of an agent be maintained as the system evolves? How should guardrail perfor-mance be measured, particularly in terms of false positive and false negative rates? How can production misclassifications be systemat-ically incorporated to refine the guardrail over time? More broadly, how can a guardrail continuously adapt to emerging attack tech-niques and distribution shifts in user requests while maintaining low latency and high reliability? Existing defenses only partially address these challenges. Rule-based and policy-based guardrails [10, 17] provide deterministic enforcement but are difficult to scale across heterogeneous agents and often fail to capture context-dependent or previously unseen attacks. Model-based guardrails [11, 15] offer greater flexibility and generalization but typically rely on expensive reasoning models and have received relatively little attention regarding continuous adap-tation, deployment at production-scale, or systematic evaluation under evolving workloads. More generally, recent systematization efforts [12] categorize existing defenses into runtime protection, secure-by-design mechanisms, and component hardening, while identifying the need for practical, adaptive defenses that can operate under real-world deployment constraints. Motivated by these challenges, we design Persona Guardrail as an adaptive, production-oriented runtime defense framework. Rather than relying solely on static rules or expensive reasoning, Persona Guardrail combines lightweight inference with a closed-loop feedback mechanism that continuously refines its functional and security boundaries using signals collected from production traffic. This design enables the framework to adapt to both emerg-ing adversarial attack patterns and shifts in user behavior while satisfying the stringent latency and scalability requirements of production agentic systems. 

## 3 Persona Guardrail Design 

Persona Guardrail is a production-grade defense framework de-signed to ensure that an agentic system operates within its intended functional and security boundaries. The framework consists of three primary components: 1) Functional Boundary Specification , 2) Input Guardrail Enforcement , and 3) Output Guardrail Enforcement .The key insight behind Persona Guardrail is that every agent has a well-defined purpose. Rather than attempting to detect every pos-sible attack in isolation, Persona Guardrail first models the intended functional boundary of the agent and then enforces this specifica-tion throughout the agent’s interaction lifecycle. Both the input and output guardrails are responsible for ensuring that the interaction remains consistent with the agent’s functional boundary. 

## 3.1 Functional Boundary Specification 

The central abstraction underlying Persona Guardrail is the func-tional boundary of an agent. Intuitively, the functional boundary characterizes the set of user interactions that are consistent with the agent’s intended functionality, security requirements, and product specifications. 

Definition 1 (Functional Boundary). Let Q denote the space of all possible user queries submitted to an agent. The functional boundary partitions Q into two disjoint subsets: [ Q = Qallow ∪ Q block , ] where 

• Q allow denotes the set of queries that are consistent with the intended functionality of the agent and should be processed. 

• Q block denotes the set of queries that should be rejected: malicious or unsafe queries, together with out-of-domain queries in deployments configured to reject them. The objective of Persona Guardrail is to approximate this parti-tion to minimize both false positives (rejecting legitimate queries) and false negatives (allowing malicious or out-of-domain queries). Pal et al. 

Since the behavior of production agents and their users continu-ously evolves, the functional boundary is not static; instead, it is continuously refined using production telemetry, attack simula-tions, and feedback collected from deployment. In practice, explicitly enumerating the sets Qallow and Qblock is in-feasible because natural language admits an effectively unbounded space of expressions. Persona Guardrail therefore represents the functional boundary using semantic descriptions that capture both the intended functionality of the agent and security constraints. 

3.1.1 Hybrid Functional Boundary Representation. We investigate three approaches for representing the functional boundary: 

• Allowlist-only 

• Blocklist-only 

• Hybrid allowlist-blocklist An allowlist-only representation specifies the categories of re-quests that the agent is permitted to process. While this approach provides strong security, it often becomes overly conservative in practice. Because it is impossible to exhaustively enumerate every legitimate user request, incomplete allowlists frequently result in elevated false positive rates and poor user experience. Conversely, a blocklist-only representation specifies the set of queries that the agent should reject. These include both security-driven and functionality-driven constraints. Security-driven entries correspond to known adversarial behaviors, such as prompt injec-tion, jailbreaks, and other attacks that attempt to manipulate the agent or violate organizational policies. Functionality-driven en-tries correspond to out-of-domain (OOD) requests that fall outside the intended purpose of the agent. Although such requests may be entirely benign, processing them can result in poor user experience, hallucinations, or unintended behavior. Whether out-of-domain queries are included in the blocklist is configurable and depends on the deployment requirements of the agent. Some applications are designed to operate as specialized assistants and therefore re-ject requests outside their intended functionality, while others may choose to answer or gracefully redirect out-of-domain requests. Persona Guardrail supports both deployment models by allowing agent owners to configure the desired functional boundary. To reduce ambiguity at inference time, the allowlist and blocklist should overlap as little as possible. Where the two specifications intersect, they present the classifier with conflicting evidence and raise classification uncertainty. Persona Guardrail therefore iden-tifies overlapping entries between the two lists and adjudicates them during guardrail construction and refinement, so that each capability falls under a single, unambiguous rule. 

3.1.2 Constructing the Functional Boundary. The functional bound-ary is constructed from multiple complementary sources. 

Agent implementation and product specifications. During initial deployment, the allowlist is generated by analyzing both the imple-mentation of the agent and its product requirements. We employ a code-oriented LLM to analyze the agent implementation and infer the classes of user requests that the agent is designed to support. This analysis considers the agent’s available tools, APIs, retrieval components, system prompts, and external resources to produce an initial semantic specification of its intended functionality. 

Production traffic analysis. Following deployment, Persona Guard-rail operates in a shadow mode during which it observes production traffic without enforcing decisions. Legitimate interactions that are not adequately represented in the initial specification are incorpo-rated into the allowlist, enabling the functional boundary to evolve alongside changing user behavior and product capabilities. 

Security-driven blocklist generation. The initial blocklist is con-structed from two complementary sources. First, it incorporates known adversarial techniques targeting agentic AI systems, includ-ing prompt injection, indirect prompt injection, jailbreaks, goal hijacking, functional-boundary violations, and tool misuse. Sec-ond, it captures classes of out-of-domain queries that lie outside the intended scope of the agent. As new attacks emerge and user interaction patterns evolve, the blocklist is continuously refined through a feedback loop, allowing the framework to adapt to both evolving adversarial threats and changing product requirements. By combining implementation analysis, production observations, and continuously evolving security knowledge, Persona Guard-rail maintains an adaptive approximation of the agent’s functional boundary that remains effective under both evolving adversarial threats and shifts in legitimate user behavior. Persona Guardrail en-forces the functional boundary at two stages of the agent execution pipeline. Before the request reaches the agent, the input guardrail determines whether the query lies within the functional boundary. After the agent generates a response, the output guardrail verifies that the generated response remains consistent with the same func-tional boundary. The following sections describe these enforcement mechanisms in detail. 

## 3.2 Input Guardrail Enforcement 

The input guardrail enforces the functional boundary before a request reaches the agentic system. Its objective is to determine whether an incoming user request should be processed by the agent or rejected. The input allowlist specifies the classes of user requests that fall within the intended capabilities of the agent, whereas the input blocklist captures malicious inputs, known adversarial attack patterns, and, optionally, out-of-domain requests that the agent is not designed to handle. To enforce the input functional boundary, we investigated four alternative architectures: 1) Embedding-based Retrieval. The incoming query is embedded and compared against representative allowlist and blocklist examples using vector similarity. The query is classified based on its nearest neighbors and a calibrated similarity threshold. 2) LLM-based Classification. A low-latency reasoning model receives the user query together with the functional boundary specification and directly classifies the query as allow or block .3) Hybrid Retrieval and LLM. Queries exhibiting very high semantic similarity to existing allowlist or blocklist examples are classified using the embedding model. Queries that cannot be classified with sufficient confidence are forwarded to the LLM classifier for seman-tic reasoning. 4) Fine-tuned Classification Model. A model is fine-tuned using agent-specific allowlist and blocklist examples to directly predict the appropriate classification. We evaluated these architectures along four dimensions: classi-fication performance, inference latency, operational complexity, and Persona Guardrail: A Production-Grade Defense Framework for Agentic Systems 

generalization across heterogeneous agents. The embedding-based approach provides the lowest inference latency but requires careful calibration of similarity thresholds and continuous maintenance of an embedding database containing representative allowlist and blocklist examples for every agent. As agents evolve, maintaining these examples becomes increasingly expensive, and the approach exhibits limited robustness to semantically novel requests. The fine-tuned classifier performs well when evaluated on agents rep-resented in its training data. However, its performance degrades when deployed on previously unseen agents or when the functional boundary evolves, necessitating periodic retraining and increasing operational complexity. The hybrid architecture improves classifi-cation accuracy by combining fast retrieval with LLM reasoning, but introduces additional infrastructure complexity and requires maintaining both an embedding index and an LLM-based inference pipeline. Based on these trade-offs, we adopt an LLM-based classifier 

using Qwen3-30B-A3B [7] as the input guardrail. We find that this approach offers the best overall trade-off across these dimensions: low operational complexity, low inference latency, strong classifica-tion performance, and generalization across diverse agentic systems. Moreover, because the functional boundary is represented semanti-cally rather than as agent-specific examples, the same model can be deployed across multiple agents with minimal customization while adapting naturally to evolving boundary specifications. 

## 3.3 Output Guardrail Enforcement 

The output guardrail enforces the functional boundary before the agent’s response is returned to the user. Its objective is to verify that the generated response remains consistent with the agent’s intended functionality, organizational policies, and security require-ments. The output allowlist specifies the expected characteristics and permissible actions of the agent’s responses, while the output blocklist captures prohibited behaviors, including policy violations, unsafe or harmful responses, disclosure of confidential information, hallucinated capabilities and other outputs that violate the agent’s functional or security boundaries. As with the input guardrail, we evaluated the same four architec-tures (embedding-based retrieval, LLM-based classification, hybrid retrieval–LLM, and a fine-tuned classifier). We adopt the same LLM-based classifier (Qwen3-30B-A3B) [7] for output enforcement, as it provides the best balance between inference latency, deployment simplicity, and generalization across diverse agents. Furthermore, using a common classification model for both input and output en-forcement simplifies deployment and maintenance while enabling a consistent semantic interpretation of the functional boundary throughout the agent execution pipeline. The output guardrail serves as the final enforcement point before the response reaches the end user and may block a policy-violating response even when an attack has influenced the agent.

## 4 Experimental Evaluation 

We evaluate Persona Guardrail on PAGE (Persona-Aware Guard-rail Evaluation) (Section 4.1), our persona-scoped benchmark spanning four AgentDojo [6] domains—Workspace, Slack, Banking, and Travel (Table 3)—under a malicious-user threat model in which an adversary interacts directly with the agent endpoint. Because PAGE labels both user turns (pre-app, evaluated by the input guard-rail) and agent turns (post-app, evaluated by the output guardrail), a single benchmark exercises both guardrails of the framework. Every configuration is evaluated on identical PAGE splits so that performance differences are attributable to the guardrail configura-tion alone. ## 4.1 The Benchmark 

Existing public agent benchmarks are designed to measure task utility, harmful behavior, or robustness against specific attacks, such as indirect prompt injection, and therefore do not provide the labels a persona guardrail needs: whether a user turn (before the agent acts) or an agent turn (before a response is returned) falls inside or outside the agent’s declared functional boundary. To evaluate Persona Guardrail under its intended malicious-user threat model, we construct PAGE (Persona-Aware Guardrail Evaluation) , a purpose-built benchmark in which every message is (i) attributed to one of the four AgentDojo personas, (ii) tagged with the turn on which the guardrail fires (pre-app/user or post-app/agent), and (iii) labelled with a request kind —benign , adversarial , out-of-domain 

(OOD), or mixed . Every label is grounded in the same natural-language allowlist that instantiates the guardrail, so the dataset and the enforced policy share a single source of truth. In total, PAGE comprises 2 ,180 labelled messages (2 ,076 synthesized around the four personas plus 104 projected from AgentHarm [1]) and is, to our knowledge, the first guardrail benchmark that jointly covers user and agent turns across four production domains with benign, out-of-domain, and adversarial intents. We release it to support reproducible evaluation of functional-boundary enforcement.

## 4.3 Overall Detection Performance 

Table 6 reports aggregate performance across configurations and Table 9 the underlying decision counts. The comparison isolates the value of the functional boundary. The baseline judge—given only a generic agent description—attains reasonable adversarial detection (92 .6%) but cannot enforce a functional boundary: it catches only 57 .3% of out-of-domain turns, giving a 25 .0% false-approved rate and 85 .7% accuracy. Introducing the persona allowlist raises out-of-domain detection to 83 .3% and cuts the false-approved rate to 9 .9%; the single iteration pass—driven entirely by inspecting the false-approved and false-blocked turns—raises out-of-domain detection further to 93 .5% and reduces the false-approved rate to 4 .7%, lifting accuracy to 95 .9%; a second refinement pass and a held-out, multi-split validation of this iterative gain are reported in Appendix A (Table 12). Crucially, the benign false-blocked rate stays essentially constant across all three configurations ( ≈ 3.7%), so these security gains do not cost utility. The blocklist-only and allowlist+blocklist configurations settle whether an explicit blocklist helps. A blocklist alone is far weaker: it reaches only 71 .2% accuracy because, although it catches 87 .2% of adversarial turns, it flags just 14 .5% of out-of-domain requests—a blocklist enumerates attacks, not the agent’s function, so it cannot recognise a competent request that merely falls outside scope (a 49 .0% false-approved rate). Adding the blocklist on top of the allow-list does not improve on the allowlist alone: raw detection edges up, but the benign false-blocked rate roughly doubles (3 .6% → 8.2%) and overall accuracy slips (95 .9% → 94 .9%). The iterated allow-list thus remains the strongest configuration: the allowlist is both necessary and sufficient to enforce the functional boundary, and a blocklist neither substitutes for it nor usefully complements it. 

## 4.4 Per-Label Analysis 

Table 7 decomposes performance by request label, and Table in Appendix A breaks the strongest configuration (iterated allowlist) down by agent. Two observations stand out. First, adversarial de-tection is high for every allowlist configuration ( ≥ 96 .8%), and even the description-only baseline and the generic blocklist catch most overt attacks (92 .6% and 87 .2%): explicit attacks are the easy case. Second, out-of-domain detection is where the functional boundary matters most—it climbs from 57 .3% (baseline) to 83 .3% (allowlist) to 93 .5% (iterated), whereas the blocklist manages only 14 .5%, con-firming that identifying competent-but-out-of-scope requests—not attack coverage—is the hard part of the problem, and that only an allowlist addresses it. Per agent, the lower Slack and Workspace OOD rates reflect the difficulty of the fluent, task-like cross-persona OOD cases described in Section 4.1.3. 

## 4.5 Ablation: Mixed Intents 

The experiments above use the three-label setting (benign, adver-sarial, out-of-domain). We additionally study mixed turns—turns that combine a valid in-scope request with an out-of-scope or ad-versarial rider—which raise a labelling question: should a turn that contains some legitimate intent be approved (allowlist-presence semantics) or blocked (because it also carries off-policy content)? We evaluate both labelling policies against the no-mixed setting on the iterated-allowlist configuration (Table 8). The guardrail blocks 92 .2% of mixed turns in both policies (its predictions do not depend on the label). Consequently, scoring mixed turns as should-approve inflates the false-blocked rate to 17 .1% and drops accuracy to 88 .6%, whereas scoring them as should-block keeps accuracy at 95 .6% with a 3 .6% false-blocked rate. In other words, the guardrail behaves as if a mixed turn is off-policy, which is consistent with a strict functional boundary that rejects any turn carrying a disallowed component. 

## 5 Production Deployment 

While the previous sections focused on the design and evaluation of Persona Guardrail, deploying guardrails in production introduces a distinct set of engineering challenges. Production agentic systems operate across multiple agents with heterogeneous capabilities, evolving product requirements, and dynamic user behavior. Conse-quently, guardrails must not only provide strong security but also remain observable, maintainable, and adaptable throughout their lifecycle, while operating within the strict latency budget imposed by their position on the critical path. 

## 5.1 Model Serving Infrastructure

Inference is disaggregated into a CPU frontend and GPU workers: a prefix-cache-aware router dispatches each request to a worker, which performs the model inference on a dedicated accelerator. The frontend and worker pools scale independently (Figure 3 in the Appendix). Because the guardrail lies on the critical path of every interac-tion, the serving infrastructure is optimized for predictable latency and high availability. ## 5.2 Serving Within a Latency Budget 

Because a guardrail runs on every request and response, its latency is judged not in isolation but against a transparency budget : it may consume only a small fraction of the host application’s end-to-end processing time before users perceive it. We express this budget as a fixed fraction of application latency, yielding a sub-100 ms P90 target for each guardrail model call. Meeting this target is made tractable by three properties of the guardrail workload. 1) Single-token, prefill-dominated inference. The guardrail is a binary classifier that emits one decision token (allow or block). Token generation is negligible, so latency is dominated by prompt prefill rather than decoding. 2) A large, shared prompt prefix. All requests share a static pre-amble containing the instructions and policy context. This preamble accounts for approximately 93% of input tokens in our workload. Reusing its cached state reduces per-request prefill to the shorter, variable user request or agent response. 3) Bounded context with cheap rejection. The small fraction of prompts that exceed the context cap (2.1% of production traffic) is rejected before GPU work is scheduled. This prevents oversized in-puts from consuming capacity or inflating latency for the remaining traffic (Appendix B). Beyond exploiting workload structure, we reduce per-call cost through model selection rather than compression alone. A mixture-of-experts model with only a few billion active parameters, served in FP8, provides the capacity of a larger model at the inference cost of a substantially smaller one. Together, disaggregated serving, prefix-cache reuse, bounded contexts, and one-token output target the prefill-dominated structure of the workload rather than generic token-generation speed. Under this design, the guardrail sustains its latency budget across the tested operating range (Figure 1). Between 5 and 50 QPS, me-dian latency ranges from 19.43 to 25.34 ms and P90 latency from 57.30 to 61.08 ms. P99 increases from 76.77 ms at 5 QPS to 104.15 ms at 50 QPS, crossing the 100 ms objective at 30 QPS. These measure-ments are single-accelerator model-call latencies with a 4,096-token context cap; they do not include application or network overhead. Accelerator utilization remains below 65% over this range. Relative to the prior general-purpose serving stack, the disaggregated design reduces guardrail-call latency by approximately 30%.

## 5.4 Guardrail Versioning and Safe Deployment 

Unlike static security policies, guardrail specifications evolve as agent capabilities and attack techniques change. Persona Guardrail maintains an independent policy for each deployed agent, contain-ing allowed and blocked intents for both user requests and agent responses. The same classifier can serve multiple agents, but each evaluation uses the intent lists associated with the current agent and interaction boundary. Candidate updates must satisfy prede-fined thresholds on security effectiveness, utility, reliability, and latency before deployment. Operationally, the editable intent taxonomy is separated from the policy enforced in production. Applying a persona to a policy copies its four intent lists into a deployment snapshot; later edits to the source persona do not affect that snapshot. A persona used by a shadow or active policy is not edited in place. Instead, it is cloned, the clone is refined, and the resulting candidate proceeds through offline evaluation, review, and rollout. Thus, each iteration creates an explicit candidate while the currently active allowlist remains unchanged. To reduce overfitting to a fixed benchmark, offline evaluation uses three complementary pools: a golden set of curated, unambigu-ous behaviors that acts as a regression floor; an adversarial set of prompt injections, jailbreaks, and difficult boundary cases; and a 

production set sampled from live traffic. Examples used to motivate a refinement are kept separate from the held-out examples used to validate it. A candidate is accepted only if it satisfies the predefined thresholds across all three pools. Once approved, the candidate snapshot is distributed as declara-tive configuration rather than as a serving-code deployment. This allows per-agent policies to be promoted independently without

## 5.5 Continuous Guardrail Evolution 

Persona Guardrail evolves primarily by refining its human-readable intent taxonomy rather than retraining the classifier. Refinement may be triggered by newly introduced agent functionality, newly observed attacks, distribution shifts, or adjudicated classification errors. These signals initiate investigation; they never modify an active policy automatically. Examples are partitioned before refinement into a discovery pool and held-out validation pools. Correlated conversations, near-duplicate examples, and variants from the same attack campaign remain in the same partition. The discovery pool is used to propose changes, whereas acceptance is measured on held-out golden, ad-versarial, and production-derived examples. Deduplication, prove-nance tracking, and per-source sampling limits reduce evaluation leakage and the risk that one actor dominates a production-derived sample. Golden and synthetic examples have labels assigned during con-struction. For otherwise unlabeled production examples, a stronger judge model is given the agent’s functional boundary and the in-teraction, but not the guardrail’s decision, and predicts whether the interaction is permitted. Judge-model disagreements with the guardrail, together with a sample of agreements, are independently adjudicated before inclusion in reported metrics. We treat an out-of-scope or adversarial interaction as the positive class: a true positive is correctly blocked, a false negative is incorrectly allowed, a false positive is a legitimate interaction that is blocked, and a true nega-tive is a legitimate interaction that is allowed. Thus, disagreement with either the judge or the active policy is not by itself treated as an error label. A separate offline proposer groups errors from the discovery pool and suggests changes only to a new draft. The proposer cannot mod-ify labels, approve its proposal, or change production policy. The resulting candidate is evaluated on the held-out pools and enters Persona Guardrail: A Production-Grade Defense Framework for Agentic Systems

## 5.6 Human-in-the-Loop Guardrail Management 

Although Persona Guardrail automates much of the guardrail lifecy-cle, human oversight remains essential for maintaining high-quality functional and security boundaries. Misclassified interactions iden-tified through production monitoring are periodically reviewed by developers or security analysts to determine whether they corre-spond to evolving user intent, product changes, or novel attack strategies. Legitimate user interactions that fall outside the current allowlist may indicate that the agent’s functional boundary should be expanded, whereas newly observed attack patterns may require updates to the blocklist. Human review is particularly valuable for validating ambiguous interactions and resolving conflicts between usability and security. Rather than directly incorporating every production observation into the guardrail specification, Persona Guardrail treats human feedback as a high-confidence signal for refining both functional and security boundaries. 

## 5.7 Multi-Agent Deployment 

Large production systems often consist of numerous specialized agents, each serving a distinct purpose while sharing common infrastructure and security requirements. Deploying independent guardrails for every agent quickly becomes difficult to manage as the number of agents increases. Persona Guardrail addresses this challenge by separating agent-specific functional boundaries from shared security knowl-edge . Per-agent functional boundaries are stored as versioned records in a relational (MySQL) store and managed through the Registry, which exposes a management API for creating, updating, and versioning each agent’s allowlists and out-of-domain defini-tions. The registry serves as the single source of truth consumed by both the tuning pipeline and the policy engine, giving agent teams a self-serve control plane while ensuring a consistent view of policy across the fleet. The shared security blocklist—covering com-mon attack patterns such as prompt injection, jailbreaks, indirect prompt injection, and prompt leakage—is maintained centrally and referenced by every agent, so a signature added once is enforced everywhere. This separation enables new attack signatures discovered in one agent to be propagated rapidly across the entire deployment while allowing each agent to maintain its own functional specification. As a result, Persona Guardrail scales efficiently to heterogeneous multi-agent deployments without requiring every guardrail to be developed and maintained independently. 

## 5.8 Results and Operational Lessons 

In production, Persona Guardrail meets its latency budget with headroom while sustaining a false-block rate below 1% and a false-allow rate below 2% across various agents. Our deployment experience highlights that maintaining produc-tion guardrails is fundamentally an ongoing operational process rather than a one-time model development task. Effective guardrails require continuous observability, standardized evaluation, struc-tured feedback collection, and disciplined deployment practices. By integrating these operational components into a unified lifecy-cle, Persona Guardrail enables organizations to maintain robust security while adapting to evolving products, user behavior, and adversarial threats. Two further lessons stand out from operating Persona Guard-rail at scale. First, a guardrail is a specialized inference workload— prefill-dominated and single-token, with a large shared prompt prefix—rather than a general-purpose chat model; meeting its trans-parency budget followed from exploiting that structure through prefix-cache reuse, frontend/decode disaggregation, and an efficient model, not from generic serving tuning. Second, decoupling policy iteration from infrastructure deployment—by expressing guardrail versions as peer-reviewed configuration changes—was essential to reconciling rapid iteration with production safety. 

Scope and Limitations. Persona Guardrail is a defense-in-depth control evaluated for direct malicious-user and out-of-domain in-teractions under the defined threat model. The reported results do not establish complete protection against indirect prompt injection, compromised components, unauthorized intermediate tool actions, multi-turn attacks, or other threats outside that model. Deployment decisions and acceptable error rates remain application-specific. 

## 6 Related Work 

Integrating LLMs with external tools, APIs etc and deploying it to serve specific a purpose introduces attack surfaces beyond tra-ditional LLM safety, including prompt injection, indirect prompt injection, goal hijacking, retrieval poisoning, and tool misuse [8, 14]. A recent systematization of knowledge [12] consolidates these at-tacks and defenses and highlights needs for scalable, adaptive, and deployable runtime protection—the gap Persona Guardrail targets. 

Security Defenses for AI Agents. Runtime defenses target specific attacks during execution. Task Shield [11] enforces task alignment ,checking that intermediate steps serve the user’s task; MELON [28] re-executes the trajectory with a masked, task-neutral user prompt and flags injection when the masked and original tool calls coin-cide; and LlamaFirewall [4], in production at Meta, chains jailbreak, chain-of-thought alignment, and code scanners over agent inputs, reasoning, and outputs. A complementary line enforces security by construction: CaMeL [5] separates control and data flow so untrusted content cannot alter the program; the Instruction Hier-archy [21], StruQ [2], and SecAlign [3] train models to prioritize trusted instructions; and Progent [20] and IsolateGPT [23] con-strain tool privileges and isolate components. These defenses chiefly address indirect , environment-mediated attacks on the agent’s in-ternal execution. Persona Guardrail is complementary: under a 

direct malicious-user threat model it models an agent’s functional boundary and enforces it with input and output guardrails, cap-turing adversarial, out-of-domain, and policy-violating turns alike. 

Guardrails for LLM Applications. Application guardrails fall into rule-based toolkits (NeMo [17]), model-based classifiers (Llama Guard [10], ShieldGemma [25], WildGuard [9]), and hybrids. Clos-est to our work, Constitutional Classifiers [19] train input and Pal et al. 

output classifiers from a natural-language policy with production-grade overhead. These are typically static, inference-time classifiers over global harm categories. Persona Guardrail instead enforces a per-agent functional boundary —an allowlist of intended intents that also rejects competent but out-of-scope requests—and treats guardrails as continuously evolving systems maintained via specification, standardized evaluation, and feedback-driven refine-ment. 

Benchmarks for Agentic Security. Agentic-security evaluation has standardized around benchmarks for attacks and utility— AgentDojo [6], InjecAgent [26], AgentHarm [1], and Agent Security Bench [27]—none of which label whether a request lies inside an agent’s intended function. Recognizing out-of-scope requests has a long lineage in task-oriented dialog, e.g., CLINC150 [13]. PAGE builds on AgentDojo’s domains but adds agent-specific out-of-domain data and labels for both user and agent turns, jointly measuring attack robustness and rejection of out-of-scope requests. In sum, prior work advances attacks and class-specific runtime, architectural, and training defenses, plus classifiers for global safety policies; Persona Guardrail contributes a production-grade frame-work for specifying, enforcing, evaluating, and continuously evolv-ing an agent’s functional boundary under a direct malicious-user threat model. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We presented Persona Guardrail, a runtime defense framework that enforces agent-specific functional boundaries through syn-chronous validation of user inputs and final agent responses. We also introduced PAGE, a benchmark covering benign, adversarial, and out-of-domain interactions across user and agent turns. On the evaluated non-mixed subset of PAGE, an iteratively refined allowlist improved accuracy from 85.7% for a generic LLM judge to 95.9%, increased out-of-domain detection from 57.3% to 93.5%, and reduced the false-approved rate from 25.0% to 4.7%. Our deploy-ment study further shows that functional-boundary classification can be served within a sub-100 ms P90 per-call latency budget using a disaggregated, prefix-cache-aware inference architecture. These results indicate that explicit, agent-specific allowlists can comple-ment attack-focused defenses by rejecting both malicious requests and requests outside an agent’s intended scope. Future work will extend Persona Guardrail in two directions. First, we plan to develop conversation-level persona enforcement that evaluates each interaction in the context of previous turns, enabling the guardrail to identify functional-boundary violations that emerge across a multi-turn conversation. Second, we plan to integrate persona enforcement at the model execution layer so that intermediate tool calls can be validated against the agent’s functional boundary before execution. Together, these extensions will expand Persona Guardrail from turn-level input and output validation to conversation- and tool-level enforcement.

## A.1 Iterating the allowlist on held-out data. 

To ensure refinement is not overfitting, we partitioned PAGE into disjoint two-way (50 /50) and three-way (33 /33 /33) splits and scored iterations on unseen data (Table 12). Testing v1 (initial), v2 (iterated, Table 6), and v3 (refined from v2’s part 2 errors, tested on part 3) showed monotonic accuracy gains on unseen data (92 .4% → 96 .0% for 50 /50; 91 .9% → 96 .1% → 96 .7% for 33 /33 /33). Furthermore, 

v3 cut false approvals (5 .1% → 2.7%) and boosted out-of-domain detection (93 .9% → 95 .8%), confirming true functional-boundary gains over overfitting.
