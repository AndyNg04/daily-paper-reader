Abstract 

Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) is increasingly used in applications where large language models (LLMs) interact with humans and other agents. Here we use social deduction games to study how RFT changes LLMs’ social behaviour. We let fine-tuned and base LLM agents play hidden-role games that require hidden-state inference, social reading and vote steering. Our results show that LLM agents do not reliably acquire social-deduction ability by directly optimizing ter-minal win–loss outcomes, suggesting that final game results provide a sparse and noisy signal for socially interactive learning. However, RFT is particularly effective at improving social reading, including tasks that require agents to infer hidden roles from public discussion, update beliefs over time and predict other agents’ future decisions. We further show that RFT can also improve social influence, including tasks that require agents to steer votes, team approvals and collective decisions, although these gains depend more strongly on behaviourally specific rewards and structured interaction settings. Finally, we show that LLMs’ ability to play social deduction games can be further improved through multi-agent social-cognitive reinforcement fine-tuning, which combines social-reading and social-influence signals during same-side multi-agent training. These learned 

1arXiv:2610.04261v1 [cs.CL] 3 Oct 2026 behaviours also receive more favourable human evaluations of strategic compe-tence, persuasiveness and social usefulness. Together, these results enrich our understanding of how RFT changes LLMs’ social behaviour and provide a step toward a behavioural learning theory for machine social intelligence.

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable progress in recent years, demonstrating strong capabilities in language understanding [1], reasoning [2–4], coding [5, 6] and decision-making across diverse domains [7–10]. These capabilities are increasingly turning LLMs from passive text generators into agents that act in interactive environments, including tool-use workflows [11], collaborative problem solving [12–15], multi-agent simulations [16, 17], human–AI decision-making environ-ments [18] and artificial societies [19–21]. At the same time, reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) has become a central post-training mechanism for adapting pretrained LLMs for goal-directed agency [22, 23]. By optimizing model behaviour with task-specific reward signals, RFT can improve reasoning quality [24], response behaviour [25] and long-horizon task performance [26–29]. Together, these trends raise a central question: how does RFT shape the social reasoning of LLM agents when they interact with other agents? Understanding how people learn to navigate social interactions, for example, how they infer others’ intentions, build trust, adapt communication and influ-ence group decisions, is a central topic across social cognition, social learning and behavioural game theory. These perspectives emphasize complementary aspects of social behaviour. Social cognition and theory of mind study how people infer others’ beliefs, intentions and likely actions from limited behavioural evidence [30]. Social learning theory examines how behaviour changes through observation, feedback and the consequences of others’ actions [31]. Behavioural game theory further studies how people cooperate, coordinate, compete and adapt in strategic interactions [32]. Together, these perspectives provide a behavioural framework for studying how LLM-based agents acquire social competence through interaction: they must read other agents from observable behaviour, update their strategies from feedback and act in ways that shape collective outcomes. In this Article, we analyse how reinforcement fine-tuning changes LLM agents’ social behaviour by letting fine-tuned agents play social deduction games against pretrained LLM agents. Social deduction games have been designed to study how agents reason and act when social identities are hidden, information is asymmetric, communication is public and decisions are made collectively. Analysing agents’ performance across these games, we find that RFT improves social reasoning in a reward-dependent way. Fine-tuned agents perform particularly well on social reading tasks, including hidden-role inference from public discussion and prediction of other agents’ future decisions. Direct outcome-only training, however, is unstable because terminal wins provide a sparse and entangled signal for dialogue, belief formation and group decisions. Social influence is not absent from learning: in controlled vote-steering and team-approval tasks, agents learn to move other players’ decisions when the reward explicitly captures the intended downstream social effect. This contrast motivates our main methodological claim: social deduction should be decomposed into behaviourally grounded social-cognitive targets, and then recombined through a training protocol that respects the multi-agent structure of the game. We therefore study same-side multi-agent RFT, where all roles on a target side are trained from shared game rollouts and terminal win rate is used as an evaluation diagnostic rather than as the primary online reward. This design tests whether learned social-cognitive behaviours transfer from local diagnostics to full-game outcomes in LLM– LLM interaction. Analysing agents’ performance across these games, we find that RFT changes LLM agents’ social behaviour in a structured but reward-dependent way. Directly optimiz-ing terminal win–loss outcomes does not reliably produce general social-deduction ability, because final game results entangle hidden-role inference, public communica-tion, vote coordination and other agents’ responses into a sparse feedback signal. By 3contrast, when the reward targets specific social-cognitive functions, RFT produces clearer improvements. Fine-tuned agents become better at social reading, including inferring hidden roles from public discussion, updating beliefs as new evidence accu-mulates and predicting other agents’ future decisions. They can also acquire social influence: in vote-steering and team-approval tasks, trained agents learn to move other players’ decisions toward strategically relevant targets, although these effects are more sensitive to the reward definition and interaction setting than social-reading gains. We then ask whether these component improvements can be converted into stronger full-game play through multi-agent social-cognitive reinforcement fine-tuning, which trains agents with social-reading and social-influence signals in complete game trajec-tories. Finally, we evaluate whether the resulting behaviours are perceived by human observers as more strategic, persuasive and socially useful. 

# 2 Results 

Using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, Qwen3-4B-Instruct, GLM-4-9B-Chat, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, we study whether reinforcement fine-tuning can improve LLM agents’ social capabilities in social deduction games, including One Night Werewolf, Werewolf/Mafia, Avalon, Secret Hitler, and Spyfall. Our main controlled experiments focus on Avalon and One Night Werewolf, where we examine two focal capabilities: social reading and social influence. 

## 2.1 Analysing RFT across social deduction games 

We start our experiments by evaluating reinforcement fine-tuning across major families of social deduction games. We group these games into three macro-families accord-ing to how hidden information becomes public evidence: Elimination, Institution, and Asymmetry. Elimination games reveal hidden roles through discussion, accusa-tions, voting, kills, and removals; Institution games reveal hidden allegiances through structured procedures such as team proposals, missions, elections, and policies; and Asymmetry games reveal hidden private knowledge or culpability through questions, answers, clues, and consistency checks. For each macro-family, we instantiate two rep-resentative games: Werewolf/Mafia and One Night Werewolf for Elimination, Avalon and Secret Hitler for Institution, and Spyfall and Deception: Murder in Hong Kong for Asymmetry. We show example game traces and decision points for each macro-family in Fig. 2. For each game, we construct a compact training environment in which the fine-tuned model controls one focal player, while the other players are implemented as fixed pretrained LLM agents and are not updated during RFT. During training, the focal player is sampled across available roles and teams whenever the game permits, rather than being tied to a single role. This mixed-role setup prevents the policy from overfitting to a fixed perspective and requires it to act under different hidden states, including innocent, adversarial, and special-information roles. Across games, the reward curves show that direct RFT on social deduction out-comes is challenging. Rather than producing uniformly monotonic improvements, training often leads to high-variance and game-dependent dynamics. The cross-game results above show that outcome-based RFT does not uniformly improve overall social deduction performance. We therefore turn to controlled experi-ments that isolate two core social capabilities required by these games: social reading ,the ability to infer hidden social states from others’ behaviour, and social influence ,the ability to shape other players’ beliefs and decisions through communication. This allows us to examine when RFT fails on aggregate game outcomes, and when it can still improve specific social skills. 

2.2.1 Social Reading 

We study social reading along three increasingly game-integrated settings. First, we test whether agents can infer hidden states such as roles or teams from observed dia-logue and actions. Second, we examine whether agents can update these beliefs as new evidence accumulates over the course of a game. Third, we analyse a role-specialized case in Avalon, where the Assassin’s success depends strongly on identifying Merlin. Together, these experiments separate static inference, sequential belief updating, and the use of reading for role-specific game success. 

Hidden-state inference. 

We first isolate social reading as a static hidden-state inference problem. In this setting, the agent observes a partial game transcript and is asked to predict latent social variables that are not directly visible from the dialogue alone. For Avalon, the target is each player’s hidden team alignment. For Werewolf/Mafia, the target is the hidden wolf set. This evaluation removes the need to optimize full-game strategy and instead asks whether RFT can improve the agent’s ability to infer social state from public behaviour, claims, votes, and outcomes. Fig. 4 shows that RFT improves hidden-state inference in both games across mul-tiple model families. In Avalon, Qwen2.5-7B improves from an early-training accuracy of approximately 0 .55 to a late-training accuracy of approximately 0 .78. Other com-pleted runs show the same qualitative trend, including Qwen2.5-14B (0 .65 to 0 .71), Qwen3-4B (0 .62 to 0 .70), and GLM-4-9B (0 .58 to 0 .69). We additionally include DeepSeek-7B as a replication model family. Early DeepSeek-Avalon runs showed that reward shaping can trade off evil-player recall against good-player accuracy, so the final Avalon DeepSeek curve is being generated with a raw-accuracy reward to avoid this drop. We therefore treat this curve as a placeholder until the stable run com-pletes. Thus, even though full Avalon outcome training is unstable, the more controlled alignment-inference task yields a clear positive learning signal across the completed model families. The Werewolf/Mafia setting shows a similar pattern. Role-specialized reading. 

The preceding analyses show that RFT can improve two basic components of social reading: inferring hidden states from observed behaviour and updating beliefs as new evidence appears. We next ask whether such reading ability can translate into improved play when the game role itself makes a specific hidden state strategically 9decisive. Avalon provides a natural test case through the Assassin role. Unlike roles whose success depends on a mixture of team coordination, proposal quality, voting behaviour, and deception, the Assassin has a sharply defined terminal reading problem: if the evil team fails to win through missions, it can still win by correctly identifying and assassinating Merlin. This makes the Assassin a useful role-specialized case study of whether RFT can turn social reading into game-level success. To test this, we run a fixed-role variant of the Avalon RFT experiment. The training protocol remains outcome-based: the focal agent receives episode-level reward accord-ing to whether its side wins, with no direct supervision on Merlin identity, role beliefs, or intermediate explanations. The difference from the mixed-role Avalon experiment is that the focal agent is held fixed to one role during training. Those experiments isolate social reading as a capability; the Assassin experiment shows a case in which the game mechanics make that capability directly payoff-relevant.

2.2.2 Social Influence 

We next study social influence, the ability to shape other agents’ beliefs and decisions through communication. Unlike social reading, where the target label is a hidden state to be inferred from evidence, social influence requires the focal agent’s language to causally affect the behaviour of other agents. This introduces a longer and noisier credit-assignment path: a message may be well formed or strategically appropriate, but the reward is realized only if other players interpret it as intended and change their votes or approvals. We therefore examine influence in two controlled settings. First, in One Night Werewolf, we test whether a Minion can steer final votes away from the hidden Werewolf and toward a specified non-wolf target. Second, in Avalon, we test whether agents can influence collective team approval decisions during proposal and voting phases. We then perform robustness checks across seeds and model families. Together, these experiments ask whether RFT can learn reliable persuasive control, or whether social influence remains less stable than the social-reading capabilities studied above. 

Vote steering. 

We first examined social influence in a vote-steering setting based on One Night Werewolf. In this task, the trained agent plays the Minion, who knows the hidden Werewolf and wins with the werewolf team. Unlike social-reading tasks, the objective is not to identify the hidden role correctly. Instead, the agent must use public discus-sion to redirect the final votes of other players away from the Werewolf and toward a non-wolf target. To avoid the confound introduced by externally fixed targets, we used a self-selection variant: the Minion chooses its own vote target during discussion and is rewarded according to whether other players’ final votes concentrate on this self-selected target while avoiding the Werewolf. This design makes the influence tar-get endogenous to the agent’s own strategy, rather than assuming that an externally specified target is always socially plausible. RFT improved self-selected vote steering for most models, but the strength of the effect varied substantially across model families (Fig. 8a). DeepSeek-7B showed the strongest improvement, increasing target vote hit rate by 28 .7% from the first 20 to the last 20 training steps. Team approval. 

Vote steering tests whether an agent can redirect individual voting behaviour in a hidden-role setting. We next consider a complementary form of social influence in Avalon, where the relevant decision is not a single player’s final vote against a sus-pect, but a collective approval decision over a proposed mission team. This setting differs from vote steering in two ways. First, the target behaviour is an aggregate team-approval outcome: the trained agent must make enough other players approve or reject the proposal in the target direction. Second, the proposal itself provides a structured social object, including the proposed members, their roles from the focal agent’s perspective, and the public history of previous votes and mission outcomes. Thus, the task tests whether RFT can make agents use public language to shift a group decision under a well-defined strategic context. For each training episode, the focal agent observes an Avalon game state and a proposed team, then generates one public response before the approval vote. Other agents first cast baseline votes without this response and then vote again after observ-ing it. The reward is based on whether the post-response vote outcome matches the target approval direction, with additional signal from the change in other players’ votes toward that direction. This design separates influence from direct action: the trained agent cannot set the outcome by itself, but must produce a message that changes how other agents vote. RFT consistently improved team-approval influence across model families (Fig. 9a). From the first 20 to the last 20 training steps, target approval outcome increased for all evaluated models: Qwen2.5-7B improved by 21 .3%, Qwen2.5-14B by 12 .5%, Qwen3-4B by 10 .6%, DeepSeek-7B by 16 .3% and GLM-4-9B by 18 .1%. These gains indicate that the approval setting provides a learnable influence signal: the trained response increasingly moves the final collective decision toward the intended direc-tion. Importantly, the improvement was not merely a consequence of generating longer responses. Response length, measured in model-specific tokens, changed heteroge-neously across models (Fig. 9b): some models produced slightly longer responses after training, whereas DeepSeek-7B improved despite shorter responses. ## 2.3 Joint Multi-Agent Social-Cognitive Reinforcement Fine-Tuning 

Having studied social reading and social influence as separable cognitive objectives, we next examine whether they can be combined into a joint multi-agent training framework. In social deduction games, reading and influence are not independent modules: an agent must infer hidden roles, anticipate how other players interpret the dialogue, and then use its own messages and actions to shift the collective decision process. We therefore evaluate social-cognitive ability at two complementary levels. First, we perform prompt-level multi-agent social-cognitive ablations without parameter updates. This isolates the behavioural effect of explicitly providing agents with social-reading and social-influence scaffolds. By comparing agents with nei-ther scaffold, only social reading, only social influence, or both, we test whether 16 the two components are individually useful and whether their combination improves group-level outcomes in matched game settings. Second, we study full-game multi-agent reinforcement fine-tuning. In this setting, the trainable policy controls all players on a target side and is optimized from com-plete game trajectories rather than isolated decisions. The online reward emphasizes intermediate social-cognitive signals, including hidden-role identification, vote coor-dination, and successful movement of group decisions toward strategically beneficial targets; terminal win rate is logged and reported as a held-out game-level diagnos-tic. This setting tests whether social-cognitive reinforcement signals can improve the behaviour of an interacting side as a whole, rather than only improving a single agent’s local prediction or persuasion accuracy. Together, these experiments ask whether social-cognitive structure is useful before learning, through prompt-level scaffolding, and whether it can be further internalized through reinforcement fine-tuning in a live multi-agent game. The prompt ablations provide a controlled estimate of the value of the social-reading and social-influence components, while the full-game RFT experiments test whether these components can be converted into improved policies under endogenous interaction, hidden information and strategic opposition. 

2.3.1 Prompt-level multi-agent social-cognitive ablations 

Before applying reinforcement fine-tuning, we first tested whether the proposed social-cognitive scaffolds change the behaviour of pretrained agents at inference time. This ablation isolates the prompt-level contribution of the two components used throughout the paper: social reading (SR), which requires agents to maintain explicit beliefs about hidden roles, and social influence (SI), which requires agents to formulate an intended effect on other players’ beliefs or actions. Because no model weights are updated in this experiment, differences across conditions reflect the effect of the social-cognitive interface itself rather than learned policy improvement. For each model, we evaluated four prompting conditions: no social-cognitive scaf-fold, SR only, SI only and the joint SR+SI scaffold. Game rules, role distributions, opponent policies and decoding parameters were held fixed across conditions. The con-trolled agents played the good side in Werewolf, and performance was measured by good-side win rate. Across all evaluated models, the joint SR+SI scaffold improved win rate relative to the no-scaffold condition, suggesting that explicit hidden-role inference and explicit influence planning provide complementary benefits in multi-agent social deduction. As shown in Table 1, the joint SR+SI scaffold improved good-side win rate for every model we evaluated, although the magnitude of the gain varied across model families. The largest improvements were observed for Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-4B, both of which gained 13 percentage points relative to the no-scaffold condition. Qwen2.5-14B and DeepSeek-7B also showed clear improvements under the joint scaffold, whereas GLM-4-9B exhibited a smaller but still positive gain. The single-component ablations reveal that the two scaffolds are not simply inter-changeable. The trained agents become more effective social-deduction players before they become fully natural social actors. 

# 3 Discussion 

Our results provide a behavioural account of how reinforcement fine-tuning can shape the social capabilities of language-model agents. In human social learning, individu-als gradually adapt their beliefs and actions by observing others, anticipating their responses and experiencing the consequences of their own behaviour. RFT imple-ments a computational analogue of part of this process. A language model produces a socially situated action, observes an outcome generated jointly by itself and other agents, and updates its policy towards behaviours associated with higher reward. The analogy should not be taken to imply that an RFT-trained model acquires human mental states or learns through the same cognitive mechanisms as a person. Rather, RFT provides a feedback-mediated mechanism through which a pretrained language policy can acquire behavioural regularities that are useful in social interaction. 24 This perspective helps explain why terminal game outcomes alone are often insuffi-cient. A win or loss is the final consequence of a long causal chain involving hidden-role inference, public communication, voting, role-specific actions, other agents’ mistakes and stochastic game events. The same terminal outcome can therefore follow from very different behaviours, and an individually useful action can occur in a losing trajec-tory. Outcome-only RFT compresses these causes into a single sparse signal and leaves the learning algorithm with a difficult multi-agent credit-assignment problem. This is reflected in the high-variance and non-monotonic training dynamics observed across the initial game suite. The result does not show that game outcomes are irrelevant. Instead, it shows that terminal outcomes are often more informative as integrative tests of an acquired policy than as the only signal from which the policy must be learned. Social reading becomes learnable when the environment exposes a tighter link between observable behaviour and a scoreable latent state. In our experiments, pub-lic claims, votes, team proposals, eliminations and mission outcomes provide partial evidence about hidden roles. The simulator can then compare an agent’s belief state with the underlying role assignment and provide feedback at the point where the infer-ence is made. RFT can strengthen social reading because the reward identifies which interpretations of the observed trajectory were useful, rather than rewarding only the eventual game outcome. The improvements in later-day and later-mission beliefs are particularly informative. They suggest that the learned policy is not merely acquiring a static prior over roles or a better output format; it is becoming more effective at integrating evidence that accumulates through interaction. Social influence is learned through a different feedback structure. The quality of an influential message cannot be determined from its linguistic form alone. Its con-sequence depends on how other agents interpret the message and whether their later decisions move in the intended direction. Influence therefore requires action-contingent feedback: the environment must connect an agent’s communicative intention to a mea-surable change in votes, approvals or other collective behaviour. The vote-steering and team-approval experiments show that this longer causal pathway can neverthe-less be optimized. Once the reward specifies both an intended social target and the downstream behaviour used to evaluate it, RFT can select messages that become increasingly consequential for group decisions. The distinction between reading and influence is conceptually important. Reading without influence produces an agent that may represent the social state accurately but cannot convert that representation into coordinated action. Influence without reading can be more problematic: an agent may become effective at moving a group while directing that movement towards a mistaken or strategically harmful target. This pattern is visible in the prompt-level ablations, where influence scaffolding is not uniformly beneficial unless it is grounded in an explicit belief state. The joint social-cognitive objective addresses this dependency by coupling an estimate of the hidden social state with an intended effect on public behaviour. In this sense, socially competent agency requires a closed loop: observe others, infer the latent situation, select a social objective, act communicatively and update from the resulting group response. 25 Same-side multi-agent training extends this loop beyond an isolated focal agent. Social deduction is inherently a team-level problem in which different roles possess different information and opportunities for action. Assigning one trainable policy to all roles on a target side allows learning to occur across several role-conditioned perspectives while preserving each player’s private observation. A single game can con-sequently provide multiple training trajectories connected by a shared social outcome. The resulting gains in held-out win rate indicate that intermediate social-cognitive rewards can produce improvements that matter at the level of the complete game. This is a stronger test than improvement on the training reward alone: it asks whether better role inference and communication combine into a more effective multi-agent policy. More broadly, these findings suggest a practical route for constructing specialized social agents. Many real applications do not require an agent to possess unrestricted “general social intelligence”. They require a narrower and auditable social function: identifying disagreement in a team, eliciting missing information, helping participants converge on a plan, adapting an explanation to a user’s beliefs, mediating a negotiation or detecting when a group decision is being driven by unreliable evidence [33, 34]. Such behaviours could be trained with RFT when the desired social state and downstream effect can be operationalized. For example, a collaborative assistant could be rewarded for correctly identifying unresolved beliefs before proposing a compromise; a tutoring agent could be rewarded for inferring a learner’s misconception and then producing an explanation that leads to a corrected answer; and a coordination agent could be rewarded for increasing agreement without suppressing relevant dissent. The central design problem in these applications is therefore not simply how to supply more interaction data, but how to define a reward that identifies the intended causal role of communication. A reward for agreement alone may train conformity rather than useful coordination. A reward for task completion may encourage an agent to dominate a discussion, conceal uncertainty or exploit predictable user responses. A reward for persuasion may increase behavioural influence without improving the truth-fulness of the underlying belief. Our results suggest that social objectives should be decomposed into at least three components: whether the agent represents the situation accurately, whether its communicative intervention produces the intended effect, and whether that effect advances a normatively appropriate collective goal. Optimizing only the second component risks producing effective but misaligned influence. This point also makes social influence a dual-use capability. The same mecha-nisms that allow an agent to coordinate a team or help a user revise an incorrect belief could be used to steer choices, exploit social biases or produce strategically deceptive communication. Stronger influence should therefore not be treated as an unqualified improvement. In deployed systems, influence-oriented rewards may need to be constrained by truthfulness, consent, user autonomy and uncertainty disclo-sure [35]. Evaluation should measure not only whether a message changes behaviour, but whether the change is epistemically justified and beneficial to the affected par-ticipants. The smaller improvement in human-like gameplay observed in our blinded evaluation is also relevant here: strategic effectiveness and human-like appearance are 26 distinct properties, and increasing one does not necessarily justify making an agent more anthropomorphic. Our cross-model results provide initial evidence that the learned behaviours are not restricted to one fixed opponent. The trained good-side policy retained strong social-reading and coordination performance against multiple model families, suggesting that it learned regularities that transfer across different discourse and decision policies. The blinded trajectory evaluation provides complementary evidence: the RFT trajectories received higher ratings for social reading, influence, overall value and strategic cor-rectness. Thus, the improvement is visible both in simulator-defined metrics and in external assessments of complete behaviour. At the same time, generalization across LLM opponents does not establish generalization to humans. Human participants may interpret confidence, deception, uncertainty and social commitment differently from model agents, and interactive human responses can alter the trajectory distribution encountered by the policy. Several limitations delimit the present conclusions. First, social deduction games provide unusually explicit hidden states, discrete decisions and well-defined team objectives. These properties make social computations measurable, but many real social environments contain ambiguous identities, changing objectives and contested definitions of success. Second, our agents interact primarily with fixed or slowly chang-ing model policies. Simultaneous learning by multiple opposing agents could produce non-stationary dynamics, strategic escalation or cyclic behaviour not captured here. Third, the influence metrics establish that messages are followed by behavioural move-ment, but they do not fully identify the mechanism of that movement. An agent may succeed through evidence, rhetorical clarity, repetition, confidence or model-specific exploitation. Controlled interventions on message content and interactive human studies will be needed to separate these mechanisms. 

# 4 Related work 

Large language models have recently been used as computational agents in a wide range of game-theoretic and interactive settings [36, 37]. In behavioural and economic games, LLMs have been evaluated as players in repeated matrix games, bargaining games, negotiation tasks and cooperation dilemmas, where their choices reveal pat-terns of self-interest, reciprocity, coordination and adaptation [38–40]. These studies show that pretrained LLMs do not behave as random policies: they often display recognizable strategic tendencies, respond to incentives and sometimes approximate human-like patterns of cooperation or competition [38, 41, 42]. At the same time, their behaviour can be highly sensitive to framing, prompt wording, model family and interaction history, suggesting that the social behaviour of LLM agents is not a fixed property of the model alone but emerges from the interaction between model priors, task structure and communicative context [38]. A related line of work studies LLMs as social agents in richer multi-agent environ-ments. LLM-based agents have been deployed in simulations of societies, collaborative problem solving, negotiation, role-playing environments and human–AI interaction settings [19–21, 43–45]. These systems highlight the ability of language models to 27 maintain social roles, exchange information, coordinate plans and generate contex-tually appropriate communication. However, they also expose persistent limitations. LLM agents may fail to maintain consistent beliefs over long horizons, over-trust or under-use information from other agents, produce socially plausible but strategically weak messages, or rely on surface-level linguistic cues rather than grounded interaction history. As a result, games and simulations have become useful behavioural probes: they make social competence observable through choices, messages and downstream outcomes rather than through isolated text responses [38, 39, 45]. Hidden-information and social deduction games extend this line of work by com-bining strategic choice with public language, asymmetric information and collective decision-making [46–51]. In games such as Avalon, Werewolf and related hidden-role settings, agents must infer latent social identities from partial behavioural evidence, communicate under uncertainty, coordinate with possible allies and sometimes per-suade or mislead other players [46, 47, 49, 51]. These environments differ from standard matrix games in an important respect: the relevant state is not fully specified by a payoff table or by the recent action history. Instead, it is partly constructed through discussion, voting patterns, claims, accusations and the interpretations that other agents form from them [39, 46]. Social deduction games therefore provide a behavioural probe of language-mediated social reasoning, where competence is expressed not only in what an agent says, but in whether its messages and decisions change downstream beliefs, votes and outcomes [46, 47, 49]. Most existing studies of LLM agents in such environments have treated the model, together with its prompt, memory or scaffold, as the object to be evaluated [46, 47, 49, 50, 52, 53]. This perspective has been useful for identifying behavioural signatures of pretrained agents: whether they can keep track of roles, infer hidden information, coordinate with partners, detect deception or produce persuasive messages [46, 47, 49, 51, 54]. It also shows why social games are difficult benchmarks. A response can be locally fluent while failing to update beliefs, a vote can be individually reasonable while harming the team, and an apparently persuasive argument can have little effect on collective decisions. These observations suggest that social competence in LLM agents is better understood as a set of behaviourally grounded functions, rather than as a single property measured from isolated responses [38, 46, 55, 56]. Reinforcement fine-tuning has become a central mechanism for turning pretrained language models into goal-directed agents [2, 8, 22, 26, 27, 57–63]. Building on reinforcement learning from human feedback, preference optimization and related post-training methods, recent work has optimized LLMs with verifiable rewards, learned reward models, model-based evaluators and trajectory-level environment feed-back [64–68]. These approaches have improved reasoning, coding, instruction following, tool use and long-horizon agent tasks, showing that post-training can reshape not only response style but also decision policies in interactive settings [2, 69]. RFT extends the study of LLMs in social deduction games from static evaluation to experience-driven learning [57, 58, 70]. In many RFT settings, feedback is relatively direct: a solution is correct or incorrect, code passes or fails tests, a tool-use trajectory succeeds or fails, or a response is preferred by a judge [2, 65, 66, 71]. In hidden-role games, by contrast, rewards are delayed, stochastic and entangled with other agents’ 28 beliefs and actions [46, 49, 70]. An utterance may be fluent but fail to persuade; a vote may be locally reasonable but damage coordination; and a successful outcome may depend on how others interpret earlier messages. In this setting, RFT is not merely a post-training technique for improving task scores, but an operational model for studying how language agents acquire socially consequential behaviour through repeated interaction. 

# 5 Methods 

We describe the experimental and training framework used to study reinforcement fine-tuning of large language model agents in social deduction games. The methods are organized into three components. First, we introduce the AgentEvolver Game Arena, which generates interactive game trajectories, records hidden and public state, and converts natural-language interactions into structured training signals. Second, we describe the multi-agent social-cognitive reinforcement fine-tuning procedure used to optimize language-model agents for social reading and social influence objectives. Third, we outline a human-in-the-loop validation protocol that can test whether the model-only behavioural effects are recognizable and useful to human players. 

## 5.1 AgentEvolver Game Arena 

AgentEvolver Game Arena is a multi-agent environment for studying language-model behaviour in socially interactive games. The arena instantiates games with hidden roles, private observations, public discussion and consequential group decisions. It serves two purposes. First, it provides a controlled simulator in which LLM agents can interact with other model agents through natural language. Second, it converts these interactions into structured trajectories containing prompts, responses, parsed actions, hidden states, intermediate social outcomes and terminal game outcomes [72, 73]. The arena was designed to support both evaluation and reinforcement fine-tuning. During evaluation, it allows us to measure whether agents can infer hidden social infor-mation or influence collective decisions. During training, it provides trajectory-level rewards that can be assigned to specific players, roles or sides of the game. We describe the arena in three parts: the game environments, the multi-agent interaction protocol and the trajectory annotation procedure used to construct rewards and diagnostics. 

5.1.1 Social reasoning game environments 

We developed AgentEvolver Game Arena as a trajectory-generation environment for studying social cognition in language-model agents. The arena implements a set of partially observable multi-agent games in which agents must infer hidden information, communicate with other players and make consequential decisions from dialogue. In this work, we used three families of social reasoning environments: One Night Were-wolf, Classic Werewolf and Avalon. These games differ in horizon length, role structure and communication protocol, but share a common computational interface. ## 5.2 Multi-Agent Social-Cognitive Reinforcement Fine-Tuning 

We fine-tune language-model agents with reinforcement signals that target two com-plementary components of social cognition: social reading , the ability to infer latent social variables from dialogue and partial observations, and social influence , the ability 31 to alter other players’ subsequent beliefs or decisions through communication. These two abilities are closely related but not identical. A player may correctly infer hid-den roles without successfully persuading others, or may temporarily influence a vote without maintaining an accurate belief state. We therefore model them as separate reward components and later combine them into a same-side social-cognitive objective whose effect on terminal game outcomes is evaluated separately. For each episode τ , the arena records prompts, responses, parsed actions, private role information, public transcripts, intermediate social outcomes and terminal game outcomes. A controlled player i ∈ C generates responses according to

5.2.1 Social reading objective 

The social reading objective rewards the agent for inferring hidden social state from the observed interaction history. In social deduction games, the relevant hidden variables include team membership, private role information, night actions and latent strategic intentions. These variables are not directly available to the agent, but they leave traces in public discussion, voting patterns and the sequence of deaths or mission outcomes. The reading objective is intended to measure whether the agent can recover this latent structure from the information available to its role. At selected decision points, the agent emits a structured belief state bi,t in addition to its public response or action. Depending on the game and phase, this belief state may include suspected adversaries, trusted players, role hypotheses or predicted future actions. The simulator compares the belief state with the hidden state available to the arena but not to the agent. For each annotated reading event, the comparison is performed by a task-specific scoring function mread . For set-valued predictions, such as the set of suspected werewolves, mread is the F1 score between the predicted and true target sets; for categorical role predictions, it is exact-match accuracy. 5.2.5 Training regimes 

We study three training regimes. In the social reading regime, the reward emphasizes hidden-state inference. This setting tests whether an agent can learn to infer latent roles or team membership from partial observations and public dialogue. In the social influence regime, the reward emphasizes downstream behavioural change. This setting tests whether the agent can learn messages that alter votes, approvals or suspicion in a desired direction. In the joint social-cognitive regime, both social-cognitive components are combined with auxiliary intermediate game events:

In same-side runs, all controlled roles on the selected side are optimized together from the same rollout, and each controlled role contributes its own prompt-response sequence to the GRPO batch. This makes it possible to ask whether a particular social-cognitive capability improves when one side of the social system is optimized as a coordinated policy. During evaluation, we run independent held-out games rather than reusing the training reward as the sole metric. We report side win rate, role-specific win rate, valid-action rate, reading accuracy, vote distributions and influence-following rates. This separation between training rewards and evaluation metrics allows us to test whether reinforcement fine-tuning produces robust social behaviour rather than merely optimizing a particular reward function [86–88]. 

## 5.3 Human evaluation 

We conducted a blinded evaluation of complete nine-player Werewolf trajectories to determine whether the behavioural changes produced by social-cognitive reinforcement fine-tuning were recognizable at the level of complete games. The evaluation compared trajectories generated by the base policy and the reinforcement-fine-tuned policy. In both conditions, the candidate policy controlled all good-side players, while the wolf side was controlled by fixed opponent models. The human-evaluation component of this study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was reviewed by the Ethics Com-mittee and was determined to be exempt from ethics review. Informed consent was obtained from all human participants. 35 5.3.1 Trajectory selection 

We constructed matched base–RFT trajectory pairs under the same game identifier and opponent condition. Candidate trajectories were retained only when they con-tained complete model-call histories and public event records, contained no fallback public speech, and satisfied the predefined structured-output validity criterion. This filtering procedure was applied identically to both policy conditions. 

5.3.2 Blinded evaluation procedure 

Evaluators reviewed complete game trajectories containing the role configuration, public discussion, votes, eliminations, night actions, and the private belief and strategic-intent records of the candidate-controlled good-side players. The identity and training condition of the candidate policy were hidden during evaluation. Each trajectory was rated on a five-point integer scale along five dimensions: overall value, social-reading quality, social-influence and coordination, human-like gameplay, and strategic correctness. Evaluators were instructed to assess whether the agents’ beliefs were supported by the evidence available at the time, whether public communi-cation and votes were consistent with their private reasoning, and whether the resulting collective decisions were strategically appropriate. They were explicitly instructed not to assign a high score solely because the good side won. All ratings were analysed at the trajectory-rating level. The same evaluation rubric, scoring interface, and trajectory presentation format were used across policy conditions. 

5.3.3 Statistical analysis 

For each evaluation dimension, we calculated descriptive statistics separately for the base and reinforcement-fine-tuned conditions. Uncertainty intervals were estimated by non-parametric bootstrap resampling over trajectory ratings. We additionally performed matched comparisons between base and RFT trajec-tories generated under the same game identifier and opponent condition. For each evaluation dimension, we recorded whether the RFT trajectory received a higher, equal, or lower score than its matched base trajectory. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided paired sign-flip permutation tests. As a robustness check, the matched analysis was repeated after excluding tra-jectories involving an opponent model that overlapped with the evaluator model family.
