ABSTRACT 

Soft tactile sensors elevate robotic touch through enhanced flexibility and adaptability, yet most existing designs depend on embedded electronics that are susceptible to interference and environmental limitations. In this work, we leverage fluid–solid interactions to develop a class of soft tactile sensors that operate entirely without electronics at the sensing site. The sensor comprises a fluid-filled elastomeric channel connected to only two external pressure sensors. Touching different regions of the elastomeric surface displaces the viscous fluid, producing distinct pressure patterns that encode both touch position and force. These signals are decoded through a machine learning framework that integrates feature extraction, soft clustering, and adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference to achieve accurate localization and force estimation. We validate this concept through single-point touch localization and force estimation in a linear (1D) sensor and extend the same sensing principle to 2D tactile mapping by routing the channel across the surface using space-filling curves, while maintaining the same minimal hardware setup. This simple approach remains effective in environments where conventional electronic sensors often fail, such as underwater or in the presence of magnetic interference. 

KEYWORDS: Tactile Perception, Robotic Skin, Soft Fluidic Sensor, Fluid-Solid Interaction, Human-Robot Interaction 2

## INTRODUCTION 

Robots transitioning from structured factory floors to unstructured environments—such as homes, healthcare settings, and collaborative workspaces—require the ability to perceive and physically interact with their surroundings 1-7 . While vision has traditionally dominated robotic perception 8-10 ,visual sensing can be unreliable under occlusions, poor illumination, or reflective and cluttered scenes 9,11 . In these contexts, tactile perception becomes crucial for applications that involve direct physical interaction with humans or the environment 1,12,13 .Embedding tactile sensors with high spatial and temporal resolution, however, remains challenging due to fabrication complexity, integration constraints, and environmental sensitivity. Many established tactile sensing technologies rely on embedded electronic components, including capacitive 14,15 , resistive 16,17 , and optical 18 , as well as hybrid architectures. While effective, these approaches can face limitations when integrated into soft or deformable elastomeric substrates, where repeated strain may lead to debonding, conductor fatigue, or signal drift 19 .Motivated by these challenges, soft tactile sensors have emerged as a promising alternative, leveraging compliant materials to better conform to complex surfaces and interactions 20,21 . A wide range of transduction mechanisms has been explored, including magnetic 22,23 , barometric 6,24-27 ,resistive 28,29 , capacitive 30-32 , optical 33-38 , and vision-based methods 39-42 . Beyond material compliance, several studies have investigated strategies to relocate or minimize electronics at the contact interface 43-46 , motivated by considerations of mechanical compliance, electromagnetic interference 47 , and system integration. Removing electronics and electrical components from the sensing site is one of several strategies investigated to improve robustness. One approach includes sensing based on acoustic wave propagation 48-52 , in which a speaker emits a sound wave into a channel embedded within a soft membrane. Deformation of the channel under an external load alters the pressure wave, enabling touch detection. In addition, wave propagation can also be detected through the solid interface via Lamb wave in a thin elastomeric layer 48 . In another approach, Soter et al. 53 proposed a vision-based tactile sensor that utilizes a network of fluid channels embedded in an elastomer. By observing fluid displacement using an externally mounted camera, the system can infer deformation and applied load. Given the importance of fluidic systems in soft robotics, particularly pneumatics, many studies have focused on developing soft pneumatic tactile sensors 54 . One technique for touch detection involves using the concept of an electrical touchpad, where arrays of pneumatic lines are arranged in a row and column formation. For instance, Zha et al. 55 fabricated a 3×3 grid of pneumatic pouches, placing pressure sensors at the end of each row and column, enabling the detection of nine contact areas using only six pressure sensors. Similarly, Choi et al. 56 introduced a 3×3 configuration of pneumatic channels embedded in an elastomer, using a multi-layered design to simultaneously actuate and sense the deformation for haptic interactions. To further expand the sensing area and enhance spatial resolution, Lampinen et al. 19 developed a 16×16 microchannel array, where 32 pressure sensors enabled dense touch detection over a larger surface area. Truby et al. reconstructed the shape of a cubic lattice prism with 3×3 axially embedded, air-filled channels and qualitatively detected the touch location by processing the corresponding nine pressure readings 54 .3

In this work, we propose a soft tactile sensing approach that exploits fluid–solid interactions in viscous fluid-filled channels combined with machine learning, enabling single-point touch localization and force estimation using only two external pressure sensors. Pressure sensors are positioned away from the contact interface, while touch-induced deformation generates characteristic differential pressure signatures that encode contact information. Unlike prior fluidic tactile systems that scale resolution through sensor count, our approach leverages data-driven inference to extract spatial information from minimal hardware. We validate this approach for single-point touch localization and force estimation, first in a linear (1D) configuration and then extended to two-dimensional tactile mapping by routing channels along space-filling curves, enabling 2D tactile mapping with the same simple hardware setup. While improved mechanical resilience is a motivating consideration of this architecture, damage tolerance and long-term durability are not quantitatively evaluated in this study. Instead, the focus is on perceiving touch position and strength with minimal sensing hardware, supported by experimental characterization, simplified modeling, and computational simulations, including operation under environmental constraints and in human–robot interaction scenarios. 

## RESULTS 

Sensor design and working mechanism 

Our sensor consists of a fluid-filled channel embedded within an elastomeric structure and connected to two solid-state pressure sensors positioned at both ends of the channel. When the elastomer surface above the channel is touched, a small volume of fluid is displaced toward the ends, creating a pressure difference between the left and right sides, ∆𝑃 = 𝑃 ! − 𝑃 ", detected by the pressure sensors. In its simplest form, the sensor features a linear channel within a rectangular elastomer block (Fig. 1A). During a touch event, interactions between the viscous fluid and the soft elastomer generate distinct pressure responses that depend on the fluid medium and touch position (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Movie 1), as well as on the applied touch force (Fig. 1C). By processing these pressure profiles with a machine learning algorithm, we can accurately estimate both the touch position and force. 

This simple design can be extended to more complex surfaces by covering them with a meandering channel using a space-filling curve. In this work, we used the Hilbert curve 57 , which covers a 2D surface with a single continuous non-overlapping path (Fig. 1D). Therefore, we can apply the same principle used for the 1D linear sensor with only two sensor readings at both extremes. Fig. 1E shows recorded pressures along the dimensionless channel length 𝑠̃ at four selected locations within each quadrant of the sensing area. While increasing the length of the channel may reduce the amplitude of the pressure signal at distant points (Fig. 1F, top), the viscous effect, reflected in the rise time, 𝑡 #,in pressure differentials (Fig. 1F, bottom), provides an extra feature that further enables the machine learning algorithm to detect touches. To translate pressure readings into touch position and force estimates, we implemented the machine learning pipeline shown in Fig. 1G. Pressure time series were recorded from multiple touch locations for both 1D and 2D sensors. From the recorded signals, we extracted key features by calculating the maximum pressure at each end for the 1D sensor and both the maximum pressure and rise time 𝑡 # at each end for the 2D sensor. This approach reduced the data dimensionality and enhanced computational efficiency compared to analyzing the full time series. 4

We then applied soft clustering 58 by partitioning the feature space into overlapping regions so each touch event can belong partially to multiple clusters. This divides the variable dataset into smaller, more homogeneous subsets, enabling assigning separate models to each cluster and reducing overfitting. The resulting membership values, together with the extracted features, were fed into an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system 59 (ANFIS) to model the nonlinear mapping between sensor outputs and touch parameters. This method achieved high-accuracy, simultaneous prediction of touch location and applied force using only two pressure sensors for both 1D and 2D sensor configurations. 

Tactile response of the 1D sensor 

We began our analysis by performing comprehensive tests on a 1D linear sensor to thoroughly characterize its response to touches applied both on-channel (directly above the channel) and off-channel (laterally offset from the channel), across channels of varying dimensions and filled with different fluids. 

On-channel touch . Fig. 2A reports the difference between maximum left and right pressure ( ∆𝑃 = 𝑃 ! − 𝑃 ") and measured normal forces 𝐹 as a function of indentation depth Δ𝑧 when the sensor is touched on-channel at 𝑥/𝑙 = 0.1 for three channel diameters ( ø = 1, 1.5, 2 mm ) filled with air, water, or oil ( 𝜇 = 100 cSt ). The touch response exhibits three distinct phases. At low Δ𝑧 , the equilibrium pressure difference ∆𝑃 is nearly zero, making touch interpretation unfeasible (Supplementary Materials S3). This behavior results from rapid backflow within the channel, which equilibrates 𝑃 !

and 𝑃 ". As Δ𝑧 increases, the channel begins to collapse, restricting the backflow. Consequently, the pressure difference becomes more pronounced with deeper indentations, leading to a larger ∆𝑃 

values (Supplementary Movie 2). This middle region defines the sensor's effective working range, where accurate touch detection is possible. Beyond a certain indentation depth, the channel becomes fully occluded, preventing further fluid displacement. In this regime, ∆𝑃 saturates and ceases to vary with increasing Δ𝑧 , rendering touch detection unreliable (Supplementary Materials S3). The measured force 𝐹 is greater for smaller channels with more elastomer volume, offering structural support, resisting indentation. Moreover, due to a slight diffusion of silicone oil into the silicone rubber, we observed that sensors filled with silicone oil exhibited slightly lower forces at high indentation depths. Across all fluids, increasing the channel diameter ø, amplifies ∆𝑃 at the same Δ𝑧 , because more fluid volume is displaced during touch, but at the same time, the sensitivity of the sensor at a small Δ𝑧 

diminishes. Therefore, the channel diameter should be chosen based on the expected range of touch intensities. Among the three media, air—being compressible—produces the smallest pressure variations. In contrast, water and oil, which are nearly incompressible, produce notably larger ∆𝑃 . The oil pressure is much higher than the water pressure due to its higher viscosity, which more strongly restricts flow between the two sides of the touch point. In the following, we investigate the effect of the off-channel touches, the rise time in pressure histories, and the channel length in 1D sensors as a transition from 1D to 2D sensor design.

This reversal can be attributed to the lower viscosity of water and the high compressibility of air, leading to a faster pressure saturation. For air and water, the rate of pressure change is the same for left and right probes, whereas in oil-filled channels, the viscosity determines the rise time.

For asymmetric indentations ( 𝑥/𝑙 = 0.1, 0.9 ), a pressure drop was observed immediately after indentation, highlighting the effect of backflow. To further investigate this effect, we systematically increased the indentation depth by increasing the applied force (Supplementary Materials S8). Fig. 3F shows the pressure history at two applied pressure levels corresponding to moderate ( Δ𝑧 = 1.1 mm ) and strong ( Δ𝑧 = 1.8 mm ) touches for channels filled with oils with three different viscosities. At moderate indentations, the pressure drop was largest for the oil with the lowest viscosity and decreased as viscosity increased. For strong touches, since the channel was nearly blocked under the indenter pressure, backflow significantly decreased, and the pressure drop diminished for all viscosities. We quantified the flux across the channel for these two cases in Fig. 3G, confirming that less viscous fluids and lighter touches result in larger backflow. Fig. 3H shows snapshots of the transient pressure contours for the low-viscosity oil under the two indentation depths considered above. Finally, we measured the pressure difference ∆𝑃 once the maximum indentation load is attained under two assumptions at different indentation depths. First, we considered an extreme case with a virtual interior wall that fully closed the channel, dividing it into two separate fluid domains, similar to the simplified theoretical model. Second, we considered an initially open channel that allows backflow. In this scenario, the channel is considered closed once the channel walls come into contact, with a small offset to ensure numerical convergence. We performed simulations for air and different oils with varying viscosities, as presented in Fig. 3I. The results show that under the channel closure assumption, ∆𝑃 is the same for all oil viscosities and slightly higher than in a compressible air medium. When backflow is allowed, the pressure quickly equilibrates for air, whereas for oil, higher viscosity leads to a larger ∆𝑃 . As expected, stronger touches always result in higher pressure differences. The nearly linear behavior of ∆𝑃 versus Δ𝑧 under the closed-channel assumption or for high-viscosity fluids confirms the validity of our simplified theoretical model in these limiting cases. 

Tactile response of the 2D Sensor 

Next, we characterized the tactile response of our proposed 2D sensor, which is an extension of the 1D design where the channel follows a meandering path to cover a 2D surface. Fig. 4A shows a 2D sensor designed with a 4th-order Hilbert curve. To better visualize the indentation response, we divided the sensor into four quadrants ( 𝑄 %, 𝑄 &, 𝑄 ., 𝑄 / ). The position along the channel was normalized by its total length as 𝑠̃ = 𝑠/𝑙 . In the following, we examine the response of the 2D sensor to on-channel touches. 8

We measured the pressures at both ends of the 2D channel while indenting the sensor at 16 locations, defined as 𝑠̃ = (2𝑖 − 1)/32 (where 𝑖 ∈ [1,16] ), and at 16 indentation depths Δ𝑧 ∈ [1 mm, 4 mm] for three fluid media (air, water, oil). The results are shown in Fig. 4B for oil ( 𝜇 = 100 cSt ) and in Supplementary Materials S9 for air and water. The findings confirm the trend observed in the 1D sensor. 𝑃 ! was higher for indentations closer to the left probe ( 𝑠̃ = 0) and lower when indenting near the right probe ( 𝑠̃ = 1) , while 𝑃 " followed the opposite trend. As in the 1D configuration, the pressure response saturated beyond a certain indentation depth, corresponding to channel collapse and the cessation of fluid displacement. For air and water, the 2D sensor is not sensitive to slight and moderate touches due to the rapid flowback. However, in the case of oil, the combination of higher viscosity, the tortuous Hilbert path, and the longer channel minimizes flowback, enhancing both sensitivity and spatial resolution for detecting slight touches. Fig. 4C zooms in on a selected indentation depth ( Δ𝑧 = 3 mm ), showing the evolution of the pressure and force across different locations and quadrants (Supplementary Movie 5). Fig. 4D looks closer at one indentation event (#6), revealing a delay ( Δ𝑡 ≈ 0.85 s ) between pressure peaks of 𝑃 ! and 𝑃 " . This finding suggests that, in addition to Δ𝑃 , the rise time can be leveraged as an additional feature in the learning process to improve touch position and force estimation. 

Learning touch position and force 

We evaluated several learning algorithms to estimate position and force in the 1D sensor and selected Fuzzy C-Mean (FCM) clustering as the most effective model based on performance (Supplementary Materials S12). We then applied FCM to the 2D data. Unlike traditional clustering methods that assign each data point to a single cluster, FCM allows data points to belong to multiple clusters simultaneously, with varying degrees of membership. This soft assignment helps capture the nonlinearity in sensor readings and reflects the continuous nature of the sensor, thereby improving the accuracy of position and force estimation. Final estimates are computed as weighted sums of cluster outputs based on membership values. 

First, we collected pressure data by indenting a 1D oil-filled sensor ( 𝜇 = 100 cSt , ø = 1.5 mm ) at coarse spatial intervals. We then visualized the feature space as a function of touch position 𝑥 and indentation depths ∆𝑧 (Fig. 5A and Fig. 5B) to identify the sensor’s effective operating range. Three zones were identified: (1) a position-insensitive zone where 𝑃 ! ≈ 𝑃 " , (2) a force-insensitive zone 

where the channel collapses and the pressure saturates , further increasing in ∆𝑧 or applied force 𝐹 ,do not result in additional fluid displacement or pressure change; and (3) a working range between the two, where both position and force can be reliably distinguished. Next, we collected a learning dataset at high spatial resolution in the working range and segmented the position and force data with FCM, as illustrated in the insets of Fig. 5A and Fig. 5B. We optimized the number of clusters by evaluating RMSE and 𝑅 & scores for both position and force estimation (Supplementary Materials S12), with 20% of the collected data reserved for validation. Fig. 5C shows the position estimation results, color-coded with ∆𝑧 . The predictions closely match the true values overall, with a slight decrease in performance at shallow indentation depths, where flowback within the channel reduces the pressure difference between the two ends. In contrast, force estimation is more challenging, particularly at deeper indentations where pressure variations become more subtle (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, due to the complex, non-linear relationship between force and pressure, simpler models such as Linear Regression, Polynomial Regression, and Multi-9

Layer Perceptron (MLP) failed to achieve the same level of accuracy as FCM (supplementary materials S12). To better understand the spatial structure of the extracted feature used for learning, we visualized the distribution of the maximum left pressure ( 𝑃 !) and the corresponding rise time ( 𝑡 #!) across the full 2D surface at representative indentation depths ( Δ𝑧 = 2 mm and 3 mm ). The pressure magnitude (Fig. 6A) exhibits a clear spatial gradient along the Hilbert curve, with stronger signals near regions closer to the sensing outlet and attenuated responses in areas farther away. In contrast, the rise time feature (Fig. 6B) varies more gradually along the channel path and provides complementary spatial information that is less sensitive to absolute pressure attenuation. The combination of these two features enhances separability in the feature space and improves robustness of position estimation, especially in regions where pressure amplitude alone becomes weak. The detailed results of the extracted feature is presented in Supplementary S13. For the 2D sensor, we identified the working range by analyzing the corresponding feature space (Supplementary Materials S13). Due to the tortuous channel geometry, the flowback was significantly reduced in the 2D configuration, resulting in an expanded working range compared to the 1D sensor. Once this range was established, we acquired a comprehensive dataset for model training. To ensure consistent spatial sampling, we focused initially on “on-channel” indentations by selecting 256 uniformly distributed points along a 4th-order Hilbert curve at each indentation depth, where 𝑠̃ ∈ [0,1] denotes the normalized arc length along the curve. Fig. 6C shows the estimated normalized position 𝑠̃ . The model achieves high predictive accuracy (𝑅 & = 99.18%, 𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸 = 0.026 ), comparable to the 1D sensor. Minor deviations occurred primarily at shallow indentation depths, especially in the second and third quadrants ( 𝑄 &, 𝑄 . ), where the pressure signal is weaker due to a longer distance from the sensing elements. Despite these localized limitations, the trained FCM model reliably maps touch locations along the curve. Real-world Cartesian positions can be recovered from 𝑠̃ using the conversion code provided in GitHub 60 .Fig. 6D shows the force estimation performance for on-channel indentations, with strong results across most of the range ( 𝑅 & = 84.74%, 𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸 = 0.66 N ). The model effectively captures force trends at low to moderate indentation levels. However, at higher forces, prediction variability increases slightly, likely due to a combination of pressure saturation and fabrication tolerances related to the 2D sensor’s more intricate geometry. To better reflect real-world scenarios, where touch interactions are not constrained to predefined trajectories, we extended the dataset to include off-channel indentations across the entire 2D surface. At each depth, we sampled a grid of 31 × 31 indentation points uniformly distributed across the area. Fig. 6E presents a heatmap of the resulting position estimation errors, showing that the model maintains low spatial error across most of the sensor area. A few larger errors were observed, primarily localized in regions where either the left or right pressure readings are weak, reducing the reliability of position estimation in those zones. The force estimation performance for the full dataset, including both on- and off-channel indentations, is shown in Fig. 6F. While the model performs reasonably well ( 𝑅 & = 82.04%, 𝑅𝑀𝑆𝐸 = 0.71 N ), a slight reduction in accuracy is observed compared to the on-channel case. This decline is partly due to the heterogeneous nature of the sensor: off-channel touches often engage stiffer structural regions, resulting in higher absolute force values. At the same time, these touches tend to 10 

displace less fluid, producing weaker pressure signals and causing the model to slightly underestimate the applied force. Moreover, for off-channel touches in 2D sensors, the probe may engage multiple locations along the meandering Hilbert curve (corresponding to different sW), leading to additional localization error. 

Adaptability to Environmental Constraints 

To validate the versatility of our design, we conducted a series of demonstrations under diverse conditions that typically challenge conventional tactile sensors, such as underwater operation and exposure to magnetic interference. We also demonstrated the effectiveness of the sensing architecture and performed real-time touch estimation with indenters of varying shapes and sizes, including on a curved substrate. Collectively, these experiments highlight the effectiveness of the proposed sensor concept and its adaptability to different environments. 

Underwater sensing. With no electronics present at the sensing site, we were able to operate underwater without the risk of short circuits or the need for insulation (Fig. 7A). The same external pressure sensors used in the standard air-based setup were employed, with the tubing simply extended outside the water. Indentation tests were performed at a depth of 𝛥𝑧 = 3 mm across nine contact points, both under dry condition and with three different water levels above the sensor ( ℎ = 10 mm, 30 mm, 50 mm ). We observed that hydrostatic pressure caused a baseline shift proportional to the water level; however, once this offset was accounted for, the pressure response to touch remained effectively unchanged across all scenarios. The differences between the dry case and all underwater conditions were minimal (< 5 Pa ), confirming that the sensor maintained its sensitivity regardless of the submersion (Supplementary Movie 6). The same behavior was observed in the 2D scenario, as presented in Supplementary Materials S14. 

Sensing under magnetic field. We investigated the impact of magnetic fields on sensor performance, since many tactile sensing modalities are prone to interference (Fig. 7B). As a representative case, we tested a commercial barometric sensor, which only responded to indentations directly above or near its surface, leaving many points undetected. With a magnetic indenter, performance degraded further, pressure readings became noisy, and communication soon failed, underscoring poor robustness in magnetically active environments. In contrast, our fluidic tactile sensor remained unaffected. Using the same indentation protocol ( 𝛥𝑧 = 2 mm, with 

𝛥𝑥 = 2.5 mm), with both plastic and magnetic indenters, we obtained nearly identical pressure patterns, a robustness also confirmed in the 2D sensor (Supplementary Materials S14). 

Effect of sensor size. We studied sensors of various dimensions through geometric modification (Fig. 7C). Two smaller sensors were fabricated—one half-sized (30 mm × 30 mm, 4 th -order Hilbert curve, channel diameter ∅/2 ) and one quarter-sized (15 mm × 15 mm, 3 rd -order Hilbert curve, channel diameter ∅/2 )—without altering the external electronics. Indentation tests were conducted every 2 mm in both axes while recording pressures and applied force, using the same training and evaluation pipeline as for the full sensor (60 × 60 mm ). For data collection, we used a 4 mm indenter and the results in both raw and normalized units showed consistent performance across scales results in both raw and normalized units showed consistent performance across scales. The mean spatial errors remained around 5% and 7% in x and y directions, and force estimation was comparable for halved and full sensors. The 15 × 15 mm sensor showed slightly reduced accuracy, likely due to its lower Hilbert curve order. Overall, the results confirm that the fluidic sensing concept allows simple geometric scaling of size and resolution with minimal hardware modification. 11 

Effect of indenter shape and size. We evaluated the robustness of the 1D sensor under varying contact conditions by testing indenters of different shapes and diameters, although the system had been trained only with an 8 mm indenter (highlighted in Fig. 7D). Data were acquired and processed in real time using an oil-filled sensor with 1.5 mm channels, without pre-collection or batch processing. Ten random indentation points were selected across the x- (position) and z- (depth) directions to simulate live estimation scenarios. Three 8 mm indenter shapes—flat, half-oblate spheroid (HOS), and hemisphere (HS)—were tested (Fig. 7D left). The mean positional error remained below 5 mm (≈5%) in all cases, confirming reliable performance beyond the geometry of the indenter used for training. The flat indenter yielded slightly higher errors due to less effective channel sealing compared to the curved HOS and HS tips, which enhanced sensing by reducing fluid backflow. Varying the HOS indenter diameter from 4 to 10 mm resulted in mean errors below 2.5 mm 

(Fig. 7D right). Notably, reducing the indenter size leads to more localized deformation and increased flowback—particularly at shallow depths—thereby weakening pressure signals and resulting in larger deviations. These results demonstrate that the sensor adapts to diverse contact conditions while maintaining low error, even under online operation. The live data collection and processing code is provided in our GitHub repository 60 .

Conformity to curved substrates. Finally, we evaluated the sensor’s performance when mounted on a non-planar substrate composed of three regions with distinct radii of curvature (Fig. 7E). Although the sensor was trained only in a flat configuration, it successfully generalized to curved geometries and accurately detected touches across regions located on both convex and concave surfaces (Supplementary Movie 7). 

Human-Robot Interaction 

We demonstrated the real-time performance of our 1D and 2D tactile sensors by integrating them with a collaborative robotic arm (UR5e, Universal Robots) to perform various human–robot interaction (HRI) tasks (Fig. 8). For the 1D sensor, the robot’s position along the X-direction was controlled using the estimated position obtained either from the sensor mounted on the table (Fig. 8A) or directly on the robot arm (Fig. 8B) (Supplementary Movie 8). Additionally, we performed position estimation for the 1D sensor mounted on the robot using different processing windows (Supplementary Movie 11). To further validate force estimation, we performed a controlled injection task by dividing the sensor’s active area (10–90 mm ) into five spatial segments (16 mm intervals) and three force ranges (0–5 N, 5–10 N, and >10 N). Each touch was mapped to a corresponding 5 × 3 grid cell representing the estimated contact position and applied force (Fig. 8C). The mapping accuracy remained high across nearly all cases, with only a slight underestimation observed at the highest force level (3rd touch), consistent with the modest reduction in high-force sensitivity previously noted in Fig. 8D (Supplementary Movie 9). For the 2D sensor, we mapped the sensor’s XY coordinates to the robot’s XZ workspace and controlled the UR5e arm position accordingly. Similar to the 1D case, we conducted tests with the sensor mounted both on the table and directly on the robot’s curved surface (Fig. 8D–E). The sensor was touched in each quadrant, and the robot successfully moved to the corresponding positions (Supplementary Movie 10). When mounted on the robot arm, the sensor’s reference frame moved together with the robot, resulting in baseline shifts in the pressure readings even in the absence of 12 

physical contact, due to hydrostatic pressure, which posed a challenge for sensing accuracy. To address this issue, we implemented a baseline correction procedure that compensated for the motion-induced drift, allowing accurate estimation of touch positions. Notably, in this configuration, the sensor conformed to the robot’s curved surface despite being trained only on flat samples, yet it continued to provide reliable position estimation across all tested points. 

DISCUSSION 

In this work, we presented a soft tactile sensor that harnesses fluid–solid interactions to transduce both position and force without requiring electronics at the sensing site, relying solely on two external pressure sensors. By increasing the fluid viscosity—from air-filled to water- and silicone oil-filled channels—we observed stronger coupling between the fluid and solid domains, thereby enhancing sensitivity to external touch. The simple 1D design can be extended to higher dimensions through a space-filling architecture, such as a Hilbert curve, enabling scalable mapping with minimal hardware and achieving accurate localization (Mean Average Error (MAE): 4 mm , <7% error) and robust force estimation across both 1D and 2D configurations. We expect this strategy to be adopted for 3D surfaces as well. To infer tactile information from pressure readings, we implemented fuzzy c-means clustering combined with an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS), which partitions the complex nonlinear feature space into soft clusters. This approach improved robustness, outperformed standard regression methods, and better captured the rich dynamics of tactile responses. Table 1 benchmarks our proposed 1D and 2D soft tactile sensors against representative state-of-the-art tactile sensing technologies that rely on interpreting pressure or acoustic profiles to infer touch position and force. Compared to acoustic-based approaches, which require 1–2 sensors to achieve moderate accuracy over limited ranges, our sensors achieve comparable or better positional and force resolution using only two external pressure sensors. In the 1D configuration, our sensor reaches a position accuracy of 1.8 mm and reliably estimates forces in the 1–10 N range, matching or exceeding the performance of pressure sensor arrays with far more elements. Extending the design into a 2D Hilbert curve layout enabled spatial mapping over a 60×60 mm² area, achieving positional errors of 3.3–4.2 mm and force estimation with 0.7 N accuracy, while dramatically reducing the number of required sensing elements, such as 7 56 , 25 26 , 32 19 , to 2 physical sensors. Furthermore, the absence of electronics at the sensing site allows reliable operation in environments that are challenging for conventional tactile sensors, such as underwater or in the presence of magnetic interference. While underwater and deep-see tactile sensing has been previously demonstrated using a range of established technologies 61-65 , our results show that the proposed sensor remains functional when submerged and therefore represents an additional sensing modality applicable to underwater and harsh environments. We conducted comprehensive experimental characterizations of the 1D sensors, complemented by theoretical modeling and fluid–solid interaction (FSI) simulations to elucidate the sensing mechanism. First, we examined the effect of fluid viscosity by comparing silicone oils of different viscosities with water-filled and air-filled channels. The results revealed that incompressibility and higher viscosity significantly enhance the pressure response, yielding greater sensitivity and an extended working range. Next, we developed a simplified physical model linking the left and right pressures to the touch position along the channel. The model captured the main steady-state trends 13 

observed experimentally, providing an analytical description of the tactile response. However, it could not fully reproduce the system’s behavior at low indentation depths, where transient effects dominate and flowback alters the dynamics. Therefore, we resorted to FSI simulations, which addressed this limitation by revealing that pressure equilibrium arises from rapid fluid flowback during the initial indentation stage. These simulations further confirmed that this effect is suppressed at higher viscosities, supporting our experimental observations. A practical limitation encountered during experimentation arose from the chemical compatibility between the fluid and the elastomer. Due to PDMS’s high permeability, many silicone oils gradually diffused into the elastomeric walls, leading to a progressive pressure drop over time 66 . This effect restricted our usable fluid selection to a specific silicone oil formulation exhibiting reduced reactivity with PDMS. While adequate for stable measurements, this constraint prevented systematic exploration of intermediate viscosities. Future work could benefit from water-based solutions (e.g., polyvinylpyrrolidone, see Supplementary Materials S5) to mitigate diffusion and enable a broader viscosity range (1–100 cSt ). Such fluids may also improve response time in 2D configurations, where pressure losses are more pronounced. Three sources of sensor calibration drift were identified, including diffusion-induced baseline changes, variations in the sensor frame due to three-dimensional motion, and shifts in ambient pressure. Diffusion effects become negligible once the system reaches steady state after fabrication and are effectively compensated by baseline adjustment, as confirmed by long-term measurements (see Supplementary Materials S4, and Supplementary S10). Motion-induced frame changes and ambient pressure variations introduce hydrostatic pressure offsets; however, in all cases, subtracting the baseline improves position estimation and yields consistent pressure responses. Overall, this approach robustly resolves calibration drift and eliminates the need for repeated model retraining, enabling reliable performance across experiments conducted months apart using a single initially trained model. Increasing the sensor size inevitably lengthens the fluidic channels, thereby increasing pressure dissipation. This effect is particularly evident in regions 𝑄 & and 𝑄 . and near the middle of the Hilbert curve—areas farthest from the reading points—where pressure decay and a reduced signal-to-noise ratio result in higher localization errors. Such behavior is intrinsic to space-filling patterns, as their total path length increases with higher Hilbert orders or larger sampled domains. Nevertheless, as shown in Fig. 6B, the rise time associated with viscous effects remains distinguishable and exhibits a more gradual spatial variation along the Hilbert curve, providing a complementary cue that is less sensitive to signal attenuation. Accordingly, fine-tuning the fluid viscosity to minimize pressure loss, together with more efficient learning algorithms, is expected to further improve localization accuracy. Furthermore, the 2D sensor exhibits non-homogeneous pressure–force behavior between adjacent channels, with intermediate regions generating larger forces for smaller pressure changes. Increasing the resolution of the space-filling curve or reducing channel spacing could yield a denser and more uniform pressure–force response across the surface. Alternatively, adding a small number of additional pressure readings—still far fewer than in conventional pressure arrays—could mitigate this effect, although this approach is not ideal. Employing more advanced fabrication methods 67 for the 2D sensor could minimize force non-uniformity within each layer, thereby improving the accuracy of force estimation. Finally, one approach is to augment continuous space-filling curves 14 

using advanced fabrication techniques, such as freeform injection molding 68 , to add an array of fluidic reservoirs (‘taxels’) that locally amplify pressure signals without increasing the number of transducers. As a more radical alternative, one could employ a maze-generation algorithm to construct a connected maze with two entrances, such that every point in the maze lies within a prescribed maximum distance from at least one outlet of the sensor, thereby preserving signal strength. From a processing standpoint, we deliberately adopted a minimal feature extraction strategy, relying on the maximum left and right pressures for 1D sensors and, for 2D sensors, the corresponding rise times. This approach ensured high computational efficiency and enabled real-time operation with limited data bandwidth. Despite its simplicity, the system achieved satisfactory localization and force estimation. Nonetheless, performance could be enhanced by incorporating richer temporal features or employing time-series-based learning models (e.g., LSTM) to better capture dynamic behaviors, particularly in 2D cases. Overall, this study establishes a baseline demonstrating how viscous fluids can enhance tactile sensing relative to air-based systems. Viscosity suppresses flowback, increases sensitivity, and broadens the detectable range, all while maintaining minimal hardware and computational requirements. Although challenges such as material compatibility, non-uniform pressure distribution, and slow response time persist, the findings provide a foundation for a simple, adaptable, and computationally efficient tactile sensing in soft robotics. Future work should investigate various touch modalities—such as multi-point contact, sliding interactions 69 , and responses to shear forces and moments—since real-world interactions often involve multiple or dynamic surface engagements. Current experiments focused on flat surfaces, but investigating angular and curved geometries and benchmarking their performance against flat configurations will be critical for applications such as collaborative robots, where contact surfaces are rarely planar and often in motion. Moreover, to account for environmental effects such as temperature-dependent changes in fluid viscosity and related properties, more sophisticated recalibration algorithms such as dynamic time warping 70 can be used in addition to baseline correction, rather than resorting to full model retraining. Finally, the developed sensor exhibits a response time on the order of seconds, partly due to the high viscosity of the selected fluid and chosen signal features. Systematic dynamic characterization highlights loading-rate–dependent behavior in this proof-of-concept (see Supplementary note S6), pointing to clear opportunities for performance optimization through viscosity tuning and advanced signal-processing approaches to enable more responsive tactile estimation for human–robot interaction, where immediate feedback is essential. 15 

METHODS Fabrication : We fabricated the 1D sensor from silicone rubber (Ecoflex 00-30, Smooth-On) following a one-step molding process. First, we 3D printed a two-part mold using a stereolithography (SLA) resin printer (Form3, FormLabs) with two alignment holes on either side for positioning the central rod (Supplementary Materials S1). The sensor's dimensions were 𝑤 × 𝑡 = 20 mm × 5 mm . To investigate the effects of channel length and diameter, we created molds with channel lengths of 𝑙 = 50, 100, and 150 mm , and diameters of ø = 1, 1.5, and 2 mm . The silicone prepolymer was degassed at -1 bar, poured into the mold, and cured in an oven at 60 °C for 20 minutes. After curing, the sensor was demolded, and the central rod was removed. Two flexible tubes (GESSULTRA-C-040-0F, Sani Tech) were then attached to both ends using Sil-Poxy (Smooth-On). Finally, the channels were filled with different fluids, including air, water ( 𝜇 = 1 cSt ), and different silicone oils (Sigma Aldrich) with the viscosity of 𝜇 = 100, 200, 1000 cSt to evaluate the effect of fluid compressibility and viscosity (Supplementary Materials S1). For the 2D sensor, we used a two-stage process. First, a mold featuring the Hilbert curve was 3D printed using SLA. The dimension of the sensing interface was 𝑙 0 × 𝑙 1 × 𝑡 = 60 mm × 60 mm × 5 mm comprised of a continuous channel in the form of a 4th-order Hilbert curve with square cross-section, and the side edge of 𝑎 = 1.8 mm , maintaining the same volume as a circular channel with 

ø = 2 mm . After pouring and curing the silicone rubber, similar to the process for the 1D sensor, the Hilbert curve section was demolded, and the surface was brushed with uncured silicone rubber to ensure that the channels remained unfilled. A separate 1.5 mm-thick sheet of Ecoflex 00-30 was then applied over the Hilbert curve, ensuring full contact without obstructing the channels. The entire assembly was cured again, resulting in a uniform 2D sensor with encapsulated channels within the elastomer. Finally, flexible tubes were attached to the sensor, and the channels were filled with silicone oil (AP100, Sigma-Aldrich). Supplementary Materials S1. 

Experimental Setup : The measurement setup consists of two liquid-compatible differential pressure sensors (Honeywell, SSCDRRV100MDAA5) connected to a data acquisition card (NI-DAQ USB-6009) that transmits recorded signals to a personal computer. To perform comprehensive testing and generate large datasets for training the machine learning algorithm, we employed a robotic arm (UR5, Universal Robots) with an attached indenter featuring a rounded tip (8mm diameter, CAD files in Supplementary Materials S15). A miniature load cell (5 lbs, Futek) was mounted to accurately measure the applied force during touch events (Supplementary Materials S2). 

Numerical simulations: The mechanical response of the sensor was modeled in COMSOL Multiphysics as a 2D fluid-solid interaction problem. The computational domain ( 𝑙 = 100 mm , 𝑡 = 5 mm ) consists of a 2 mm fluid channel flanked by a solid material. Since the plane strain assumption would imply that the fluid domain extends out of the plane, this cannot sufficiently constrain the in-plane deformation, we opted for an axisymmetric formulation where the rotation axis was placed at the lower edge of the sensor ( 𝑟 = 0 ), Supplementary Materials S8. In this case, any in-plane displacement of the solid material results in circumferential stress due to the nonzero material’s Poisson’s ratio, thus effectively providing a natural spring boundary condition. For 𝑟 → ∞ , the plane strain condition is recovered. The solid domain consists of silicone rubber (Ecoflex 00-30), modeled as a nearly incompressible Neo-Hookean hyperelastic isotropic material model with a density of   

> 𝜌 += 1070 kg/m -

and a shear modulus of 𝐺 = 27 kPa . The fluid domain inside the channel is treated 16 

as a single-phase, laminar Newtonian flow under isothermal conditions, characterized by a weakly compressible fluid density 𝜌 $, "/$ = 1000 kg/m - and dynamic viscosity 𝜇 $. The evolution of pressure over time was analyzed by solving the Navier-Stokes equations in a transient framework. Gravity effects are neglected. The structural problem is governed by the nonlinear elasticity equations, assuming large-strain behavior, geometric nonlinearity, and isotropic material properties. The solid is discretized using quadratic Lagrange elements in a free tetrahedral mesh, which is refined along the contact boundaries. The accuracy of the flow field along the radial channel cross-section is not considered relevant, as we only want to resolve the pressure profile along the main channel axis. The fluid field and boundary layer were therefore coarsely discretized using standard piecewise linear interpolation elements (type P1 + P1). See Supplementary Materials S8 for the mesh realization in the indenter region. All channel walls are modelled with no-slip fluid flow conditions, while the deformable structure obeys a dynamic Neumann condition based on fluid-structure interaction forces. The indentation of the soft sensor is implemented as a force-controlled experiment. A Gaussian pressure distribution with peak magnitude 𝐹 012 at the indenter centerline 𝜇 = 𝑥 and width 6𝜎 = 𝑑 345/46/" is ramped up over time with a constant velocity of 𝑣 = 120 N/s , see Supplementary Materials S8. The contact between the sensor's top and bottom walls is modeled using a dynamic penalty formulation with a sufficiently small contact offset, ensuring that the fluid domain topology remains unchanged upon contact. Data collection: We first collected 1D data using position-controlled indentation at 9 x-axis locations ( 𝑥 = [10, 90] mm with Δ𝑥 = 10 mm ) and 41 depths ( z = [0,4] mm with Δ𝑧 = 0.1 mm ). Each dataset contained 𝑃 !, 𝑃 " and 𝐹 . The lower bound of the working depth was defined between the first detectable pressure difference ( Δ𝑝 = 0.05 kPa at 𝑥 = 10 and 90 mm ). The upper bound of the working depth where both Δ𝑃 and the average pressure ( 𝑃 f) changed by <1% relative to the previous step. Within this range, data were collected at 41 x-locations ( Δ𝑧 = 0.1 mm) For each indentation, maximum 𝑃 !, 𝑃 " and 𝐹 were extracted, paired with position, and stored for training. For the 2D sensor, preliminary data were obtained from 16 locations along the normalized Hilbert curve (𝑠̃ = (2𝑖 − 1)/32 , 𝑖 ∈ [1,16] ), and 21 depths ( 𝑧 = [0,4] mm with Δ𝑧 = 0.2 mm ). The working depth range was defined as in the 1D case, with the added condition of a minimum pressure threshold ( 𝑃 > 0.01 kPa ), due to weak responses at shallow depths. Two datasets were then acquired: (1) on-channel indentations at 256 points along a 4th-order Hilbert curve ( 𝑠̃ = [0,1] Δ𝑠̃ = 1/256 , 16 × 16 resolution), and (2) combined on/off-channel indentations over a 31 × 31 uniform grid ( 𝑥, 𝑦 ∈ [0, 60] mm , Δ𝑥 = Δ𝑦 = 2 mm ). For all indentations, the maximum 𝑃 !, 𝑃 ", rise times (𝑡 !, 𝑡 "), and 𝐹 were extracted, paired with spatial coordinates and depths, and stored for training. 

Machine learning: We developed a hybrid framework combining fuzzy C-means (FCM) clustering and an Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) to predict indentation position and force from pressure response. For the 1D case, inputs were 𝑃 ! and 𝑃 "; for 2D, rise times ( 𝑡 !, 𝑡 ") were also included. Output labels were the indentation position 𝑋 and force 𝐹 . Data were shuffled (random state = 7) and split into 80% training and 20% validation sets. First, FCM clustering reduced dataset dimensionality and improved learning by assigning membership degrees across clusters, with overlap controlled by the fuzzification coefficient to capture pressure-signal nonlinearities at different depths. The optimal number of clusters was 17 

chosen using RMSE and mean error. MATLAB’s Fuzzy Logic Toolbox fcm function performed clustering, and genfis3 function generated a fuzzy inference system, where each cluster defined one fuzzy rule as the basis of the ANFIS model. Next, the clustered dataset was trained to an ANFIS model (Takagi–Sugeno fuzzy system with ANN learning). The five-layer architecture performed fuzzification, rule activation, normalization, first-order Sugeno consequents (optimized by least squares), and output aggregation for 𝑋 and 𝐹 .Membership functions were tuned via gradient descent, while consequents were refined by a hybrid least-squares update. This approach enabled accurate nonlinear mapping of pressure signals to tactile variables (Supplementary Materials S11). For comparison, we also tested Linear Regression (LR), Polynomial Regression (PR, second-order), and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) trained with the LBFGS solver (random state = 1, max iterations = 1500). All were implemented in MATLAB R2024a (Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox for LR/PR; Deep Learning Toolbox for MLP). 

Human-Robot interaction: The robot experiments were conducted using a UR5e manipulator integrated with the tactile sensing system through a TCP/IP communication interface between MATLAB and Python. The data collection and estimation processes were performed in MATLAB, where pressure readings were processed in real time to estimate the contact position and applied force. The estimated values were then transmitted to Python to control the robot’s motion accordingly. For the 1D experiments, the sensor recorded data over a 5 s interval, and a Nordson Ultimus V high-precision dispenser was used to inject the material during testing. The 2D experiments employed the same communication setup and electronics, but the recording duration was extended to 10 s to accommodate the longer settling time of the fluid pressure after contact. In this case, both the maximum pressure and the pressure rise time were extracted as features for position estimation. The robot control and sensor communication scripts are available in the project’s GitHub repository 60 .

S1. Sensor fabrication 

Fig. S1 shows the steps-by-step fabrication process of both 1D and 2D sensors. For the 1D sensor, silicone rubber is injected into a custom-designed mold (A–C), cured, and subsequently connected to tubes and sensor electronics after fluid filling (D). The 2D sensor fabrication begins with pouring silicone rubber into a 2D mold (E–G), followed by curing and the insertion of a 3D-printed Hilbert curve to define the internal channel (H). Silicone is then brushed over the surface (I), and once the mold is set, the Hilbert curve is removed to reveal a clear channel (J). A pre-cured silicone rubber layer is carefully placed over the top while the brushed layer remains tacky to ensure bonding without bubbles (K). Finally, the structure is completed by attaching tubes, filling the channel with oil, and connecting to external sensing components (L).

S2. Experimental setup 

This setup is designed to characterize the response of the soft fluidic sensor under controlled indentation. A Universal Robots UR5 robotic arm is used to move a probe and apply indentation to the sensor in a position-controlled manner. The robot is programmed via a Python script using the UR_RTDE library (available on GitHub 1). The probe is mounted on a miniature S-beam load cell (LSB200, 5 lbs, FUTEK) to measure the applied force, which is critical since the UR5 does not provide force feedback. The load cell is powered through a DC power supply and connected to a load cell amplifier, with readings acquired through a National Instruments USB-6009 DAQ system and recorded in MATLAB. The soft fluidic sensor is connected to two differential pressure sensors (SSCDRRV100MDAA5, Honeywell) that measure the pressure response during indentation. Pressure data is also collected via the same DAQ at a sampling rate of 100 Hz. The entire system is synchronized to ensure accurate measurement of both pressure and force during each indentation cycle.

(A) Overview of the setup, showing the UR5 robotic arm, power supply, data acquisition system (DAQ), fluidic sensor, and computer. (B) Close-up view of the probe mounted to a miniature load cell. The soft sensor is connected to two differential pressure sensors, and all signals are recorded via a DAQ. The UR5 is position-controlled using a Python script. The setup enables synchronized measurement of applied force and internal pressure response during robotic indentation.

S4. Diffusion of silicone oil into Ecoflex 00-30 

To characterize the diffusion of silicone oil into the Ecoflex 00-30 in our sensor, we filled a fresh 1D sensor with silicone oil (viscosity: 100 cst ). The pressure drop was monitored daily over a 14-day period, with the channel refilled to its original baseline pressure after each recording. As shown in Fig. S5, the rate of pressure loss decreased significantly after day 6. By the end of the two-week period, the daily drop stabilized at less than 0.1 kPa . This result shows that the diffusion of the silicone oil reaches a plateau. While a negligible pressure loss persists, it can be effectively managed by subtracting the baseline during data processing and periodic oil replacement. The working range of this specific sensor is 2-5 kPa ,making the pressure drop negligible compared to the working range.

S5. Effect of working fluid viscosity on sensor response 

Silicone oil diffuses into PDMS (Ecoflex 00-30), which limited our experiments to relatively high silicone oil viscosities. To probe intermediate-viscosity regimes while minimizing diffusion effects, we therefore employed water-based polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)(K90, Sigma Aldrich) solutions as a model system for viscosity tuning, without introducing additional interfacial or chemical complexity. We varied the PVP concentration to achieve viscosities of approximately 20 cSt and 50 cSt , that fall between water (≈1 cSt )and our tested silicone oil (100 cSt ). To compare the rise time and pressure value of the PVP solution with silicone oil and water, we performed an experiment analogous to Fig. 2C and 2D at the indentation depth of Δ𝑧 = 2.5 mm . Fig. S6A shows the pressure distribution along the normalized sensor length ( 𝑥/𝑙 ) for different fluids in a 100 mm 

1D sensor. As viscosity increases, a larger pressure drop is observed along the channel. Compared to water, the PVP solutions exhibit a noticeably steeper pressure decay, indicating increased viscous resistance to flow. However, the pressure drop remains lower than that of 100 cSt silicone oil, which shows the most pronounced attenuation along the sensor length. The corresponding rise-time behavior is shown in Fig. S6B. For water, the rise time decreases with increasing distance from the sensing region, consistent with rapid fluid redistribution in low-viscosity media. In contrast, both PVP solutions display a spatial trend similar to silicone oil, with the rise time increasing along 𝑥/𝑙 . Nevertheless, the absolute rise times of the PVP solutions remain significantly lower than those of silicone oil (≈0.4–0.7 s for PVP versus ≈0.6–0.9 s for silicone oil), reflecting faster pressure propagation due to their lower viscosity. Fig. S6C shows the pressure difference Δ𝑃 between the left and right channels measured at 𝑥/𝑙 = 0.1 as a function of indentation depth. An increase in Δ𝑃 is observed with increasing fluid viscosity. Water produces the smallest pressure contrast, while the PVP solutions generate intermediate values, and silicone oil yields the largest pressure difference. This trend shows that higher viscosity increases differential pressure, which is directly related to improved sensing sensitivity. In Fig. S6D, a closed-up view of the pressure difference at smaller indentation depths shows that higher-viscosity fluids extend the sensor's effective working range. Specifically, increased viscosity reduces flowback during indentation, delaying pressure balance and enabling detectable pressure differences at larger Δ𝑧 values. As a result, the onset of measurable Δ𝑃 shifts to higher indentation depths as viscosity increases. The pressure responses shown in Fig. S6E further illustrate the dynamic trade-offs associated with fluid viscosity. While silicone oil (100 cSt ) produces the largest steady-state pressure response, it also exhibits a slow relaxation process, requiring some seconds to return to baseline after unloading. In contrast, the PVP solutions show much faster pressure recovery, with settling times closer to those of water while maintaining notably larger pressure amplitudes. This intermediate behavior highlights the advantage of optimizing the working fluid viscosity to combine enhanced pressure contrast with rapid response and recovery, making it a promising strategy for improving both sensitivity and temporal resolution in real-time sensing applications. Finally, it is notable that the viscosity of water-based solutions can depend on temperature; therefore, accounting for temperature variations may be beneficial when precise decoupling of thermal effects from pressure measurements is required under fluctuating ambient conditions. 8

When the contact duration is shorter than the characteristic rise time, the signal does not fully develop before unloading. Hence, Baseline correction (Fig. S9B) does not eliminate this effect, confirming that it is not caused by drift but by the sensor’s transient response. A zoomed-in view of the pressure signal at 𝑥/𝑙 = 0.5 (Fig. S9C) further illustrates this effect. For short holding times, the pressure does not reach the same steady-state level observed for 𝑡 = 1 s and 𝑡 = 2 s 

even though the applied force profile (Fig. S9D) reaches comparable maximum values. This confirms that the reduced pressure amplitude is not due to weaker indentation, but rather due to insufficient time for fluid redistribution and pressure equilibration within the channel. 12 

Importantly, although the peak pressure is reduced for short contacts, the overall temporal evolution of the signal follows the same characteristic pressure profile across all holding times. The difference lies in the moment the touch is released: when the contact ends earlier, the pressure decays before reaching its steady-state maximum. Therefore, the unloading phase and the transient pressure drop still contain valuable information about the touch event. Since our current feature extraction strategy primarily relies on maximum pressure (and rise time), fast touches whose duration is shorter than the characteristic rise time may be underestimated. However, because the pressure signal preserves its dynamic shape and encodes information in both the rising and falling phases, future work could incorporate richer temporal features, such as partial-rise characteristics, slope information, or decay dynamics, to improve detection across a wider range of contact durations. 13

Gravity effects 

Gravity effects are neglected in both the fluid and structural domains due to their negligible influence at the scale of a 2 mm horizontal channel. Hydrostatic pressure variations (20 Pa for a water-filled channel) and self-weight-induced deformation are orders of magnitude smaller than the dominant pressure-driven and elastic stresses in the system. 

Fluid Domain 

The channel is horizontally oriented, so gravity acts perpendicular to the main flow direction. Our model focuses on the axial pressure difference between the 𝑃 ! and 𝑃 ) ends. Vertical hydrostatic pressure variations within each cross-section are negligible due to the small channel height. For a 2 mm diameter 16 

water-filled channel ( 𝜌 = 1000 kg/m / ), the hydrostatic pressure difference is approximately ( Δ𝑃 123 =𝜌𝑔ℎ ≈ 20 Pa ), which is insignificant compared to the typical driving pressures in the kilopascal range. Gravity effects in the fluid domain are therefore neglected.

. Finite element modeling of the 1D sensor indentation response. ( A) Mesh of fluid (yellow) and solid (gray) domains, with gap defined as the shortest distance between channel walls. Indenter displacement Δ𝑧 is the change in radial position of the top surface. ( B) Gaussian load profile with peak (asterisk) and holding time Δ𝑡 (open circle). ( C, E ) Indenter displacement and gap differ due to solid deformation, with closure defined when the gap is smaller than or equal to the numerical offset. ( D, F) Higher fluid viscosity delays peak response and maintains a larger gap under the same load. 

Structural Domain 

Gravity is also negligible in the structural response of the Ecoflex material. The channel's bottom is supported, eliminating deformation due to its own weight. While the top could theoretically sag, this effect is minimal for two reasons. First, the hoop stress from internal pressure ( kPa range) is orders of magnitude greater than gravitational stress, with a typical ratio of ( 10 / − 10 4 ). Second, the channel is embedded in a larger Ecoflex body, providing additional support that suppresses sagging. This is consistent with experimental observations: even unsupported Ecoflex tubes of 2 mm diameter maintain a circular shape. Pressurization further stabilizes the cross-section. In summary, gravity effects are negligible in both the fluid and structural domains and are omitted from the model. 

FSI Solver details 

The transient simulation employs an implicit second-order Backward Differentiation Formula (BDF) time-stepping scheme with an adaptive physics-controlled (free) step size. The fluid-solid interaction problem is solved using a segregated solver with Anderson acceleration due to its computational efficiency. Relative and absolute tolerance are set to 10 54 and 10 56 respectively, with a maximum number of iterations per time step set to 20 to balance computational efficiency and accuracy (<1% variation in displacement and velocity fields). To improve stability, a damping factor of 0.7 is applied to the fluid-solid interaction update. Iterations are solved with direct solvers for the fluid velocity and pressure, the fluid-structure interaction (PARDISO), and the solid displacement field (MUMPS). 17

S11. FCM–ANFIS modeling approach 

In this study, we developed a model to estimate the position and magnitude of the applied force on our soft tactile sensor using a combined fuzzy C-means (FCM) clustering and adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS) approach.

S12. Learning results 

Model comparison: To evaluate the accuracy of the FCM, compared its performance with four conventional regression methods: multi-layer perceptron (MLP), support vector regression (SVR), linear regression (LR), and polynomial regression (PR). These methods were previously applied in our earlier work 4. The comparison was performed using the 1D sensor data for different channel diameters and media. As shown in Figs. S16 and S17, the FCM model consistently outperforms the other methods for both position and force estimation. While MLP provides high accuracy in position estimation and, in some cases, performs comparably or slightly better than FCM, it shows significantly lower accuracy in force estimation due to the complex and nonlinear nature of the pressure–force relationship in the system. Among the remaining methods, SVR demonstrates moderate accuracy but exhibits sensitivity to viscosity and channel diameter, whereas LR shows noticeably weaker performance, particularly in capturing nonlinear behavior.
