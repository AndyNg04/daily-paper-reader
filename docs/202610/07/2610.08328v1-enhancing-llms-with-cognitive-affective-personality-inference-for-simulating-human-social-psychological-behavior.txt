## Abstract 

Large language models are increasingly used to simulate human participants in social and behavioral studies, yet static persona prompting typically maps a par-ticipant profile and an experimental scenario directly to a response, entangling stable dispositions with situation-specific interpretations. To address this limitation, we introduce SPIN , a cognitive-affective personality system-inspired inference pipeline for simulating human social-psychological behavior. Specifically, SPIN implements this structured inference process through three zero-shot LLM calls that compile a task-blind participant core, elicit condition-specific cognitive-affective states, and read out decisions from those states, thereby reusing stable personality structure while routing each trial-specific response through an explicit state rep-resentation. We evaluate SPIN on two reconstructed social-psychological study families spanning uncertainty reasoning and pluralistic ignorance, across four base LLMs. Compared with blank, demographic, narrative, and chain-of-thought prompt variants, SPIN consistently delivers the strongest overall alignment perfor-mance across base LLMs and study families. Ablations and state analyses further show that both personality compilation and structured state elicitation contribute to the gains, and that the elicited states shift interpretably across informational and normative conditions. These results suggest that structured personality-state inference can improve benchmark-level behavioral alignment beyond richer per-sona descriptions or generic multi-step reasoning. Our code is publicly available at 

https://github.com/DDanel0727/spinn .

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being used as synthetic respondents in social and behavioral research. Conditioned on demographic attributes, attitudinal profiles, or richer life-history descriptions, they can approximate aggregate response patterns in surveys, economic games, and classic behavioral experiments [1–5]. Recent large-scale replications further show strong correlations between simulated and actual treatment effects across dozens to hundreds of studies [6, 7]. However, for scalable pilot studies and preliminary theory testing, surface plausibility is insufficient: model-generated behavior should align with the human behavioral summaries that social-science studies are designed to measure. 

> †Corresponding to: Dongyuan Li and Renhe Jiang. Preprint. arXiv:2610.08328v1 [cs.SI] 6 Oct 2026

Existing evidence shows that this alignment remains fragile. LLM-generated samples may compress human variability, miscalibrate effect magnitudes, and miss subtle cross-condition contrasts [8–11]; even when aggregate means are well matched, response distributions can exhibit lower variance and reduced demographic sensitivity than real survey data [12]. Individual responses can also drift from the assigned personas [13]. A central modeling limitation lies in end-to-end prompting: whether the prompt contains no profile, demographics, or a detailed narrative, the model usually maps the available participant description and scenario directly to an answer. Stable dispositions and situation-specific interpretations are then inferred in one generation step, making it difficult to reuse a participant consistently while remaining sensitive to small manipulations. We operationalize simulation quality as benchmark-level behavioral alignment: matching simulated responses to theory-relevant human behavioral summaries—including choice rates, rating statistics, and cross-condition contrasts—rather than recovering full individual psychologies. To this end, we introduce SPIN (Structured Personality-Informed IN ference), a zero-shot staged inference pipeline inspired by the Cognitive-Affective Personality System (CAPS) account of stable dispositions and situation-contingent behavior [14, 15]. SPIN uses three LLM calls to compile a task-blind personality core, elicit a condition-specific cognitive-affective state, and read out the benchmark-facing decision from the elicited state. Importantly, stagewise input restrictions prevent personality compilation from seeing the trial, prevent state elicitation from accessing the raw profile, and ground decision readout in the elicited state. The compiled core is reused across trials, while states are recomputed for each trial or item-level condition. We evaluate SPIN on two reconstructed social-psychological study families: Shafir and Tversky’s uncertainty-reasoning paradigms [16] and Prentice and Miller’s pluralistic-ignorance experiments [17]. Across four LLMs, SPIN achieves the strongest benchmark-level alignment among blank prompting, demographic conditioning, narrative backstories, and chain-of-thought variants. Ablations show that both task-blind personality compilation and condition-specific state elicitation matter, and aggregate state analyses show interpretable shifts across informational and normative conditions. These results support the narrower claim that structured personality–state inference can improve benchmark-level behavioral alignment beyond richer persona descriptions or generic multi-step reasoning. Overall, our main contributions can be summarized as follows: • We formalize social-psychological behavior alignment as benchmark-level matching of theory-relevant human behavioral summaries, including choice rates, rating statistics, and cross-condition contrasts, and identify a core modeling limitation of end-to-end prompting: stable participant dispositions and condition-specific interpretations are entangled within a single generation step. • We introduce SPIN, a zero-shot CAPS-inspired staged inference pipeline that separates response generation into task-blind personality compilation, condition-specific cognitive-affective state elicitation, and state-grounded decision readout. SPIN uses structured intermediate representations and stagewise input restrictions to route trial-specific responses through an explicit personality–state bottleneck. • We evaluate SPIN on two reconstructed social-psychological study families spanning uncertainty reasoning and pluralistic ignorance across four base LLMs. SPIN achieves the strongest benchmark-level alignment over blank, demographic, narrative, and their chain-of-thought augmented variants; ablations and aggregate state analyses further provide diagnostic evidence that both personality compilation and structured state elicitation contribute to these gains. 

## 2 Related Work 

LLMs have been widely studied as synthetic respondents for surveys, economic games, and behavioral experiments, with evidence that demographic, attitudinal, or biographical conditioning can reproduce some aggregate human response patterns [1–4, 6, 7]. At the same time, validation work shows that such simulations can compress human variability, miscalibrate effects, miss subgroup or cross-condition structure, and drift from assigned personas; recent benchmarks therefore emphasize held-out human outcomes and normalized behavioral summaries rather than raw generations alone [8, 11, 9, 10, 12, 13, 18]. Existing remedies mainly enrich the persona or add generic reasoning steps, including narrative backstories, character-consistency methods, chain-of-thought, and context-navigation strategies [19–23]; however, these do not by themselves ensure consistent participant reuse across trials or isolate the situation-specific state that mediates a decision. A complementary line uses 2cognitive and psychological theory to analyze or structure LLM behavior, from cognitive benchmarks and value-tradeoff models to cognitively faithful decision processes, belief elicitation, and executable models of others’ routines [24–28]. This work builds on that direction by using CAPS [14, 15] to separate stable dispositions from situation-specific activation for benchmark-level social judgment alignment. A fuller discussion is provided in Appendix A. 

## 3 Task Formulation 

Let P = {p1, . . . , p N } be a set of participants, each associated with a profile xp containing de-mographic, behavioral, or personality information, and let T = {τ1, . . . , τ M } be a set of trials, each specifying a participant-facing scenario, an experimental condition, a response space, and a measurement protocol. For each profile–trial pair (xp, τ ), a model generates a benchmark-compatible response, which the benchmark evaluator maps into study-level behavioral summaries such as re-sponse proportions, rating statistics, and cross-condition contrasts. We define benchmark-level behavioral alignment as matching these simulated summaries to the corresponding human targets, rather than recovering each participant’s full latent psychology or complete individual response trajectory. The prevailing approach implements response generation as a single LLM inference call: 

ˆYp,τ ∼ fLLM (xp, τ ), (1) where the participant profile and the trial stimulus are concatenated into a monolithic prompt. Although straightforward, this formulation asks the model to infer stable participant tendencies and condition-specific interpretations within the same generation step, so the same profile may be reused inconsistently across trials and subtle condition changes may be under- or over-weighted in the final response. SPIN addresses this by separating stable personality compilation, condition-specific state elicitation, and decision readout. We introduce these stages in detail in the following subsections. 

## 4 Methodology 

We present SPIN, a structured, personality-informed inference pipeline for social-psychological behavior alignment that decomposes the generation of behavioral responses into three sequential stages, inspired by the CAPS theory [14, 15]. Rather than inferring behavior in a single pass from a raw participant profile and task description, SPIN first compiles stable personality characteristics, then activates a transient cognitive-affective state conditioned on the current situation, and finally produces a benchmark-facing decision grounded in that state. In the remainder of this section, we first illustrate the overall architecture of SPIN (Section 4.1), followed by a detailed breakdown of its three inference stages (Sections 4.2–4.4) and the structural design principles intended to improve behavioral alignment (Section 4.5). 

4.1 Framework Overview: A CAPS-Inspired Architecture 

Among cognitive theories of personality and behavior, CAPS offers a particularly useful account of why human behavior is simultaneously stable and variable: it posits a network of stable cognitive-affective units—encodings, expectancies, goals, values, and self-regulatory plans—that are selectively activated by situational features, producing predictable if–then patterns of situation-behavior variation rather than uniform responses. This distinction between stable dispositions and transient situational activations directly addresses the two limitations of end-to-end prompting identified above, explaining how both contribute to observable behavior through a structured mediating process. We instantiate this distinction with a small fixed vocabulary of dispositional traits and situational state variables, designed not as a direct import from a single psychometric inventory but as a compact modeling interface covering recurrent axes of social judgment and strategic interaction. Appendix B summarizes the dimension-level rationale. We operationalize this insight as a three-stage inference pipeline (illustrated in Figure 1). Algorithm 1 summarizes the inference proce-dure. Several design choices shape how this decomposition works in practice. The compila-tion stage receives only the participant profile and is entirely task-blind, so the resulting per-sonality core is constructed without access to trial-specific information. The elicitation stage then receives the compiled core together with the trial stimulus, but not the raw profile text, so personality-related information at this stage is mediated through the structured core rather than through unrestricted profile conditioning. The decision stage receives only the elicited state(s) and the trial, with no access to either the core or the original profile. Taken together, these stagewise input restrictions create an explicit representational bottleneck at inference time, in-tended to encourage behavior to depend on intermediate representations rather than on direct access to upstream raw input. A second key choice concerns reuse and compositionality: the personality core is computed once per participant and cached across trials, whereas elicited states are recomputed for each trial or item-level condition as needed. This separation mirrors the CAPS-theoretic distinc-tion between chronic accessible units and their situational activation—a single stable personality foundation gives rise to different internal states under different experimental conditions, which in turn produce different behavioral responses—allowing the pipeline to simultaneously maintain a coherent participant identity across tasks and exhibit appropriate sensitivity when the situational context changes, the two requirements that end-to-end prompting (Eq. (1)) cannot structurally guarantee. Finally, the pipeline follows a causal ordering that reflects the temporal and logical structure of human decision-making in experimental settings: who the participant is precedes what the situation 5activates, which in turn precedes what the participant does. This ordering is not merely a notational convenience. By forcing the model to commit to a personality representation before seeing the task and to commit to an internal state before producing a response, we prevent later stages from retroactively reshaping earlier representations to fit a desired answer. Recent empirical studies on LLM-based social learning have demonstrated that forcing an agent to explicitly articulate its internal cognitive state (e.g., a probabilistic belief) prior to executing a discrete action successfully surfaces latent, human-like cognitive biases that are otherwise smoothed over by end-to-end generation [27]. Each stage’s output is finalized before the next stage begins, making the source of each behavioral decision explicit and traceable. 

## 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup Study Selection. We evaluate SPIN on two reconstructed social-psychological study families, with detailed scenario descriptions deferred to Appendix C. The Shafir–Tversky family studies reasoning under uncertainty by holding decision payoffs fixed while varying the informational state [16]. It includes Prisoner’s Dilemma variants, a computerized Newcomb task, and an information-seeking decision problem, from which we extract discrete observables such as cooperation, one-box choice, and willingness to pay for information. The Prentice–Miller family studies pluralistic ignorance in campus drinking attitudes by separating private attitudes, perceived peer norms, and action-relevant social responses [17]. Its observables include self–other comfort judgments, perceived norm gaps, dispersion patterns, and downstream behavioral indicators. 

Data Extraction. We build benchmark trials from the structured task records of Liu et al. [18] 2,aligned with the original articles for the two study families. Each record separates participant-facing protocol fields from reference-outcome fields: the former specify participant setup, condition assignments, trial materials, response options, item order, and measurement scales, whereas the latter store the human findings used only for evaluation. This separation prevents outcome leakage, since prompts expose only participant-facing materials and empirical outcomes remain held out until scoring. The records also define answer-normalization rules that map model outputs to the observables reported in the source studies, including condition-level choice rates for Shafir–Tversky and rating means, gaps, dispersion, and action-related outcomes for Prentice–Miller. 

Alignment Metrics . Following recent validation work for LLM-based behavioral simulations [10, 7, 4], we evaluate alignment at the level of theory-relevant behavioral summaries rather than raw response strings. For each scenario family, model outputs are first normalized into the same observable quantities reported in the human experiments, such as choice rates, and condition gaps. SPIN obtains the highest Avg. score for all four backbones and ranks first on both study-level alignment scores in every model block. A backbone-paired robustness check (Appendix E) shows that SPIN’s improvement is positive on every backbone for every baseline in both study families, with 40 out of 40 paired differences positive. The strongest overall result appears with Gemini-3-flash, where SPIN reaches an Avg. of 0.875. The improvement is especially large for DeepSeek V3.2, where the best baseline reaches 0.770 while SPIN reaches 0.869. Across models, the same qualitative pattern holds: SPIN improves the two-study macro-average without trading off one study family 7against the other. The primary-family scores indicate where these gains arise. In the Shafir–Tversky family, SPIN most consistently improves the components that require sensitivity to informational condition, especially information seeking and the Prisoner’s Dilemma triad. This pattern is consistent with the intended role of SPIN’s state-elicitation stage: the model must preserve a stable participant representation while updating its situational interpretation as the opponent information changes. In the Prentice–Miller family, the gains are generally smaller because several baselines already capture much of the aggregate norm structure, but SPIN still obtains the strongest study-level alignment for every backbone. This suggests that the method not only improves the formatting of local responses, but also helps preserve distinctions between private attitudes, perceived norms, and downstream social responses. These aggregate results support the narrower hypothesis that separating personality compilation, situational state elicitation, and decision readout can improve benchmark-level summary alignment in the socially structured tasks considered here. Notably, SPIN’s gains are consistent across all four backbones and both study families, and the largest on condition-sensitive components. The ablation study below tests this hypothesis more directly by removing individual components of SPIN, while the case studies inspect whether the improved scores correspond to interpretable changes in the elicited states rather than to only better final-answer calibration.

The smaller but systematic degradation from SPIN_wo_compilation suggests that direct raw-profile conditioning is not a full substitute for an explicit task-blind participant core. The SPIN_freeform_intermediate ablation distinguishes SPIN from generic multi-step prompt-ing. Despite preserving the staged procedure, replacing the fixed trait/state schema with uncon-strained natural-language reasoning reduces Avg. for every backbone, with the largest drops on 8Shafir–Tversky (0.089–0.152). This suggests that SPIN’s gains depend on a structured intermediate state, not merely an extra reasoning pass. Although SPIN_freeform_intermediate remains com-petitive on Prentice–Miller and slightly exceeds SPIN for Qwen 3.5-max, this exception is not robust, indicating that structured intermediates matter most when alignment requires both stable identity and condition-sensitive updating.

## 6 Conclusion 

This paper formalized social judgment and decision alignment as matching simulated behavioral summaries to theory-relevant human targets, and introduced SPIN, a zero-shot CAPS-inspired pipeline that stages task-blind personality compilation, condition-specific state elicitation, and state-grounded 9readout. Across two reconstructed study families and four base LLMs, SPIN improves alignment over blank, demographic, narrative, and their chain-of-thought augmented variants. Ablations show that task-blind personality compilation and structured state elicitation both contribute, while state analyses suggest that intermediate states shift interpretably across informational and normative conditions rather than as generic reasoning traces. SPIN should therefore be viewed as a structured inference method for behavioral alignment, not as a validated psychological measurement model; broader study coverage and cost-matched baselines remain important directions.

A.1 LLM Social Simulation: Capabilities, Limitations, and Evaluation 

Large language models have increasingly been used as synthetic respondents in social and be-havioral research. Early work showed that LLMs can approximate human response patterns in economic games [1], replicate directional effects in classic psychological experiments [2], and gen-erate subgroup-sensitive survey responses when conditioned on demographic or partisan cues [3], motivating broader interest in LLMs as scalable surrogates for human participants [30, 31]. Subse-quent work extended this evidence in both scale and ecological richness. Large-scale replication studies report strong correspondence between simulated and human treatment effects across pre-registered or scenario-based experiments [6, 7]; agent-based approaches enrich the simulation with persistent memory, interviews, social interaction, or individualized histories [29, 4]; and specialized or hybrid systems further combine LLMs with human-experiment corpora or causal models [5, 32]. These results establish that LLMs can produce surprisingly plausible aggregate behavioral patterns across diverse social and cognitive settings. At the same time, a parallel literature cautions against treating such correspondence as sufficient evidence of human-like behavior. From a causal-inference perspective, apparent LLM–human agreement may reflect prompt artifacts rather than genuine behavioral alignment [8]. Empirical validation studies show that LLM-generated samples can compress population variance, miscalibrate effect magnitudes, distort causal estimates, and fail to recover subgroup heterogeneity [11, 9]. More recent frameworks emphasize that reproducing aggregate means is insufficient without also matching distributional structure, cross-condition contrasts, and the intended evidential role of the original human study [10]. Individual-level simulation raises further concerns: personas can drift from assigned characteristics over interaction [13], and broader position papers identify diversity, bias, sycophancy, alienness, and generalization as unresolved challenges for LLM social simulation [12]. These limitations motivate more structured evaluation targets. Early studies often emphasized aggregate correlations or effect-direction recovery [1, 2], whereas later work distinguishes surface-level response similarity from stronger claims about cognitive or process equivalence [30]. Benchmark efforts such as HumanStudy-Bench separate participant-facing protocols from held-out reference outcomes and normalize model outputs into the observables reported in source studies [18]; related large-scale replications also score theory-relevant behavioral summaries, including choice rates, rating statistics, and treatment contrasts [7]. SPIN follows this evaluation view: the target is not to recover full individual psychologies, but to improve benchmark-level alignment with the summaries that social and behavioral studies are designed to measure. 

A.2 Persona Conditioning and Multi-Step Prompting 

A common way to steer LLM behavior toward a target population or individual is persona condition-ing: augmenting the prompt with demographic attributes, personality descriptors, social identities, or biographical context. Such conditioning can yield subgroup-level opinion distributions that track survey marginals [3], induce trait-consistent text under explicit personality descriptors [19], and produce reliable inventory-style responses under generic persona prompts [33]. These results sug-gest that LLMs encode enough social and psychometric regularities to generate persona-consistent surface behavior. However, persona prompting remains brittle for social simulation. Demographic steering reduces, but does not eliminate, misalignment between LM-generated and human opinion distributions [34]; in-context impersonation can recover some human-like patterns while amplify-ing sociocultural biases [35]; and richer character modeling often requires fine-tuning or explicit consistency optimization [36, 13]. More broadly, persona-prompted models can compress inter-individual variability and generate stereotyped responses, especially for underrepresented groups [37]. Thus, simply adding more profile content does not guarantee stable or condition-sensitive behavioral alignment. A separate prompting literature improves reasoning by decomposing generation into intermediate steps. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting and its zero-shot variant elicit intermediate rationales before final answers [21, 22]; self-consistency, least-to-most prompting, decomposed prompting, and tree-of-thought search further improve reliability by sampling, decomposing, routing, or searching over reasoning paths [38–41]. In LLM social simulation, Kong et al. [23] similarly distinguish context formation from context navigation, showing that complex decision environments often require both 14 accurate task representation and guided reasoning. These methods motivate controls for whether SPIN’s gains come merely from additional reasoning. Our comparison with CoT baselines and free-form intermediate ablations addresses this possibility: generic multi-step reasoning can add computation, but it does not enforce a reusable participant representation or a condition-specific state bottleneck. This distinction is consistent with recent evidence that explicitly articulating internal cognitive states before action can reveal human-like biases otherwise smoothed over by end-to-end generation [27]. 

A.3 Psychological Theory as Computational Scaffolding for LLM Behavior 

Psychological theory has been used to study LLMs along two related directions. One line treats LLMs as objects of psychological analysis: models are administered cognitive tasks or personality probes to assess whether their outputs exhibit human-like biases, heuristics, traits, or learning patterns [42–44]. Benchmarks such as CogBench systematize this approach by comparing LLM behavior against human baselines across cognitive experiments [24]. Cognitive models have also been used to interpret alignment-relevant value trade-offs in LLMs, such as how models balance informational, social, and presentational utilities in pragmatic communication [25]. These studies show that psychological paradigms are useful diagnostic tools, but they primarily evaluate the behavior of the existing model rather than specifying how behavioral responses should be generated. A second line uses psychological concepts as design constraints for language agents. CoALA proposes a broad cognitive-architecture view of language agents, organizing memory, action, and decision modules through concepts from cognitive science and symbolic AI [45]. Other work analyzes LLM decisions through dual-process theory [46] or manipulates affective framing, showing that anxiety-like prompts can change exploratory behavior and induce systematic biases [47]. Recent cognitively faithful decision models further argue that alignment should capture not only choices but also the heuristic or rule-like processes by which humans transform information before deciding [26]. More directly related to social simulation, explicit belief elicitation before action can surface latent human-like biases that are otherwise smoothed over by end-to-end generation [27], while individualized backstory generation can improve value-grounded responses [20]. Related work on modeling others’ minds as code represents routine behavior as executable scripts inferred from sparse observations, suggesting another route for imposing structured, interpretable behavioral representations [28]. Together, these findings suggest that intermediate psychological or computational representations can affect downstream behavior rather than serving only as post-hoc explanations. Personality theory provides a further motivation to separate a stable individual structure from situation-specific activation. The Cognitive-Affective Personality System (CAPS) explains personality in terms of stable if–then patterns: cognitive-affective units such as encodings, expectancies, goals, values, and self-regulatory plans are selectively activated by situational features [14, 15]. This account is especially relevant to participant simulation, where a model must preserve a coherent participant identity while producing differentiated responses to changes in informational state, social target, or normative framing. However, most LLM simulation approaches still rely on either enriched persona descriptions or generic reasoning traces, leaving open the question of how to operationalize personality theory as a structured inference process for benchmark-level behavioral alignment. 

## B Trait and State Dimension Rationale 

The SPIN intermediate vocabulary is part of the method specification. It is designed as a compact interface for social judgment and strategic-interaction settings rather than as a direct operationalization of a single psychometric scale, following the CAPS-inspired distinction between chronic dispositions and situation-specific activation used in the main method section. The five trait dimensions span the primary stable behavioral axes implicated in cooperation, fairness, and strategic reasoning: sensitivity to unequal outcomes, responsiveness to social expectations, tendency to reciprocate or punish, preference for resolving ambiguity, and baseline inclination to avoid exploitation or loss. The six state dimensions capture transient psychological activations that a specific scenario may trigger: emotional intensity, internal motivational tension, activated other-regarding concern, momentary trust, situation-specific vulnerability appraisal, and defensive impulse. Together, the vocabulary is designed to cover the main sources of behavioral variation present in the social-dilemma and judgment-under-uncertainty tasks included in our evaluation. 15 Alternative vocabularies could be substituted within the same pipeline when extending to other task families; evaluating the sensitivity to such vocabulary choices is left to future work. ## C Experimental Scenario Information 

This appendix section summarizes the experimental scenarios considered in the main evaluation. 

C.1 Shafir and Tversky (1992) 

One experimental source used in this paper is Shafir and Tversky’s Thinking through Uncertainty: Nonconsequential Reasoning and Choice [16]. This article is a canonical study of the disjunction effect and related violations of Savage’s sure-thing principle. It is directly relevant to our setting because it provides behaviorally matched paradigms in which the underlying incentive structure is held fixed while the informational state changes, making it possible to test whether a model preserves stable decision tendencies while still producing condition-sensitive variation under uncertainty. Our benchmark instantiates three tasks from this scenario family. The first is a one-shot Prisoner’s Dilemma presented in three matched informational conditions: the participant does not know the other player’s action, is told that the other player cooperated, or is told that the other player competed. This task is central because it isolates the effect of uncertainty while holding payoffs fixed; the human target includes the characteristic reversal in which cooperation is highest in the unknown condition. The second task is a computerized version of Newcomb’s Problem, which probes whether the agent prefers the diagnostically attractive one-box option over the dominance-consistent two-box option. The third is an information-seeking Prisoner’s Dilemma variant in which the participant may pay to reveal the opponent’s action before deciding, making uncertainty itself an explicit object of choice. Together, these tasks define the Shafir–Tversky scenario family in our benchmark as a compact evaluation setting centered on reasoning under uncertainty. The family is useful not only because it provides measurable aggregate targets, but because it probes whether a model captures the qualitative reorganization of preference induced by informational uncertainty across closely related decision formats. 

C.2 Prentice and Miller (1993) 

Another experimental source used in this paper is Prentice and Miller’s Pluralistic Ignorance and Alcohol Use on Campus: Some Consequences of Misperceiving the Social Norm [17]. This article studies how individuals misperceive the prevailing social norm and how those misperceptions shape private attitudes, behavioral adjustment, and social alienation. Its central claim is that students often believe that they are more personally uncomfortable with campus drinking culture than their peers, even when many peers privately hold similar reservations. The article is therefore directly relevant to 16 our setting because it separates private attitude, perceived norm, and downstream behavior, which closely matches the kind of latent mediation structure that our framework aims to model. This article is especially useful for social-psychological behavior alignment because it does not stop at documenting a static self–other gap. It connects misperception to inferences about both abstract campus-level norms and the attitudes of proximate others, to beliefs about how homogeneous peers appear relative to the self, and to behavioral sequelae when people feel deviant from what they believe others believe. Our benchmark instantiates a cross-sectional protocol from this scenario family built around the following substantive ingredients. Participants report private comfort with campus drinking alongside an estimate of the typical peer’s comfort. The materials support evaluation of pluralistic ignorance at the level of means and of dispersion: the tendency for attributed peer attitudes to be both shifted and less variable than self-ratings. The protocol also elicits comfort judgments about the self, friends, and the average student under counterbalanced question order, so that apparent gaps are not artifacts of a fixed item sequence. This probes whether a model represents local and global social targets coherently when both are available. Finally, the materials include a policy-relevant block modeled on campus restrictions on alcohol service. After eliciting how participants read their standing relative to others’ attitudes, the task measures willingness to undertake collective action and related outcomes associated with social alienation, connecting perceived normative discrepancy to inhibition of public engagement rather than to attitude reports alone. Together, these ingredients define the Prentice–Miller scenario family as operationalized in our benchmark. They foreground the distinction between private attitudes and attributed norms, the structure of self–other gaps across social targets, second-order beliefs about the variability of others, and behavioral correlates of feeling misaligned with the perceived crowd. That makes the family well suited for testing whether a model represents not only what an agent personally believes but also what it attributes to others and how those attributions reorganize expressed behavior.

AST = 13 (FIS + FPD + FNC ) .

17 PF-IS: information-seeking calibration. Let apay be the proportion of the model that chooses to pay for information, computed among the valid pay /no_pay responses, and let hpay be the corresponding human proportion. The information-seeking family contains one component: 

FIS = S(pay) , Bpay = 1 .

PF-NC: Newcomb calibration. Let aB be the model proportion selecting box_b_only among valid box_b_only /both_boxes responses, and let hB be the human rate of one-box. The Newcomb family is 

FNC = S(box _b_only) , Bbox _b_only = 1 .

PF-PD: Prisoner’s Dilemma triad calibration. Let rU , rC , and rK denote the cooperation rates in the unknown, known-cooperate, and known-compete conditions, respectively, with model and human versions a· and h·. The family includes the three level components S(rU ), S(rC ), and S(rK )

with B = 1 , two condition-gap components, 

S(rU − rK ), S(rU − rC ),

with B = 2 , and one ordering component O({U, C, K }). The family score is the mean of these six components: 

FPD = mean {S(rU ), S (rC ), S (rK ), S (rU − rK ), S (rU − rC ), O ({U, C, K })}.

This separates marginal cooperation calibration from the structural pattern emphasized by the original study. 

D.2 Prentice and Miller (1993) 

For the norm-misperception scenario family, the evaluator extracts scalar rating responses and compares them with the human summary statistics reported in the benchmark. The Prentice–Miller headline score is 

APM = 14 (FML + FNG + FDA + FSA ) .

PF-ML: mean-level calibration. This family compares directly observed rating means. It includes Study 1 self-comfort and average-student estimates, plus Study 2 means for self, friend, and average-student targets under both self-first and average-first question orders. If M is this set of mean components, then 

FML = mean m∈M S(m), Bm = 10 .

PF-NG: normative-gap structure. This family compares self–other contrasts and target orderings. It includes the Study 1 average-minus-self gap, the Study 2 friend-minus-self and average-minus-friend gaps under each order condition, and the pairwise ordering agreement among self, friend, and average-student means within each order condition: 

FNG = mean {S(g1), S (gfriend −self 2,o ), S (gaverage −friend 2,o ),Oo({self , friend , average }) : o ∈ { self -first , average -first }} ,

where B = 10 for the rating-gap components. These terms operationalize pluralistic ignorance as both gap magnitude and the ordering of perceived social targets. 

PF-DA: dispersion alignment. This family uses Study 1 standard deviations for self-ratings and average-student estimates, together with their SD gap: 

FDA = mean {S(σself ), S (σaverage ), S (σself − σaverage )}, B = 10 .

It captures the variance pattern behind the “illusion of universality,” namely that attributed peer attitudes are less variable than private self-ratings. 18 PF-SA: social-action alignment. This family compares the downstream policy-related outcomes in the Study 4. For each perceived-deviance group g ∈ { others more negative , others the same }

and outcome y ∈ { signatures , hours , reunions }, the evaluator scores the group mean S(μg,y ). It also scores the group contrast S(μsame ,y − μmore negative ,y ) for each outcome: 

FSA = mean y {S(μmore negative ,y ), S (μsame ,y ), S (μsame ,y − μmore negative ,y )}.

The bound is B = 100 for the signatures and the reunion percentages and B = 10 for hours. The benchmark scores only instruments that can be aligned directly to published marginal summary statistics; experimental sections without such comparables are left out of the scored protocol.

E.1 Behavioral Consequences of Removing State Elicitation 

Figure 2 compares the full SPIN pipeline, the w/o elicitation ablation, and the original hu-man targets across the five Shafir–Tversky condition groups. The plotted quantity is always a condition-level response rate in [0 , 1] : cooperation rate for the three Prisoner’s Dilemma conditions, 

P (Box B only) for Newcomb, and the rate of paying for information in the information-seeking task. We focus on this family because it combines matched uncertainty manipulations with compact, directly comparable behavioral observables. The figure is diagnostic rather than inferential: Table 2 gives the aggregate alignment effect, while Figure 2 shows how removing elicitation changes concrete observable behavior. 19 The main pattern is directional but condition dependent. Removing elicitation pushes the model toward higher rates of the focal action across all five groups, but not uniformly: in the known-compete Prisoner’s Dilemma condition, both variants remain near the floor, indicating that the state layer is not simply suppressing cooperative or focal responses. Larger gaps appear in the unknown and known cooperative Prisoner’s Dilemma conditions, Newcomb, and information seeking, where the ablated model approaches a less selective high-response policy. In the unknown condition, elicitation appears to moderate when trust is warranted; in known cooperation, it reduces an otherwise very high cooperation tendency, although SPIN still overestimates cooperation relative to humans. Similarly, elicitation tempers one-boxing in Newcomb and costly information acquisition in the information-seeking task. Thus, Figure 2 should not be read as showing uniform condition-level improvement, but as evidence that explicit state construction helps calibrate the final readout to the situational structure. Without that layer, the decision process becomes less selective about when to endorse the focal action, supporting the view that elicited states are behaviorally consequential intermediates rather than only interpretability artifacts.

## I Additional Discussions 

I.1 Limitations and Future Work 

SPIN should be understood as a structured inference interface for improving benchmark-level behavioral alignment, not as a validated psychological measurement instrument. Its trait and state 35 variables are generated by the base-LLM under a hand-designed schema, and their interpretability comes from their constrained semantics and systematic response to task conditions rather than from independent psychometric validation. The same architecture could support alternative vocabularies, learned schemas, or task-specific state spaces; future work should evaluate how sensitive the method is to these design choices and whether externally measured personality or belief variables can be incorporated as anchors. More broadly, the method improves how an LLM organizes profile and situation information at inference time, but it does not guarantee a faithful individual-level simulation or causal recovery of human cognitive mechanisms. SPIN also introduces additional inference calls and structured JSON outputs, so part of its advantage may reflect better decomposition, format control, or compute allocation rather than the CAPS-inspired schema alone. Future work should compare against stronger cost-matched structured prompting baselines and study whether intermediate states remain stable under prompt paraphrases, alternative decoding settings, and unseen social domains. Experimentally, our current evaluation is limited to two reconstructed study families and four base-LLMs, so the evidence supports effectiveness on these benchmark-level summaries rather than broad claims about all social judgment tasks. Extending the evaluation to more study families, preregistered replications, and participant-level holdout data is an important next step. 

I.2 Compute Resources 

All experiments are inference-only and do not involve model training, fine-tuning, or local GPU inference. The local machine is used only for experiment orchestration, API calls, output parsing, and metric computation; our runs are executed on a desktop workstation with an AMD Ryzen 7 7800X3D 8-core CPU and approximately 32 GiB of RAM. The computational cost is therefore dominated by hosted LLM API latency rather than local hardware. In our setup, each complete evaluation pass for one backbone and one method variant is completed within one hour, with small variations due to API throughput and network conditions. 

I.3 Broader Impacts 

Improved synthetic-participant methods can help researchers prototype study designs, stress-test behavioral hypotheses, and identify framing-sensitive benchmark outcomes before conducting costly human-subject studies. However, such methods also carry risks if simulated responses are treated as substitutes for human evidence, used to make unvalidated claims about real populations, or interpreted as individual-level psychological measurements. These risks are particularly relevant in social and behavioral domains, where model outputs may reflect biases from the underlying LLMs, prompt design, or benchmark reconstruction choices. Our work mitigates these risks only partially by targeting benchmark-level aggregate alignment, separating participant-facing protocols from held-out outcomes, and explicitly limiting SPIN’s interpretation to a structured inference interface rather than a validated psychological measurement model. Therefore, SPIN should be used for methodological analysis and preliminary simulation, not for individual assessment, high-stakes decision-making, or to replace human-subject validation.
