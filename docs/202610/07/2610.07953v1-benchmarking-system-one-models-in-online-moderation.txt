Abstract 

Online moderation systems must apply changing platform policies, community rules, and prior decisions while producing decisions that can be audited and routed to human review. We eval-uate whether System One Models, which accept natural-language context but return typed choices, probabilities, or scores, can support this setting. Across five moderation benchmarks, 

Jev is competitive with specialized or published reference systems, matching or exceeding them in several policy-grounded and harmful-content settings. We then use controlled information conditions to separate written rules, retrieved precedents, and restrictions on the candidate an-swer space. Jev generally benefits from retrieved precedents, improving exact policy selection and harmful-content discrimination when the answer space is held fixed. Laya is less consis-tent: retrieval often shifts its positive prediction rate or no-violation rate without improving discrimination. Jev confidence can support selective review in several settings, although it is not consistently calibrated; Laya confidence is less useful for ranking errors. These results show a promising future for SOM-powered content moderation. 

Keywords: System One Models; content moderation; retrieval-augmented decisions; Jev; Laya 

# 1 Introduction 

Online platforms must continuously decide which user-generated content can remain visible, which content requires intervention, and which policy justifies that intervention. At the scale of contem-porary social media, these decisions cannot rely exclusively on manual review, motivating extensive research on automated content moderation and harmful-content detection [10, 13, 14, 41]. Auto-mated systems have consequently been developed for tasks ranging from offensive-language and hate-speech detection to the enforcement of broader platform policies. Yet content moderation is not simply a classification problem. What constitutes a violation depends on the policy being enforced, the community in which the content appears, and the contextual interpretation of both the message and the governing rules [10, 14]. The same expression may therefore receive different moderation decisions under different normative frameworks. Traditional Machine Learning (ML) approaches provide an effective mechanism for detecting pre-defined categories of harmful content, and specialized classifiers remain strong baselines for tasks such as toxicity and offensive-language detection [10, 15, 41]. Their decision boundaries, however, 1arXiv:2610.07953v1 [cs.SI] 6 Oct 2026 are generally learned with respect to a fixed annotation scheme and training distribution. Changes in moderation policy, community norms, or violation taxonomy can consequently require new an-notated data or model adaptation. Recent Large Language Models (LLMs) provide a different possibility: rather than encoding the moderation standard only during training, they can receive natural-language policies and examples directly at inference time. This has motivated emerging approaches to policy-as-prompt moderation and, more broadly, growing interest in LLM-based mod-eration systems [5, 32]. Such systems make it possible to condition decisions on changing policies and demonstrations, although their suitability for operational moderation also depends on whether their outputs can be made predictable, interpretable, and useful for downstream decision pipelines. System One Models (SOMs) offer an interesting additional design point in this space. SOMs ac-cept unstructured textual states and task descriptions, similarly to language models, but map them directly to typed, non-autoregressive outputs such as categorical choices, probabilities, or ordinal scores. Jev and Laya, the two SOM implementations considered in this work, therefore combine an interface capable of consuming natural-language context with structured outputs resembling those of conventional classifiers [1, 29]. For moderation, this combination is particularly appealing: asystem could potentially interpret a written policy or previously moderated examples while still returning an explicit decision and associated probability rather than unrestricted generated text. Our contribution is threefold. First, we provide a controlled framework for evaluating content moderation under distinct sources of decision context, separating written policy, retrieved prece-dents, and restrictions on the available answer space. Second, we provide, to our knowledge, the first broad evaluation of SOMs for online content moderation, covering both exact policy enforcement and conventional harmful-content detection across five benchmarks. Third, we evaluate whether the probabilities and confidence values returned by typed SOM interfaces are sufficiently informative to support selective moderation and human review. Our experiments address four research questions. 

RQ1 asks whether SOMs can apply written moderation policies and previously decided cases. RQ2 

asks whether retrieved precedents improve the underlying decision signal or primarily alter the op-erating point or available answer space. RQ3 asks whether SOM probabilities and confidence scores provide useful estimates of uncertainty for moderation workflows. Finally, RQ4 asks whether the observed behavior extends from explicit policy enforcement to generic harmful-content detection. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews automated and policy-grounded con-tent moderation, retrieval-based decision support, and System One Models. Section 3 introduces the benchmark tasks, datasets, SOM information conditions, and output interfaces. Section 4 presents the experimental results and uncertainty analyses. Finally, Section 5 discusses the implications, limitations, and directions for future work. 

# 2 Related Work 

In the following, we review relevant work in content moderation, in-context learning and SOMs. 

Content moderation. Automated moderation research has often been organized around detecting predefined categories of harmful text. Benchmarks such as OLID and HateXplain fix annotation schemes for offensive language, hate speech, and related labels [27, 44], while specialized systems such as Detoxify expose toxicity-oriented category scores or binary decisions [15]. Industrial mod-eration systems follow a similar fixed-taxonomy template at larger scale: the OpenAI moderation model, for example, is described as a classifier for categories such as sexual content, hateful content, violence, self-harm, and harassment, built with explicit taxonomies, labeling instructions, quality 2control, robustness checks, and active learning [26]. This paradigm has well-documented limits. Broad surveys emphasize that datasets differ in how they define hate, abuse, offense, and toxicity, which targets they cover, how labels are annotated, and which languages are represented [10, 34, 41, 42]. Even within a single dataset, separating hate speech from merely offensive language is difficult [6]. Reported performance can be inflated by dataset-specific artifacts and weak validation protocols [2], while functional tests expose failures that aggregate scores conceal, including reliance on identity terms and difficulty with counter-speech [36]. Toxicity and hate-speech classifiers can also inherit annotation biases, producing disproportionate scores for identity-related or dialectal language [7, 31, 38]. Platform-governance research offers a different diagnosis of this heterogeneity. Moderation is an institutional practice that defines acceptable participation and allocates work between automated systems and human reviewers [11, 14, 35]. At platform scale, moderation is better understood as a system of mass speech administration than as a sequence of isolated classification decisions [8, 12]. The rules themselves vary substantially, as platforms publish different guidelines [39] and e.g. Red-dit communities maintain their own rule sets [9]. A single learned decision boundary is therefore an incomplete target when the operative standard changes across platforms, communities, and policies. 

Retrieval and in-context learning. Policy-conditioned moderation has also been studied as an interface problem. Binary question answering decomposes evolving moderation policies into ex-plicit checks, making policy changes easier to localize than in a monolithic classifier [30]. Llama Guard places a safety taxonomy directly in the prompt and uses an LLM-based safeguard model to classify unsafe inputs and outputs [16]. Policy-as-prompt work formalizes a related paradigm in which natural-language policies are supplied at inference time, while also highlighting sensitivity to prompt formulation, organizational governance, and accountability [32]. Empirical studies of LLMs on community moderation report useful but uneven performance across rules and communi-ties [19, 21], and reviews of LLM-based moderation describe prompting and fine-tuning as the main routes beyond fixed classifiers [5]. Written rules are rarely sufficient by themselves; institutions often interpret them through prior decisions. Legal theory treats precedent as a commitment to decide like cases alike, while recog-nizing that the difficult judgment lies in deciding which features make two cases relevantly simi-lar [40]. Case-based reasoning formalizes related ideas computationally, including in legal argumen-tation [3, 20]. Moderation reseaarch has begun to use precedent in analogous ways. On Reddit, Crossmod used cross-community moderation decisions as signals to assist moderators in communi-ties with limited local training data [4]. In current language-model systems, precedents enter through retrieval and in-context demonstra-tions. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) introduced the use of an external non-parametric memory to condition model outputs [22]. In-context learning research shows that what demon-strations communicate is not straightforward. Selection matters: semantically similar examples can outperform random ones [23], and learned retrievers can further improve demonstration selec-tion [37]. Yet demonstrations can remain useful even when their input–label mappings are partly corrupted, because they also communicate format, input distribution, and label space [28]. Related work distinguishes task recognition, in which examples identify a task the model already knows, from task learning, in which the model acquires the input–label mapping from the demonstrations themselves [33]; larger models are more capable of overriding semantic priors when labels are flipped or replaced by semantically unrelated tokens [43]. Demonstrations can also induce majority-label, recency, and common-token biases [46], and model performance is sensitive to demonstration or-der [24]. 3System One Models. System One Models are a recent advancement that consume natural-language states and task descriptions but return typed, non-autoregressive outputs, such as a choice over candidates, a probability for a proposition, or a score on an ordinal scale [1, 29]. Their name echoes the fast, non-deliberative mode of judgment in dual-process accounts of cognition [18]. For moderation pipelines, this interface offers an interesting opportunity because it combines properties that usually belong to different system classes: like policy-as-prompt LLMs, SOMs can receive rules and precedents at inference time; like conventional classifiers, they return structured decisions and numerical scores directly, without parsing generated explanations. 

# 3 Methodology 

We evaluate Jev v1.13 and Laya v0.3 on two related but distinct content-moderation tasks: policy-violation detection , in which decisions are grounded in an explicit set of community or platform rules, and harmful-content detection , in which the model directly predicts whether a piece of content belongs to a harmful or offensive class. We first formalize the two tasks and their associated bench-marks, and then introduce the information conditions and typed SOM interfaces used throughout the experiments. Code and prompts used in this experiments are available in a dedicated GitHub repository 1.

3.1 Benchmark Tasks and Datasets 

Policy-violation detection. Let x denote a target social-media message and let R = {r1, . . . , r m}

denote the set of rules applicable to the target. The model predicts an outcome 

y ∈ { 0, 1, . . . , m },

where y = 0 denotes No rules broken and y = j denotes a violation of rule rj . This formulation induces two evaluation tasks. The first is binary violation detection ,

v = I[y̸ = 0] ,

which asks whether any rule has been violated. The second is exact policy selection , which addi-tionally requires the model to identify the annotated rule when v = 1 .We evaluate this task on two policy-grounded benchmarks: • PluRule [17] evaluates community-specific moderation on Reddit. Each instance contains two comments from the same discussion: one violates a named subreddit rule, while the other is treated as compliant with that rule. The dataset retains the rules and community metadata required to evaluate both binary violation detection and exact rule selection. We consider both a high-support setting based on the six largest hydrated communities and the published test-set setting across the complete set of available communities. • HateModerate [47] evaluates enforcement of Facebook’s hate-speech policy. Its examples are organized around 41 policy labels, allowing us to evaluate both whether a sentence violates the policy and which specific policy category applies. After grouping exact duplicates within each released split, removing two sentences with contradictory annotations, and excluding 175 sentences associated with multiple positive policies, which cannot be represented by our single-choice formulation, we retain 3,309 training sentences and 3,302 test sentences.

Harmful-content detection. The second task removes the explicit policy from the decision problem. Given a social-media item x, the model predicts 

h ∈ { 0, 1},

where h = 1 denotes harmful or offensive content according to the benchmark annotation. Unlike policy-violation detection, the model is not required to map the content to a rule. The task therefore measures whether the SOM can recover the benchmark’s content-level decision boundary directly from the target and, when retrieval is enabled, from previously decided examples. We consider three datasets: • OLID [44] contains English tweets annotated for offensive-language detection. We use its first annotation level, distinguishing offensive ( OFF ) from non-offensive ( NOT ) content. After removing exact duplicates and contradictory annotations, the evaluation population contains 14,046 posts. • HateXplain [27] contains social-media posts annotated as hateful , offensive , or normal . We restrict the analysis to the offensive and normal classes, yielding 14,123 posts after duplicate and contradiction removal. • ModerationBench [25] contains real-world multimodal Bluesky posts with binary Safe /Unsafe 

moderation decisions. Because the evaluated SOMs operate on textual states, we construct a text-based adaptation by combining each post with a neutral description of its associated media generated by OpenGVLab/InternVL3_5-4B-Instruct . Starting from 3,712 posts, we re-move entries without usable textual or media content, contradictory exact-input groups, and redundant consistent duplicates. The resulting population contains 3,689 posts, of which 2,584 are labeled Safe and 1,105 Unsafe. Appendix E reports sensitivity analyses under alternative caption generators. For OLID and HateXplain, we additionally use the Detoxify family [15] as a task-specific external reference. Detoxify is a family of models trained on toxicity detection. Note that none of the datasets employed here was present in Detoxify’s training data. 

3.2 SOM Information Settings 

We vary two sources of decision context: written normative material and retrieved precedential 

cases. Table 1 summarizes the resulting conditions and candidate-answer inventories. Retrieved precedents are selected by embedding messages, using as embedding model qwen3-embedding:4b [45], and ranking admissible cases by cosine similarity. Unless otherwise specified, each target receives 5the five nearest precedents, excluding the target itself and respecting the task domain; for example, PluRule precedents must come from the same subreddit. For OLID and HateXplain, precedents are retrieved leave-one-out from the corresponding cleaned evaluation population. A precedent contains the previous content and its observed decision. Outcome labels are usually semantic, but HateModerate also includes an opaque RAD control in which policy labels are replaced with arbitrary consistent identifiers. The rule-inventory variants separate information effects from answer-space effects. All exposes the complete applicable ruleset. Represented exposes rules supported by the designated precedent corpus; in the tables this appears as corpus-represented or train-represented depending on the source corpus. Retrieved exposes only rules appearing among the retrieved violating precedents. Thus, 

General-all versus NAD-all isolates the effect of adding precedents while holding the complete answer space fixed, whereas General-retrieved versus semantic RAD isolates the effect of adding written rule descriptions under the retrieved candidate set. For harmful-content detection there is no independent written ruleset, so the matched augmentation contrast is Direct versus semantic 

RAD .

3.3 SOM Decision Interfaces 

We evaluate two SOM implementations, Jev and Laya. Information settings are kept conceptually distinct from the typed interface used to obtain a decision. Depending on the experiment, we employ three SOM interfaces. • Choice selects one outcome from a finite candidate set. It returns the selected option, a probability distribution over the available options, and a separate confidence value. Choice is required for exact policy selection and is also evaluated as a binary interface on OLID, HateXplain, and ModerationBench. • Noul evaluates a binary proposition and returns a probability in [0 , 1] , without a separate confidence field. We use it for binary harmful-content detection and for category-wise toxicity questions. • Score positions an input on an ordinal scale specified by two to ten textual anchors and can return values between adjacent anchors. We use this interface in the exploratory comparison with Detoxify category-score vectors. Thus, the information condition determines what evidence is supplied to the model, whereas the SOM interface determines how the decision is elicited . Comparisons intended to measure augmen-tation effects always retain the same interface. 

3.4 Cross-System Controls 

Jev and Laya differ in their available context budgets and provider-specific input serialization. We therefore construct shared request content before either system is invoked whenever the two imple-mentations are compared. The target state, instructions, candidate answers, retrieved precedents, and deterministic text shortening are fixed independently of the evaluated SOM. For PluRule and HateModerate, we retain the original complete Jev representation as Jev-full 

and additionally evaluate Jev-compact , which receives the same Laya-compatible content as Laya. All direct Jev–Laya comparisons in these experiments use Jev-compact. The OLID, HateXplain, and ModerationBench experiments use shared representations directly and therefore do not require separate full and compact Jev conditions. 63.5 Evaluation Strategy 

Our evaluation separates four aspects of moderation performance: correctness of the final modera-tion outcome, binary detection quality, discrimination independently of the decision rule, and the operational usefulness of model uncertainty. For policy-violation detection , the primary metric is Exact-Match Accuracy (EMA), which re-quires the model either to identify the annotated violated rule or to correctly select No rules broken .Because the same EMA can arise from substantially different moderation behaviors, we addition-ally examine class-conditional diagnostics. On PluRule, where each violating target is paired with a compliant target, we report Violating-Rule Recall (VRR), measuring the fraction of violating targets for which the exact annotated rule is recovered, and No-Violation Recall (NVR), measuring cor-rect compliant decisions. On HateModerate, whose cleaned evaluation population is substantially skewed toward violating examples, we instead supplement EMA and VRR with binary violation accuracy and violation recall. These measures allow us to distinguish successful policy selection from operating points dominated by either compliance predictions or indiscriminate flagging. In all policy-violation experiments, the primary retrieval contrast is General-all versus NAD-all , which adds retrieved precedents while holding the complete policy inventory and answer space fixed. For 

harmful-content detection , we distinguish operating-point classification performance from the quality of the underlying decision signal. Accuracy and F1 summarize operating-point performance, while AUROC measures the ability of the returned score to discriminate harmful from non-harmful content independently of the decision rule. For OLID and HateXplain, the primary matched augmentation analysis uses Noul, with AUROC as the primary endpoint; Choice provides a complementary fixed-decision comparison with Detoxify. On ModerationBench, AUROC is evaluated under both Choice and Noul. Accuracy and F1 characterize the resulting operating point. We evaluate uncertainty separately from predictive performance. For binary decision probabili-ties, probability quality is summarized using predictive Brier score , computed against the class label. For explicit Choice confidence, confidence Brier score is computed against exact choice correctness, and correctness AUC measures whether confidence ranks correct decisions above errors. To assess whether confidence can support selective moderation, we additionally report the coverage and ac-curacy of predictions whose reported confidence satisfies C ≥ 0.8. These quantities respectively measure how much content could be handled automatically at that confidence threshold and how reliable those retained decisions would be. For the exploratory comparison between SOM category scores and Detoxify outputs, we retain two complementary similarity measures: cosine similarity captures agreement in the relative pro-file across toxicity categories, whereas mean absolute error (MAE) captures disagreement in score magnitude. Additional measures used for diagnostic or sensitivity analyses are reported in the Appendix. This includes alternative threshold metrics, calibration measures based on binning, held-out and representation-sensitivity checks, rule-cluster breakdowns, and results under alternative caption rep-resentations. For the primary matched performance contrasts, and for selected secondary contrasts discussed explicitly below, we report 95% bootstrap confidence intervals computed on the natural sampling unit of each benchmark. In PluRule, we resample complete discussion pairs so that the violating and compliant comments belonging to the same pair remain together. In HateModerate, OLID, HateXplain, and ModerationBench, we resample target items and preserve the pairing be-tween the two compared conditions. For AUROC contrasts, each bootstrap replicate recomputes AUROC separately for the matched Direct and RAD predictions before taking their difference. Differences and confidence intervals are computed from unrounded estimates, while tables display rounded values. 4.1 Detection of Rule Violation 

We first evaluate policy-grounded moderation on PluRule and HateModerate. In both benchmarks, the model must distinguish violating from compliant content and, for violating targets, identify the applicable rule. 

Main Result 

• Performance is moderate overall: Jev consistently outperforms Laya in exact policy selec-tion, but neither system is close to perfect. • Retrieved precedents reliably improve Jev when the full rule inventory is fixed, with gains of +12.35, +5.20, and +22.20 EMA points across the three evaluations. • Laya benefits much less from precedents and often behaves more like a broad violation detector than a precise policy selector. • The precedent effect is strongest on HateModerate, substantial in high-support PluRule communities, and smaller on the PluRule test intersection.

4.2 Detection of Harmful Content 

We next evaluate whether the behavior observed in policy-grounded moderation extends to harmful-content detection, where no explicit written policy is supplied. We consider OLID and HateXplain as text-only offensive-content benchmarks and ModerationBench as a broader Safe /Unsafe moderation task over Bluesky content. As described in Section 3, all cross-system comparisons use a shared input representation constructed before either SOM is invoked. In this setting, Direct provides only the target and decision question, whereas RAD-semantic additionally provides five semantically similar labeled precedents. 10 Main Results 

• Jev shows competitive harmful-content detection, matching or exceeding Detoxify on F1 and clearly outperforming Laya across most settings. • Retrieved precedents generally improve Jev’s decision signal, increasing AUROC on OLID, HateXplain, and ModerationBench, although gains in threshold-dependent metrics are not universal. • Laya is substantially less consistent: retrieval degrades discrimination on OLID and Hat-eXplain but improves it on ModerationBench. • ModerationBench shows the clearest cross-system retrieval benefit: precedents improve discrimination for both Jev and Laya, unlike OLID and HateXplain. 

Text-only offensive-content detection. After removing exact duplicates and contradictory annotations, the evaluation populations contain 14,046 OLID posts and 14,123 HateXplain posts. We first compare the binary Choice formulation of Jev and Laya with the five Detoxify variants [15]. Table 5 reports Accuracy and F1. On OLID, Detoxify obtains the highest accuracy, with the Multilingual variant reaching 81.0%. Jev Direct instead attains the highest F1, 66.1%, closely followed by Detoxify-Multilingual at 65.7%. Adding precedents does not improve Jev’s Choice result on this dataset: RAD-semantic lowers accuracy from 74.4% to 73.5% and F1 from 66.1% to 63.3%. Laya shows a larger deterioration in accuracy, from 72.6% to 59.6%, despite a modest increase in F1. The pattern differs on HateXplain. Jev RAD-semantic reaches 66.1% accuracy and 65.9% F1, above its Direct condition and the external Detoxify reference values shown in the table. Jev Direct already obtains an F1 of 65.5%, compared with at most 57.0% for Detoxify. Laya remains less consistent: retrieval reduces accuracy from 59.6% to 56.0%, although F1 rises substantially. Thus, the operating-point results already indicate that the effect of precedents is model- and dataset-dependent. 

Effect of precedent augmentation. We isolate the contribution of retrieved precedents more directly through the Noul interface. For each target, Direct and RAD-semantic ask exactly the same binary offensiveness question and differ only in whether the five retrieved labeled examples are supplied. Table 6 shows a consistent positive retrieval effect for Jev. 4.3 Confidence and Selective Prediction 

Having evaluated predictive performance and the effect of precedents, we finally examine whether the confidence returned by the Choice interface can identify reliable decisions and support selective moderation. The Choice interface returns a confidence value separately from its option probabili-ties. We evaluate this quantity against exact choice correctness: exact rule selection or a correct 

No rules broken decision in policy-selection tasks, and the annotated binary outcome in harmful-content tasks. Deterministic single-option cases that do not invoke the model are excluded from confidence analysis; consequently, Exact Acc. may differ from the population-level EMA reported above, and coverage is computed over model-inferred decisions only. Table 8 mixes primary pol-icy conditions and selected diagnostic conditions: the matched General-all policy condition for PluRule, the augmented condition with the highest population-level EMA for HateModerate, and semantic RAD for harmful-content detection. Additional held-out Choice-confidence diagnostics and predictive-probability sensitivity analyses are reported in the Appendix. Jev confidence is useful for selective review across both policy-selection and harmful-content settings. High-confidence subsets are consistently more accurate than the unfiltered predictions and often retain a substantial fraction of cases, especially on ModerationBench. Laya’s confidence is less reliable for automation: even when confidence Brier is competitive, as in HateModerate RAD-opaque, correctness AUC and high-confidence coverage remain weak. This separation between probability quality, correctness ranking, and accepted-set accuracy reinforces the need to report calibration and selective prediction together rather than treating confidence as a single scalar guar-antee.

Limitations and future work This work is not exempt from limitations. We evaluate only two SOM implementations, so differences between Jev and Laya should not be generalized to all structured-output models, nor attributed solely to architecture. Finally, retrieved precedents may reproduce historical inconsistency or bias; provenance makes that risk visible, but does not elimi-nate it. Future evaluations should test additional SOMs and generative baselines under the same controlled information conditions, vary the number and diversity of retrieved precedents, and study 

when a model should rely on retrieved cases versus the complete written policy. Moderation settings also require support for multiple simultaneous violations, multilingual and community-specific de-ployment studies, drift monitoring, group-conditioned error analysis, and calibration methods tied to human review capacity. In practice, confidence should become part of an audited review policy, 14 evaluated jointly with coverage, appeal outcomes, and class-specific error costs.

23 D Additional Harmful-Content Diagnostics 

The main text reports the metric subset defined in Section 3: Accuracy and F1 for operating-point behavior, AUROC for discrimination, and predictive Brier where probability quality is directly evaluated. This appendix reports the additional threshold, ranking, and calibration diagnostics used to interpret those results. 

D.1 OLID and HateXplain Threshold Diagnostics 

Table 15 expands the binary Choice results reported in the main text with positive-class precision and recall. These measures help characterize the operating point of each model but are not used as primary endpoints. The additional metrics clarify the operating-point differences summarized in the main text. On OLID, Jev Direct obtains substantially higher recall than the Detoxify variants, whereas Laya shifts toward higher recall under RAD at the cost of accuracy. The contrast is particularly pronounced on HateXplain: Laya Direct is highly conservative, with 72.3% precision but only 6.1% recall, while RAD moves it toward a substantially more permissive decision boundary. 

D.2 Noul Probability Diagnostics 

Table 16 reports the complete diagnostics for the matched Direct–RAD Noul experiment. In addition to the main-text Accuracy, F1, and AUROC measures, we report precision, recall, average precision (AP), predictive Brier score, ten-bin Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and the percentage of posts classified as offensive (Pos.). AUROC and AP evaluate the continuous Noul probability, while the classification measures and Pos. use the fixed threshold p(Offensive) > 0.5.The probability diagnostics reinforce the distinction between discrimination and operating point. For Jev, retrieval improves AUROC, AP, predictive Brier, and ECE on OLID and improves the corresponding probability measures on HateXplain while reducing the fraction of posts classified as offensive. For Laya, RAD instead shifts the predicted class distribution strongly toward the positive class. The predicted-offensive rate rises from 34.3% to 74.3% on OLID and from 60.8% to 97.4% on HateXplain. This shift explains why recall can increase despite deteriorating AUROC, predictive Brier, and calibration. 

D.3 Detoxify Category-Vector Sensitivity

Each model receives the post alone and independently eval-uates the six categories shared by all Detoxify variants: toxicity, severe toxicity, obscenity, threat, insult, and identity attack. Noul returns a probability for each proposition, while Score places each category between absence and presence anchors. Neither interface receives Detoxify predictions, category definitions, written rules, or retrieved precedents. Table 17 compares the resulting six-dimensional vectors with Detoxify. Jev aligns more closely with Detoxify than Laya on both OLID and HateXplain, although neither Noul nor Score dominates on both similarity measures. # E ModerationBench Caption-Model Sensitivity 

The main ModerationBench experiment uses neutral media descriptions generated by OpenGVLab/InternVL3_5-4B-I 

The source snapshot also provides independently generated descriptions from Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct 

and openbmb/MiniCPM-V-4_5 . We repeat all four Direct/RAD and Choice/Noul conditions for both SOMs with each caption representation. As predeclared for this extension, each caption model uses its maximum deterministic clean population rather than a shared intersection. The resulting pop-ulations contain 3,673 Qwen posts (2,580 Safe and 1,093 Unsafe), 3,689 InternVL posts (2,584 Safe and 1,105 Unsafe), and 3,685 MiniCPM posts (2,583 Safe and 1,102 Unsafe). The differences arise because caption availability and the exact text used for duplicate and contradiction detection vary by caption model. Consequently, comparisons across caption models combine a representation effect with a small population-composition effect; Direct–RAD comparisons within one caption population remain exactly matched. Table 20 reports the complete pooled results. MiniCPM yields the strongest Jev accuracy and F1 in every corresponding condition. Its RAD-Noul configuration reaches 89.1% accuracy, 82.5% F1, .950 AUROC, and .890 average precision. The retrieval effect is nevertheless consistent across caption generators: Jev RAD raises Choice accuracy by 2.8, 2.7, and 1.6 percentage points for Qwen, InternVL, and MiniCPM, respectively, and raises Noul accuracy by 3.2, 3.0, and 2.2 points. Laya also behaves consistently across caption generators. RAD-Choice reaches approximately 66% accuracy in all three populations, whereas RAD-Noul reaches 95.8–96.3% recall but only 50.5–52.0% accuracy because it predicts Unsafe too often.
