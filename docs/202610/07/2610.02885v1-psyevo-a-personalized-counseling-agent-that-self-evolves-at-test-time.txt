ABSTRACT 

Mental health disorders affect a substantial proportion of the global population, yet a persistent shortage of trained practitioners leaves the majority without ad-equate care. Large language model (LLM)-based counselors present a promis-ing direction for delivering scalable conversational psychological support. Of-fline model training alone leaves limited room to adapt to individual clients or to learn from ongoing therapeutic interaction at test time. We introduce PsyEvo, an LLM-based counseling framework that enables both client-specific person-alization and response-policy improvement at test time through three compo-nents: Hierarchical Bayesian Skill Policy (HBSP) personalizes what intervention to apply by maintaining a per-client skill posterior updated from session feed-back; Inter-session Listwise Preference Optimization (LiPO) improves how the selected skill is expressed by updating a shared response adapter from cross-client preference evidence; and State-conditioned Ordinal Credit Assignment (SOCA) supplies candidate preferences and trajectory credit to the two com-ponents through consistency-checked comparisons and ordinal projection. In simulated-client evaluation with shared online cohort adaptation, PsyEvo ob-tains 7.684 Overall on PsychEval and exceeds every component variant in each of three matched runs. Removing individual components lowers mean over-all score by 0.138–0.171 under the shared configuration, supporting conditional contributions within the complete scaffold. Our code is available at https: //github.com/Lingxi-mental-health/PsyEvo .Keywords: test-time learning; multi-session counseling; personalized counseling; ordinal credit assignment; preference optimization; self-evolving agents. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Mental health disorders have become a major global health challenge, with nearly one billion indi-viduals affected and a substantial share of the worldwide disease burden attributed to conditions such as depression and anxiety (World Health Organization; 2025). Psychological counseling is a widely adopted evidence-based intervention, yet training qualified counselors requires years of supervi-sion, and the available workforce falls far short of demand (British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy; World Health Organization, 2022). In this context, large language models (LLMs) provide a scalable conversational interface that could expand access to psychological support. Research on LLM-based counseling spans supervised fine-tuning (SFT), preference optimization, and agent-based mechanisms. SoulChat and CPsyCoun provide counseling and empathetic dialogue data for SFT, whereas PsyCoPref supplies preference comparisons for response optimization (Chen et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; 2026a). MusPsy and PsychAgent incorporate memory, planning, and skill-related mechanisms to support longitudinal interaction (Wang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). These approaches extend counseling support from individual responses to multiple sessions. This motivates two complementary learning objectives: personalization, which tailors intervention selection to a particular client, and counselor self-evolution, which updates the response policy using cross-client experience. To this end, we present PsyEvo, a test-time learning framework for multi-session psychological counseling that addresses both objectives over a frozen backbone through three components: Hier-archical Bayesian Skill Policy (HBSP) for personalized intervention selection via a per-client skill posterior, Listwise Preference Optimization (LiPO) for shared response-policy improvement via cross-client preference graphs, and State-conditioned Ordinal Credit Assignment (SOCA) for con-structing candidate preferences and trajectory credit from relative comparisons and realized feed-back. Our contributions are as follows: (1) we introduce PsyEvo, which combines client-private skill posteriors with a shared population response adapter over a frozen backbone, using HBSP for personalized skill selection and LiPO for cross-session response-policy learning; (2) we propose SOCA, which converts compressed self-evaluation feedback into candidate preferences through consistency-checked comparisons and into delayed credit through within-trajectory ordinal prop-agation; and (3) we establish positive conditional component contributions across three matched PsychEval runs and evaluate a related evidence-fusion rule on SummEval, where it achieves the highest mean agreement with human judgments among the compared evaluators.

3 EXPERIMENTS 

3.1 DATASETS AND BASELINES 

Our main counseling study uses a 100-client PsychEval cohort across five therapy modalities (Pan et al., 2026); SummEval (Fabbri et al., 2021) tests evidence aggregation beyond counseling against human judgments. Supplementary MusPsy figures for 100 profiles over six sessions are retained in Appendix K.1 (Wang et al., 2026). For counseling, we compare PsyEvo with eleven baselines, seven counseling-specific systems and four general-purpose models (Table 1), and with a Qwen3-32B variant trained with DPO (Table 3); for summary evaluation, we compare SOCA with six evidence-aggregation baselines that share its Qwen3-32B backbone. PsyEvo uses a Qwen3-32B counseling backbone with a rank-8 LoRA adapter optimized by LiPO. Internal candidate judgments also use Qwen3-32B; external PsychEval reporting uses Gemini-3-Flash on completed transcripts (Appendix G). PsychEval follows the PsychAgent interaction protocol, with BGE-M3 retrieval and DeepSeek-V3.1 client simulation; MusPsy follows its benchmark-specific client-state, six-session interaction, and evaluation protocols (Yang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). In PsychEval, PsyEvo updates its shared adapter across clients in the online evaluation cohort, while each private skill posterior uses only that client’s feedback. Appendices A, B, C, and I detail the datasets, baselines, learning settings, and training costs. 3.2 EVALUATION METRICS 

On PsychEval, we report four metric families derived from its 18 evaluation instruments (Pan et al., 2026). Counselor-shared (C-Shared) summarizes common dimensions of counseling quality, in-cluding working alliance and relational authenticity. Counselor-specific (C-Spec) uses CTRS, MITI, 5PSYEVO · PREPRINT 

TES, PSC, and EFT-TFS for CBT, behavioral, humanistic, psychodynamic, and postmodernist ther-apy, respectively. Client-shared (L-Shared) summarizes common simulated-client scores, including affect and session alliance; client-specific (L-Spec) uses BDI-II, STAI, CCT, IPO, and SFBT indica-tors for the corresponding modalities. Metric definitions are detailed in Appendix D. Overall is the unweighted average of the four reported family scores. For MusPsy, we report the Working Alliance Inventory–Short Form (WAI; Goal, Task, Bond, and their average) and positive and negative PANAS affect scores on a 1–5 scale (Wang et al., 2026), averaged over six sessions and 100 clients using the official MusPsy evaluation code and judging prompt. While for SummEval, we compute Spearman’s ρ between automatic and human ratings for coherence (COH), consistency (CON), fluency (FLU), and relevance (REL) within each source-document group and macro-average across groups (Fabbri et al., 2021); Mean averages the four dimensions. Higher values are better for all metrics except negative PANAS. Appendix D lists the constituent instruments, aggregation rules, and judging-prompt protocols. 3.3 MAIN RESULTS

3.6 ANALYSIS 

We describe client-specific skill rankings, session-wise outcomes, and a longitudinal case to examine learning-state changes alongside counseling behavior.

At the same state-space slot, different clients can receive different top-ranked skills (Fig-ure 3): within-modality median pairwise disagreement ranges from 5.6% (CBT) to 14.3% (PDT), while most slots agree. These differences document client-specific ranking adaptation; the ablation (Table 3) tests its conditional contribution, but does not isolate client matching from residual learn-ing (Appendix F). 4 RELATED WORK 

LLM-based counseling has progressed from multi-turn empathetic dialogue within a session (Liu et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023) through report-grounded reconstruction (Zhang et al., 2024) and counselor-style modeling (Xie et al., 2025) to multi-session agent systems with memory and plan-ning (Wang et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). PsyEvo combines client-private skill posteriors with a shared response adapter updated at test time. Personalization and test-time adaptation have been explored separately: LaMP and OPPU person-alize generation via user histories or parameter-efficient modules (Salemi et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2024), while Self-Refine, Reflexion, and ExpeL enable iterative self-improvement through feedback loops (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). In preference optimization, DPO learns from pairwise comparisons (Rafailov et al., 2023) and LiPO provides a general learning-to-rank framework (Liu et al., 2025); MICA derives mixed credit from user-state potentials (Zhang et al., 2026b). Our LiPO objective uses SOCA support-strength weights and group normalization; the original LiPO-Lambda variant uses gain and rank-discount weights. LLM-based evaluation can exhibit presentation bias and compressed scores (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Reward-model training can also produce prompt-independent reward distributions (Song et al., 2026), a distinct is-sue from the self-evaluation score compression addressed here. SOCA constructs ordinal evidence from consistency-checked comparisons without learning a reward model. 

5 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We have presented PsyEvo, a test-time learning framework that combines HBSP for client-specific skill selection, LiPO for cross-client response-policy improvement, and SOCA for candidate pref-erences and ordinal trajectory credit. This separation allows individual intervention preferences to evolve alongside a shared response policy while keeping the backbone frozen. Matched PsychEval ablations show consistent conditional component contributions across three runs; the separate Sum-mEval study examines evidence aggregation against human judgments. 9PSYEVO · PREPRINT 

Several limitations remain. The automatic evaluations use simulated clients and LLM judges, so their scores characterize model behavior rather than clinical efficacy. Shared online adaptation within the scored cohort does not establish transfer to unseen clients, and individual component removals do not isolate the joint benefit of both learning paths or each SOCA mechanism. Broader system comparisons mix evaluated and published results, while the small human-rating pilot has limited agreement and remains descriptive. Future work will examine transfer to disjoint client cohorts, isolate the learning mechanisms with matched controls, and broaden independent human evaluation. Extending this framework to clinician-supervised settings will also require assessment of crisis handling, privacy, and policy drift, alongside whether personalized skill selection and shared policy learning improve continuity across sessions. 10 PSYEVO · PREPRINT

The graph constructor requires one two-order judgment for every unordered candidate pair. Both support values refer to the same original candidate after reversing presentation order. Agreement must be strict on the same side of 0.5; an exact tie or or-der disagreement produces no edge. To apply the configured stability floor of 0.1, the backend must pass it explicitly, since the general graph routine defaults to a zero floor. Equal-strength edges are or-dered by a deterministic hash of the group identifier and unordered candidate indices before acyclic admission. Pointwise scores cannot create or reverse edges; they only break equal-depth selection ties. An empty graph therefore falls back to pointwise selection but is not admitted to preference training. For realized outcomes, exact ties share a midrank, and a common shift followed by clip-ping preserves the mean; the reference solver uses bisection. Discounted returns are accumulated backward from the supplied terminal reward. These interfaces consume numerical feedback and do not determine which model or prompt generated it. 

Feedback flow and optimization. Pointwise candidate scores and two-order comparison supports describe alternative responses at the current state, before a response is delivered. Their role differs from realized-turn feedback, which describes the simulated client’s reaction after delivery, and from terminal feedback available after the session. The former supplies local preference edges for LiPO; the latter supplies trajectory returns for HBSP. The preference dataset contains candidates, edge directions, and numerical weights, not gradients between groups. LiPO differentiates policy log probabilities while holding these saved judgments fixed; SOCA does not backpropagate through 17 PSYEVO · PREPRINT 

evaluator calls. The frozen, adapter-disabled preference reference specifies the denominator in Eq. 5; it is a distinct role from the internal evaluator that supplies comparison support. External transcript scores are a separate reporting channel (Appendix G). 

Population weighting and batch units. Each buffered item is a complete local graph identified by client and group, with modality and generating policy version retained. Pooling these items does not introduce comparisons between different clients. In lipo.py , each graph loss is divided by its retained edge count, not by the sum of support-strength weights; graph losses are then averaged equally. Clients consequently contribute in proportion to their admitted group counts, rather than receiving equal client weights. The listwise grouping preserves local candidate sets, while the ob-jective itself remains a weighted pairwise logistic loss; it does not define a likelihood over complete candidate permutations. The optional autograd implementation detaches reference log probabilities; the backend must compute them with the adapter disabled and matching response-token masks. The reference batch sampler counts preference pairs as retained graph edges. Under the PsychEval con-figuration, exactly 320 edges with at least 20% from each of five modalities requires 64 edges per modality, together with at least 32 groups. It selects whole graphs without truncation and leaves the buffer unchanged if no admissible batch exists. Its deterministic selection does not reconstruct the experimental sampling order or identify the accounting unit of the separate archived training trace in Appendix I. 

Session boundaries and failed updates. One scheduler is shared by all clients in a learning scope. Starting a session captures the active adapter identifier; later acceptance of a new version does not change that session’s identifier. Under the PsychEval schedule, five completed sessions make an update eligible, but training still waits for an admissible batch. Training must produce a separate candidate checkpoint, which is checked by a backend validation callback before the active reference advances. An exception or rejection returns the reserved graphs and retains the previous active reference; returning evidence is not a rollback of arbitrary in-place model changes. The backend must preserve the active checkpoint and implement physical loading, cleanup, and recovery. 

Benchmark configurations. Benchmark-specific configurations follow the corresponding source protocols for PsychEval and MusPsy, while SummEval uses the original summary data and human annotations (Pan et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Fabbri et al., 2021).
