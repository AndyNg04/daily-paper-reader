## Beyond Task Completion: Measuring Interaction Cost in Terminal User Interfaces 

RUIDA HU, Harbin Institute of Technology, Shenzhen, China

CUIYUN GAO, Harbin Institute of Technology, Shenzhen, China With rapid advances in large language models (LLMs), terminal user interfaces (TUIs) have become increasingly popular for development and operations. Successfully completing a task through a TUI does not mean that the task is easy to perform. Because task success alone does not fully capture TUI usability, evaluation should also measure the effort involved in understanding and operating the interface. Despite this growing adoption, research on TUIs remains limited, and no prior work has systematically evaluated their usability. Existing usability assessments rely mainly on costly human assessment or LLM-generated ratings and reports. Neither provides a repeatable measure of task-level interaction effort grounded in actual task execution. To address these gaps, we propose Agent-as-a-User , an evaluation paradigm that places an LLM agent in the user role to provide repeatable measurements of task-level interaction effort. Specifically, we introduce 

Agent-KLM , an analytical model that separates agent-side costs into interpretation and operation dimensions to establish a structural correspondence with human interaction. To operationalize the Agent-as-a-User paradigm, we implement TUINaut , a framework that records realized interaction trajectories and verifies task outcomes using actor-independent oracles. We construct TUINaut-Bench and conduct an empirical study of 84 tasks across 15 real-world TUIs. The study includes six human participants, five observation–evaluator configurations, and we apply the paradigm to 45 TUIs independently generated by three leading generation stacks. We find that similar aggregate success rates can mask substantial differences in which tasks humans and agents complete, whereas their interpretation and operation costs follow correlated task rankings, especially for operation. Across all five observation–evaluator configurations, completion correspondence remains weak while cost rankings remain aligned. TUIs generated by current leading agent frameworks can implement correct functionality, yet their task-based usability still needs improvement. These findings position Agent-as-a-User as a repeatable and execution-grounded paradigm for measuring task-based usability. All artifacts for TUINaut and TUINaut-Bench are available at https://github.com/kinesiatricssxilm14/ Agent-as-a-User. Anonymized trajectories for all runs are available at https://tuinaut.pages.dev/. 1 Introduction 

The spread of large language models (LLMs) has given terminal user interfaces (TUIs) a larger role in development and operations. These interfaces now take many forms, such as file managers, system monitors, database clients, version-control tools, and coding agents [1, 8, 32, 36, 38]. Compared with conventional graphical user interfaces (GUIs), TUIs render their interfaces in terminals and rely more heavily on keyboard input, often through mode-dependent keys with limited visible cues. These characteristics make it important to study how easily users can complete concrete tasks. However, research on TUI usability remains limited. The upper design omits these cues, forcing users to scan blindly, try actions, and infer whether those actions succeeded. The same tasks can therefore impose different demands on understanding the interface and acting through it. These examples show that task completion alone cannot characterize task-based usability . We therefore consider two complementary forms of evidence from actual executions: task completion, which records whether the goal is reached, and interaction cost, which captures the demands of interpreting the interface state and carrying out the next action. Meanwhile, software evaluation still focuses primarily on correctness [10, 18, 21, 46], and us-ability assessments leave the interaction cost of a task unmeasured. Existing usability assessments rely mainly on human studies or LLM-generated ratings and reports. Human studies can reveal interaction friction and subjective experience, but they are expensive to repeat across applications and revisions and difficult to scale [17, 20, 24, 35]. Recent LLM-based systems simulate users, generate think-aloud reports, predict ratings, or organize UX feedback [12, 14, 30]. These systems provide useful qualitative evidence, but their ratings and reports remain judgments rather than measurements grounded in verified task execution. Therefore, existing approaches do not provide a repeatable measure of interaction cost grounded in actual task execution. To address this measurement problem, we propose Agent-as-a-User , an evaluation paradigm that places an LLM agent in the user role to evaluate task-based TUI usability. The agent executes each task under a fixed configuration, with a fresh context and keyboard actions only. Under this setup, the agent serves as a controlled experimental instrument that observes the same rendered TUI and acts through the same application input boundary as a human user. Building on the classical Keystroke-Level Model (KLM), we introduce Agent-KLM , an analytical model that separates agent-side interpretation cost and operation cost and relates them structurally to human interaction. We implement TUINaut , a framework that records realized interaction trajectories and verifies task outcomes using actor-independent oracles. Together, the paradigm, analytical model, and framework support an empirical study organized around three research questions. RQ1 asks whether agent completion and the two costs correspond to human interaction on the same tasks. 

RQ2 asks whether that correspondence still holds for plain, semantic, and image observations, Beyond Task Completion: Measuring Interaction Cost in Terminal User Interfaces 3

because humans and agents differ most in what they see. RQ3 applies the paradigm to the task-based usability of TUIs generated by three leading coding agents. To answer these questions, we construct TUINaut-Bench with 84 tasks across 15 real-world TUIs. The study includes six human participants, five observation–evaluator configurations span-ning plain, semantic, and image input, 3,528 agent attempts, and 45 TUIs independently generated by the three stacks. The results show that similar aggregate success rates can hide substantial differences in which tasks humans and agents complete. By contrast, their interpretation and operation costs follow correlated task rankings, with the strongest relationship for operation. This pattern recurs across the five configurations even though completion correspondence remains weak. Applying Agent-as-a-User to generated TUIs further shows that correct functionality can coexist with higher interpretation cost and no consistent operation advantage. Together, these findings position Agent-as-a-User as a repeatable and execution-grounded paradigm for measuring task-based usability. This paper makes three contributions: 

• An evaluation paradigm for task-based TUI usability. We propose Agent-as-a-User and empirically characterize its human correspondence, stability across observation settings, and application to generated TUIs. 

• An interaction-cost model for agent task execution. We introduce Agent-KLM to separate interpretation and operation costs and operationalize it in TUINaut through verified task execution, alternative screen representations, atomic key-event accounting, repeated trials, and trajectory analysis. 

• A reproducible TUI benchmark. We construct TUINaut-Bench from 84 validated tasks over 15 recent real-world repositories, with reproducible fixtures, generation prompts, fingerprints, reference executions, and deterministic multi-level oracles. 

2 Agent-KLM: Modeling Interaction Cost for Task-Based Usability 

We introduce Agent-KLM , an analytical model that separates agent execution into interpretation cost and operation cost . This separation draws on classical KLM’s distinction between mental preparation and physical operators. We first review this basis and then define the agent costs and corresponding human measures. 2.2 Agent-KLM Interaction Cost 

The two dimensions provide a shared analytical structure for describing human and agent interac-tion. Interpretation cost captures the resources used to determine what to do, whereas operation cost captures the keyboard input used to execute the chosen action. Classical KLM motivates this separation. At a keyboard-controlled interface, 1 the pointing and drawing operators do not apply, and no homing is required between input devices. The response term 𝑇 𝑅 captures system processing rather than actor-side activity, so it is not included in either dimension. Fig. 2 maps the two dimensions to role-specific human and agent measures. For humans, in-teraction time from the initial screen to the first submission provides an observable proxy for interpretation demand, while total physical atomic key events quantify operation. This interval does not isolate cognitive effort, but approximates the time required to understand, plan, and complete the interaction. For agents, reasoning tokens across all turns quantify inference, while total virtual atomic key events quantify operation. The human measures support correspondence analysis but are not components of Agent-KLM. Agent-KLM uses the two dimensions to define agent-side costs rather than predict execution time. This cutoff is intended to minimize potential overlap with model pretraining data while retaining a sufficiently large pool for screening. 3.2 End-to-End Evaluation with TUINaut 

TUINaut-Bench provides the applications and task assets, while TUINaut turns them into exe-cutable and verifiable evaluations. Fig. 3 summarizes this process in three stages. 

3.2.1 Task Provisioning. As shown in Fig. 3(a), TUINaut prepares each task in an isolated Docker environment and exposes its TUI through a host-managed Tmux session [31]. It first starts the benchmark image as a long-lived Docker container and materializes the task fixtures at their designated paths in the Docker sandbox. TUINaut then runs docker exec -it from the Tmux session to start the task through a persistent interactive terminal shared by human and agent execution. Inside the container, the entrypoint runs the initialization script to reset task state, configure the environment, and prepare the fixture data before launching the TUI. Provisioning finishes when the initial screen is rendered. Every run therefore begins with the pinned application configuration, initialized fixtures, and a clean task state. Section 3.3 details the shared mechanisms used for subsequent human and agent execution. In addition, to reduce false positives from oracle assertions that might otherwise accept a generic or accidentally reached state, TUINaut uses task fingerprints as an additional safeguard. We examine every task and apply fingerprints wherever its inputs and expected outcome can naturally incorporate a task-specific marker, resulting in 30 of the 84 tasks. For each such task, the fingerprint generator derives short tokens from a SHA-256 hash of the project and task identifiers. As required by each task, TUINaut substitutes these tokens into the initialization script, task fixtures, oracle assertions, and task description. The expected token thus becomes part of both the task instance and its completion criteria, allowing the oracle to verify the intended task-specific result. 

3.2.2 Human–Agent Task Execution. Fig. 3(b) shows the human user and keyboard agent in corre-sponding task-execution roles. After provisioning, the TUI is already running inside the Docker sandbox and rendered through the host Tmux session. Both receive the same task description and interact with this running TUI through the three shared stages of observation , interpretation , and 

action , which correspond to (𝑜 𝑖 , 𝑟 𝑖 , 𝑎 𝑖 ) in Eq. 2. For the human user, the running TUI first renders the screen. The user views this screen with human eyes, and the human brain interprets it. The user then presses keys as physical key events, which reach the TUI as terminal input. For the keyboard agent, capture pane obtains a TUI screenshot of the same rendered screen. The LLM core updates context from this screenshot and generates an action. Tmux send-keys then issues those keys as virtual key events, which likewise reach the TUI as terminal input. The TUI renders the updated screen for the next observation, and either role continues this loop until submit / exit. TUINaut records the resulting screen–key trajectory for either role. During this process, TUINaut records the timestamp of every physical or virtual key event for later analysis. 8 Ruida Hu, Yuanhao Wang, Chao Peng, Yakun Zhang, and Cuiyun Gao 

3.2.3 Independent Task Verification. As shown in Fig. 3(c), once either the human user or keyboard agent submits the task or exits the TUI, TUINaut activates the verification engine without sending a further key to the TUI. Following the software-testing notion of a test oracle [3], we predefine assertions for each task’s expected final state. The engine evaluates three classes of assertions. Shell-state and filesystem assertions query the resulting Docker state through docker exec , whereas screen assertions inspect the final host Tmux pane. The aggregate result is PASS only when every assertion passes and FAIL otherwise. Fig. 4 details the six assertion primitives used to compose each task-specific oracle. (1) Shell-state assertions: check_shell executes a command in the container and compares its output with the expected output. (2) Filesystem assertions: check_filesystem searches configured roots for a file containing the specified path and content tokens. check_filesystem_content searches for content without constraining the path. (3) Screen assertions: check_screen verifies that specified text appears on the final screen. check_screen_absent verifies that specified text does not appear. check_screen_highlight verifies that specified text is visually highlighted. Detailed implementations of these checks are available in our repository.

In the host Tmux ses-sion , Tmux creates an interactive pane and its pseudo-terminal (PTY). A PTY provides a bidirec-tional interface that lets a process communicate as if it were attached to a conventional terminal [28]. A human enters physical keystrokes into this pane, whereas the agent injects virtual keys with Tmux send-keys . To connect this pane to the Docker sandbox , TUINaut in-vokes docker exec -it . Docker creates a container-side PTY and attaches it to the TUI’s standard input and output. The connected PTYs form the bidirectional bridge shown in Fig. 5. Input from either actor reaches the same TUI stdin. The TUI sends rendered output in the opposite direction as ANSI state containing text, color, and cursor information. Tmux retains the resulting screen in the host pane, where TUINaut reads it with capture-pane . Observation and action therefore use the same terminal transport rather than separate paths into the container. The Docker sandbox remains alive after the TUI exits, preserving the final filesystem and shell state for the oracle in Fig. 3(c). 

3.4 Keyboard Agent Design 

Section 3.3 places both actors on the same rendered TUI and input boundary. We next describe how the keyboard agent implements Eq. 2 as a controlled turn-based loop. Beyond Task Completion: Measuring Interaction Cost in Terminal User Interfaces 9

3.4.1 Interaction Loop. Each turn instantiates one tuple (𝑜 𝑖 , 𝑟 𝑖 , 𝑎 𝑖 ). Observation. The host-side Interaction Controller captures the current Tmux screen and encodes it using one of three observa-tion representations. The plain representation preserves the visible terminal characters and layout as unstyled text. The semantic representation augments the same text with XML-like <fg:color> 

and <bg:color> tags that encode ANSI foreground and background colors. The image representa-tion renders the terminal screen as a PNG image. The controller then adds 𝑜 𝑖 to the LLM context. 

Interpretation. The LLM interprets the current observation in light of the interaction so far, and we record the reasoning tokens it produces as 𝑟 𝑖 . Action. The agent must emit exactly one 𝑎 𝑖 from the four permitted actions: type , press , submit , or wait . The controller rejects any other output as invalid. It executes a valid action and, when the interaction continues, captures the resulting screen to begin the next turn. Three of these actions align with human behavior at the shared execution boundary. The agent uses type to enter a literal text string and press to issue a functional key or key combination, such as Enter or Ctrl + A . This separation distinguishes textual input from keyboard commands. The agent uses submit to express the same completion decision as a human submission, and the controller intercepts it rather than delivering it to the TUI. When processing may still be underway, 

wait pauses and captures the screen again without sending another key. 

3.4.2 Execution Scope and Recording. To keep this loop within the task-execution role, each task uses a fresh LLM context containing only the task description and action protocol. Throughout a run, the agent has no access to source code, the filesystem, process state, or oracle output. The controller dispatches application-bound keyboard input through Tmux and normalizes it into atomic key events using the counting rule in Section 2.2. The loop ends on submission, TUI or session exit, or a predefined execution limit, after which the actor-independent oracle evaluates the final state. We record the screen observations and actions together with the reasoning-token and atomic key event counts.

We recruit six participants to independently complete all 84 tasks. Each participant has more than five years of programming experience and is proficient in Linux and terminal use. None participated in the repository selection or task construction described in Section 3.1. The six participants therefore approach the benchmark as experienced terminal users without prior knowledge of its task designs or intended interaction paths. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided informed consent before the study. We implement a human experiment harness for macOS and Linux that mirrors the keyboard agent setup in task initialization, sandboxed execution, oracle verification, and trajectory logging. The principal difference is observation delivery: participants view the Tmux pane directly in a maximized desktop terminal rather than receiving an encoded screen representation. 4.3 Metrics 

We characterize TUI usability through task completion and the interaction trajectories required to achieve it. Task oracles verify successful completion, while recorded trajectories quantify Agent-KLM’s operation and interpretation costs and reveal how users and agents interact with the TUI. 

4.3.1 Task Completion. Success rate (𝑆𝑅 ) measures task completion. An attempt succeeds only if it passes every assertion in the task-specific oracle. For each task, human 𝑆𝑅 is the mean of the six participants’ binary first-attempt outcomes, while agent 𝑆𝑅 is the mean of three fresh-context run outcomes. Profile-level 𝑆𝑅 is the mean of these task-level rates across all 84 tasks. 

4.3.2 Interaction Costs. Following Section 2.2, we operationalize the two interaction-cost dimen-sions with role-specific measures. For human trajectories, interaction time ( 𝑇 𝐻 ) serves as a proxy for interpretation demand, and physical atomic key event count ( 𝑁 𝐻 ) measures operation. For agent trajectories, reasoning token length ( 𝐿 𝐴 ) and virtual atomic key event count ( 𝑁 𝐴 ) serve the corresponding roles. 𝑇 𝐻 spans the initial screen to the first submission, whereas 𝐿 𝐴 totals reasoning tokens across turns. 𝑁 𝐻 and 𝑁 𝐴 follow the shared counting rule in Section 2.2. In particular, we do not compare the raw values of 𝑇 𝐻 and 𝐿 𝐴 , but compare their task rankings instead because they use different units. 

4.3.3 Keyboard Trajectory Characteristics. To characterize interaction composition, we group atomic key events by their observable forms into four classes: printable characters and symbols, 

navigation keys for cursor or focus movement, deletion keys, and control keys for commands and modifier combinations [26, 45]. We use observable forms because the same printable key may enter text or invoke a command depending on the TUI state. To compare trajectory order, we construct 

raw-key sequences and class sequences , with the latter mapping events to the four classes and collapsing consecutive repetitions. For each representation, we compute normalized Leven-shtein similarity as 1 − 𝑑 Lev (𝑠 1, 𝑠 2)/ max (| 𝑠 1 |, |𝑠 2 |) and normalized longest-common-subsequence Beyond Task Completion: Measuring Interaction Cost in Terminal User Interfaces 11 

(LCS ) similarity as 2 |LCS (𝑠 1, 𝑠 2)|/(| 𝑠 1 | + | 𝑠 2 |) . Together, the two representations and two similarity measures yield four trajectory-similarity indicators. 

4.3.4 Statistical Analysis. We aggregate observations by task, using success proportions for com-pletion and medians for interaction costs and pairwise trajectory similarities. Correlation analyses use Spearman’s 𝜌 for pairwise task rankings and Kendall’s 𝑊 for agreement across configurations. We compute 95% percentile confidence intervals from 10,000 hierarchical bootstrap resamples at the applicable repository, task, participant, and agent-run levels [9, 19]. Each interval is reported as 

[𝐿, 𝑈 ], where 𝐿 and 𝑈 are its lower and upper bounds. 

4.4 Research Questions 

To systematically evaluate Agent-as-a-User, we formulate the following three research questions (RQs). They progress from human–agent correspondence, through the effect of observation on that correspondence, to the task-based usability of generated TUIs: 

RQ1. How do agent completion, the two interaction costs, and the resulting paths correspond to human interaction on the same tasks? 

RQ2. How do plain, semantic, and image observations affect that correspondence, given that humans see the rendered screen and the agent receives a discrete representation? 

RQ3. What is the task-based usability of TUIs generated by three leading coding agents, relative to the reference TUIs? 

5 Results 

Our analysis covers 3,528 agent attempts and 504 human first attempts from 6 participants across 84 tasks. To answer RQ1, we compare human and agent evaluations of the reference TUIs to identify similarities and differences in completion, the two Agent-KLM costs, and interaction paths. To answer RQ2, we examine how different visual observation encodings affect those measurements across five observation–evaluator configurations. To answer RQ3, we compare each reference TUI with its three independently generated counterparts using the same completion, interpretation, and operation measures. 

5.1 RQ1: Human–Agent Behavioral Relationship 

Success rate alone cannot establish human–agent usability correspondence. 6 Discussion 6.1 Case Study: How TUI Design Shapes Interaction Cost 

To illustrate more concretely how TUI design shapes interaction cost, Fig. 8 presents three rep-resentative paired cases from actual task executions. They cover information visibility, action discoverability, and outcome feedback, matching those in Fig. 1: blind scanning versus guided navi-gation, hidden affordances versus visible prompts, and silent failures versus immediate feedback. 

Information visibility concerns how clearly the interface presents task-relevant content and system status. P10/T07 asks for Redis ACL users and their rules. Both implementations complete all three runs, but one presents the requested accounts in a table without conflicting messages. The other displays the ACL state while a red unknown command message reports that an ACL operation failed. Mean interaction demand rises from 17.0 to 67.0 AKE and from 3.0k to 21.7k reasoning tokens. The visible failure makes otherwise readable information harder to trust and interpret.

6.2 Implications 

6.2.1 For Researchers. Our findings suggest three research directions: 

Evaluate usability through task execution. Functional correctness shows whether a required state can be reached, but not how the interface supports users in reaching it. This study begins with TUIs because they combine GUI-like visual states with CLI-like keyboard interaction that can be recorded and replayed. The same approach can be extended to web, mobile, desktop, and other interactive software when observations, actions, and outcomes can be captured and verified. This study provides an initial human calibration. An important next step is to test whether the observed relationships transfer across interface types, tasks, users, and evaluators. 

Broaden the evidence captured by automated evaluation. Agent-as-a-User currently pro-vides execution-grounded evidence of task completion and interaction demand. It does not directly capture subjective experiences such as satisfaction and confusion, learning over time, or accessibil-ity for users with different abilities. An important direction is to develop automated evidence for these aspects and determine where human evaluation remains necessary. 

Diagnose usability problems, not only score interfaces. A promising research direction is to develop methods that trace increases in interpretation or operation demand to specific interface states and interaction steps. These methods could classify causes such as poor visibility, discover-ability, or feedback and return actionable evidence to developers or coding agents. Such diagnosis could support iterative generation and revision while preserving task completion. 

6.2.2 For Developers. Our findings suggest two development practices: 

Make interaction quality an explicit acceptance criterion. Implementations with the same functional scope can differ substantially in completion and interaction demand. During requirements and acceptance testing, development teams can pair functional tests with representative tasks that specify what users must see, which actions must be discoverable, and how outcomes are confirmed. 18 Ruida Hu, Yuanhao Wang, Chao Peng, Yakun Zhang, and Cuiyun Gao 

They can then evaluate completion, interpretation, operation, and trajectory evidence together rather than accept an implementation based on its final state alone. 

Use execution-grounded evaluation to prevent usability regressions. All visual interfaces require usability evaluation. Agent-as-a-User provides quantitative task-execution evidence that can complement human evaluation for a more comprehensive assessment of usability. Once repre-sentative tasks, outcome checks, and baseline costs exist, development teams can rerun them to detect interaction regressions even when functional tests continue to pass. The same comparisons can rank candidate implementations in generated-software workflows. Together, automated checks and human evaluation can inform development and release decisions. 

6.3 Limitations and Future Work 

Human calibration. Our human study recruits six experienced terminal users and cannot cover all user populations. Nielsen and Landauer’s model suggests diminishing returns after the first few users in homogeneous formative usability studies [35]. Our six participants therefore provide an initial homogeneous calibration rather than population-wide validation. The main conclusions are further supported by hierarchical bootstrap inference with 95% confidence intervals from 10,000 resamples over repositories, tasks, participants, and agent runs, and the cost relationships recur across all five evaluator configurations. Validation with more diverse users and counterbalanced task orders remains future work. 

Measurement scope. Usability spans effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction for specified users, goals, and contexts [16], and no single measure captures it fully [15]. We focus on task-based usability, using task completion and interaction demand as reproducible execution evidence. This scope excludes subjective experience, learning, and accessibility. The evaluator experiments test a common paradigm rather than rank models. The keyboard agent is intentionally minimal, with fresh context and no external tools, skills, or complex context management. This design keeps the agent as closely aligned with a human user as possible and reduces the influence of other agent configurations. We also repeat each task three times and report five configurations separately. Objective measurement of additional usability dimensions remains future work. 

Benchmark scope. The 84 tasks from 15 repositories cannot cover all TUI domains and work-flows. This size follows from the screening, manual task design, and oracle validation in Section 3.1, which prioritize reproducible execution, reliable oracles, and recent repositories with less risk of pretraining overlap. Because task mining also had to remain reproducible, these tasks cannot cover every function of the original TUIs, which is unavoidable. Future work can utilize task-mining methods to expand the dataset. Moreover, this study is a first step toward execution-grounded measurement of task-based TUI usability. It can be applied to other interactive settings such as web and mobile applications. 

7 Related Work 7.1 Automated and Agent-Based Usability Evaluation 

KLM and GOMS predict skilled human performance by decomposing a specified method into elementary operators [6, 7, 22]. CogTool partially automates this analysis but still produces predicted execution time [23]. These models assume a largely error-free expert path and therefore do not capture exploration or recovery on a newly encountered interface. Heuristic inspection and earlier automation likewise complement participant studies rather than replace them [17, 34]. Recent LLM systems mainly simulate users, collect think-aloud reports, predict ratings, or score interfaces as judges [5, 11, 12, 14, 29, 30]. Questionnaires and preference studies similarly capture subjective experience rather than verified task execution. These approaches provide useful evidence, Beyond Task Completion: Measuring Interaction Cost in Terminal User Interfaces 19 

but they do not measure interaction cost from verified task executions. Agent-as-a-User instead proposes an execution-grounded paradigm in which a fixed evaluator executes predefined tasks, deterministic oracles verify completion, and Agent-KLM records interpretation and operation costs along the realized trajectory rather than a judged usability score. 

7.2 Evaluating Agents and Generated Interactive Software 

Computer-use and terminal benchmarks such as OSWorld, Terminal-Bench, and TUA-Bench eval-uate whether agents can complete tasks in real environments [8, 32, 42]. Their target construct is agent capability, and programmatic oracles separate traces from completion judgments [3]. Recent TUI testing work compares LLM-guided and random exploration for coverage and crash discovery [36]. Our oracles instead encode user goals, and Agent-KLM characterizes the interac-tion cost of reaching those goals. Generated-interface work such as AUI-Gym uses computer-use agents to judge newly generated GUIs by task solvability and navigation success, then redesigns those sites toward agent-native completion [27]. Agent success on a generated website is a de-sign objective, not a measurement of TUI interaction demand. Prior work therefore shows that agents can operate interfaces, generate UX feedback, and test interactive software. The remaining gap is a repeatable way to measure TUI interaction cost from verified task executions without an LLM usability score. TUINaut addresses this gap through actor-independent oracles, atomic keyboard traces, agent-native interpretation expenditure, human relationship characterization, and observation-representation sensitivity. 

8 Conclusion 

In this paper, we conduct an empirical study of Agent-as-a-User for measuring task-based TUI usability. We introduce Agent-KLM to separate interpretation and operation costs, and develop TUINaut and TUINaut-Bench to support reproducible evaluation. We evaluate them across 84 tasks, 15 reference TUIs, six human participants, five observation–evaluator configurations, and 45 generated counterparts. We find weak human–agent completion correspondence but aligned cost rankings, especially for operation. This pattern persists across observation settings. Generated TUIs can complete the required tasks, yet their task-based usability still needs improvement. These findings position Agent-as-a-User as a repeatable and execution-grounded paradigm for measuring task-based usability.
