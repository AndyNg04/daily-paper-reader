## ABSTRACT 

Artificial intelligence (AI) agents promise to extend what users can accomplish beyond their existing expertise, but they also raise the risk of producing plausible-looking work that users may struggle to evaluate. We ask how task-relevant expertise and trust in AI shape agent-assisted work. To isolate these factors, we conduct a think-aloud study in which 20 professionals with varying data-science

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

General-purpose AI agents built on large language models (LLMs) are moving beyond answering questions to carrying out multi-step work, for example, writing code, analyzing data, and creating presentations. As these agents take on more of a task, the human’s role shifts from doing the work to steering it: framing the problem, deciding what to delegate to an agent, and verifying what comes back [31]. This shift delivers on a central promise of generative AI in that it enables people to take on tasks beyond their existing expertise [12]. For example, a product manager can "vibe code" an interactive prototype by describing its desired behavior for an agent to help implement rather than relying entirely on an engineer to implement it [27]. More generally, an analysis of workplace ChatGPT use found that 16.8% of workplace ChatGPT use involves people taking on work outside their own occupation [45]. Yet widespread concern about “workslop”, i.e., AI-generated work that looks complete but is low-quality [28], raises questions about how effectively people can steer and evaluate AI-assisted work, particularly outside their domain of expertise. AI-assisted deliverables routinely contain fabricated citations [62], implausi-ble analyses [51], or factual errors [24]. This suggests that even when an artifact appears complete, workers may not understand it well enough to verify its quality, modify it, or carry it forward into subsequent work. The workslop concern is especially acute when working with agents because they are explicitly designed both to expand the range of tasks people can take on and to carry those tasks out with less oversight [16]. Contemporary agent interfaces increasingly make it easy for users to waive approvals, suppress interruptions, and cede oversight to the agent [1, 39]. Subsequently, users are encouraged to delegate work further beyond their exper-tise while also being nudged toward placing greater trust in an agent’s ability to execute that work correctly [65]. To design agents that extend what workers can accomplish with AI while reducing the risk of low-quality outputs, we need to un-derstand what shapes workers’ use of these systems, particularly when they work on unfamiliar tasks. We therefore ask: how do a worker’s expertise relative to a task and their trust in AI shape AI-assisted work? Our specific research questions are: RQ1 How do expertise and trust in AI influence AI-assisted 

work outcomes , including task completion and human un-derstanding? Existing datasets of human–agent in-teractions [3, 43] often capture trajectories and outputs, but not the human characteristics: we may know what someone did on a data-science task, for example, without knowing how much relevant expertise they brought to it. From these datasets, it is also difficult to isolate the effects of expertise and trust because tasks and agent setups may vary across users. Controlled experiments can hold these factors fixed, but often rely on short, bounded tasks rather than substantive end-to-end professional work [9, 61]. Studying these relationships therefore requires a common, substantive task and agent environment in which workers with different profiles can be observed throughout a work cycle. We address this challenge with an in-person think-aloud study in which 20 professionals — designers, product managers, soft-ware engineers, and data scientists — worked on a data-science take-home task previously used in hiring at a major U.S. software company. This design lets us define expertise relative to a common task, recruit participants spanning different levels of that exper-tise, and observe everyone working on the same problem with the same agentic tool. Crucially, the task is substantive enough that plausible-looking AI output can still contain fundamental errors, making both human steering and understanding consequential and observable. Specifically, the task asks participants to prototype an employee-attrition model using a dataset that deliberately lacks an attrition label, a limitation explicitly identified in the task re-quirement. Participants must construct a proxy label and reason about what the resulting analysis can legitimately demonstrate. By default (one-shot prompting), the AI agents introduce label leakage 

where pipelines construct the proxy label using the same variables subsequently used to predict it, yielding high accuracy without meaningful evidence of attrition prediction. For each participant, we use a survey to capture characteristics such as task-relevant expertise, and collect full interaction logs, screen and think-aloud recordings, their task solution, and a post-task interview. Together, these data let us examine how workers’ profiles and interaction pat-terns relate to the completeness and validity of their task solutions, as well as to their demonstrated understanding of the work. We found that experts more often decomposed the problem, in-vestigated assumptions, and steered the agent, whereas novices Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work 

more often delegated substantive decisions and struggled to eval-uate its output. Greater involvement was associated with better understanding but less complete notebooks, highlighting a tension between developing understanding and producing a finished arti-fact within the available time. Trust was associated with confidence, engagement, satisfaction, and cognitive load, but not with artifact quality. Participants who trusted the agent more felt more confident and engaged in the task, reported greater satisfaction, and experi-enced lower cognitive load. However, their artifacts were not rated more highly. We also found that what people said about delegation often differed from what they actually did. Stated willingness to delegate was only weakly related to how involved people remained during the task, whereas observed delegation was closely associated with task completion and understanding. Our results offer an empirical view of AI-assisted work among professionals completing a real-world task. We view our findings as hypothesis-generating and discuss their implications for model builders, end users, and assessment designers. In summary, this paper makes the following contributions: 

• A holistic framework with hypothesis-generating think-aloud data. • An in-depth analysis of workflows by expertise and trust. 

We provide mixed-methods (quantitative and qualitative) evi-dence on how expertise and trust jointly shape both the process 

and the outcomes of agentic work on a realistic task, identifying behaviors that predict success. 

• Interaction recipes, failure modes, and design implica-tions. We characterize the failure modes that distinguish work-ers by expertise and trust, and translate them into implications for design and practice for building and teaching people with varying levels of expertise to work with agents. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

We situate our work in three areas: (1) expertise in AI-assisted work, (2) trust and delegation comfort, and (3) prompting behaviors and interaction trajectories that shape AI use. In short, prior work either characterizes behavior without calibrated human characteristics like expertise and trust, or measures task outcomes without capturing impacts on humans using realistic tasks. Our study is designed to join all of these aspects (as shown in Figure 2). 

## 2.1 Expertise in AI-Assisted Work 

As LLMs continue to take on more complex work that requires different domain knowledge and skills, a lot of work has explored the relationship between expertise and effective use of AI in work. In customer-support, consulting, and coding tasks, prior work has found that LLMs reduce task completion time [46], increase qual-ity [14, 44], and could serve as a leveler , where less-experienced and lower-performing workers benefit more [8, 47]. On the other hand, there are studies that show LLMs as an amplifier for domain experts, which benefit experienced people more in terms of task performance [32] and productivity gains [13]. Meanwhile, some studies found LLMs to be a inhibitor for domain experts, showing that using AI slows down professionals in open-source develop-ment [4], reduces quality in long-horizon tasks [18] and LLM-hard tasks [14], and increases maintenance and review burdens dispro-portionately on experts [64]. Interestingly, Becker et al. [4] also found a perceived-versus-objective productivity gap, as developers perceived a 20% speedup, while AI actually led to a 20% slowdown. Prior work also surfaced potential moderator variables, such as time (e.g., limited time could make LLMs a leveler that compresses skill differences, versus unlimited time making LLMs an amplifier that benefits experts more [32]). However, these studies focus on task outcomes such as productivity and quality, leaving out the important impact that human-AI interaction has on humans with different levels of experience [34]. Moreover, there is no unified framework that describes those studies’ setup, task characteristics, user populations, interaction behaviors, and target outcomes to explain the different results. Our work addresses this gap by exam-ining how workers with different expertise interact with the same agent on the same realistic task, and explores different outcomes including task completion, validity, human understanding, and hu-man perception, all described in a unified framework (Figure 2). 

## 2.2 Trust and Delegation in AI-Assisted Work 

Beyond expertise, the impact of users’ trust in AI is also widely explored, especially in AI-assisted decision-making literature [50]. Calibrated trust is important to human-AI collaboration, as over-reliance can produce harmful outcomes, but underreliance can be unproductive [29]. Some prior work showed that calibrating human self-confidence enhances human-AI team performance and encour-ages more rational reliance and aligned delegation on AI [35, 49], and subjective trust in AI is a primary driver of delegation [6]. However, there is also empirical evidence that trust may not in-form users’ appropriate reliance on AI systems [23], and reported trust and actual reliance can diverge [55]. For example, a worker might trust that an agent is highly accurate yet still prefer not to delegate because the task is personally meaningful or high-stakes, or the worker values authorship [26]. Recent work found that self-rated delegation comfort contributes to human–agent collaboration skill more than trust [52], but how these affective factors influence human-agent collaborative work outcomes is still unclear. Much of the prior literature focused on exploring the relation-ship between trust (an affective attitude) and reliance (a behavior) [25] in decision-making tasks such as image classification or del-egation tasks such as operating autonomous vehicles [63]. Many interventions have also been developed, primarily targeting simple task outcomes (e.g., accuracy), affective outcomes (e.g., calibrated trust), or behavioral outcomes (e.g., calibrated reliance) [10, 50]. As modern LLM-based agents are designed to be more autonomous and can perform much more complex tasks in knowledge work, how human trust impacts delegation and leads to diverse work outcomes needs further examination [40]. We aim to address this gap by exploring the role of trust and reliance in complicated AI-assisted knowledge work tasks using generative agents. In our study, by collecting human characteristics, interactions, and work outcomes, we aim to explore how factors Ma, et al. 

like trust influence delegation behaviors and how that shapes the downstream task performance and human experiences. 

## 2.3 Prompting Behaviors and Human-Agent Interactions 

A growing body of work analyzes how people interact with LLMs across multi-turn interactions and how different interactions or prompting behaviors might impact task outcomes. From open-source programming data [3, 18, 58], writing in the wild [43], to specifically collected datasets from courses [19, 30, 38], quantita-tive user studies [42, 54], and qualitative lab studies [41, 66], many studies have attempted to answer the question: what interaction behaviors lead to good outcomes? While most work operationalized good outcomes as task success, performance, and productivity [52], some explored the human aspects, such as improving learning [33, 60] and reducing overreliance [29]. These works often attempted to surface taxonomies to describe effective vs. ineffective behaviors with regard to their target out-comes. For example, defining the audience, communicating tone and style, and providing examples might contribute to session suc-cess [52]; students asking for explanation or guidance during AI interaction (adaptive help-seeking) achieved higher exam grades than students who simply asked AI to produce the full solution [60]; and behavioral indicators such as careful task comprehension and fine-grained navigation lead to low overreliance [29]. Together, prior work provides useful descriptions of interaction behavior, but few studies provide the full picture of how users with differ-ent characteristics can shape different interaction trajectories, and subsequently influence diverse work outcomes. We propose a framework (Figure 2) to situate our work within it and more clearly describe the full story. We address this gap by connecting users’ cognitive and affective profiles to their interaction trajectories and to separate measures of task completion, artifact validity, and understanding. 

## 3 METHOD 

To study how expertise and trust shape AI-assisted work, we need to jointly capture, under the same agent and task environment setup, who the worker is, how humans interact with the agent, and 

how good the outcome is in terms of both task completion, validity, and human factors such as understanding and stress (Figure 2). Our framework (Figure 2) mirrors prior human-AI interaction models in the relevant factors such as human/AI and context/task characteristics [34, 36, 57]. To organize different human character-istics , we draw on educational and psychological taxonomies that distinguish cognitive and affective dimensions [7]. We measure domain expertise as cognitive factors, and trust and comfort with delegation as affective factors. We further distinguish character-istics measured before the task from task-dependent experiences, such as cognitive load, engagement, and understanding, which are 

human outcomes (human cognitive and affective states) beyond task quality and efficiency. In this work, we conduct a controlled, in-person think-aloud study (Section 3.1) with 20 professionals (Section 3.4) that cap-tures full end-to-end interaction trajectories together with rich context and outcome measures (Section 3.3). This data uniquely joins human profiles (Section 3.4.1), interactions, and human and task outcomes, which enables rich quantitative and qualitative anal-ysis to answer our research questions (Section 4). Our think-aloud study was designed to explore how cognitive and affective human factors shape behavior and outcomes, and we leave large-scale confirmatory studies for future work (Section 5.3). 

## 3.1 Study Procedure 

Each session lasted approximately two hours, one-on-one with an author, and proceeded through six segments: (1) a setup and onboarding (∼20 min); (2) a pre-session survey (∼10 min) capturing background, domain and AI-use skill, and affective measures; (3) the main task (30 min), an AI-assisted data-analysis task; (4) two 

subtasks (30 min) performed without AI; (5) a post-session survey 

(∼10 min) capturing cognitive load, understanding, engagement, confidence, and reflection; and (6) a debrief (∼10 min) with a short semi-structured interview. For the main task, participants worked in VS Code with GitHub Copilot in agent mode . To hold the model constant across partici-pants, we fixed the underlying model to claude-sonnet-4-6 . The full log was exported from Copilot and uploaded with the artifacts by the participants at the end of the study. 

## 3.2 Task Design 

The main task was adopted from a real take-home assessment for a data scientist role from the organization, which allowed multiple days for interviewees without using AI. This is a task where neither a human nor a model alone can adequately complete it within 30 minutes, and we limit the time on task to simulate time-constrained scenarios that often happen in real work, and also to observe what workers will prioritize under time constraints. The main task asked participants to act on a realistic, intention-ally under-specified product request: design and build a Minimum Viable Product employee attrition model that predicts whether an employee leaves the company or not using a workplace-analytics dataset 1 to inform whether the feature merits engineering invest-ment. Participants were asked to produce a Jupyter notebook con-taining their recommended modeling approach and rationale, end-to-end code, example outputs (predictions, feature importance, eval-uation metrics), and a discussion of production considerations. 2

The task was deliberately designed to require handling ambi-guity : the dataset lacks a real attrition label (i.e., whether the employee left the company or not), so the dependent variable must be engineered or simulated. The task brief explicitly called out this dataset limitation, and we specifically look for participants’ understanding of this key gap after task completion. We chose an open-ended, low-verifiability task precisely because such tasks are where human interaction and human judgment are expected to matter most. ## 3.3 Measures 

As we fixed the task and agent used in our study, we collected four classes of measures corresponding to the framework (Figure 2, sum-marized in Table 1): human characteristics (from the pre-survey), interaction measures (from logs and think-aloud), task outcomes (from artifacts), and human outcomes (from post-survey and post-interview). Some survey measures were adopted from prior work [32, 52], and full surveys are in Section B.2. 

Human characteristics (cognitive and affective traits) . Ex-pertise is measured with self-report Likert scales for domain/data-science skills, including years of experience, frequency of use in work, and task-specific confidence (summarized in Section 3.4.1). The pre-survey also captures affective factors, i.e., reliance on AI, trust in AI outputs, comfort delegating (including high-stakes tasks),

Interaction process . From the logs, recordings, and think-aloud transcripts, we collect full prompt-response trajectories and interac-tion metadata (number of turns, revision count, time per step, agent plans and traces). We manually coded these trajectories. To surface the workflows and usage strategies that emerged, we inductively coded the trajectories. And separately, to classify the extent of ob-served delegation, we deductively coded each session according to the five levels of the human-agent delegation scale [15, 53]. 

Inductive: workflow characterization. To understand how partici-pants worked with agents, two authors open-coded the think-aloud transcripts, screen recordings, and prompt trajectories [56]. We fixed only the workflow phases ( plan , implement , review , outcome )and let the strategy within each phase emerge from the sessions, yielding codes such as copy-paste versus explore and decompose at planning, and surface review versus critical guidance at review. The two authors coded all 20 sessions independently, reached strong agreement after constructing the codebook (Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.84) [37], and then resolved every disagreement through discussion. Follow-ing common practice for interpretive qualitative analysis, we report agreement as an indicator of coding stability and do not treat these codes as measurements to be tested statistically. 

Deductive: observed delegation level (H1–H5). To understand how much humans actually delegate tasks to agents during the inter-action, two authors deductively coded each session on a five-level human-agent delegation scale capturing how much of the decision the human retained [15, 53]. The annotation achieved substantial agreement (Cohen’s quadratic-weighted 𝜅 = 0.65), and disagree-ment was resolved via discussions. Participants with annotated H ≥ 3 (H1 autonomous agent → H5 full human involvement) are referred to as hands-on and the rest hands-off in this paper. The same H1–H5 scale is administered as a single self-report item pre-and post-session, which lets us compare stated against observed 

delegation on one scale (Section 4.2.2). 

Task outcome . For the main-task notebook, as polished-looking notebooks are not always well-reasoned, we measure outcomes on two axes: completeness and validity . We score each notebook against a fixed rubric with explicit no-/partial-/full-credit anchors per item (0 /1/2 points each). Completeness covers the task’s four required outputs: a stated algorithm recommendation with ratio-nale, executed end-to-end code, example outputs, and a production discussion (4 items, /8). Validity covers ambiguity and common mis-takes in data processing (explicitly address the missing label), label construction (reasonably simulate attrition), modeling (avoid label leakage), and evaluation (avoid test set leakage) stages (4 items, /8). Any validity item may instead be marked N/A when the condition it assesses never arose—for instance, model and evaluation cannot be assessed in a notebook that never trained a model, so validity is reported as a proportion of each notebook’s applicable maximum. Grading was performed by two members of the research team with data science expertise. To support this process, we provided the graders with an LLM-based grading aid (OpenAI gpt-5.6-terra )that produced a preliminary score, a justification, and citations to the notebook cells used as evidence. Human outcome . ## 3.5 Data Analysis 

Because both completeness and validity task outcome axes are bounded sums of 0 /1/2 items with pronounced floor and ceil-ing clustering, and Shapiro–Wilk rejects normality, we use non-parametric rank methods throughout: Spearman’s 𝜌 for association, Mann–Whitney 𝑈 for two-group comparisons, and reported with the rank-biserial correlation 𝑟 as the effect size [5]. Three notebooks scored 0 /8 on completeness are dropped from validity analysis (3/4 or 4/4 NA validity items), which measures the absence of an artifact rather than poor reasoning. Two of these par-ticipants did not get to plan the solution and are thus dropped from understanding analyses (4/5 NA understanding items). Therefore, the final samples are 𝑁 = 20 for completeness, 𝑁 = 17 for validity, and 𝑁 = 18 for understanding.

## 4.1 RQ1: Experts understand but may not finish; trust shapes the experience, not the artifact 

4.1.1 Expertise showed up in whether the work was right and under-stood, not whether it was finished. When we graded the notebooks participants produced in terms of completeness and validity (Fig-ure 5), we observe that expertise is weakly positively associated with notebook validity and human understanding, and negatively associated with completeness; however, expertise is not clearly distinguishable from the notebook artifact. Further analyzing each rubric item (Figure 6), we see that novices’ notebooks are more complete than experts’ (by 14.6% on average), especially in terms of execution and outputs. Only 2 of 8 experts produced a fully executed pipeline and example outputs, compared to half of novices (6/12). Among the four notebook validity rubric items, the overall validity gap is 9% (experts 59% - novices 50%), so experts present artifacts that appear more valid in data processing, model construction, and evaluation aspects. In terms of human understanding, in-domain experts averaged more than twice the understanding of novices ( 𝑀 = 51%, 𝑆𝐷 = 44% vs. 𝑀 = 22%, 𝑆𝐷 = 21%; Cohen’s 𝑑 = +0.86). Realizing missing label is the least differentiating question, which is expected because this information is directly provided in the task brief. However, there are still 3/8 experts and 5/12 novices who could not answer whether the dataset has the attrition label or not. 

Expertise raised the ceiling on understanding, but not the floor . While some experts achieved the highest understanding scores in the study, three of eight scored zero (as shown in Figure 7a). Low-understanding experts were very hands-off when using AI; for example, P16 fully handed the work to three subagents and two loops, chose not to examine any intermediate thinking traces, and ran out of time before they could review the final output. In contrast, novices clustered at the lower end of the distribution, with none scoring above 60%. For example, no novice realized circularity in the model they implemented, while 2 experts did. Similarly, more than half of the experts (4/7) could explain the features their model used, while only 1 of 10 novices was able to do the same. Taken together, in terms of the human understanding outcome, expertise seems necessary but not sufficient: it raised the ceiling on understanding without raising the floor. Note that there can also be different types of understanding that the workflow influ-ences differently, such as implementation understanding, dataset understanding, interpretation understanding, etc. We focus on im-plementation understanding in our study, and future work can explore more types of understandings’ impact (Section 5.3). 

The diligence tax: completion and understanding are in-versely stocked . From the think-alouds, we can observe that hu-man understanding and task completion are often two objectives produced by different workflows (Section 4.2.1) and trade off with each other. A staggering case is novice designers P3 and P13, who achieved 100% coverage in their notebook but walked away with 0% post-task understanding, which means that they used AI to produce a complete notebook, but they did not realize the missing label in the dataset and could not explain how their model was constructed. Splitting participants at the midpoint of the understanding score yields seven high-understanding and eleven low-understanding par-ticipants (Figure 7a). We can observe that high-understanding par-ticipants produced notebooks that were less than half as com-plete (𝑀 = 30 .4% , 𝑆𝐷 = 36 .0) as those of low-understanding par-ticipants ( 𝑀 = 76 .1% , 𝑆𝐷 = 29 .3); Mann-Whitney U 𝑝 = .015, rank-biserial 𝑟 = −0.69 (Figure 7b). On validity, the difference runs the other way but is not significant (high-understanding 62% > low-understanding 51%). 

Why understanding distinctly matters . Participants them-selves treated understanding as an outcome distinct from comple-tion, for three reasons. The first is responsibility : P13 separated the two axes explicitly — “it’s not very hard to complete the task. To complete the task well is a different question entirely” — and P17 tied it to stakes, “if a task is not well-defined and I need to present the result to leadership and be responsible for it, I would not just one-shot it, in which case I won’t understand what that AI did” . P14 described their deliberate counter-effort, “I’ve also been trying to keep myself honest and research what I don’t know so I can own my work output” .The second is the downstream cognitive cost of the absence of understanding, such as in comprehension and debugging. P9 named the mechanism by which comprehension and debugging fail under delegation: “if it generates a lot of code at once, then it becomes difficult for me to go through it and understand, not to mention fix its mistakes” . P20 similarly named the cost of not having built the notebook themselves: “when you’re doing it yourself from the get-go, it’s much easier. . . If you use AI to build the whole process, then it’s difficult for me to go back. It’s actually a friction. . . If I had done the feature exploration myself, this wouldn’t overwhelm me as much, because I would immediately know what I need to look at.”

4.3.2 If the target is validity: domain judgment or careful review needed, reading does not suffice. As shown in Table 4, the moves that work for validity are explore & decompose at planning (achieving 65% validity in the final artifact), manual steering at implementation (62%), and critical guidance at review (62%). Note that reading behaviors, although showing engagement, is not critical interrogation and may create a false sense of fluency and leave mistakes or misunderstandings undiscovered in surface-level reviews, as also highlighted in prior research [11]. In our study, P19 narrated “I don’t understand it and cannot really pick out issues, but I will keep reading” . P3 also tried to read line-by-line and narrated most of the agent response. Both P19 and P3 ended with a low validity score of 38%. When the target is validity, people can fail to apply judgments: novices may not be able to apply domain-related supervision, but even experts may miss nuanced model errors when they need careful checking. For example, 11 of the 15 submitted notebooks with implemented models contained label leakage, and none of those authors (4 experts, 7 novices) noticed. Only two participants noticed or explicitly avoided leakage at all in their understanding questions, P20 and P10: both are domain experts and data scientists themselves. This label leakage failure is hard to capture, as it needs true understanding of the input and output variables, or noticing the superficially high model accuracy and carefully checking the label and feature construction. This failure detection behavior reproduces in the human-only subtasks, where no agent was involved. As shown in Figure 12, most defects asserted in the text of the artifact were raised by more than half of the participants; for example, the error to filter out non-adopters of Copilot from the analysis was caught by 70% of participants. On the contrary, defects that require checking a claim against its evidence were raised by almost nobody. For example, "null result never stated" reported near-zero correlations of -0.005, but the interpretation text instead describes what a negative correla-tion would mean. None of the participants caught this error buried in the output that requires checking claims against the evidence. 

4.3.3 If the target is understanding: work incrementally and ask ques-tions. All seven understanding outcomes come from the question-asking (both during planning and review) or critical-guidance paths (Table 4). Two participants describe the technique to build under-standing incrementally. P10 framed incremental complexity as a human requirement rather than a model one: “it’s more kind of for the human interpretability to start from light and then incrementally [increase complexity]. . . now you can give the agent more freedom, and it can do it as complex as needed” . P9 described decomposition for the same purpose — “the agent performs better if you give it the task step by step instead of giving everything at once. And then if I do it step by step, then I will be able to understand what it’s doing” —with the failure mode it avoids stated directly: “if it generates a lot of code at once, then it becomes difficult for me to go through it and understand” . P20 also adopted a simplicity principle to produce that understanding: “I’m gonna apply the Occam’s razor principle here and just go with the most straightforward signal. . . this strategy has worked best for me to also keep track of what’s going on, consistently” .Moreover, sessions ending in human understanding ( 𝑛 = 7) aver-age 23% completeness and 62% validity, and also participants land-ing in the understanding are mostly experts (5/7, 71%). Strategies for human understanding could cost completeness (Section 4.1.1), and under a tight timeline, it can be very hard for novices to develop appropriate understanding. For example, P17 (a novice designer) spent the session asking substantive conceptual questions such as what right-censoring means for an attrition label, but could not recall the specifics afterwards. Our study measures understanding as short-term recall, but long-term memory and learning can take longer time or repetitions to develop (Section 5.1). Question-asking may buy comprehension of the artifact at hand; but it does not, in one session, buy the domain knowledge that would let a novice build the next one. 

## 5 DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK 

Our observations connect to how people work with code-generating agents, to how we should assess that work, and to how tools might adapt to the person behind the prompt. 

## 5.1 Slop and Sink vs. Stop and Think: Fast and Slow Thinking in AI-assisted Work 

Our results suggest one pathway through which AI-assisted work can become workslop [28]: delegation can make the artifact ad-vance faster than the worker’s ability to understand and verify it. Task-relative expertise changes this risk, but does not eliminate it. Novices were especially vulnerable because they more often adopted completion-oriented workflows, delegating substantive decisions and reviewing the resulting artifact at the surface level. Yet experts could also end the task with little understanding when they delegated heavily or postponed inspection and underestimated agent-running time (Section 4.1.1). This perspective extends prior work’s distinction between ac-celeration and exploration on AI-assisted programming [2] and resonates with the fast and slow thinking literature [21]. The fast Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work 

workflow accelerates throughput, while the slow workflow, which includes decomposing the problem, questioning assumptions, and steering the agent, helps explore and build understanding (Sec-tion 4.3.3). Our study suggests that problems arise when acceler-ation becomes the default even when the worker lacks the task-relative expertise needed to slow down and supervise the result. Our data-analysis task, being open-ended and under-specified, enabled both fast and slow thinking, but not everyone could take up the slow strategy . Higher-expertise participants could interrogate the agent’s assumptions, probe the missing-label ambiguity, and use the interaction to refine what the analysis should even be. Lower-expertise participants (even with low trust in AI) could be confined to acceleration with agents: they could drive the agent toward a complete-looking artifact, but lacked the domain knowledge to explore or verify. We expected high-trust users to delegate to AI and accelerate, skipping exploration. But participants who reported high trust could revert to iterative exploration (especially experts) when the task was unfamiliar. Participants strongly believe understanding matters for downstream tasks, and even high-trust participants are unwilling to trade understanding for task completion under time constraints. As the agent rapidly expands the artifact, the amount of work the human must later understand and check also accumulates. Under bounded time, users may never catch up, leaving them with output that is easy to hand off but difficult to own. In this sense, an unpaid “diligence tax" might turn into a “verification debt" in the long run [64]. Whether “slow is fast” in the long run, i.e., whether the understanding built now pays off in speed and quality later, is a question left for future work (Section 5.3), and it connects to a longer line of work on the trade-off between speed, quality, and learning in AI-assisted work [18]. 

Implications for end-users: building expertise needs a nec-essarily slower workflow . The practical advice for a worker whose goal is to grow, or who will have to defend the result, is to counteract the desire to automate: deliberately slow down , iterate, ask, and verify rather than accept a finished file. Genuine upskilling requires sustained, active engagement, such as building with the method repeatedly, or focused training. Incidental explanation that accompanies a one-off task completion may just result in superficial parsing without understanding. For novices in unfamiliar task do-mains, developing understanding is harder and slower. Within our 30-minute task, even novices who iteratively engaged in question-asking with the AI could fail to develop durable understanding and were unable to recall the method post-task. Humans need repeated exposure to learn new concepts (e.g., seven practice opportuni-ties to master a knowledge component [22]), and according to our participants, learning new skills often takes days or weeks (e.g., learning GitHub commands as a designer). 

## 5.2 Look Beyond Artifacts: Building Human Profiles from Interaction 

In our study (Section 4.2), we observe how expertise and trust shaped how people prompted, including which task dimensions they steered, where they questioned assumptions and made critiques, and what they left to defaults; interaction traces carry a readable signal of the worker’s profile. For example, in our study’s human-only subtasks, designers asked why, challenged the problem space, and focused on visuals; PMs asked about the deliverable and its audience; data scientists and software engineers went straight for the method. A profile inferred from behavior could therefore be quite fine-grained, distinguishing not just expert from novice but the axis of a person’s expertise. Current models’ and tools’ default assumption is that the human is a capable supervisor or delegator who wants the whole task com-pleted with minimal interruption [20]. However, this assumption can fail to support novices when they need more scaffolding, or even for experts to properly supervise agent work. In our study, out of all 15 participants with a model implemented (9 novices and 6 experts), 78% of novices and 67% of experts fail to realize the silent label circularity error buried in a complete-looking notebook (Section 4.3.2). A system optimized to hand over the whole output as fast as possible is, targeting task completion rather than human understanding, can be actively counterproductive: it maximizes the surface area for a silent error the user is least equipped to catch. 

Implications for tool builders: infer expertise and reliance, and design for human oversight . 0As a worker’s expertise is legible in how they interact (Section 4.2), an agent can in principle sense, on the fly, whether it is working with an expert or a novice, and it should not respond agnostically. This opens a design space for profile-aware support: tools that infer a user’s expertise and disposition from their behavior and tailor scaffolding accordingly, for example, by surfacing the judgment calls a novice is skipping, suggesting the next question an expert would ask, or recommending tasks and prompts matched to the outcome the person is trying to reach. Such adaptation could help calibrate delegation, nudging over-delegating novices toward verification. To design for human understanding and supervision in the loop, less is more: people need smaller increments, mandatory check-ins, and automatically triggered self-verification loops (or a foregrounded “what to check” surface) that do not assume the human will audit what comes back. Additionally, not all human characteristics are equally inferable from interaction traces. Expertise was relatively legible in our study through behaviors such as decomposition, methodological steering, and critical review (Section 4.2.1). Trust, as an attitude, actually diverges from delegation reliance, which is the observed behav-ior (Section 4.2.2). For example, a trusting expert may choose to delegate less and manually steer a lot when facing an unfamiliar task; a skeptical novice may have to delegate more than they prefer because they do not have the domain knowledge to provide any useful oversight. For adaptive systems, this suggests that a system may be better positioned to infer whether a user is currently behaving in a hands-on or hands-off way, and whether their behavior exhibits task-relevant expertise, than to classify them as inherently “trusting” or “skeptical.” A hands-off expert and a hands-off novice may need different support, just as two equally trusting users may behave very differently depending on their task context, task familiarity, and expertise. Profile-aware agents should therefore condition support on observable interaction behavior and task-relative expertise. 

Implication for assessment makers: look at the interaction and human, not just the artifact . Our results also have direct Ma, et al. 

implications for anyone using AI-assisted work products to assess people, instructors making take-home assignments, employers de-signing interview tasks, etc. As agent capacities keep improving, AI will increasingly close the gap in surface completion; the discrim-inating signal moves to what the worker understands and more nuanced quality: whether humans can justify their choices, answer follow-up questions, recognize what is missing or wrong, and ex-tend the work. Assessment should therefore collect beyond the artifact to probe expertise and understanding directly, e.g., from interaction logs and a short defense or a live discussion. 

## 5.3 Limitations and Future Work 

These are hypothesis-generating findings from a small ( 𝑁 = 20), single-organization sample performing one data-analysis task with one agent under a time limit, and our expertise and trust measures are self-reported (Section 3.4.1). The patterns we describe should be read as directional and grounded in qualitative evidence rather than as confirmed effects. As the 30-minute main task left little room for extended plan iteration or output critique, the human-only subtasks cannot be validated as proxies for main-task interaction or outcomes. A natural next step is a follow-up in which participants continue the task offline and prepare to defend it in a stakeholder discussion, logging their interactions again, so that exploration, verification, and understanding can be observed over a longer horizon. Addi-tionally, there can be different types of understanding developed during different stages of the workflow, such as understanding of the problem space, chosen methods, or calculated results. Future work could dive deeper into how different understandings trade off with task outcomes and are shaped by the interactions. Future work should also observe iteration and evaluation behaviors that a short lab session cannot elicit and to test whether isolated-subtask skill predicts full-task performance at larger scale. Establishing which behaviors drive outcomes, and whether profile-aware scaffolding actually helps people work or learn better, will require larger, longer, and more varied studies. Our study provides a hypothesis-generating exploration of the impact of expertise and trust factors on AI-assisted work task and human outcomes. To better map out the whole landscape of AI-assisted work inputs and outputs (as portrayed in Figure 2), future reviews or empirical studies could systematically vary agent and task characteristics and measure other task, human, and agent outcomes. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we set out to examine how a worker’s expertise and their trust in AI shape agent-assisted work, and we instan-tiated a framework linking human profiles, interaction, and out-comes in a think-aloud study of 20 professionals on a realistic data-analysis task. We observed that expertise mattered less for a finished, complete-looking artifact than for whether the result was valid and whether the worker could understand and defend it. Experts and novices worked in legibly different ways. Experts decomposed plans, steered the agent, and interrogated its output, while novices pasted and accepted what came back. Those work-flows bought different things, as the path to understanding is dis-tinct from the path to coverage. Our study and analysis open up interesting questions to further explore human factors and outcome variables in AI-assisted work. We believe that future tools and as-sessments should be built to better serve diverse human expertise and targeted needs.

This is a research study task. You are not being evaluated—there are no right or wrong answers. We are interested in how you approach and work through the task using AI assistance. Please think out loud as you work. Narrate your reasoning, decisions, and any confusion. Your think-aloud is recorded and transcribed as part of the study. 

Background 

This task is inspired by a real-world data science request. It is intentionally open-ended and underspecified in some areas—handling ambiguity is part of the task. You are expected to identify gaps, ambiguities, and make reasonable assumptions where needed. 

Task 

You have been tasked with a PM request to design and create an MVP employee attrition model using Viva Insights dataset. The goal is to build a prototype that will inform whether this feature is worth investing engineering resources. The PM is interested in understanding: 

• If we were to create an employee attrition model, what would this look like? 

• What would be the inputs and outputs of the model? 

• What factors should we take into account when thinking about bringing this model into production? 

Data 

A starter sample Person Query dataset ( pq_data ) is available via the vivainsights Python package (installed via: pip install vivainsights ) if you haven’t. 

Note: This dataset is known to be incomplete. An attrition label column is missing and will need to be engineered or simulated— identifying and addressing this gap is part of the task.

## B.1 Human-Profile Factor Composition 

The human-profile factors (Section 3.4.1) are each computed as the mean of their 𝑧 -scored constituent items. Items are ordinally encoded (Section B). These factors are internally reliable (Table 2). Cronbach’s 𝛼 ≥ 0.80 is considered reliable, while mean inter-item correlations 

> 0.2 indicate items are representative of the same content domain; > 0.4 means some items could be redundant [48, 59]. While in-domain expertise and delegation trust are moderately correlated ( 𝑟 = 0.48)—skill and willingness-to-delegate are related but not interchangeable. The exact item membership is: 

• In-domain expertise (28 items): for each of the seven skill areas (Section B), four items— years of experience (yrs ), frequency of performing the skill without AI (freqNoAI ), self-rated confidence in one’s own ability (Hconf ), and frequency of using AI to perform the skill (freqAI )— giving 7 × 4 = 28 items. In pre-survey terms, these are the “years of experience in each skill area,” “how frequently do you use each skill,” “how confident are you in your own ability,” and “how frequently do you use AI to perform each task” matrices. Impact of Expertise and Trust on AI-assisted Work 

• AI-delegation trust (4 items): trust in AI for data-science tasks (att_trust_ai_dstask ; “I trust GenAI/LLMs to produce reliable outputs for data analysis tasks”); trust without oversight (att_trust_ai_nooversight ; “How much do you trust AI agents to execute tasks accurately without human oversight?”); comfort delegating data-science tasks (att_delegate_ds ; “I am comfortable delegating data analysis tasks to an AI agent”); and comfort delegating high-stakes tasks (att_delegate_highstakes ; “How comfortable are you delegating high-stakes professional tasks to an AI agent?”).

## C ANNOTATION, GRADING, AND VERIFICATION DETAILS 

This appendix documents how the derived analysis variables were produced. The procedures differ in kind: the expertise ×trust archetypes 

are computed deterministically from the survey factors (Section 3.4.1); the notebook outcome grades (completeness ×validity) are produced with an LLM and then verified by two authors; and the workflow codes and observed-delegation levels are coded directly by two authors from the sessions (Section 3.3). Section C.1 reports the inter-rater reliability, and all three are human-verified and reconciled. 

Model and parameters . All LLM-based annotation and grading used a single Azure OpenAI deployment, queried through the chat-completions API. Table 6 lists the settings. We did not set a sampling temperature (the deployment’s default was used); rather than rely on sampling determinism, we constrained the output to a JSON schema, anchored every rubric item to explicit scoring criteria, and had human annotators verify the results (Section C.1). 

Table 6: LLM annotation/grading configuration.         

> Setting Value Provider / API Azure OpenAI, chat completions Deployment (model) gpt-5.6-terra
> API version 2024-12-01-preview
> Sampling temperature not set (deployment default) Response format json_object (schema-constrained) Calls one completion per notebook / per annotation unit; cached per participant

Workflow codes and observed-delegation levels . The workflow characterization and the H1–H5 observed-delegation level are coded by two authors directly from the think-aloud transcripts, screen recordings, and prompt trajectories, not by the model; the procedure and its reliability are described in Section 3.3 and Section C.1. The LLM deployment below is used only for the notebook outcome grades. 

Main-task notebook grading . Each notebook is graded in a single chat completion per notebook. The grading instrument is the completeness × validity rubric of Table 7 (eight items, each scored 0/1/2). For every item, the model must return a structured score, a 1–2 sentence justification, and the notebook cell numbers used as evidence. Two authors then split and verified every grade against the notebooks, each covering half the sample (10 notebooks, 80 item scores), correcting the model where it made a mistake. All completeness and validity results in the paper use these human-verified scores; the model scores are retained only for the agreement/accuracy analysis in Section C.1. The system prompt is reproduced below (the study brief and rubric JSON are interpolated where indicated).

## C.1 Inter-Rater Reliability and Human Verification 

Three of the paper’s analyses reported human verification: the completeness ×validity notebook grades, the holistic observed-delegation level, and the four-phase workflow codes. We report the agreement below. 

Completeness and validity (human-verified scores) . Two authors split the 20 notebooks (10 each) and independently verified LLM scores all eight rubric items against Table 7, giving 160 item scores. Compared against the LLM’s scores on the same 160 items, exact agreement is 90 .6% (completeness 91 .2%, validity 90 .0%); the quadratic-weighted 𝜅 is 0 .88, and the mean absolute difference is 0 .12. Disagreements are small and mildly optimistic on the model’s side of the 144 numeric (non-NA items): LLM scored higher than human on 10, lower on 4, and identical on the remaining 130. 

Observed delegation level (H1–H5) . The holistic delegation level annotated on each session (the observed axis of Figure 10 and the correlations in Section 4) was coded independently by two authors for all 20 participants. On the ordinal H1–H5 scale, exact agreement was 50% and within-one-level agreement 100% (quadratic-weighted 𝜅 = 0.65)—disagreements were never more than one adjacent level. The two coders then adjudicated every disagreement to a single reconciled level, and all reported observed-delegation results use the reconciled levels. 

Workflow strategy codes . The four-phase workflow codes behind the Sankey (Figure 9) and the strategy-outcome table (Table 4) were coded independently by two authors for all 20 participants. Over the four phases (80 decisions), agreement was 91 .2% with Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.84. By phase, agreement was 85% ( 𝜅 = 0.77) for plan, 90% ( 𝜅 = 0.78) for implement, 100% ( 𝜅 = 1.0) for review, and 90% ( 𝜅 = 0.81) for outcome. Disagreements were resolved to a single agreed code, which the analysis uses. Ma, et al.

n/a: only when the notebook contains no engagement with the data at all (empty or abandoned before any inspection). 

label_validity 

(validity) Constructs a justified proxy/engineered label: be-haviorally plausible assumptions grounded in actual dataset columns (e.g., high after-hours load or low col-laboration raising risk); randomness/noise rather than a purely deterministic rule; a plausible base rate for the stated horizon (roughly 5–20%). 

0: no label constructed; purely random assignment with no behavioral grounding; or nonsensical/inverted assumptions with no justification. 

1: label built but purely deterministic (a model can re-derive it exactly), weakly justified assumptions, or a base rate outside the 5–20% range without justification. 

2: plausible column-grounded assumptions, with noise where a deterministic rule would otherwise be exactly recoverable, and a base rate in roughly the 5–20% range. 

n/a: only when the notebook never attempted to construct or simulate a label (deciding not to build one and saying why is a scoreable choice, not n/a). 

model_validity 

(validity) Avoids or squarely addresses leakage and circularity: does not present a model trained on a label derived from its own features as having learned real attrition, and excludes or flags features that trivially encode the label. Dropping a composite risk score while keeping the features it was computed from does not count; temporal separation (training on prior weeks to predict a held-out later week) is sound and is not penalized. 

0: circular setup presented as a real result—the label is derived from the same-period features the model is given (including dropping the composite score but keeping its direct inputs). 

1: circularity acknowledged but near-perfect metrics still framed as meaningful, or only partial feature hygiene. 

2: explicitly managed: excludes/flags label-defining features, and/or separates the independent and depen-dent variables by time. 

n/a: only when the notebook never trained a model. 

eval_validity 

(validity) Methodologically sound evaluation: a held-out split or cross-validation, no train/test contamination (e.g., fitting scalers or selecting features on the full data), and metrics appropriate to the class balance rather than accuracy alone. 

0: no held-out evaluation, or the model is evaluated on data it trained on. 

1: a split exists but flawed: preprocessing fit before the split, no stratification with a rare label, or accuracy-only reporting on imbalanced data. 

2: clean held-out or cross-validated evaluation with metrics suited to the class balance. 

n/a: only when the notebook never trained a model.
