ABSTRACT 

Sequential social dilemma benchmarks capture diverse social interactions, but rarely test whether agents can infer hidden environmental rules through repeated interaction while adapting to other agents. We introduce OpenSocialJax , which extends Clean Up and Commons Harvest across an open-ended distribution of latent environment configurations, systematically varying spatial layouts and hid-den rules governing tool crafting, payoffs, and resource dynamics. Each episode samples one configuration from tens of millions of possibilities and holds it fixed across five trials. We evaluate eight large language models, including three closed source models and five open source models. Our evaluation tests whether mod-els can discover hidden rules and adapt to other agents in homogeneous and mixed teams. We find that rule discovery often improves performance but does not reliably sustain cooperation, as the cleaning burden becomes concentrated among fewer agents in OpenCleanup and every team eventually exhausts the shared resource in OpenHarvest. In our mixed arena, one cooperative agent was able to sustain the public good in OpenCleanup at a cost to its own return, but could not prevent resource depletion in OpenHarvest. Together, these results highlight the difficulty of adapting jointly to unfamiliar environmental dynam-ics and the changing behavior of other agents. Code and demo are available at 

https://github.com/cooperativex/OpenSocialJax .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Developing agents that can use experience to adapt across diverse and unfamiliar tasks is an im-portant challenge. In multi-agent settings, adaptation requires agents to infer not only how the environment operates, but also the behaviors, capabilities, and objectives of other agents. This is particularly challenging in sequential social dilemmas, where individual and collective interests may conflict. Consequently, it is crucial to develop benchmarks that systematically vary hidden environmental rules and dynamics. Such benchmarks enable controlled evaluation of how agents adapt to unfamiliar environmental mechanisms and the social dilemmas they induce. Existing environments have made substantial progress in evaluating agents in sequential social dilemmas. Melting Pot (Leibo et al., 2021; Agapiou et al., 2022) provides a diverse suite of so-cial environments with procedurally generated elements, while SocialJax (Guo et al., 2026) offers efficient JAX implementations of widely used social dilemma environments. However, these bench-marks are not designed to vary the hidden environmental dynamics underlying a social dilemma or evaluate how agents discover and adapt to them over repeated interactions. Prior open-ended and meta learning environments have substantially expanded the diversity of set-tings available for training and evaluating adaptive agents. XLand (Team et al., 2021) constructs a vast, procedurally generated multi-agent task space, while XLand-MiniGrid (Nikulin et al., 2024) provides a JAX-based implementation of 2D XLand. Craftax (Matthews et al., 2024) offers a JAX-based benchmark for open-ended reinforcement learning. However, these environments do not ex-1arXiv:2610.13710v1 [cs.MA] 9 Oct 2026 Preprint plicitly examine how adaptation to changing environmental rules interacts with the social dilemmas those rules induce. From the perspective of agent adaptation, large language model (LLM) agents offer a promising means of studying how agents reason about and respond to unfamiliar interactive environments. Prior work has shown that LLMs can use textual descriptions of game states to reason, plan, and select actions in interactive games (Wu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). These capabilities may allow them to infer latent environmental rules, reason about the behaviors and objectives of other agents, and revise their strategies as new evidence becomes available (Meta Fundamental AI Re-search Diplomacy Team (FAIR)† et al., 2022). Nevertheless, their ability to adapt when environ-mental rules and social dependencies vary jointly remains unclear. These gaps motivate benchmarks for open-ended experimentation that systematically vary hidden environmental rules and the social dependencies they induce. To address this need, we introduce OpenSocialJax, as shown in Figure 1, which extends Clean Up and Commons Harvest (Leibo et al., 2021; Agapiou et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2026) with newly designed procedural generators for en-vironment layouts and hidden mechanisms. Each episode samples a latent configuration that re-mains fixed across multiple trials, allowing agents to accumulate evidence and revise their strategies through interaction. Resampling the configuration across episodes produces a broad and controllable distribution of unfamiliar conditions rather than a fixed collection of predefined scenarios. This de-sign enables controlled investigation of how LLM-based agents explore unfamiliar settings, infer hidden mechanisms, and adapt to the evolving behaviors of other agents while navigating conflicts between individual and collective interests. To evaluate how effectively LLMs reason and adapt in sequential social dilemmas, we consider eight recent models spanning diverse model families and capability levels. The closed source set includes GPT-6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026a), GPT-6-Sol (OpenAI, 2026b), and Claude Opus 5.5 (An-thropic, 2026). The open source set comprises Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026b), Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026a), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Xu et al., 2026), Gemma 4 (Team et al., 2026), and GLM-5.3-Flash (Zeng et al., 2026). In summary, our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce OpenSocialJax, a benchmark of sequential social dilemmas whose map layouts and hidden rules are drawn from tens of millions of configurations. OpenSocialJax moves beyond dilemma benchmarks with fixed rules to test joint environmental and social adaptations. Second, we develop a protocol with repeated trials for eval-uating LLM agents over long horizons, in which agents must adapt to both hidden environmental dynamics and the behavior of other agents. Agents maintain their own notes between trials, pre-serving their discoveries and enabling us to analyze how they respond to changing environmental dynamics in social dilemmas. Third, we evaluate eight recent open source and closed source fron-tier LLMs in both homogeneous populations and mixed arenas. We analyze the interplay between exploration guided by hypotheses, exploitation of the discovered rules, and free riding. Even the strongest model reasons explicitly about the dilemma in its own notes, yet still ends up depleting the shared resource or being exploited by others. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Open-ended and meta-learning environments. Open-ended environments are important for study-ing agents adaptation and meta learning. There are several previous studies (Wang et al., 2016; Duan et al., 2016; Finn et al., 2017; Zintgraf et al., 2019) which focus on tasks with simple distributions and policy adaptations. More benchmarks have since encompassed diverse robotic tasks (Yu et al., 2020), latent causal structures (Wang et al., 2021), survival environments (Matthews et al., 2024), and physics-based control tasks (Matthews et al., 2025). XLand (Team et al., 2021) constructs a large procedurally generated task space in which goals, objects, and environmental configurations. XLand-MiniGrid (Nikulin et al., 2024) provides a lightweight JAX implementation of Xland. In multi-agent settings, XLand (Team et al., 2021) provides a procedurally generated task space span-ning cooperative and competitive interactions. AdA (Team et al., 2023) studies rapid adaptation to novel tasks through interaction. MAESTRO (Samvelyan et al., 2023) jointly constructs curricula over environmental parameters and co-player policies, while Multi-Agent Craftax (Matthews et al., 2024) extends open-ended exploration to heterogeneous agents and complex cooperative tasks. 2Preprint

Sequential dilemmas environments. Sequential social dilemma environments study temporally extended conflicts between individual and collective interests (Leibo et al., 2017; Perolat et al., 2017; Hughes et al., 2018). Melting Pot provides a diverse suite of such environments (Leibo et al., 2021; Agapiou et al., 2022), while SocialJax offers efficient JAX implementations (Guo et al., 2026). JaxMARL (Rutherford et al., 2024) is a general-purpose MARL library that includes Coin Game and STORM, two grid-world environments that support the study of social dilemmas. In contrast to these predefined environments and scenarios, OpenSocialJax varies hidden environmental mechanisms, enabling the study of adaptation across diverse latent social tasks. 

LLM agents in games. LLM-based agents have been evaluated in interactive games requiring rea-soning, planning, exploration, and learning from experience (Wu et al., 2023; 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Costarelli et al., 2024; Paglieri et al., 2025). Other studies examine their cooperation, com-petition, negotiation, and deception in multi-agent games (Meta Fundamental AI Research Diplo-macy Team (FAIR)† et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024; Abdelnabi et al., 2024). Recent work has also studied LLM-based agents in repeated games and social dilemmas, including matrix games, iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma, public-goods games, intertemporal dilemmas, and sequential social dilemmas (Akata et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024; Willis et al., 2025; Piedrahita et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025; Gallego, 2026; Tewolde et al., 2026). Closest to our setting, GovSim (Piatti et al., 2024) shows that societies of LLM agents often deplete a shared resource, Mosquera et al. (2025) evalu-ate LLM-augmented agents in MeltingPot (Agapiou et al., 2022), and Concordia (Vezhnevets et al., 2023) supports generative agent-based simulation of social interaction. However, how LLM agents infer latent game rules and adapt to varying environmental mechanisms and inter-agent dependen-cies remains underexplored. 

3 OPEN SOCIAL JAX 

We present OpenSocialJax, a benchmark for evaluating environmental and social adaptation by LLM agents in sequential social dilemmas. To study open-ended adaptation, each environment procedu-rally varies its layout and hidden rules, creating a distribution of latent conditions. Agents must infer unfamiliar game mechanisms through interaction and adjust their strategies in response to the observed behaviors of other agents. 3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Environment formulation. Each environment defines a distribution p(z) over latent environ-ment configurations. A configuration z ∼ p(z) induces a partially observable Markov game (POMG) Gz = ( N , Sz , {A i}i∈N , {O i}i∈N , Pz , {Ωi,z }i∈N , {R i,z }i∈N , ρ 0,z , γ ) , where N is the 3Preprint set of agents, Sz is the state space, and Ai and Oi are the action and observation spaces of agent 

i. The transition function Pz (s′ | s, a) determines the next state distribution given state s and joint action a, while Ωi,z (oi | s) defines the local observation received by agent i. The reward function 

Ri,z (s, a) specifies the reward of agent i, ρ0,z is the initial state distribution, and γ is the discount factor. The latent configuration z determines the environment layout and hidden mechanisms and may therefore affect the state space, transition dynamics, observations, rewards, and initial state distribution. An episode samples one configuration z ∼ p(z) and consists of K trials Ez = {τ 1 

> z

, . . . , τ Kz }. At the beginning of each trial, the environment state and agent positions are reset with the unchanged latent configuration z. The summarized interaction history available to each agent is retained across trials, allowing it to refine its understanding of the latent configuration. 

Sequential social dilemmas under varying environments. For a fixed latent configuration z, let 

Πzc and Πzd denote the sets of cooperating and defecting policies. Consider a joint policy containing 

l cooperating policies and m defecting policies, where l + m = N . We denote the corresponding average returns of cooperating and defecting policies by Rzc (l) and Rzd(l).Following prior work (Leibo et al., 2017; Hughes et al., 2018), the induced game Gz constitutes a 

sequential social dilemma when: (i) mutual cooperation is preferred to mutual defection, Rzc (N ) >Rzd(0) ; (ii) mutual cooperation is preferred to being exploited by defectors, Rzc (N ) > R zc (0) ; and (iii) either the fear property or the greed property (or both) holds: fear requires Rzd(i) > R zc (i) for sufficiently small i, whereas greed requires Rzd(i) > R zc (i) for sufficiently large i. The curves Rzc (l+1) and Rzd(l) form the Schelling diagram of Gz , representing the expected returns from cooperating and defecting when l co-players cooperate (Schelling, 1973; Perolat et al., 2017). Across episodes, z

varies the layout, hidden environmental mechanisms, and inter-agent dependencies while remaining unobserved by the agents. Agents must therefore infer the active configuration through interaction and adapt their strategies to both the environment and the observed behaviors of other agents. 3.2 ENVIRONMENT DESIGN 

Building on the Clean Up and Commons Harvest (Leibo et al., 2021; Agapiou et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2026), we develop two procedurally generated environments: OpenCleanup and OpenHarvest. Each environment combines generated layouts with hidden rule systems that can produce diverse environmental mechanisms and inter-agent dependencies. The active configuration is not revealed to the agents and must be inferred through interaction. Because agents adapt concurrently, their behaviors also change the social conditions faced by others. Agents must therefore adapt to both unfamiliar environmental mechanisms and the evolving behaviors of their agents. 

OpenCleanup. OpenCleanup retains the public goods dilemma of Clean Up. Agents receive re-wards only by collecting apples, but apples grow only when the shared river is sufficiently clean. We extend the environment with a hidden tool system whose crafting rules and effects vary across episodes. Figure 2a illustrates the environment components, the sampling of a latent configuration, and its instantiation during interaction. Each waste cell has one of five hues and one of three shades, producing 15 visually distinct waste types. Dark waste must first be converted to mid waste, then to light waste, before it can be cleared into water. All three shades contribute to river pollution. Agents collect base tools from four types of tool stations. Their two most recent pickups form an ordered pair, with both order and repetition allowed, producing 16 possible pairs. Seven of these pairs are assigned distinct cleaning effects. Five are hue-specific cleaners, each clearing light waste of one particular hue; one converts mid waste of any hue to light waste; and one converts dark waste of any hue to mid waste. The remaining nine pairs have no effect. The seven effects are assigned to different tool pairs without replacement. The hidden tool system therefore contains P (16 , 7) = 16! 9! = 57 ,657 ,600 possible rule sets. At the beginning of each episode, a rule set, a dominant waste hue, and a map layout are sampled and remain fixed across all trials. These conditions are hidden from the agents, who must explore the map, construct candidate tool pairs, fire them at waste, and infer their effects.

Agents obtain rewards by consuming apples, but the probability of regrowth depends on how many apples remain nearby. We extend the original environment by introducing apple colors and visible ripeness stages, together with hidden reward and regrowth rules conditioned on both. OpenHarvest contains five possible apple colors and six orchard patches. At the beginning of each episode, the color of each patch is sampled independently with replacement, allowing the same color to appear in multiple patches. The spatial arrangement of the patches and the agent spawn locations are also randomly generated. Each apple progresses through three visible stages: unripe, ripe, and overripe, after which it rots and disappears. After an apple is consumed, its empty tree cell can regrow only while other apples remain nearby, with more neighboring apples producing faster regrowth. In contrast, a tree whose apple rots regrows after a fixed delay regardless of nearby apples. The sampled patch colors, layout, and hidden rules remain fixed across all trials within the episode. For each of the five possible colors, OpenHarvest independently assigns three reward levels and three regrowth levels to the three ripeness stages. The reward level determines the reward obtained by consuming an apple, while the regrowth level scales the base regrowth rate determined by nearby apples. Because the two assignments are sampled independently, the stage providing the highest immediate reward need not produce the fastest regrowth. Each assignment has 3! = 6 possible orderings, giving (3!) 2×5 =610 = 60 ,466 ,176 possible hidden rule sets. 3.3 LLM A GENT INTERACTION PROTOCOL 

We design the protocol so that agents must discover the game from their own experience and can improve over repeated play. Each agent can never see the hidden rule, the other agents’ prompts 5Preprint or scores, and it cannot communicate with them. Instead of a growing conversation history, each agent relies on two kinds of memory. The harness keeps an objective record of what the agent has experienced, such as the effects of its own actions, while the agent keeps free-form notes in which it states its own conclusions and plans. Because the map and the hidden rule stay the same across the five trials of an episode, this memory lets agents accumulate evidence and revise their strategy from one trial to the next. At every step, each agent is queried independently with a fixed system prompt, which explains the rules and the reply format, and a user message built by the harness. The user message contains the agent’s egocentric 11 × 11 view, the actions available to it with their immediate effects, the rule evidence from its own experience, its recent steps, what it has seen other agents do, summaries of earlier trials, and its notes. The model replies with a JSON object that specifies one action and at most one edit to the notes; an invalid reply leaves the agent in place for that step. When a trial ends, the model rewrites its notes into a short list of lessons, and the environment is reset on the same map and rule. Only the rule evidence, the trial summaries and these lessons carry over to the next trial. Full prompts, the reply format and model settings are given in Appendices A and B. 

4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate three closed source models, GPT-6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026a), GPT-6-Sol (OpenAI, 2026b) and Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, 2026), and five open-source models, Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026b), Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026a), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Xu et al., 2026), Gemma 4 31B (Team et al., 2026) and GLM-5.3-Flash (Zeng et al., 2026), each with fixed inference set-tings (Appendix B). Our primary evaluation uses homogeneous teams of five instances of the same model. For each environment, every model is evaluated on 3 seeds; each seed samples one latent configuration and defines one episode of five trials of at most 400 steps, and an OpenHarvest trial ends early once no apple remains. We also run a mixed-model arena with Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6-Sol, GLM-5.3-Flash, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and Qwen3.8-27B, using the same seeds, prompts and inference settings. Appendix B summarizes the settings and configurations of experiments. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS 

We first examine how effectively homogeneous populations perform and adapt across repeated trials. Figure 3 reports the average agent return over the five trials in OpenCleanup and OpenHarvest. For each configuration, returns are first averaged across the five agents, and each curve then shows the mean across different seeds, with shaded regions indicating standard deviation. Because the latent configuration remains fixed while the environment resets between trials, changes in return indicate whether agents can use experience from earlier trials to improve their subsequent behavior. This comparison captures both initial performance under unfamiliar conditions and adaptation through repeated interaction. Claude Opus 5.5 is the strongest model in both environments. In OpenCleanup, as illustrated in Figures 3a and 3b, its return jumps after the first trial and stays at that level for the remaining trials. Almost every model improves from the first to the second trial, but for several of them the gain does not last. Their return peaks in the second trial and then drifts back down. In OpenHarvest, as illustrated in Figures 3c and 3d, every model performs better than the scripted greedy policy (hand-crafted greedy policy that can eat apples as soon as possible), but only Claude Opus 5.5 and gpt-6-sol clearly separate from it. Figure 4 tracks how much of the hidden rules each agent has discovered. In OpenCleanup, agents learn nearly all the recipes they will ever know in the first two trials; after that, the curves are flat. No model’s agents learn every recipe, because an agent only learns a recipe from its own shots. Moreover, an individual agent does not need to discover all hidden rules to perform well, since agents can collectively solve the task by specializing in different subsets of the rules. For example, agents can specialize in different tools and divide the work of cleaning the river. This behavior is also evident in the agents’ trajectories: Claude Opus 5.5 observes which tools other agents are using and then selects its own tool to complement their actions. In OpenHarvest, by contrast, knowledge of the color–ripeness payoffs continues to grow across all five trials, and most models end up knowing a similar share. In both environments, agents observe and optimize only their own rewards, while their actions also affect the opportunities available to others. This mismatch between individual incentives and col-lective outcomes gives rise to two social dilemmas, which we verify using the Schelling diagrams in Appendix C.1. OpenCleanup presents a public-goods dilemma, where cleaning is individually costly but enables apple growth that benefits all agents. OpenHarvest presents a common resource dilemma, where harvesting yields immediate individual rewards but excessive consumption reduces future regrowth for everyone. Figure 5 examines how agents navigate these tensions and how evenly rewards are distributed among them. 

Contribution to the public good. In OpenCleanup (Figures 5a and 5b), we measure how widely the cleaning is shared within a team using the effective number of cleaners. For nearly every model, this number decreases across trials, indicating that cleaning becomes concentrated among fewer agents while the others increasingly focus on harvesting. Total cleaning separates specialization from free-riding, as shown in Appendix C.2. Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemma 4 31B keep cleaning and returns high, whereas GPT-6-Sol, GPT-6-Luna, Qwen3.8-27B and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash clean less in later trials and earn less. The agents’ notes help explain how this shift toward free-riding develops. When agents observe that others are cleaning, they may conclude that the river can remain productive without their own contribution. After a successful trial, some agents explicitly decide to rely on others to clean. As one agent notes, “I can rely on other agents’ cleanup while focusing on harvesting.” Because agents see only their own apple rewards, they may overlook how much the team’s cleaning contributed to those rewards. As a result, they may attribute the high reward primarily to harvesting and continue to free-ride in later trials. Claude Opus 5.5 maintains the broadest participation in cleaning, although it follows the same downward trend. Qwen3.5-4B is the only exception. No model keeps the orchard alive for a full trial. The open-source models and the GPT models strip it early in every trial, and their orchard lifetime does not improve with experience, which matches their returns staying close to the greedy baseline. Claude Opus 5.5 keeps the orchard productive for much longer by correctly inferring that it should wait for apples to regrow. Its orchard lifetime peaks in the second trial and then shortens (Figure 5d). Its agents’ notes show why. From the third trial on, almost all of them conclude that holding back does not pay because the others strip the apple patches anyway, for example “Saving apples for regrowth rarely pays; eat the good ones before rivals do” .

Efficiency and equality. The third column (Figures 5c and 5f) plots each model’s mean return against the Gini coefficient of returns within a trial. In both environments, Claude Opus 5.5 com-bines the highest return with one of the most even distributions, so efficiency does not come at the cost of fairness. In OpenCleanup, gpt-6-sol distributes returns just as evenly but at a much lower level. The models with the lowest returns show the most unequal distributions, although the Gini coefficient is less reliable when few apples are eaten. In OpenHarvest, all models except Claude Opus 5.5 cluster near the greedy baseline with similar inequality. Current frontier models show promise in navigating social dilemmas, but none of the models fully resolve either dilemma. In OpenCleanup, agents initially contribute actively to river cleaning, but across successive trials they increasingly learn to free-ride, leaving progressively fewer agents to bear the cleaning burden. In OpenHarvest, the shared resource is exhausted within each trial. Stronger models delay these failures rather than avoid them. 4.4 OPEN SOCIALJAX ARENA

5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduced OpenSocialJax , an environment suite for evaluating how multi-agent systems adapt to unfamiliar environmental dynamics in sequential social dilemmas. OpenSocial-Jax extends Clean Up and Commons Harvest with procedurally generated layouts and hidden rules governing tool effects, rewards, and resource dynamics, producing tens of millions of possible con-figurations. We evaluated eight proprietary and open source language models in homogeneous teams and a mixed model arena. The models discovered many hidden rules and often improved after the first trial, but rule discovery did not reliably lead to sustained cooperation. Across trials, cleaning became concentrated among fewer agents in OpenCleanup, while every team eventually depleted the shared resource in OpenHarvest. These results show the difficulty of adapting simultaneously to unfamiliar environmental dynamics and the changing behavior of other agents. 

Limitations and Future Work. OpenSocialJax is implemented in JAX for fast parallel simulation, but training learning-based agents that adapt to the latent configuration remains an open challenge. Future work could extend the multi-trial protocol with explicit communication and more sophisti-cated theory of mind mechanisms to study how agents resolve social dilemmas under uncertainty. We hope OpenSocialJax provides a foundation for developing agents that discover environmental rules, anticipate collective behavior, and sustain cooperation in unfamiliar social environments. 9Preprint

A LLM A GENT 

Every agent is an independent call to the same model. At each step, each agent receives the system prompt of its environment (Listings 1 and 2) and one user message that describes the current state from its own point of view (Listings 3 and 4). The model replies with one JSON object that contains its action. Agents do not talk to each other, and a model never sees another agent’s messages, notes or scores. Agents can only see others’ actions when they in the observation space. 

Observation. The user message starts with a progress line (trial, step, and the agent’s own score so far in the trial), followed by these blocks: • RULES YOU KNOW : what the agent has established from its own experience, maintained by the harness. In OpenCleanup, this is every pick-up order the agent has fired with and the effect of those shots on each waste color. In OpenHarvest, it lists the points paid by each color and ripeness stage the agent has eaten, the regrowth times of cells it saw emptied, and the ripening and rotting times it observed. This block persists for the whole episode. • YOUR NOTES : free-form notes written by the agent itself (see below). • EARLIER TRIALS : one summary per finished trial, written by the harness: what the agent did and what it saw of the others. • OTHER AGENTS : where each other agent was last seen and what it was doing, limited to the agent’s own view. • YOUR PLAN : the agent’s current and previous goals. • LAST STEP and RECENT STEPS : the outcome of the last action, events in view, and the last 15 steps. • NOW : position, facing and hand, and an 11 × 11 egocentric view drawn north-up with absolute coordinates. OpenHarvest adds the patches seen so far and the emptied cells the agent is waiting on. • ACTIONS NOW : the available actions and what each would do (e.g. which cell a move lands on, or which waste a shot would hit). 

Memory across steps and trials. Agents keep three kinds of memory. The harness keeps RULES YOU KNOW and EARLIER TRIALS up to date. The agent itself writes its notes, applying at most one edit per step (add an entry, replace or delete an entry by its number, or none). When a trial ends, the model is asked to rewrite its notes into a short list of lessons and plans for the next trial . Only this list is carried into the next trial; the step-by-step entries are dropped. If the rewrite fails, the agent starts the next trial without notes. The agents’ own lessons are the source of the quotations in Appendix D. 

Frozen agents and early stopping in OpenHarvest. An agent hit by a zap is sent back to a spawn point and cannot act for 12 steps. During these steps, the model is not called and no prompt is recorded. When no apple is left on the map, no apple can appear again, since an empty cell only regrows next to hanging apples. The harness then skips the rest of the trial; this does not change any return. Listing 1: System prompt, OpenCleanup (as shown to agent A).

The points it pays depend on its colour and its stage (hidden; you read them off the points you got), and the stage you eat it at also sets how fast that cell comes back (2.3). 2.3 REGROWTH. An empty tree cell can grow a new apple, which always starts unripe. This is the only way a trial gives you more apples than the ones it started with. - A cell whose apple was EATEN grows a new one ONLY while apples are still hanging close to that cell. With no apples left around it, it NEVER grows one. A patch eaten bare therefore produces nothing for the rest of the trial, no matter how long you wait beside it. - The more apples are still hanging CLOSE TO an empty cell, the sooner it grows a new one, and the drop is steep, not gradual: with several apples around it a cell usually comes back within the trial; with only two it takes many times longer; with a single one it rarely comes back at all; with none it never does. Only the apples near that cell count for it: apples hanging in another patch do nothing for it. - How fast an eaten cell grows one also depends on its colour and on the ripeness stage its apple had when it was eaten. This part is hidden: watch the cells you empty. - A cell whose apple ROTTED grows a new one by itself shortly afterwards, whatever is around it. 2.4 Moves are absolute compass moves and do not change your facing; only turning does. Ground and empty tree cells are walkable; walls and other agents block you. Table 3 shows what changes between the first trial and the later ones. In trial 1, agents spend a quarter to a third of their steps testing pick-up orders, and most teams clear their first cell only after 60 to 70 steps. From trial 2 on, the recipes are carried over in RULES YOU KNOW and the layout in the notes. Exploration almost stops, the first cell is cleared within the first 10 to 25 steps for most models, cleaning in the first 50 steps rises several-fold, and apples appear much earlier. Claude Opus 5.5 stands out already in trial 1, when it clears its first cell after about 20 steps. From trial 2 on, its teams do nearly twice as much early cleaning as the next model, and their first apples appear within about 50 steps. C.4 TOOL USE 

Agents that know more recipes pick up tools more often. Within the same run and trial, an agent’s number of known recipes correlates with its pick-ups (median Spearman ρ = 0 .71 over 108 run– trials) and tool switches ( ρ = 0 .82 ). This does not make them less efficient. In trials 2–5, agents that know two or three recipes clean about 15.6 cells per 100 steps spent crafting or cleaning, against 9.3 for agents that know one. Knowing several recipes lets an agent finish a cell (dark → mid → light 

→ water) by itself, which requires switching tools. Table 4 shows that most models need close to the minimum of two pick-ups per working tool. Claude Opus 5.5 needs fewer than two because it exploits the sliding window: one extra pick-up can turn one recipe into the next.
