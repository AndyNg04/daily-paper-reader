## ABSTRACT 

Although users may express different personalization needs across tasks, these task-specific behaviors can reflect shared underlying preference mechanisms. Ex-isting LLM personalization methods often focus on surface-level interactions or undifferentiated histories, overlooking the shared mechanisms underlying these behaviors. We introduce PersonaFactors , a framework that distills reusable and interpretable personalization factors from heterogeneous user interactions. We construct a library of thirteen PersonaFactors by extracting evidence-grounded rationales and clustering recurring patterns across tasks, and develop a dynamic factor-guided generation framework that selects factors relevant to each user’s his-tory and current request. Across four personalization benchmarks and four back-bone models, Dynamic PersonaFactor achieves the best score on every reported metric. Representation analyses further show that PersonaFactors and user his-tory induce partially overlapping low-dimensional representations, and that their joint use yields complementary shifts. Taken together, these findings suggest that factor-based and history-based personalization can mutually reinforce each other. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for personalized applications such as question answering (Su et al., 2025; Salemi & Zamani, 2025), content creation (Salemi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026), and task assistance (Cai et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025). These applications require adapting both the content and presentation of responses to individual users, whose knowledge, goals, constraints, and preferences can lead to different expectations for similar requests. Existing personalization methods broadly follow two strategies. Model adaptation learns user pref-erences from historical interactions and incorporates them into the model through parameter adapta-tion, user representations, activation steering, or decoding control (Zhang et al., 2025b; Choi et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). These methods provide direct control over generation but typically require additional training or access to model internals. Context conditioning keeps the underlying model unchanged and represents user preferences as text. It retrieves relevant past interactions or summarizes them into user profiles and memories (Su et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025c), then includes this information in the prompt to guide personalized generation. There are two key challenges in turning user history into effective personalization guidance. First, identifying reusable personalization mechanisms. Historical interactions are highly task-specific, while the same underlying preference may appear differently across scenarios. We therefore need to abstract shared mechanisms beyond surface behaviors. Second, selecting the right mechanisms for each request. Not every user preference is relevant to every query, and using all factors may introduce redundant or conflicting guidance. The challenge is to identify the subset that is most relevant to the current task and most influential for generation.

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Personalization Methods. Existing personalization methods mainly operate in either model space or natural-language space. Model-space methods adjust model behavior through fine-tuning, parameter-efficient adaptation, preference learning, activation steering, or decoding-time control (Zhang et al., 2025b; Choi et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Nie et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). They can directly control generation, but typically require access to model parameters, internal activations, or output distributions, making them unsuitable for black-box LLMs. Natural-language-space methods instead use user history, profiles, retrieved records, structured reasoning, or long-term memory as external context (Su et al., 2025; Du et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Noh et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025). These methods support both black-box and white-box models, but user preferences often remain entangled in raw histories or a single profile. PersonaFac-tors extend this line of work by providing an interpretable and dynamically composable factor library mined from large-scale data across personalization scenarios, thereby facilitating more effective use of user histories. 

Personalization Benchmarks and Positioning. Existing benchmarks evaluate personalization through task metrics, LLM-as-a-Judge, or user-specific reward models, covering classification, rat-ing, question answering, and short- or long-form generation (Salemi et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024; Salemi & Zamani, 2025; Zollo et al., 2025; Su et al., 2026). These benchmarks span diverse tasks, but most studies focus on a single task or scenario and use user preferences primarily as an evalu-ation target. CoPA is closest to our work. It introduces cognitive factors for personalized question answering, but uses them only as evaluation dimensions (Su et al., 2026). In contrast, Person-aFactors extract evidence-grounded personalization factors from user histories and responses across multiple scenarios, then dynamically select and compose relevant factors for each request, thereby making these factors reusable representations for generation. ## 3 METHOD 

We introduce PersonaFactors , reusable personalization mechanisms that capture how responses should adapt to users across different scenarios. Our method consists of two stages. We first con-struct a shared factor library from heterogeneous user interactions through rationale extraction, clus-tering, and mechanism canonicalization (Figure 1). Given this library, we dynamically select factors relevant to a user’s history and current request, then incorporate them as natural-language guidance for personalized generation (Figure 2). 3.1 CROSS -S CENARIO PERSONA FACTOR CONSTRUCTION 

Inspired by the cognitive–affective processing system (CAPS) theory (Mischel & Shoda, 1995), we seek recurring personalization mechanisms whose manifestations vary across situations. We construct a shared library F = {f1, . . . , f K }. Our construction covers common personalization scenarios, such as question answering, preference prediction, and short- and long-form text gen-eration. These scenarios are drawn from the training splits of LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024) and LongLaMP (Kumar et al., 2024), together with the full datasets of HiCUPID (Mok et al., 2025) and CoPA (Su et al., 2026). We randomly sample up to 5,000 instances per constituent task, obtain-ing 66,018 instances across 14 task-specific subsets. As illustrated in Figure 1, we first infer, for each sample, the possible reasons for the user’s preference for the personalized response, grounding these reasons in the user’s history and current query. We then hierarchically cluster these reasons to identify shared personalization mechanisms across scenarios. Finally, we name and canonicalize the clusters to distill personalization dimensions and factors expressed in natural language. A con-struction model Mc performs rationale extraction, mechanism naming, and canonicalization, while a separate model Maudit audits the resulting factor mapping.

Mechanism Naming and Canonicalization. For each cluster, we deduplicate high-membership rationales and select up to 200 representative examples, prioritizing coverage across theoretical la-bels, mechanism descriptions, and evidence patterns. The construction model uses these examples to name the shared adaptation mechanism rather than summarize surface topics or tasks. We then canonicalize the candidate factors by merging equivalent or synonymous names for the same con-crete mechanism. Each candidate maps to exactly one canonical factor; uncertain matches remain separate. An independent audit–repair procedure checks for incorrect or missed merges, with the full procedure provided in Appendix B.6. This process yields 13 PersonaFactors organized into four dimensions, summarized in Table 1. Each factor describes a reusable user–response alignment property rather than a fixed psychological trait or a numerical user attribute. For example, Schema Congruence concerns whether an explanation fits the user’s knowledge and familiar concepts, whereas Constraint Consistency concerns whether it respects the user’s practical constraints. Complete definitions are provided in Appendix B.7. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models. We evaluate PersonaFactor-guided personalization on four backbones: the API-based DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) and Gemini-2.5-Flash (Comanici et al., 2025), and the open-weight Qwen3-8B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025) and Gemma-3-4B (Team et al., 2025). We use the score-based selector when sequence likelihoods are available and the prompt-based selector for black-box APIs without likelihood access. Implementation details are provided in Appendix C.1. 

Benchmarks. We evaluate on four personalized generation benchmarks: LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024), LongLaMP (Kumar et al., 2024), LaMP-QA (Salemi & Zamani, 2025), and Personal-LLM (Zollo et al., 2025). LaMP and LongLaMP are used to construct PersonaFactors, with their validation splits serving for evaluation, whereas LaMP-QA and PersonalLLM are reserved for down-stream testing. LaMP covers news headline generation, scholarly title generation, and tweet para-phrasing, while LongLaMP covers abstract, product review, and topic generation; both use user histories and current inputs and are evaluated with ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L. LaMP-QA assesses personalized long-form question answering across Art and Entertainment, Lifestyle and Personal Development, and Society and Culture using an LLM-as-a-Judge protocol. PersonalLLM measures open-domain preference alignment with heterogeneous reward models. Together, these benchmarks span diverse tasks, domains, and evaluation paradigms, enabling assessment of cross-task general-ization and robustness. Detailed statistics are provided in Appendix C.2. 

Baselines. We compare six methods that differ in how they use user history and personalization factors. Direct generates a response from the current input without access to user history. RAG 

retrieves history items relevant to the current input and provides them as context to the model. RAG-SelfFactor uses the current query and retrieved user history to generate query-specific factors that augment the retrieved context. The corresponding prompt is provided in Appendix C.3. RAG-CoPA 

augments retrieval-based personalization with CoPA factors (Su et al., 2026). Static PersonaFactor 

builds on the same RAG procedure and uses a fixed set of pre-constructed personalization factors. 

Dynamic PersonaFactor also builds on RAG, but selects the factors relevant to each current task dynamically. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS

4.4 EFFECT OF THE NUMBER OF HISTORY ITEMS 

We vary the number of user-history items and compare RAG, Static PersonaFactor, and Dynamic PersonaFactor on Qwen3-8B to assess sensitivity to history size, especially for sparse user profiles. As shown in Figure 3, performance improves rapidly with the first few items and then gradually saturates. Dynamic PersonaFactor outperforms both baselines across nearly all history lengths, with the clearest advantage in the low-history regime, suggesting its effectiveness in cold-start settings. At larger history sizes, mild non-monotonic fluctuations show that additional interactions do not guarantee continued gains, as irrelevant or competing cross-scenario preferences may dilute task-relevant signals. Overall, the results suggest that Dynamic PersonaFactor benefits from selectively structuring historical evidence rather than merely increasing context length. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced PersonaFactors, a framework that distills reusable and interpretable personalization factors from heterogeneous user interactions and selects them dynamically for each request. Across 9Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 four benchmarks and four backbone models, Dynamic PersonaFactor achieves the best score on ev-ery reported metric, consistently outperforming history retrieval and static factor guidance. Selector ablations show that combining task-relevance and generation-influence signals is more effective than either signal alone or matched-size random selection. Representation analyses using Static Person-aFactor show partially shared low-dimensional structure between factor- and history-induced shifts, while their combination produces additional representation changes. Together, these results suggest that organizing user history through reusable, request-specific factors provides a practical way to improve personalization across tasks and model families. Limitations are discussed in Appendix D.

## A RELATED WORK DETAILS 

Model-Space Personalization. Model-space personalization encodes user information in model parameters or internal representations. Prior work adapts model behavior to individual users through user-level fine-tuning, parameter-efficient adaptation, collaborative preference learning, or inference-time control. PROPER and CoPL respectively use progressive adaptation and collabora-tive preference learning for user modeling (Zhang et al., 2025b; Choi et al., 2025), while Qiu et al. model personalized differences between users in a latent space (Qiu et al., 2025). Other approaches steer model behavior through activation directions or adjust preference-aware output distributions at decoding time (Zhang et al., 2025a; Nie et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). These methods directly control generation, but typically require access to model parameters, activations, or output probabil-ities. 

Natural-Language-Space Personalization. Natural-language-space personalization instead injects user history as external context through in-context learning. Retrieval-augmented approaches select behavioral records relevant to the current request, while profile-based methods summarize dispersed interactions as explicit natural-language preferences. Long-term memory systems organize and re-trieve user information for personalized agents (Pan et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025). Su et al. generate and compress user profiles for personalized question answering (Su et al., 2025), whereas Du et al. optimize the profile used for generation with contextual bandits (Du et al., 2026). RPM and TRIPLE further use structured reasoning or behavioral profiles to guide personalized generation (Kim et al., 2026; Noh et al., 2026). These methods preserve user information in a readable form and do not require modifying the base model. 

Personalization Benchmarks. Personalization benchmarks employ evaluation signals at different levels to measure whether a model adapts to a user. LaMP covers personalized classification, rating, and text generation, using conventional classification, regression, and reference-based generation metrics (Salemi et al., 2024). LongLaMP extends this setting to personalized long-form text gener-ation (Kumar et al., 2024). Such evaluations enable standardized comparisons but characterize user preferences only indirectly. LaMP-QA targets personalized long-form question answering without reference answers and uses an LLM-as-a-Judge to assess whether a response addresses user-relevant aspects derived from the question (Salemi & Zamani, 2025). PersonalLLM ensembles reward mod-els to simulate diverse user preferences and evaluates personalized generation using the resulting user-specific rewards (Zollo et al., 2025). CoPA develops a cognitive-factor-based evaluation frame-work, using an LLM-as-a-Judge to score responses along each factor and assess their alignment with users’ cognitive preferences (Su et al., 2026). Among these works, CoPA is most closely related to ours. It distills personalization factors from a cognitive perspective, but uses them only as evaluation dimensions in a cognitive question-answering setting. It neither examines whether factors are shared and transferable across scenarios nor uses them to guide generation. In contrast, our method extracts reusable personalization factors from multi-scenario user histories and dynamically selects and composes relevant factors for each request. The resulting natural-language guidance enables personalized generation across scenarios.

## D LIMITATIONS 

Our study has several limitations. First, PersonaFactor construction relies on language-model su-pervision for rationale extraction, mechanism naming, and canonicalization, which may introduce systematic errors or model-dependent biases. Second, the factor library is induced from a finite collection of benchmarks and may not cover preference mechanisms that arise in other domains, cultures, or interaction modalities. Third, dynamic factor selection requires additional inference and 34 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 therefore incurs computational overhead relative to history retrieval alone. Finally, our experiments focus on text-based personalization and do not explicitly model temporal changes in user prefer-ences. The representation analyses establish shared structure and complementarity between History and PersonaFactor, but do not by themselves establish a causal mechanism. 35
