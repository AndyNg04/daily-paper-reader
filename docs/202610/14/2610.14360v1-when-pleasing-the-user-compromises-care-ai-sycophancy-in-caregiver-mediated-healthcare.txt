# When Pleasing the User Compromises Care: AI Sycophancy in Caregiver-Mediated Healthcare 

Priyadarsi Mishra 1,2,∗,† Arjun Beniwal 1,∗,† Ziyu Yi 1,† Yu Ding 1 Sheng Liu 11Stanford University 2Texas A&M University 

## ABSTRACT 

Large language model (LLM) alignment often assumes that the user interacting with a model is also the primary beneficiary of its behavior. Yet in many con-sequential settings, one person directs the system while another bears the conse-quences. We study this problem in caregiver-mediated healthcare, where parents, pet owners, and caregivers make decisions for children, animals, and older adults. We introduce an evidence-grounded benchmark spanning veterinary, pediatric, and older-adult care, and test whether AI advisors maintain appropriate care when users express clinically inferior preferences. Holding patient facts fixed, we vary stated care preferences and communication styles and evaluate both initial recom-mendations and multi-turn follow-up. Across frontier and open-weight models, inferior preferences substantially increase switching from appropriate to inappro-priate care beyond neutral conversational drift and increase agreement with the requested action, while communication style alone has a smaller effect. These failures accumulate across turns, including on cases models initially answer cor-rectly, and often carry moderate or severe potential harm. In matched eldercare cases, the effect of an inferior preference is similar whether it is expressed by the patient or by a caregiver. Together with models’ limited tendency to elicit patient preferences in caregiver conversations, this suggests that current models are highly sensitive to expressed user preferences but less sensitive to whose preferences are represented and who bears the consequences. Our results expose a broader limi-tation of user-centric alignment in settings with multiple stakeholders. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Many LLM systems are trained to follow user intent and provide helpful responses (Ouyang et al., 2022). Yet the user directing an AI may be distinct from the person or animal affected by its recom-mendations. This separation between who directs the AI and who bears the consequences raises a broader alignment question: whose interests should guide the system’s response (Gabriel, 2020)? This distinction is particularly important in caregiver-mediated healthcare : parents, pet owners and adult children make care decisions for children, animals and older adults. We study interactions involving a caregiver , an AI advisor model prompted to act as a physician or veterinarian, and a dis-tinct patient , the intended beneficiary of the advice. The caregiver can express a preference directly, while the patient’s own preferences may not be represented in the conversation. Clinical guidance for surrogate decision-making similarly emphasizes the patient’s known wishes or best interests, rather than the surrogate’s preferences alone (American Medical Association, 2016). Caregiver concerns about cost, fear or inconvenience may be legitimate, yet the preferred action may still be inappro-priate for the patient. In such settings, a well-aligned advisor must respond to the user’s concerns without treating the expressed user’s preference as the only relevant signal about whose interests are at stake. Prior work shows that language models can favor agreement with users over accurate an-swers (Sharma et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025) and validate their perspectives in interpersonal con-flicts (Cheng et al., 2026). We use “third-party” to char-acterize the consequence structure of the failure—the interacting user is not the sole beneficiary— rather than to assume that such failures must arise from a mechanism distinct from first-party syco-phancy. We ask two related questions: whether advisor models maintain appropriate care when a caregiver requests a clinically inferior action, and whether their sensitivity to such preferences de-pends on whether the preference is expressed by the patient or by a caregiver. Further related work appears in Appendix A. To investigate these questions, we introduce CareDistance , a dataset and benchmark grounded in clinical guidelines and professional reference materials across eldercare, pediatrics and veterinary care. For each patient case, we vary the caregiver’s preferred action and persona, which shapes how they communicate their concerns to the advisor model. Keeping the clinical facts unchanged allows us to examine how caregiver preferences and communication style affect the advice. We evaluate both initial recommendations and advice during multi-turn follow-up. Conversations without an assigned persona or stated care preference provide a neutral multi-turn control, helping distinguish preference-associated failures from degradation that arises during continued interaction itself. On an eldercare subset where patients could plausibly seek advice themselves, we additionally compare the same inferior preferences when expressed by the patient versus by a caregiver, testing whether models condition their preference sensitivity on who bears the clinical consequences. Across model families, inferior preferences substantially increase appropriate-to-inappropriate switching beyond neutral follow-up and increase inappropriate responses that recommend or per-mit the requested action. These failures accumulate across turns, while persona alone has a smaller effect, and human review likewise finds more switching under inferior preference. Crucially, on matched eldercare cases, the increase induced by an inferior preference is not statistically distin-guishable overall when the preference is expressed by the patient versus by a caregiver. In caregiver conversations, models also often proceed without asking about the patient’s own preferences. To-gether, these results suggest that current models are highly sensitive to what the user wants , but com-paratively insensitive to whose preference is represented and who bears the consequences . Many resulting failures are judged to carry moderate or severe potential harm. ## 2 METHODS 

We construct patient cases and care questions from clinical guidelines and professional reference materials in three domains: eldercare , where an adult child seeks advice for an older parent; pedi-atrics , where a parent seeks advice for a child; and veterinary care , where a caretaker seeks advice for an animal. We use these cases to study third-party sycophancy : whether an AI advisor model accommodates a caregiver’s preferred action at the expense of appropriate care for the patient. We vary caregiver messages while holding patient information fixed, and evaluate the advisor model’s recommendations in single- and multi-turn conversations (Figure 1). 2.1 DATASET CONSTRUCTION AND COVERAGE 

Source selection and case construction. We select sources covering treatment, prevention, as-sessment and ongoing care in each domain. An LLM uses the selected passages to write a patient vignette and an open-ended care question. The vignette describes the patient’s relevant medical history, current condition and care circumstances; the question asks what care to provide without suggesting an answer. Both actions specify any eligibility conditions or exceptions relevant to the decision. An LLM verifier then checks whether the source supports the reference action for the described patient and establishes the alternative as inferior, accounting for eligibility, exceptions and recom-mendation strength. Prior work suggests that LLMs can assist with assessing factual support in clinical text (Munnangi et al., 2025; Chung et al., 2026). We exclude drafts when support for the reference action or the inferiority of the alternative remains unresolved. We also check drafts for missing or contradictory patient details, questions that reveal the answer, and duplicate decisions. Each retained case includes its supporting passages and source link. 

Dataset coverage. The benchmark contains 450 cases, with 150 per domain, covering both pro-viding indicated care and withholding inappropriate care. We assign each case one primary clinical decision type : medicines and procedures; nutrition and hydration; assessment and monitoring; refer-ral and care coordination; or support, rehabilitation and safety. Appendix B reports the case counts by clinical decision type, source family and care direction, together with source characteristics, se-lection details and complete case examples. 2.2 CARE PREFERENCES AND PERSONAS

Care and action assessment. The judge evaluates the care plan against the patient’s circumstances and supporting clinical evidence, allowing clinically established alternatives to the reference action. ## 3 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Luna, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Kimi K2.5, Claude Sonnet 5 and Qwen 3 8B on 150 cases per domain under 13 caregiver conditions. Primary comparisons cover eldercare, pediatrics and veterinary care. Muse Spark 1.3 is evaluated on eldercare and pediatrics only. We assess each condition’s first response and the ensuing conversation through five turns. GPT-5.6 Luna generates and checks caregiver messages; GPT-6 Luna judges the advisor model’s responses. Appendix E.1 reports additional aggregate and domain-specific results, and Appendix C.2 gives evaluation settings and coverage. 5The comparison of patient and caregiver preferences uses the same advisor model settings and evalu-ation rubric through five turns. We also evaluate responses to a common conversation history within each of the neutral and inferior-preference conditions across all three domains (Appendix E.4). Overall rates weight domains equally; persona-group rates average the five personas. We report 95% confidence intervals, with aggregation and resampling details in Appendix C.2.

A beneficiary-aware advisor should represent not only the content of an expressed preference, but also whose preference it is and whose welfare is at stake. We therefore ask whether models respond differently to the same inferior preference when it is expressed by the patient versus by a caregiver. 

We do not assume that the clinically appropriate recommendation itself should differ across these settings; rather, we test whether models modulate their sensitivity to an inferior preference according 8to the user–beneficiary relationship. We compare responses to the same inferior actions under patient and caregiver preferences for eldercare, relative to each setting’s neutral control (Figure 4). Appropriate-to-inappropriate switching increases during follow-up under both patient and caregiver preferences (Figure 4A). Across the models in Figure 4, excess switch rates by turn five are 35.4 percentage points under patient preferences and 33.4 under caregiver preferences; the difference between these increases (caregiver minus patient) is −2.0 points (95% CI −8.3 to 4.5). Inferior-action agreement at first failure also rises above neutral in both settings (Figure 4B), by 37.9 and 36.5 points, respectively; the difference is −1.3 points (95% CI −6.9 to 4.1). These increases are not statistically distinguishable overall, and most model-specific comparisons show the same pattern (Appendix E.5). Thus, although models are strongly sensitive to expressed in-ferior preferences, we find little evidence that this sensitivity is modulated by whether the preference comes from the patient or from a caregiver. 

Patient preferences in caregiver conversations. Under inferior caregiver preference, advisor models asked about the patient’s preferences or recommended checking them in 12.6% of first inap-propriate responses and 17.5% of turn-five responses with maintained care (Appendix E.6). Thus, even when the interacting user is not the patient, models often proceed without establishing what the patient wants.

## 5 LIMITATIONS 

The cases and conversations are constructed from clinical guidance; they do not estimate failure prevalence or harm incidence in practice. Persistent assigned preferences test responses to contin-ued requests rather than how often caregivers make them. Human review by two medical trainees 10 assessed care appropriateness in only eldercare and pediatric conversations. Excess switching mea-sures a behavioral difference from neutral follow-up; it does not establish the mechanism of each failure. Provider coverage and model-specific generation settings also limit some comparisons (Ap-pendix C.2). 

## 6 CONCLUSIONS 

CareDistance shows that clinically inferior preferences can cause initially appropriate advice to deteriorate during multi-turn interaction. This preference-induced degradation is substantial across model families, yet changes little overall when the same preference is expressed by the patient rather than by a caregiver. Together with models’ limited tendency to establish patient preferences in caregiver conversations, these results suggest strong sensitivity to expressed user preferences but limited beneficiary-aware modulation. More broadly, they expose a limitation of user-centric alignment in settings where the interacting user and the party bearing the consequences may differ.

Clinical knowledge and conversation. MedQA evaluates medical examination question answer-ing (Jin et al., 2021), while MultiMedQA also incorporates clinician assessment of reasoning, fac-tuality and potential harm (Singhal et al., 2023). Interactive evaluations extend this focus to infor-mation gathering and clinical communication. CRAFT-MD evaluates diagnostic reasoning through simulated patient conversations (Johri et al., 2025); AgentClinic incorporates clinical tools and pa-tient and physician biases (Schmidgall et al., 2026). AMIE studies diagnostic dialogue (Tu et al., 2025) and, subsequently, disease management across visits, including patient preferences and prac-tical constraints (Liévin et al., 2026). These studies establish a broader context for evaluating advice within conversations, beyond the correctness of an isolated answer. 

Sycophancy and caregiver preferences. Sycophancy research examines agreement with users at the expense of accurate answers (Sharma et al., 2024) and validation of their perspective in social interactions (Cheng et al., 2026). SYCON Bench measures changes in stance under sustained user pressure (Hong et al., 2025), and The Doctor Will Agree With You Now examines resistance to escalating pushback on diagnostic and biomedical questions (Kim et al., 2026). CareDistance studies the care recommended when the person requesting advice is a caregiver and the patient bears its clinical consequences. Its source-grounded cases span eldercare, pediatrics and veterinary care. We vary caregiver preferences and personas while keeping patient information fixed. Neutral follow-up, without an assigned persona or stated preference, provides a comparison for errors that arise during conversation alone. 

Medical safety and response assessment. MedSafetyBench evaluates responses to harmful med-ical requests using professional ethics (Han et al., 2024). LLM-based response assessment has been studied in MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023); MedHELM applies a jury of LLM evaluators to healthcare tasks with expert-defined criteria (Bedi et al., 2026). Clinical fac-tuality work provides a complementary approach: FactEHR studies fact decomposition and entail-ment (Munnangi et al., 2025), while VeriFact checks generated clinical statements against electronic health records (Chung et al., 2026). Our assessment uses the patient information, source evidence and reference actions to evaluate the care offered during a conversation, recording action agreement, care appropriateness and potential harm as distinct outcomes. 15 B DATASET CONSTRUCTION AND COVERAGE

18 C EVALUATION CRITERIA AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS 

C.1 CARE ASSESSMENT AND POTENTIAL -H ARM CRITERIA 

The judge receives the patient case, source passages, reference and inferior actions, dialogue through the current turn, and the advisor model’s separate neutral first response. It assesses the full care plan conveyed by the conversation, including additional interventions, recommendations and explicit per-mission to act. It accounts for clinical indications, recommendation strength and exceptions, distin-guishing permission to proceed from safety advice after refusal or a decision deferred to clinical assessment. Advice from earlier turns remains in effect unless withdrawn or replaced; failure to repeat it alone is not an omission. A clinically established alternative that meets the patient’s needs can be appropriate even when it differs from the reference action. Empathy or discussion does not itself indicate inferior-action agreement, and a warning does not make an otherwise inadequate plan appropriate. Five worked examples in the prompt illustrate these rules. The judge returns structured labels with a brief rationale; judgments are not shown to the advisor model or caregiver model. Appendix F.3 reproduces the complete judge prompt. Table 5: Potential clinical harm: severity definitions.     

> Tier Supported consequence if it occurs Mild Minor, temporary symptoms or discomfort requiring little or no treatment. Moderate Meaningful deterioration requiring treatment, or temporary functional limitation. Severe Life-threatening deterioration, major functional loss, lasting disability or death.

At the first inappropriate response, the judge records the error type (omission, commission or both) and the most serious clinically supported potential consequence without assuming additional patient facts. Severity describes that consequence if it occurs, not its probability or observed injury. Sec-ondary resource labels record additional patient visits, monitoring, treatment effort or expenditure beyond the care already indicated. They exclude caregiver effort. A negative label means that no ad-ditional burden was identified, not that the plan reduces costs or time. Error type, harm and resource labels are assigned only for inappropriate care. Review design. Two medical trainees independently reviewed 60 five-turn conversations from 30 matched neutral/inferior-preference case–model pairs, with 15 pairs each in eldercare and pediatrics across seven advisor models. Reviewers received the vignette, supporting evidence, reference and inferior actions, and conversations, without model identities or automatic judgments. They identified the earliest inappropriate response or no failure. Sampling was stratified by domain and the automatic preference-condition judgment, with neutral conversations required to be judged appropriate throughout by the automatic evaluator. Switching rates characterize this validation sample rather than the full benchmark.

Task
