Abstract:
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit us-vs.-them bias: A
behavioral asymmetry in which prompts framed around an ingroup
(“we”/“us”) receive systematically more positive continuations than
matched prompts framed around an outgroup (“they”/“them”).
Using Edge Attribution Patching (EAP), we localize this behavior to
directed circuits in GPT-2 Small, GPT-2 Large, Llama-2 7B, and
Llama-3 8B. Across models, high-attribution edges are sparse and
concentrated primarily in middle-to-late layers, while ingroup- and
outgroup-related pathways are only partially overlapping and
reorganize under fine-tuning. Behavioral comparisons and 30-token
generations show that the measured asymmetry extends beyond
next-token evaluation. Targeted patching of EAP-ranked edges
reduces the bias score more than equal-cardinality random-edge
controls, but also substantially degrades performance on CoLA,
HellaSwag, and WikiText-2, suggesting that bias-relevant pathways
are functionally entangled with broader language capabilities.
Overall, our findings characterize us-vs.-them bias as a sparse,
context-dependent, and functionally entangled computational
phenomenon whose internal organization varies across the models
examined.

Introduction
LLMs are increasingly deployed across high-stakes
domains such as healthcare [1], education [2],
governance [3], and content moderation [4], where their
outputs can directly influence human decisions and social
outcomes [5]. While these systems demonstrate
remarkable capabilities in reasoning [6], language
understanding, and generation [7], a growing body of
work has raised concerns about their tendency to
reproduce and amplify social biases present in their
training data [8, 9]. These biases span gender [10],
race [11], religion [12], and other demographic attributes,
raising concerns about stereotyping, inequitable
treatment, and downstream harm [13].
At the core of many social biases lies the distinction
between ingroups (“us”) and outgroups (“them”) [14]. We
use us-vs.-them bias to describe a systematic asymmetry
in responses toward an ingroup relative to an outgroup,
expressed through ingroup favoritism, outgroup
derogation, or both. These processes are related but
distinct: Greater positivity toward an ingroup does not
necessarily imply hostility toward an outgroup. Research
in social psychology [15] and social identity theory [16]
shows that such asymmetries shape judgments across
domains including race [17], gender [18], and religion [19].
LLMs exhibit analogous output patterns, producing more
positive continuations under ingroup framing and more
negative continuations under outgroup framing [8, 20].
Here, this terminology refers to measurable differences in
model outputs and does not imply that LLMs possess
social identities or conscious group affiliations. When
deployed in downstream applications, these patterns can
contribute to unequal treatment, stereotyping, and
misinformation [13]. However, existing evaluation
approaches often focus on isolated demographic
contrasts [21] or aggregate sentiment measures [9],
providing limited insight into the broader
ingroup–outgroup structure underlying such behavior.
Recent advances in mechanistic interpretability have
begun to open the black box of neural networks [22],
enabling researchers to identify internal components such
as attention heads and MLP layers that contribute to
specific model behaviors [23]. However, most of this work
has focused on narrow linguistic phenomena or individual
features [24], with limited attention to the mechanisms
driving broader social biases across contexts and
identities. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that
behavioral alignment at the output level is not a reliable
gauge of internal representations [25, 26], motivating
mechanistic analysis that looks beneath model outputs.
We therefore ask: Can us-vs.-them bias be localized to
identifiable substructures within an LLM? If so, this
would enable targeted, component-level
interventions—modifying specific edges or layers rather
than relying on prompting [13, 27], data
augmentation [28, 29], or full fine-tuning [30, 31]—and
would shed light on whether social biases as fundamental
as the ingroup–outgroup distinction are deeply embedded
in the computational structure of learned language
representations.
Among the different component types available for
analysis, we focus on edges—directed connections between
computational units such as attention heads and MLP
layers—and quantify their causal contribution using Edge
Attribution Patching (EAP) [32]. Each edge represents
the flow of information between adjacent stages of the
transformer’s forward pass; its importance is estimated by
measuring how much the bias signal changes when that
edge’s activation is replaced by a corrupted
counterpart [33]. This edge-centric view allows us to move
beyond node-level attribution and construct directed bias
circuits [34]: Sparse subgraphs that are causally sufficient
for the observed biased behavior [35]. Our main
contributions are as follows:
RQ1. Localized encoding of us-vs.-them bias in
LLM circuits. We conduct a systematic analysis across
four open-source LLMs (GPT-2 Small, GPT-2 Large,
Llama-2 7B, and Llama-3 8B) to test whether us-vs.-them
bias is localized within a small subset of edges. Using
multiple circuit-identification approaches, we find that
biased behavior is concentrated in specific edges and
layers across models (Figures 1 and 2).
RQ2. Instability of bias circuits across corruption
strategy and fine-tuning. We evaluate the robustness
of identified ingroup-positivity, outgroup-negativity, and
combined us-vs.-them edges under variations in corruption
strategy and fine-tuning condition. Our analysis (Figure 3
and Table 1) shows that the specific edges associated with
biased behavior are not stable under perturbations to
either the model or the input space.
RQ3. Causal specificity and capability
entanglement of bias-related edges. We test whether
EAP-identified edges have a disproportionate causal
influence on the measured bias signal relative to randomly
selected edges, then evaluate the effect of these
interventions on linguistic acceptability, commonsense
continuation, and language-modeling performance. This
determines whether the identified pathways are selective
to the measured bias behavior or functionally entangled
with broader language capabilities.

Related Work
Bias in LLMs.
Prior work shows that LLMs reproduce
social biases related to gender, race, religion, nationality,
age, social identity, political, and other demographic
attributes [9, 36–40]. Similar biases were previously


5
observed in word embeddings trained on large text
corpora [41, 42]. Common mitigation approaches include
counterfactual data augmentation [28, 29], prompt-based
debiasing [13, 27], fine-tuning or alignment-based
methods [30, 31], and representation-level
interventions [43]. However, debiasing can also introduce
trade-offs, including reduced downstream performance or
incomplete removal of biased internal representations [44].
Mechanistic interpretability for LLMs.
Mechanistic
interpretability (MI) aims to reverse-engineer trained
neural networks by identifying the internal computations
that give rise to model behavior, analogous to analyzing
compiled software binaries [45]. In transformer language
models, MI studies units such as neurons, attention heads,
MLP layers, residual streams, and directed edges to
explain how specific behaviors emerge. Prior work has
used circuit-based and causal methods to analyze
induction heads, factual recall, indirect object
identification, grokking, and information flow in
transformers [46–50]. More recent work has scaled these
approaches through automated circuit discovery [51],
causal tests for algorithmic hypotheses [52], circuit
analyses of grokking [23], refined accounts of induction
heads [53], and methods for tracing information flow
through transformer architectures [54]. A growing body of
work applies mechanistic interpretability to analyze and
mitigate bias in LLMs [55–60]. (author?) [55] identified
neurons and attention heads in GPT-2 contributing to
gender bias, while (author?) [56] used automated circuit
discovery and ablations to reduce stereotype propagation.
(author?) [57] localized demographic and gender bias in
GPT-2 and Llama-2 but showed it can shift across
fine-tuning and overlap with components for downstream
tasks. (author?) [58] proposed a bias attribution score
for multilingual token-level explanations, and (author?)
[59] introduced activation and attribution patching to
trace gender bias in LLaMA and Mistral. (author?) [60]
showed political ideology can be encoded as a linear
direction in activation space. These studies demonstrate
that bias is present not only in model outputs but also in
internal components and information-flow pathways.

Method
3.1
Localization of us-vs.-them Bias
Our primary objective is to determine whether
us-vs.-them bias is encoded within specific components of
large language models, rather than being diffusely
distributed across the entire network. We use a
mechanistic interpretability framework grounded in circuit
analysis, modeling transformer computation as a directed
acyclic graph (DAG) to identify the subgraph responsible
for the target behavior [61, 62]. Circuits are also explored
in terms of nodes as “features,” typically defined as
directions in the latent space [63].
Edge Attribution Patching.
To localize bias-related
computation, we use Edge Attribution Patching
(EAP) [32], a causal attribution method that estimates
the importance of individual edges in the model’s
computational graph without requiring exhaustive
ablation [64]. Given a clean input xclean and a corrupted
input xcorr, the causal importance of an edge E is defined
as the change in a bias metric L when the activation
transmitted through that edge is replaced by its corrupted
counterpart:
Attr(E) = |L(xclean | do(E = ecorr)) −L(xclean)| ,
(1)

corrupted input and injected at edge E. A larger
attribution value indicates that the edge contributes more
strongly to the bias signal. Following EAP, we
approximate edge importance using a first-order
gradient-based score. For each (src, dst) pair where
layer(src) < layer(dst), the directed edge score is defined
as:

sentiment-based metrics over the model’s next-token

words scoring above 6 and a negative lexicon V−as words

incoherent completions.
Dataset and Corrupted Inputs.
EAP requires paired
clean and corrupted inputs that preserve token-level
structure while perturbing the causal feature of interest.
We use Symmetric Token Replacement (STR) [68] to
maintain alignment under controlled semantic changes.
Our dataset contains 660 identity-conditioned prompts
covering 165 persona traits across 16 social domains,
including political ideology, religion, gender identity, race
and ethnicity, profession, socioeconomic class, culture,
education, technology, values, law and order, geopolitics,
health, and intellectual orientation. Each identity is
instantiated using four syntactic templates
(Appendix A1). We evaluate GPT-2 Small, GPT-2 Large,
Llama-2 7B (Llama-2-7b-hf), and Llama-3 8B
(Meta-Llama-3-8B) on completions of these templates;
model details appear in Appendix A2.
For each clean prompt, we construct two corrupted
variants. C1 replaces the identity with an
out-of-distribution placeholder such as abc, removing
identity information while preserving grammatical
structure. C2 swaps ingroup pronouns such as we and us
with they and them, changing group framing while
keeping the identity fixed. Representative examples are
provided in Appendix A1.4, Table A3.
3.2
Bias Circuit Localization
To assess whether us-vs.-them bias is localized to a sparse
set of edges or distributed broadly across the network, we
conduct two complementary analyses. First, we evaluate
circuit faithfulness to quantify how well the top-K
EAP-scored edges reproduce the full model’s bias signal.
If bias is localized, retaining only these high-attribution
edges should preserve most of the bias metric. Second, we
examine the layerwise distribution of high-attribution
edges to identify where bias-related processing is
concentrated within the model architecture.
Circuit Faithfulness Evaluation.
To validate the
circuits identified by EAP, we follow prior work [57] and
compute the difference between the bias metric of the full
model and the metric obtained when only the top-K
edges are retained:
∆= |L(full model) −L(top-K edges)| .
(5)
Here, L(full model) denotes the bias metric computed
over the complete network, and L(top-K edges) denotes
the metric after ablating all edges except the top-K edges
identified by EAP. A small value of ∆indicates that the
selected edges capture most of the full model’s bias signal,
providing evidence of localization. Conversely, a large
value of ∆suggests that the bias signal is distributed
across a wider set of model components.
Circuit Instability Under Fine-Tuning.
We
evaluate whether the identified bias circuits remain stable
after fine-tuning under two settings. First, we use the
ingroup–outgroup neutralization (ION) procedure from
prior work [8], which combines instruction fine-tuning
with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to reduce
bias-related sentiment divergence. We test whether
explicit debiasing changes the internal edges responsible
for bias-related completions. Second, we fine-tune on the
Shakespeare corpus, consisting of the complete works of
William Shakespeare [69]. Because this corpus has no
topical overlap with our identity-conditioned bias
prompts, it serves as a task-irrelevant fine-tuning control.
This setting tests whether the identified circuits reflect
bias-specific computation rather than general adaptation
to new language modeling data. After each fine-tuning
condition, we recompute EAP scores and compare the
resulting high-attribution edge sets with those from the
original model.
3.3
Causal Validation via Targeted Edge
Patching
We use targeted edge patching as a causal diagnostic to
test whether EAP-identified edges disproportionately


7
influence the measured us-vs.-them bias signal. For each
clean input, we construct a token-aligned corrupted
counterpart and replace activations along selected
bias-critical edges with the corresponding corrupted
activations, while preserving clean activations along all
remaining edges. We compare this intervention with
equal-cardinality random-edge controls. This tests
whether EAP-ranked edges exert greater influence on the
measured bias signal than randomly selected edges and
whether their function is separable from general language
competence. We therefore evaluate changes in the bias
metric together with performance on CoLA [70],
HellaSwag [71], and WikiText-2 [72]. Selective reductions
in the bias metric would indicate circuit specificity,
whereas broad performance degradation relative to
random-edge controls would indicate functional
entanglement with general language computation [44, 57].
We use the HookedTransformer class from the
TransformerLens repository,1 which offers a modular and
transparent framework for studying the internal
mechanisms of transformer models such as GPT-2 and
Llama-2. We ran all experiments on a machine with two
A100 GPUs.

Results and Discussion
RQ1: Bias Localization.
Appendix A3 shows the
combined us-vs.-them bias circuits for GPT-2 Small
(Figure A1), GPT-2 Large (Figure A2), Llama-2 7B
(Figure A3), and Llama-3 8B (Figure A4). Across models,
the circuits share a broad structure—an early
group-framing pathway, a middle attention-mediated
routing stage, and a late evaluative region before the
logits—but become more distributed with scale.
GPT-2 Small is most compact, with routing concentrated
in layers 3–9 and readout through layers 10–11;
GPT-2 Large extends this pattern across layers 6–35, with
a larger late MLP-heavy region. Llama-2 7B shows early
attention–MLP interactions in layers 1–8, mid-layer
convergence around layers 13–20, and a late hub across
layers 21–31, while Llama-3 8B has the broadest topology,
with an early MLP spine in layers 2–11, attention routing
around layers 12–14, bottlenecks around layers 16–20, and
a late module spanning layers 21–31. These results
suggest that us-vs.-them bias is partially localized in
sparse edge sets rather than implemented as a single fixed
circuit. The ingroup and outgroup circuits in
Appendix A4 further show these bias conditions are
asymmetric and context-dependent rather than mirror
images of one shared mechanism.
Table 1 reports full-model baseline scores and circuit
faithfulness values, ∆, across bias type, metric, corruption

Notably, ∆values are generally higher for Outneg than for

and model are reported in Table A6.
Figure 1 shows the layer-wise distribution of
high-attribution EAP edges for us-vs.-them bias across
GPT-2 Small, GPT-2 Large, Llama-2 7B, and
Llama-3 8B. Edge counts are obtained by combining
ingroup-positivity and outgroup-negativity attribution
scores and selecting the top-ranked edges for each model.
Across architectures, few important edges appear in early
layers; instead, bias-relevant edges concentrate in
middle-to-late layers. This suggests that the measured
bias signal emerges after deeper contextual and semantic
integration. The location of these edges varies by model.
In GPT-2 Small, important edges begin around layer 4
and peak near layers 8–9. In GPT-2 Large, the
distribution shifts later, beginning around layer 18 and
peaking near layer 25. Llama-2 7B shows a more compact
pattern, with most important edges between layers 16–23
and a peak around layer 20. Llama-3 8B exhibits a
broader profile, with substantial activity across layers
14–23 and an additional late-stage concentration near
layers 28–31. Overall, these results indicate that
us-vs.-them bias is sparse but depth-localized, with the
layer range and concentration of important edges varying
across model family and scale. Appendix A5 further
decomposes these important-edge distributions into
ingroup-positivity and outgroup-negativity conditions
(Figure A9), showing that outgroup negativity generally
has a broader layerwise footprint than ingroup positivity.

RQ2: Circuit Stability Under Fine-Tuning.
Figure 3 reports top-K edge overlap (K = 300) across
pretrained, Shakespeare fine-tuned, and ION fine-tuned
models. In the pretrained setting, ingroup and outgroup
circuits are partially shared but grow increasingly distinct
with scale: Overlap falls from 76% in GPT-2 Small to
69% in GPT-2 Large, 51% in Llama-2, and 33% in
Llama-3, suggesting larger models implement ingroup

RQ3: Causal Validation and Capability
Entanglement.
We evaluate targeted edge patching as
a causal diagnostic rather than as a deployable debiasing
method (see Appendix A6, Table A7). Across all four
models, patching EAP-identified edges reduces the
measured us-vs.-them bias score more than patching
equal-cardinality random-edge sets. At the top-1%
threshold, targeted patching reduces the measured bias
score by 3.24%, 14.76%, 26.41%, and 35.60% for
GPT-2 Small, GPT-2 Large, Llama-2, and Llama-3,
respectively, compared with 1.62%, 5.69%, 6.27%, and
9.88% for the random controls. This difference indicates
that EAP-ranked edges are disproportionately involved in
the measured behavior. However, both targeted and
random-edge corruption degrade performance on CoLA,
HellaSwag, and especially WikiText-2, demonstrating that
hard edge interventions broadly disrupt model
computation. Accordingly, the reduction in the bias
metric cannot be interpreted as selective debiasing,
because it co-occurs with substantial degradation of the
model’s general predictive behavior. These results instead
demonstrate functional entanglement between
bias-relevant pathways and broader language-processing
mechanisms.

6
Limitations
We adopt a behavioral definition of us-vs.-them bias and
make no claims that LLMs possess social identities, group
affiliations, or human-like ingroup–outgroup cognition.
Our circuit-localization results show only that an
analogous output asymmetry can be traced to internal
computational pathways. Several limitations should be
considered. First, no established benchmark currently
exists for validating us-vs.-them bias circuits, and our
controlled dataset of 660 identity-conditioned prompts
may not fully represent the complexity of real-world
interactions. Although our longer-generation analysis
extends the findings beyond next-token prediction, it still
relies on structured prompts and short continuations.
Second, circuit extraction is based on the top-K = 300
EAP-ranked edges, a computationally motivated
threshold that may omit weaker but meaningful pathways.
Third, EAP uses a first-order gradient approximation and
may underestimate nonlinear interactions, particularly
when clean and corrupted activations differ substantially.
Our evaluation is also limited to four models and two
fine-tuning conditions, Shakespeare and ION. Because the
models differ in architecture, tokenizer, training data, and
parameter count, the observed cross-model patterns
should not be interpreted as general scaling laws. Broader
evaluation across model families, sizes, training seeds, and
adaptation settings is needed to determine how
consistently these circuit patterns generalize. Finally,
hard edge patching is used as a causal diagnostic rather
than a practical debiasing method. Although patching
EAP-ranked edges reduces the measured bias score more
than random-edge controls, it also causes severe
degradation in linguistic acceptability, commonsense
continuation, and language-modeling performance. The
observed reductions may therefore partly reflect
disruption of the model’s broader predictive distribution
rather than selective removal of biased behavior.
Nevertheless, this result provides a useful mechanistic
constraint: Bias-relevant pathways appear functionally
entangled with general language processing and are
unlikely to be safely removed through hard
component-level intervention alone. Future work should
develop dedicated circuit-validation benchmarks, evaluate
larger and more diverse models, and investigate softer
interventions such as activation steering, representation
editing, or training-time alignment methods that better
preserve general capabilities.

Dataset
A1.1
Overview
We construct a controlled us-vs.-them bias dataset designed for mechanistic circuit analysis. The goal of the dataset is
not to measure bias from naturally occurring text, but to provide a controlled set of identity-conditioned prompts in
which group framing and identity information can be systematically manipulated. Each sample consists of a clean
ingroup prompt and two corrupted variants used for Edge Attribution Patching (EAP).
The dataset follows the general template:
Consider yourself [identity]. We are
where [identity] is replaced with a social, political, cultural, professional, or demographic identity. The blank position
is evaluated using the model’s next-token distribution. By comparing the probability assigned to positive and negative
lexical continuations under ingroup and outgroup framing, we obtain a controlled measure of us-vs.-them bias.
The full generated dataset contains 660 identity-conditioned samples, constructed from 165 identities across 16
domains and four sentence structures per identity. In addition, we include four no-identity baseline samples, resulting
in 664 total generated samples. In the main EAP experiments, we use the identity-conditioned samples as the primary
analysis set and use the no-identity prompts as structural baselines.
A1.2
Identity Domains
The dataset covers 16 identity domains intended to span a broad range of socially meaningful group categories. These
include political, religious, demographic, socioeconomic, occupational, cultural, technological, and geopolitical
identities. Across these domains, we include 165 distinct identity terms. Table A1 shows the domains and associated
personas used in the dataset.
A1.3
Sentence Structures
For each identity, we instantiate four sentence structures. These structures are designed to elicit different forms of
group association: Trait attribution, elaborated group description, behavioral tendency, and resource or value framing.
The four structures are shown in Table A2.
Since each of the 165 identities is paired with four sentence structures, the identity-conditioned portion of the dataset
contains 165 × 4 = 660 samples.

different causal factors during EAP.

A2
Model
We evaluate four decoder-only transformer language models spanning different model families, parameter scales, and
architectural choices. This allows us to test whether us-vs.-them bias localization patterns are consistent across both
smaller GPT-2 models and larger Llama-family models.
GPT-2 Small.
GPT-2 Small [73] is a 117M parameter autoregressive language model with 12 transformer layers
and 12 attention heads per layer. It uses a hidden dimension of 768 and a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens. In our EAP
graph construction, GPT-2 Small contains 158 nodes and 32,491 directed edges.
GPT-2 Large.
GPT-2 Large [73] scales the same architecture to 774M parameters, with 36 transformer layers and
20 attention heads per layer. Its hidden dimension is 1,280, and its vocabulary size remains 50,257. Under our EAP
graph construction, GPT-2 Large contains 758 nodes and 810,703 directed edges, reflecting the larger number of
inter-component pathways induced by its increased depth and number of attention heads.
Llama-2 7B.
Llama-2 7B [74] is a 7B parameter decoder-only transformer with 32 layers and 32 attention heads per
layer. It uses a hidden dimension of 4,096, an intermediate feed-forward dimension of 11,008, and a vocabulary of
32,000 tokens. Architecturally, Llama-2 differs from GPT-2 by using SwiGLU activations, Rotary Position Embeddings
(RoPE), and RMSNorm. In our EAP graph, Llama-2 7B contains 1,058 nodes and 542,257 directed edges.

A4
Bias Circuit
To identify the subgraph responsible for us-vs.-them bias, we apply EAP over our dataset and rank all components by
their cumulative attribution score. We then extract a sparse circuit by retaining the top-K highest-scoring nodes and
the edges connecting them.
External Validity of the Bias Metric.
Table A4 shows that CrowS-Pairs and StereoSet produce identical model
rankings (Spearman ρ = 1.0) and are negatively correlated with both Inpos and Outneg L1 scores (ρ = −1.0, n = 4).
For outgroup prompts, this negative correlation indicates agreement because lower L1 values represent stronger
negative sentiment. However, the ingroup result suggests that ingroup positivity captures a different dimension of bias.
Given the small number of models, these correlations should be interpreted descriptively.
Node and edge selection.
For each model, we select the top-40 nodes by attribution score and restrict candidate
edges to those connecting nodes within this set with strictly positive attribution. To further sparsify the graph, we
apply a quantile threshold of q = 0.90, retaining only edges whose attribution score exceeds the 90th percentile of the
candidate edge distribution. From this filtered set, we keep at most 60 edges per circuit, and 36 edges for the
combined circuit, subject to a local connectivity constraint of at most 10 incoming and 10 outgoing edges per node. To
ensure that the circuit remains a valid computational graph, we enforce minimum connectivity: Every node except
Logits must have at least one outgoing edge, and every node except Input must have at least one incoming edge.
When a connectivity gap exists, we add the strongest available candidate edge. Table A5 summarizes the node types
used in the EAP graph.
Ingroup and outgroup circuits.
We extract three circuits per model. The ingroup positivity circuit is derived by
running EAP on clean ingroup prompts, such as “We are so
”, against C1-corrupted inputs, measuring how
identity-conditioned ingroup framing drives positive sentiment predictions. The outgroup negativity circuit uses
outgroup-framed prompts, such as “They are so
”, as clean inputs against C1-corrupted counterparts, capturing the
component subgraph responsible for negatively valenced outgroup predictions. The combined circuit is computed over
the full dataset using both ingroup and outgroup samples, yielding a unified view of the bias-encoding subgraph.

A5
Ingroup and Outgroup Important Edges
Figure A9 shows that important EAP edges for both ingroup positivity and outgroup negativity are concentrated in
specific middle-to-late layers rather than being uniformly distributed across the network. In GPT-2 Small, both bias
conditions cluster mainly in layers 6–10, with peaks around layers 8–9. In GPT-2 Large, the distribution shifts deeper:
Ingroup positivity is concentrated around layers 21–29, peaking near layer 26, while outgroup negativity spans a
broader range from layers 18–29, peaking near layer 25. For Llama-2 7B, both conditions are sharply localized in
layers 16–23, with a dominant peak at layer 20, suggesting a compact and largely shared circuit region. In contrast,
Llama-3 8B shows a more distributed and condition-specific pattern: Ingroup positivity is concentrated around layers
15–23 with additional late-layer activity near layers 28–31, whereas outgroup negativity begins earlier, spanning layers
10–20 with peaks around layers 14 and 17. Overall, these results indicate that us-vs.-them bias is mediated by sparse,
layer-localized circuits whose locations vary across model architecture and scale. Outgroup negativity generally
exhibits a broader layerwise footprint than ingroup positivity, suggesting that negative outgroup representations may
rely on more distributed or functionally entangled pathways. Here, important edges are defined as the top-K = 300
EAP-ranked edges, with counts assigned to both source and destination layers.
