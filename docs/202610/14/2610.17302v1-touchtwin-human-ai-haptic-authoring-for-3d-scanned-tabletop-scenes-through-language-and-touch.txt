Introduction
A phone video can capture how a place looks, but not how it feels.
Modern reconstruction methods [42, 54, 82] can turn casual scans
into detailed 3D environments for virtual tours, e-commerce, and
telepresence [56], yet recreating how those environments feel re-
mains a largely manual authoring problem. Each surface must be
associated with haptic properties that a device can render during
interaction, requiring expertise that most users do not have. Au-
tomating this process is appealing, but errors in inferred material
properties can become immediately apparent through touch: a card-
board box that looks plausible but feels like wood is still a poor
haptic representation. This creates an interaction-design challenge
beyond simply improving recognition accuracy: how can imperfect
automation help people create convincing haptic experiences while
still giving them intuitive ways to identify and correct what the
system gets wrong?
Existing approaches occupy two ends of this automation–control
spectrum. Vision–language models (VLMs) offer a way to infer ma-
terial properties directly from visual scans, but their predictions
remain unreliable in unconstrained scenes. On a small six-scene
benchmark, for example, a locally runnable VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-
3B [5]) identified the exact material for 13 of 24 surfaces. At the
other extreme, manual haptic authoring tools give users precise
control, but typically require them to specify or tune low-level prop-
erties such as friction, stiffness, and vibration spectra [59, 66, 67, 69].
Neither approach is well suited to non-experts who want to repro-
duce how a scanned environment feels: full automation provides
too little control when predictions fail, while manual authoring
demands too much haptic expertise.
Rather than treating material recognition as a final prediction,
we treat it as the start of an interaction. TouchTwin uses automa-
tion to create an editable haptic draft that users can feel, evaluate,
and revise. The system proposes material regions and their hap-
tic responses, while users correct errors by renaming materials or
repainting region boundaries. Crucially, these corrections operate
at the same semantic level as the system’s predictions: users can
touch a box that feels wrong and type “cardboard,” rather than ma-
nipulating friction coefficients or vibration spectra. This propose–
feel–revise workflow combines automatic scene interpretation with
human perceptual judgment and control, allowing imperfect recog-
nition to remain useful without requiring users to become haptic
experts.
TouchTwin implements this approach by turning a casual phone
scan into an editable haptic scene. The system proposes surface
regions and material descriptions, then uses a model trained on
measured material interactions to generate corresponding haptic
responses. Users can feel the scene and revise the descriptions or
region boundaries, with each edit updating the rendered haptics.
TouchTwin targets material-category fidelity: it aims to make each
region feel like the right kind of material, rather than reproduce
the exact vibration and tapping response of the scanned physical
specimen.
We evaluated this workflow with 12 participants who compared
the generated scenes directly with the physical objects. Although
the automatic proposals were only partially accurate, participants
rated them 5.4/7 as useful starting points. After a median of 6.6
minutes of editing, perceived realism increased from 4.0 to 5.6/7,
and scoring the saved material layers against ground truth showed
material-family accuracy increasing from 52% to 92%. Generated-
model distance to the individual reference samples, however, did
not reliably decrease.
We further explored how the same interaction supports both
reproducing existing scenes and creating new haptic experiences.
Participants used freeform material descriptions to author textures
without a physical referent, while generally preferring this mode
for creative work and the automatic proposals for reproducing a
real scene. Together, these results suggest a broader role for AI in
haptic authoring: rather than needing to produce a correct final
experience, AI can provide semantically meaningful starting points
that people can experience, interpret, and refine through touch.
Our contributions are (1) a propose–feel–revise interaction model
for human–AI haptic authoring, (2) TouchTwin, an end-to-end sys-
tem realizing this approach for 3D-scanned tabletop scenes, and
(3) empirical evidence that users can improve imperfect automatic
haptic drafts through brief semantic and spatial edits.
2
Related Work
2.1
From Haptic Texture Rendering to
Touchable 3D Scenes
Rendering convincing virtual textures requires reproducing percep-
tual cues that arise during physical contact. Fine texture is largely
perceived through vibrations generated during surface exploration.
The duplex theory distinguishes coarse spatial cues from a vibration-
sensitive channel that depends on motion [37], and scanning vibra-
tions and their Pacinian-weighted spectra help explain differences
among fine textures [10, 11]. These cues remain important during
tool-mediated interaction: a rigid probe preserves texture percep-
tion [44, 88] along dimensions such as roughness, hardness, and
stickiness [12, 47, 55], and users tend to attribute stylus vibrations
to the contacted surface [17].

Data-driven rendering exploits these effects by recording physi-
cal tool–surface interactions and resynthesizing them during vir-
tual contact [65]. The Penn Haptic Texture Toolkit, for example,
models 100 measured materials using force- and speed-dependent
autoregressive (AR) vibration models [19, 21]. Subsequent work
incorporates contact transients and speed-dependent vibration to
improve realism [18, 45], and explores alternative learned represen-
tations [1, 35, 41]. Other displays reproduce texture cues through
electrovibration, voice coils, or audio-driven actuators [8, 59, 77].
These methods generally assume that a material model has al-
ready been measured or selected, often through instrumented cap-
ture [14, 46, 57, 58]. Together, these approaches provide increasingly
rich ways to render how a virtual surface feels once its haptic prop-
erties have been specified.
Extending these techniques from individual surfaces to com-
plete virtual environments introduces a different problem: how
should haptic properties be assigned across a 3D scene? Prior sys-
tems make scenes touchable through passive or retargeted physical
proxies [4, 15, 36, 74], encountered-type devices [3, 79], shape dis-
plays [49], or fabricated microgeometry [24, 25], as well as feedback
rendered by grounded force-feedback arms [53] and handheld hap-
tic controllers [16, 48, 84], among other approaches [20, 83]. Some
approaches explicitly connect captured physical environments to
virtual ones; Oasis, for example, uses matched real objects to make
reconstructed rooms physically explorable [75]. These approaches
generally depend on matching physical matter, explicitly authored
haptic properties, or material models that have already been mea-
sured or selected.
TouchTwin instead addresses the upstream problem of assigning
haptic properties to a casually captured scene that has not been
instrumented for haptic measurement. It associates scene regions
with material descriptions and maps those descriptions to models
grounded in previously measured tool–surface interactions. This
trades specimen-level fidelity for easier capture: a phone scan can
provide the starting point for a touchable scene without measuring
every surface individually. The remaining challenge is one of au-
thoring: how people can specify, evaluate, and revise material feel
throughout the scene without manipulating haptic signals directly.
2.2
Authoring Haptics: From Signal Timelines
to Language
Haptic authoring remains difficult for non-experts because design-
ers must translate intended experiences into device- and signal-level
parameters [67, 72]. Prior tools move beyond direct signal editing
through example-driven editors [66, 69], tactile animation [68],
crowdsourced proxy ratings [70], mid-air haptic design tools [71],
adaptive tactons [40], and vocalized prototyping [23]. Language is
particularly promising because people describe haptic experiences
using sensory and metaphorical terms rather than signal param-
eters [73]. A taxonomy of 79 haptic authoring tools documents
natural-language description as a growing interaction approach
and identifies broader gaps in multimodal authoring support [64].
Therefore, we designed TouchTwin to use a named material at-
tached to a spatial region as its primary authoring unit, rather than
exposing waveforms or spectra.
Recent generative approaches make semantic authoring increas-
ingly feasible by mapping higher-level inputs to haptic signals. Prior
systems include GAN-generated pen vibrations [80, 91], Haptic-
Gen’s text-conditioned vibration [78], ChatHAP’s conversational
generation and modification of vibrotactile effects [51], latent-
diffusion approaches [85], and perceptually trained audio-to-vibration
mappings [50]. Related work generates visual–tactile patches, print-
able microgeometry, 3D forms, and shape-display behaviors [27,
29, 31, 61]. These systems primarily generate individual effects or
artifacts rather than assigning and revising material feel across a
captured 3D scene.
TouchTwin builds on Qian et al.’s language-guided texture model [60],
which maps text descriptions to force- and speed-dependent vibra-
tion and tapping responses through a latent space trained on mea-
sured tool–surface interactions. Their work demonstrated language-
based generation for isolated material patches on a kinesthetic hap-
tic device, but did not address captured scenes, spatial segmenta-
tion, or correction of automatically inferred materials. Scene2Hap is
closer in scene-level scope: it assigns vibrotactile feedback through-
out authored VR environments and models propagation between ob-
jects [39], but does not provide a human correction loop or ground
its feedback in measured contact interactions.
TouchTwin uses language not only as an input for human au-
thoring, but also as a shared representation between human and
AI. Automatic descriptions and user revisions pass through the
same text-to-haptics path. Combined with editable spatial regions,
this shared representation lets the same interface support both
automatic proposals and human revisions. TouchTwin therefore
uses language to support an iterative human–AI process in which
generated haptics can be felt, interpreted, and revised.
2.3
Material Recognition as an Editable
Proposal
Automatically assigning haptic properties to a captured scene re-
quires inferring material information from visual input. Material
recognition has been supported by large-scale image datasets [9],
contrastive vision–language models [62], promptable segmenta-
tion [43, 63], and instruction-tuned VLMs [5]. Other work connects
material appearance with contact acceleration and sound [76], with
tactile measurements [30, 86, 90], and with language [89], while
multi-view reconstruction and depth completion recover 3D scenes
from casual phone video [32, 82]. These methods provide the in-
gredients for proposing what surfaces might be made of. However,
visual recognition cannot observe every property relevant to touch,
such as compliance, and plausible visual predictions can produce
conspicuous haptic errors. TouchTwin therefore treats recognition
as an editable proposal.
This approach builds on mixed-initiative human–AI interaction,
in which automation assists users while leaving its outputs under-
standable and overridable [2, 38]. Simply adding human correc-
tion does not guarantee better team performance [7]. TouchTwin
presents a setting in which human and AI have complementary
information. The vision model proposes materials across a scene,
while a person with access to the physical referent can compare

those generated haptics with the real surfaces. The user then cor-
rects the same semantic unit that the AI proposed—a named mate-
rial region—through language or spatial editing.
Related 3D authoring systems also combine generated or in-
ferred content with human control. Language instructions revise
NeRFs [33], construct mixed-reality environments [22], edit radi-
ance fields [81], and support generative 3D design [52]. Style2Fab
provides a relevant interaction precedent; it automatically segments
meshes and classifies segments as functional or aesthetic, after
which users can review and revise those classifications before ap-
plying generative styles [26]. TouchTwin extends this proposal–
and–correction approach to haptic scene authoring, where users
can revise both the material description and the region to which it
applies.
Taken together, prior work provides many of the capabilities
needed for AI-assisted haptic scene authoring, but not the interac-
tion loop we study. Haptic rendering can resynthesize measured
material responses, language-based generation makes those re-
sponses accessible without signal-level expertise, and visual models
propose material properties from captured scenes. TouchTwin con-
nects these capabilities through named material regions that AI
can propose, people can evaluate through touch, and people can re-
vise through language and spatial editing. We found no end-to-end
system suitable as a direct baseline; our comparisons therefore use
the automatic proposal before revision and the naming baselines in
§4. A rated oracle-label condition would be needed to separate the
effects of recognition error from the remaining rendering gap (§7).
3
TouchTwin: A Propose–Feel–Revise Haptic
Authoring System
TouchTwin implements a propose–feel–revise workflow (Figure 2).
It represents a scene as editable material layers, each coupling
a spatial region and natural-language description with generated
haptic responses. From a phone scan, TouchTwin proposes an initial
layer stack. Users explore it with a haptic stylus and revise material
descriptions or region boundaries; each edit updates the feedback
without exposing low-level signal parameters.
3.1
Interaction Design and Workflow
A named material layer is the shared unit manipulated by the auto-
matic pipeline and the user: a mesh region, material description, and
the haptic models generated from that description. The pipeline ini-
tializes these layers from the scan, but its proposals remain editable.
Users therefore correct the same representation that the system
produces rather than switching to signal-level controls. Users al-
ternate between feeling and revising the layers. They compare the
rendered feel with a physical scene or an intended result, relabel
a surface that feels wrong, and repair a boundary or add a layer
when the spatial decomposition is wrong. Relabeling invokes the
same text-to-haptics path used for the automatic proposal. Thus,
the system might propose “varnished wood” and the user replace it
with “rough foam” without translating that correction into vibra-
tion or friction parameters. The loop supports replication, in which
users correct a draft against physical objects, and expression, in
which they begin with an empty stack. In both cases, users specify
what a surface should feel like and where that description applies;
TouchTwin handles how it is rendered.
3.2
Creating the Automatic Haptic Draft
TouchTwin creates its draft from a ∼30-second phone video. VGGT
recovers camera poses, depth, and a colored point cloud [82]; a
monocular diffusion model completes missing depth [32]; and TSDF
fusion produces the mesh. We remove unreachable surfaces, re-
pair small holes, and smooth a copy for haptic interaction. These
steps matter because holes can trap the haptic proxy, disconnected
fragments create barriers, and surface noise can mask synthesized
texture. We evaluate the resulting geometry in §4.1. The system
then builds named layers in three steps. SAM 2.1 segments capture
frames; we project their masks onto the mesh and combine observa-
tions by multi-view voting [63]. Qwen2.5-VL describes the material
and texture in a clear view of each region [5]. Finally, the language-
to-haptics model in §3.4 generates vibration and tapping responses
from each description. The result is deliberately a draft. Appearance
cannot reveal properties such as compliance, so plausible visual
classifications may still feel wrong.
3.3
Revising the Draft through Language and
Spatial Editing
The Viser-based browser interface [87] provides three operations
corresponding to semantic, boundary, and omission errors: relabel,
region repair, and add/delete (Figure 3). Relabel corrects a region
whose material feels wrong. The user selects its layer and types a
new name or description, such as replacing “varnished wood” with
“rough foam” (Figure 3a). TouchTwin regenerates the haptic mod-
els after typing settles and updates the running renderer without
restarting the scene. Region repair corrects a layer that covers too
much or too little surface. Users seed faces, then a SAM-assisted
selector expands or shrinks the selection using visual similarity, the
material description, and mesh adjacency (Figure 3b). They can also
transfer individual faces between neighboring layers. Add/delete
creates a named layer for an untextured region or removes one and
returns its faces to the base (Figure 3c). Color overlays show each
layer’s extent, and the haptic display identifies the material under
the stylus. The same operations support freeform authoring from
an empty stack.
3.4
Language as the Haptic Authoring Interface
Natural-language descriptions connect author intent to the ren-
dered signals. Users can enter material names such as “cardboard”
or richer descriptions such as gritty but cushioned. VLM proposals
and user descriptions follow the same text-to-haptics path, so a cor-
rection directly replaces the AI’s semantic proposal. We use Qian
et al.’s language-guided texture model [60]. It embeds a description
with CLIP ViT-L/14 [62] and maps the embedding into a latent space
learned from tool–surface interactions with 100 measured materi-
als [19]. The decoder generates force- and speed-dependent sliding
vibrations and speed-dependent tap responses. Thus, open descrip-
tions produce combinations of dynamics learned from measured
contact rather than arbitrary signals.

3.5
Haptic Rendering and Implementation
TouchTwin uses a grounded Phantom Touch with 3-DoF force
feedback (3D Systems) and an MM3C Haptuator voice-coil actuator
(TactileLabs) mounted near the stylus tip (Figure 4). Following the
HaTT framework [19, 21], the Phantom renders contact, friction,
and taps at a 1 kHz servo rate; the Haptuator renders sliding-texture
vibration.
At each contact, the renderer identifies the active layer on the
smoothed haptic mesh. A proxy algorithm renders stiff contact and
the selected material’s measured friction coefficient. Impacts trigger
speed-dependent tap transients. During sliding, an autoregressive
texture model conditioned on tangential hand speed and contact
force produces vibration above a 5 mm/s threshold. The Haptuator
places this signal near the simulated contact point, avoiding atten-
uation through the grounded device’s linkage. The 1 kHz signal is
sent over a 48 kHz audio channel with ∼1 ms buffers and ∼10 ms
reported output latency. Appendix A.4 reports renderer parameters.
Updated haptic models are generated outside the real-time servo
loop and published atomically, preventing generation from blocking
the 1 kHz contact loop or exposing a partial update. Users can
therefore feel a revised material without restarting the scene.

Characterizing the Automatic Haptic Draft
Before studying how people revise TouchTwin’s drafts, we charac-
terize the starting point itself: whether its geometry supports haptic
exploration, what material errors it contains, and how much correc-
tion remains. The workflow does not require a correct final scene,
but it does require a draft that users can explore and meaningfully
revise.

product when available and one from the same category otherwise.
Each scene contained four ground-truth (GT) surfaces (24 total). For
scoring, we resolve each open-ended VLM description to its nearest
library material in the model’s language-aligned latent space. We
report exact-material accuracy, accuracy within ten author-defined
families (wood, metal, plastic, foam/rubber, leather, fabric, paper,
ceramic/stone, abrasive, and carbon). We also use two signal-space
distances. Assignment distance compares the measured models of

4.1
Haptic Usability of the Reconstructed
Geometry
A visually acceptable mesh may still impede touch: holes can trap
the haptic proxy, disconnected fragments create barriers, and sur-
face noise can mask texture feedback. We therefore assessed geomet-
ric properties that directly affect stylus interaction. Compared with
Poisson reconstructions of the same VGGT point clouds, TouchTwin
reduced boundary-defect edges from 932–2,483 to 0–4 and compo-
nents from as many as 229 to 1–2 (Table 1). All intended surfaces
were proxy-reachable; residual planar warp was 0.4–1.8%, with
near-zero median dihedral roughness after smoothing. This does
not establish a generally superior reconstruction method, but veri-
fies a usable substrate for these near-planar scenes.

5
User Study: Evaluating Propose–Feel–Revise
Haptic Authoring
We conducted a within-subjects study of whether non-experts find
an automatic haptic draft useful, can improve it through semantic
and spatial edits, and can use the same mechanism for freeform
authoring.
5.1
Study Goals and Design
The study had three stages spanning replication and expression.
In the first two, participants compared an automatic draft with its
physical scene, rated its realism, edited until satisfied, and rated
the result, intent match, control, and workload. The two scenes

were counterbalanced and chosen from the six-scene benchmark
(Figure 6, Table A.1): exact-material accuracy was 75% for the easier
scene and 50% for the harder. Training used a separate scene.
In the third stage, participants began with an empty layer stack
and used the same operations without an automatic draft or physical
referent. They rated name–feel match, expressivity, and workload.
This exploratory stage examined expression rather than serving as
a replication baseline.
An earlier pilot included scratch, automatic-only, and correction
conditions to compare time and workload. Participants treated
scratch authoring as creation rather than replication, making those
comparisons uninterpretable. We excluded the pilot sessions and
instead compare automatic with revised drafts within participant;
freeform authoring remains a separate exploratory task.
5.2
Participants and Apparatus
Twelve right-handed participants (7 men, 5 women; ages 18–34;
Table A.2) took part under approval from the Anonymous Univer-
sity Institutional Review Board. Participation was voluntary and
uncompensated, and all participants gave informed consent. Eight
had little or no prior haptics experience, and none were haptic-
design experts; five had no 3D-tool experience. Sessions lasted
40–80 minutes.
Participants used the browser interface and haptic display de-
scribed in §3 (Figure 4). During replication, the physical objects
remained within reach for comparison. Scenes were reconstructed
beforehand, questionnaires ran in the browser, and TouchTwin
logged editing operations. Appendix A.1 provides the instruments.
5.3
Measures
For replication, the primary outcome was scene_match (1–7; “how
closely does the virtual scene feel to the real objects”), measured
before and after editing. Participants also rated intent match and
control. Secondary measures included raw NASA-TLX [34], SUS [6,
13], authoring-mode preference, time, and logged semantic and
spatial edits.
For freeform authoring, named_match measured whether each
region felt like its user-provided name. Participants also rated ex-
pressivity, control, and workload. These self-ratings assess intent–
output consistency, not physical realism.
Artifact-based measures from saved layers included face-weighted
material-family accuracy, GT-material rank among the 100 library
materials, and generated-model distance between a generated re-
sponse and the measured GT model. Together they distinguish
category fidelity (the appropriate kind of material) from specimen
fidelity (a particular sample’s response).
5.4
Analysis
Our confirmatory analysis averaged each participant’s auto-to-
revised realism change across the two scenes and tested it against
zero using a paired 𝑡-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test; we report
within-participant effect size 𝑑𝑧. Per-scene descriptives and analy-
ses of the 24 participant–scene pairs, presentation order, freeform
authoring, and artifacts were exploratory and uncorrected.
The NASA-TLX Performance item is reverse-anchored (0 = per-
fect, 100 = failure); two participants appeared to reverse it. We
recoded those responses as 100 −𝑥. Leaving them unchanged or
omitting the item did not alter the interpretation. Only the final
participant saw clarified anchors.
6
Results
6.1
The Automatic Draft Provides a Useful
Starting Point
Before editing, participants rated automatic drafts 4.00/7 (𝑆𝐷=1.38)
for realism but 5.4/7 as useful starting points. Comments identified
specific mismatches, including a smooth-feeling black region and
cardboard labeled as wood. Participants could diagnose these er-
rors through touch and revise the descriptions rather than author
responses from scratch.
6.2
Brief Revision Improves Perceived Realism
Mean realism increased from 4.00/7 before editing to 5.58/7 af-
terward (Figure 9). The mean participant-level change was +1.58
(𝑆𝐷=0.73), 𝑡(11)=7.48, 𝑝<.0001, 𝑑𝑧=2.16. Eleven participants im-
proved, one was unchanged, and none worsened (Wilcoxon on
nonzero changes, 𝑝=.001). Across 24 participant–scene pairs, 21
improved and none worsened. In exploratory, uncorrected compar-
isons, the harder scene improved by +1.92 points versus +1.25 for
the easier; second scenes improved by +2.00 versus +1.17 for first
scenes, though scene and order are confounded.
Revisions took a median of 6.6 minutes per scene (IQR 5.3–9.6).
Participants rated intent match 5.8/7 and control 5.5/7 after editing;
mean recoded raw NASA-TLX was 21.7/100. Thus, brief revision
produced a large perceived-realism gain with relatively low work-
load.
6.3
Participants Primarily Revise through
Language
In 14 of 24 replication trials, participants only relabeled materials;
the others combined relabeling with brush strokes, SAM-assisted
expansion, or face transfer; boundary repairs concentrated on the
easier scene (8 of those 10 trials). Named layers therefore often
turned an incorrect material assignment into a semantic correction
rather than a geometry edit.
The logs contained 294 generated-label events (247 relabels and
47 labels for new layers). After removing partial prefixes recorded
as typing settled, 150 committed descriptions remained; 69 labels
appeared in final scenes. Of those final labels, 91% were multi-word
and 91% used vocabulary absent from the 100 library names. They
ranged from “cardboard” to “loud, black plastic sheet, super low
friction, grainy like super fine sand.” However, we did not compare
free text with a searchable picker, and open-vocabulary use was
not associated with realism improvement (Spearman 𝜌=.16, 𝑝=.62).
These data describe how participants revised drafts, not whether
free text caused the realism gain.

Specimen Fidelity

6.5
From Replication to Freeform Haptic
Authoring
In the freeform stage, participants began with an empty layer stack
and created a median of four material regions in 3.8 minutes. They
rated named-material match at 5.08/7 (𝑆𝐷=1.24), above the scale
midpoint (𝑡(11)=3.03, 𝑝=.012); expressivity averaged 4.58/7 (Fig-
ure 10). Because participants evaluated their own descriptions with-
out a physical referent, these ratings indicate perceived intent–
output consistency, not that another person would identify the
generated materials in the same way.
Two participants rated named-material match at 3/7; one said
the regions felt too similar. The generated signals nevertheless
remained distinct: across 64 freeform-layer pairs, median signal
distance was 0.62, and only 6% fell below the minimum distance
among automatic proposals (0.24). Some signal differences may
therefore have been insufficiently salient through the stylus.
Preferences depended on the authoring goal. Nine participants
preferred starting from scratch for creative authoring, two preferred
correcting an automatic proposal, and one was indifferent. Their
comments favored drafts when a physical scene supplied a repli-
cation target, but an empty scene when they wanted expressive
control.
6.6
Usability and Participant Perspectives
SUS averaged 76.5/100 (“good”; range 62.5–95), and freeform NASA-
TLX averaged 19.9/100. Four themes in participants’ comments fur-
ther characterized the experience. (T1) Roughness: virtual textures
often felt bumpier than their physical counterparts. (T2) Near-miss
recognition: errors such as cardboard labeled as wood were no-
ticeable through touch and correctable through relabeling. (T3)
Missing compliance: the rigid proxy could not reproduce bulk
softness such as that of foam. (T4) Tool mediation: a stylus con-
veyed fewer cues than direct hand contact. Thus, semantic errors
could often be revised, whereas missing display cues could not be
recovered by changing a description.
7
Discussion
7.1
Imperfect AI Can Be Useful When Its
Output Is Experiential and Editable
TouchTwin’s central interaction finding is not that its automatic
material recognition was consistently correct, but that an imperfect
draft remained useful when users could experience and revise it.
Touch exposed mismatches that looked plausible on screen, and
participants usually corrected them by renaming a region rather
than rebuilding its geometry or tuning a signal. This makes the
target sense part of the feedback loop: the generated output is
inspected through touch, and the resulting perceptual judgment
guides the next edit. The pattern extends mixed-initiative interac-
tion principles [2, 38] by suggesting that sensory AI output should
be both editable and directly experienceable. For replication tasks,
a person who can access the physical object could revise a scan
before sharing it with remote shoppers, museum visitors, or collab-
orators. The supplemental video illustrates this workflow on stock
videos of an antique typewriter and a fabric scene, although these
examples are demonstrations rather than evaluations. Such uses
fit TouchTwin’s category-level goal; inspection or quality-control
applications would additionally require specimen- and defect-level
fidelity.
7.2
Language as a Shared Representation for
Human–AI Haptic Authoring
Language did more than replace sliders. Material descriptions formed
a shared representation across the automatic proposal, the interface,
and the haptic generator. The system proposed a named region, the
user interpreted and edited that name, and the generator converted
the revised description into a response. Users therefore did not have
to translate a felt mismatch into spectral, friction, or force param-
eters. Their frequent use of multi-word descriptions outside the
library vocabulary also suggests that the shared representation can
accommodate distinctions users naturally want to express. How-
ever, the study did not compare free text with a searchable material
picker, so it does not establish that language is always the most
efficient input method. Its demonstrated value here is continuity
between human and AI representations: both parties act on the
same understandable unit while a model grounded in measured
interactions handles the mapping to renderable signals [60].

7.3
Different Roles for AI in Replication and
Expression
Participants wanted different levels of AI initiative for different
goals. When reproducing a physical scene, the automatic proposal
provided a starting point and hypotheses that could be checked
against a referent. When creating a new haptic scene, most par-
ticipants preferred an empty canvas, where suggestions were less
useful than control over what to express. This distinction argues
against a single default role for generative assistance: AI can lead
with a draft when a target exists, but recede to optional suggestions
when intent is still being formed. The latter turns surface-level
haptic authoring into a descriptive task—for example, allowing a
VR author to explore “brushed aluminum” and “powder-coated
steel” without first learning signal design [67, 72]. More generally,
sensory authoring tools should adapt initiative to whether users
are matching an external referent or developing an expressive one.
7.4
Design Implications for Human–AI Sensory
Authoring
Align the AI output with the user’s correction unit. In TouchTwin,
a named material region is simultaneously what the system infers,
what the user feels, and what the user can revise. This alignment
turns an inference error into a local correction instead of forcing
users to discard the draft or manipulate a lower-level representation.
Other sensory authoring systems could apply the same principle
by exposing sound sources, lighting regions, or physical behaviors
as editable units whose boundaries and meanings remain legible
throughout the pipeline.
Ground open-ended controls in measured experience. Lan-
guage gives users a broad vocabulary, but a sensory interface must
ultimately produce a physical signal. TouchTwin maps descriptions
through a model trained on measured tool–surface interactions, al-
lowing expressive input while keeping output tied to the dynamics
the renderer can represent. This grounding does not guarantee a
match to a particular specimen, and unsupported properties such
as compliance cannot be recovered by more descriptive wording.
Designers should therefore make the coverage of the underlying
measurements visible and combine open-ended input with bounded
alternatives when predictable output is more important than ex-
pressivity.
Evaluate both editable outcomes and fidelity beyond the
interaction loop. Participants could directly optimize whether a
scene felt plausible and whether regions represented the intended
material category; both measures improved after editing. Distance
to the recorded physical specimen did not reliably improve, be-
cause users neither saw that metric nor entered exact library iden-
tities. Evaluations of sensory AI should likewise separate perceived
quality, task-level semantic agreement, and physical signal fidelity.
Reporting these levels together clarifies what human revision can
repair, what remains a renderer or sensing limitation, and which
application claims the evidence supports.
8
Limitations and Future Work
Participants, scenes, and uses. Our 12 participants were ages
18–34, mostly engineering students, at one site with one device.
The two before/after scenes are fixed effects and do not support
generalization across scenes. All six captures are near-planar table-
top arrangements, favorable to reconstruction, mask lifting, and
VLM cropping; curvature, clutter, occlusion, specularity, and thin
structures remain untested. Participants also saw visibly blurry re-
constructions while rating feel, creating a possible visual confound.
Future studies should include more diverse participants, devices,
scenes, and capture conditions. Touchable scans may eventually
support nonvisual access, but our participants were sighted and a
stylus may not be the preferred interface; that direction requires
participatory design with blind and low-vision users.
Measures, validity, and ground truth. Participants knew they
had edited before re-rating each scene, so demand effects could
inflate the subjective improvement. The physical objects anchored
both ratings, three participant–scene pairs remained unchanged,
and material-family accuracy computed from the saved layers in-
creased from 52% to 92%. This convergence strengthens the realism
result but does not eliminate the possible bias. Neither measure
establishes fidelity to a particular specimen: a description can iden-
tify the right category without producing the signal nearest to one
recorded sample. We also defined the ten material families, and
another taxonomy could change the family-level results. Some dis-
continued library products were replaced by another brand of the
same category, so the measured GT exemplar did not always match
the object on the desk. Editing did not improve generated-model
distance on the harder scene (§6.4), and realism was measured per
scene rather than per region. A rated oracle-label condition would
separate recognition error from the remaining rendering gap, while
a searchable library-picker baseline would test the contribution of
free-text input. Per-region psychophysics, physical-sample 2AFC
comparisons, and blind cross-participant identification would pro-
vide stronger tests of name–feel correspondence.
Recognition and rendering scope. The automatic proposals
selected the exact material for only 13 of 24 surfaces in our small
benchmark, so fidelity-sensitive uses still require human review and
revision. The model also cannot represent bulk compliance in food
or soft tissue, the hand of a fabric, or thermal cues. Holding each
1 kHz texture sample across the audio buffer without reconstruction
filtering introduces energy in the Pacinian band and may explain
reports of excess roughness (§3.5). Layer transitions do not cross-
fade, and we did not validate our use of the contact binormal rather
than the surface normal. Two participants found distinct textures
hard to distinguish through the stylus, and reconstruction errors
also remain consequential. Follow-up work should measure coil
spectra and end-to-end latency, add transition filtering, validate
rendering direction, and incorporate compliance or thermal displays
where the application requires those cues.
Authoring at scale. A median 6.6-minute edit is plausible for a
single product or small scene but costly across a room or building,
and the present workflow assumes that one person revises one layer
stack. Larger deployments will require faster on-device processing,
reusable or shared material layers, and higher-level operations such
as “make everything on the left metallic.” Evaluating those mecha-
nisms on larger scenes and in multi-author workflows is necessary
before the approach can support environment-scale authoring.

9
Conclusion
TouchTwin treats AI-generated haptic content as an experiential,
editable draft rather than a final prediction. It turns a phone scan
into named material regions that people can feel and revise using
the same semantic representation as the automation. In our study,
participants used this propose–feel–revise loop to improve imper-
fect replicas and used the same mechanism to create new material
experiences, while preferring different levels of AI initiative for
replication and expression. These findings suggest a broader direc-
tion for human–AI sensory authoring: align the AI’s output with
the user’s correction unit, ground open-ended input in measured
experience, and evaluate both outcomes users can perceive and
fidelity limits they cannot.
