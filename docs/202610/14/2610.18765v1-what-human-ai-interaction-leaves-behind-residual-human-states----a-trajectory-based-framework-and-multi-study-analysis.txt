# Abstract 

Human–AI Interaction is commonly evaluated by what an artificial agent enables during use, yet interaction can also alter the human who acts next. We introduce Residual Human States, a trajectory-based framework for representing the potentially multidimensional human state carried forward after interaction with AI, relative to a relevant alternative trajectory. The framework distinguishes assisted output from human-state transition and derives four empirical propositions: multidimensionality, interaction-policy dependence, horizon dissociation, and temporal persistence. Its central claim is that assisted performance does not by itself determine the human state carried forward. We examine these propositions through four empirical cases with different inferential roles. Published medical-training evidence illustrates how one AI interaction history can be as-sociated with both slightly higher observed accuracy on some later unaided cases and transmission of a systematic decision bias on others. A second diagnostic-learning study shows that later unaided behaviour differs across AI-support policies, while also revealing that aggregate accuracy cannot by itself distinguish improved discrimination from a shifted response criterion. In a classroom experiment, generative-AI tutoring substantially improved assisted practice performance without a corresponding detectable gain on a subsequent unaided exam. A longitudinal reanalysis of personalised AI dialogue further shows that residual contrasts can evolve across post-interaction horizons and that such change may arise from the comparison trajectory as well as from the focal intervention. Evaluating interactive AI systems therefore requires attention not only to contemporaneous performance, but also to which human states alternative interaction trajectories produce, relative to which counterfactuals, and how durably.

# 1 Introduction 

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly evaluated through two closely related questions: what the model can do, and what humans can accomplish with it in a Human–AI Interaction (HAI) context. Both remain arXiv:2610.18765v1 [cs.HC] 13 Oct 2026 Residual Human States under Human–AI Interaction A Preprint 

essential. Yet they share a temporal boundary: evaluation commonly ends with the assisted episode. That boundary becomes incomplete when interaction with AI changes the person who will later reason, decide, learn, challenge, transfer, or intervene. Recent evidence shows why this distinction matters. Generative-AI support can raise practice performance without a detectable corresponding gain in later unaided performance under some interaction policies (Bastani et al., 2025a); AI-supported practice can also produce positive transfer (Lira et al., 2025); AI interaction can transmit systematic bias into later unaided decisions, alongside slightly higher observed accuracy on other cases (Vicente et al., 2025a); and later unaided diagnostic accuracy can differ across ways of structuring AI support, particularly whether trial-level feedback is available (Cabitza and Vicente, 2026a). Other work shows that improved AI-assisted reasoning need not be accompanied by correspondingly accurate metacognitive monitoring (Fernandes et al., 2026), while longitudinal intervention evidence demonstrates that differences created by AI-mediated support can change across immediate and delayed post-interaction horizons (Corbett and Tangen, 2026a). The common lesson is not that AI assistance uniformly improves or erodes human capability. It is that assisted performance does not determine the human state carried forward .A closely related recent account is the concept of fluent dependence proposed by Anderson and Zaza (2026), which identifies a validity problem arising when AI-mediated output satisfies evaluative criteria without corresponding development of the human capability that the output appears to demonstrate. Their framework makes the output–capability gap explicit and proposes paired AI-mediated and AI-withdrawn tasks as a diagnostic strategy. The present paper addresses a broader trajectory question. Rather than asking only whether mediated performance overstates human capability, we ask how alternative histories of Human–AI Interaction leave differences across multiple dimensions of the human state, relative to relevant alternative trajectories, and how those differences evolve across post-interaction horizons. Several established literatures explain parts of this divergence. Cognitive offloading shows why distributing cognitive work across external resources can be adaptive rather than pathological (Risko and Gilbert, 2016); extended-cognition accounts similarly resist treating unaided performance as the only legitimate benchmark (Clark, 2025). Research on trust and reliance shows that advice-taking changes with experience (Lee and See, 2004; Chacon et al., 2022; Eckhardt et al., 2025), while work on learning and skill distinguishes current task success from later acquisition, transfer, or decay (Yan et al., 2024; Macnamara et al., 2024). More recent work makes temporality explicit through algorithmic habituation, repeated-exposure designs, path-dependent interaction, and coupled human–AI dynamics (Herrera, 2026; Mladin et al., 2026; Wang and Baiocchi, 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Park et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Each of these literatures, however, addresses a particular outcome, mechanism, or timescale; they do not yet provide a common way to describe how several dimensions of the human state differ after one interaction history compared with another. The unresolved problem is therefore one of representation and empirical coordination, broader than either longitudinal exposure or the validity of inferring human capability from AI-mediated output: how should we describe the multidimensional human state produced by one history of human–AI interaction relative to another? This paper proposes a trajectory-based framework for that higher-level problem. Its central object is the 

residual human state : the role-relevant, potentially multidimensional state carried forward by the human after a history of HAI. Rather than asking only what a human–AI configuration produces at episode t, we distinguish the observable output Yt from the human state Xt+1 that enters the next episode. The paper makes three contributions through the Residual Human States framework. First, it introduces a common state-transition representation in which competence, judgment, reliance, motivation or metacognition, bias, agency, and other domain-relevant dimensions can change simultaneously without being assumed commensurable. Second, it extends the evaluation object beyond the output–capability relation by defining residual human-state change relative to an alternative interaction trajectory and by making post-interaction horizon explicit, from which four empirical propositions are derived concerning multidimensionality, policy dependence, horizon dissociation, and temporal persistence. Third, it coordinates four empirical cases within this trajectory-relative framework while deliberately matching analytical depth to the inferential role of each case. The four cases are not treated as estimates of a common effect size or as equally strong tests of a common latent construct. The contribution is therefore not the generic claim that AI use can affect later human behaviour, nor that such effects may change over time. It is the coordination of these phenomena within a trajectory-relative representation that distinguishes assisted output from the human state carried forward, makes the comparison trajectory part of the estimand, and indexes residual differences explicitly by post-interaction horizon.

# 2 State of the Art 

The purpose of this section is not to provide a comprehensive review of Human–AI Interaction, but to identify the conceptual elements required to represent what interaction leaves in the human after assistance. Several established literatures already explain important parts of this problem. Cognitive offloading and extended cognition clarify why dependence on external resources is not equivalent to deterioration; research on trust and reliance shows that interaction changes how humans use algorithmic advice; learning, bias, and metacognition demonstrate that different human outcomes can diverge; and longitudinal work on habituation, repeated exposure, and path dependence establishes that these effects unfold over time. Taken together, however, these literatures do not yet provide a common representation of the human state produced by an interaction history. They typically focus on particular outcomes, mechanisms, or temporal processes rather than on how multiple human dimensions can be compared across alternative interaction trajectories and post-interaction horizons. The subsections that follow therefore review the literature selectively around this representational problem. Each subsection is organised around one guiding question: how to distinguish a changed human from a missing external resource (Section 2.1); whether different dimensions of the human state change together or diverge, and whether this depends on task and domain (Section 2.2); and how such changes depend on interaction history and evolve after interaction ends (Section 2.3). The final subsection identifies the representational gap that motivates the Residual Human States framework. 

2.1 Cognitive offloading, extended cognition, and assisted performance 

When performance falls after AI is removed, how can we tell whether the human has changed or whether a useful external resource is simply missing? Answering this question requires distinguishing functional dependence on an external cognitive resource from a change in the human produced by the history of using that resource. This distinction is essential for residual human-state analysis because a decline in performance when assistance is removed may reflect the loss of a useful external component rather than deterioration of the human state itself. The idea that cognition can be reorganised through technological coupling long predates contemporary AI. Licklider’s man–computer symbiosis framed humans and computers as contributors to a joint process (Licklider, 1960); the extended-mind thesis later argued that external resources can, under appropriate conditions, become constitutive of cognitive activity (Clark and Chalmers, 1998). Generative AI makes this perspective newly salient because external systems now participate in formulation, explanation, synthesis, planning, and reasoning (Clark, 2025). Cognitive offloading provides the direct psychological foundation. External resources can alter task demands, reduce unnecessary burden, and improve performance (Risko and Gilbert, 2016). This establishes an important boundary for residual human-state analysis: poorer performance after a useful resource is removed is not, by itself, evidence that prior use damaged human competence. Functional dependence on a tool must be distinguished from a change in the human produced by the history of using it. Evaluation has often approached this coupling through contemporaneous outcomes: whether human–AI combinations outperform humans alone, AI alone, or the stronger component. Meta-analytic evidence shows substantial heterogeneity and cautions against assuming synergy (Vaccaro et al., 2024). Its primary object, however, remains configuration performance while human and AI operate together, not the capability, strategy, disposition, or metacognitive state carried by the human into later activity.

2.2 Learning, reliance, bias, metacognition, and human adaptation 

Do different dimensions of the human state change together after AI interaction, or can they diverge, and does this depend on the task and domain in which the interaction takes place? Once functional dependence is distinguished from human-state change, a second problem appears: interaction with AI can alter several human outcomes that need not move together. Competence, reliance, bias, metacognition, and related dimensions therefore need to be considered separately before they can be coordinated within a common post-interaction representation. Trust and reliance research has long shown that reliance should be calibrated to system capability and context rather than maximised indiscriminately (Lee and See, 2004). More recent HAI work treats reliance as dynamic and sensitive to advice quality, task structure, and interaction design (Eckhardt et al., 2025). Longitudinal evidence further shows that advice-taking changes with repeated exposure (Chacon et al., 2022). The same temporal distinction appears in learning and skill. Bastani et al. (2025a) report that unrestricted generative-AI assistance can substantially raise practice performance while reducing later unaided mathematics performance, whereas a scaffolded tutor mitigates much of that effect; as revisited in Section 5.3, the reduction under unrestricted assistance is sensitive to small-sample cluster-robust inference, whereas for the scaffolded tutor no later difference was detected despite large assisted gains. Lira et al. (2025) report improved later unaided writing after AI-supported practice. In medical training, Vicente et al. (2025a) show that trainees who received AI advice reproduced an AI system’s systematic bias in later unaided decisions, together with slightly higher observed accuracy on cases where the AI had been correct. Cabitza and Vicente (2026a) further report a policy-specific difference in later unaided accuracy on cases governed by a hidden diagnostic criterion, which the authors interpret as evidence of knowledge transfer. As discussed in Section 5.2, the observed accuracy probe also admits an alternative response-criterion interpretation, so the present paper uses this study primarily to test interaction-policy dependence rather than to identify the underlying learning mechanism. Metacognition adds another dimension. Fernandes et al. (2026) report that LLM assistance improves logical-reasoning performance while users remain poorly calibrated in judging their own performance, demonstrating that better assisted output and better self-monitoring need not move together. Anderson and Zaza (2026) integrate one important subset of these findings under the construct of fluent dependence , which reframes AI-mediated performance as a construct-validity problem (Section 1). Residual human-state analysis has a different object: the difference in human state produced by one interaction trajectory relative to another, which may involve competence but also judgment, reliance, metacognition, internalised bias, and agency, and does not require the comparison trajectory to be AI-free. Together, these findings show that competence, reliance, bias, and metacognition can respond differently to AI-mediated interaction. A general account therefore cannot use any one of these variables as a universal proxy for the human state. The same evidence also spans mathematics, writing, medical diagnosis, and logical reasoning, which raises the further question of whether such divergence depends on task and domain. Because these studies also differ in interaction policy, population, outcome, and design, they cannot isolate a domain effect. Within the framework developed below, task and domain enter through the contextual conditions Kt (Section 3.1), so domain dependence is treated as an open empirical question rather than as an established property. 

2.3 Repeated exposure, path dependence, persistence, and recovery 

How do these changes depend on the history of interaction, and do they persist, fade, or recover relative to an alternative trajectory once interaction ends? A multidimensional account also requires an explicit temporal structure. Residual differences can depend on the order, repetition, and duration of interaction, and they may persist, attenuate, strengthen, converge, or recover after the AI-containing episode has ended. The relevant problem is therefore not only whether interaction history matters, but how trajectory-relative differences evolve across post-interaction horizons. Habituation provides one explanatory vocabulary for repeated interaction. Passive reliance can become normalised through repeated HAI, with potential consequences for deliberation, judgment, autonomy, intention, and second-order agency (Herrera, 2026). Algorithmic habituation has likewise been framed as gradual accommodation to recurrent AI-mediated patterns (Mladin et al., 2026). Repeated-exposure methodology and large-scale user–LLM evidence further show why exposure histories and early interaction patterns matter (Wang and Baiocchi, 2026; Zhu et al., 2026).

Path dependence is related but broader than habituation. Formal models now represent human capability and delegation as coupled variables and analyse longer-term human–AI coevolution across multiple timescales (Park et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). These developments establish that neither “time matters” nor “interaction history matters” is a novel proposition by itself. What remains less systematically integrated is the evolution of a trajectory contrast after interaction. Corbett and Tangen (2026a) provide an especially useful example: a personalised corrective AI dialogue produced a larger immediate reduction in psychology misconceptions than a textbook refutation or neutral AI dialogue, and both corrective interventions remained superior to the neutral control two months later. The published longitudinal analysis already tested a Condition × Time interaction on a common complete-case sample; however, the comparisons highlighted in the article combine samples and adjustments, and comparator-specific changes in the contrast were not estimated with their uncertainty. This pattern illustrates why persistence, decay, and convergence should be represented as empirical properties of a residual contrast rather than assumed from an immediate post-test. 

2.4 The representational gap 

The framework proposed here also builds on established traditions rather than replacing them. The distinction between performance during acquisition and what is retained afterwards is central to research on learning versus performance and desirable difficulties (Soderstrom and Bjork, 2015); the horizon-dissociation proposition developed in Section 3.5 transposes it to settings in which the assisted output is produced jointly with an AI system. Work on the ironies of automation and on automation complacency and bias showed that automated support can let the manual and cognitive skills needed when automation fails deteriorate through disuse (Bainbridge, 1983), and can reduce operators’ monitoring of automated aids and bias their decisions towards automated recommendations (Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010), and recent observational evidence from colonoscopy reports lower unaided adenoma detection after routine exposure to AI assistance (Budzyń et al., 2025), a pattern of the kind the framework is designed to represent. Formally, the residual contrast defined in Section 3 is a contrast between potential outcomes under two treatment trajectories evaluated at a given horizon, in the sense of causal inference for time-varying treatments (Robins, 1986; Hernán and Robins, 2020). What the present framework adds is not this causal notation, but the coordination of several human-state dimensions in one object, the explicit treatment of the comparison trajectory as part of the estimand, and the decomposition of temporal change into focal and comparator movement. The literature therefore leaves us beyond a generic “longitudinal gap.” HAI is temporally adaptive, assisted performance can diverge from later human outcomes, interaction policy can matter, and residual change is not uniformly positive or negative. What remains missing is a higher-level representational layer connecting these findings. Three requirements recur: 

G1. A common human-state object. The affected human cannot be represented adequately by performance, reliance, or competence alone when different dimensions may change simultaneously and in different directions. 

G2. A trajectory-relative contrast. Post-interaction change cannot generally be inferred from a simple pre–post comparison because the relevant comparison trajectory also evolves through practice, time, and experience. The counterfactual must therefore be another trajectory rather than an immutable baseline. 

G3. A cross-domain temporal language. The framework must accommodate learning, habituation, bias, metacognition, skill change, persistence, and recovery without asserting a single mechanism or universal timescale. Together, these requirements motivate a trajectory-based representational layer in which the human state is treated as a distinct evaluation object, residual change is defined relative to an alternative interaction trajectory, and post-interaction horizon is made explicit. 

# 3 Method: A Trajectory-Based Residual Human-State Framework 

The purpose of the framework is to provide a common analytical language for human changes that are currently studied through separate constructs such as learning, reliance, bias, metacognition, habituation, and recovery. Rather than treating these phenomena as manifestations of a single psychological mechanism, we represent them as potentially distinct changes in a multidimensional human state produced by an ordered history of Human–AI Interaction. The method therefore proceeds in four steps. First, it separates assisted

task performance from the human state carried forward after interaction. Second, it represents that state as the result of an interaction trajectory rather than a single episode. Third, it defines residual human-state change relative to a relevant alternative trajectory and makes post-interaction time explicit. Finally, it derives observable residual-state regimes and four empirical propositions that can be instantiated using domain-specific measures. The resulting framework is thus a representational and measurement-oriented method: it specifies the objects to be compared and the contrasts to be estimated without assuming that different human-state dimensions are directly observable, commensurable, or generated by the same underlying process.

3.5 Empirical propositions derived from the framework 

The framework yields four propositions that organise the empirical analysis. 

P1: Multidimensionality. Alternative interaction trajectories may produce simultaneous residual con-sequences that differ in substantive and evaluative meaning. The representational implication is that post-interaction change need not be adequately described by a single scalar outcome: one trajectory may improve one observed dimension while worsening another. Whether such multidirectional consequences reflect distinct latent components of the human state is a separate empirical question. A common underlying mechanism may generate different observed consequences across tasks or case classes, and distinct state components may themselves be correlated. P1 is therefore a representational proposition about the possibility and evaluative relevance of multidimensional residual change; the empirical case examined below establishes the need for a non-scalar description of observed consequences, not the independence of multiple latent human-state components. 

P2: Policy dependence. Residual outcomes can depend on how AI capability is delivered, not merely on whether AI is available. Formally, for policies P1̸ = P2, the induced trajectories can satisfy ∆Xres (Γ( P1), Γc)̸ = ∆ Xres (Γ( P2), Γc).

# 4 Empirical Application of the Residual Human States Framework: Four Studies 

This section translates the representation into an empirical strategy. For each of four cases selected to examine complementary implications of Propositions P1–P4, we identify the interaction trajectory, the comparison trajectory, the observable post-interaction probe, and the horizon at which that probe is observed, preserving the domain-specific meaning of the original outcomes. The cases deliberately play different inferential roles. Study 1 (P1) uses published post-interaction aggregate evidence to illustrate why a scalar description of residual change can be inadequate. Study 2 (P2) uses released participant-level data to estimate direct between-policy contrasts on a subsequent unaided behavioural probe. Study 3 (P3) reproduces and extends the original clustered regression analysis with direct policy contrasts and small-sample cluster-robust inference. Study 4 (P4) reconstructs the released longitudinal data and estimates comparator-specific temporal contrasts under a common repeated-measures specification. The empirical objective is therefore structured triangulation rather than methodological symmetry: analytical depth is matched to the proposition and to what each original design can identify. 

4.1 Study selection and common analytical strategy 

The empirical component is a theory-driven coordinated secondary analysis, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Studies are selected because they satisfy four criteria: (i) an intervention contrast involving AI-mediated interaction; (ii) an observable human outcome during or after interaction that can be mapped to a domain-specific state probe; (iii) publicly accessible data or analysis materials; and (iv) complementary alignment with one of Propositions P1–P4. The studies differ in domain, outcome scale, intervention, and temporal horizon; no pooled effect size is assumed or estimated. The purposive selection is intended to demonstrate how the framework can be operationalised across distinct designs; it does not estimate how frequently these patterns occur in the wider HAI literature. The analysis code was developed with the assistance of a generative AI tool (Claude, Anthropic); all scripts were reviewed by the authors, and the secondary analyses of Studies 2–4 are reproduced from the released data by the code described in the Data and code availability section. 4.2 Study 1: Published illustration of multidirectional residual consequences 

Vicente et al. (2025a) recruited 86 medical students and assigned them to an AI-assisted group or an unassisted control group. During the assisted phase, the simulated AI was accurate on most cases but systematically wrong on one specific class of difficult cases. Both groups subsequently completed an unaided phase. The design therefore provides a useful illustration of post-interaction consequences because the critical outcomes are observed after AI support has been removed. For P1, we use the published Phase 2 aggregate results as an empirical illustration rather than as a framework-specific participant-level reanalysis. Two observed consequences are distinguished. The first is an accuracy-oriented outcome on case classes for which the AI had supplied correct guidance. The second is a bias-oriented outcome reflecting later reproduction and generalisation of the AI’s systematic error pattern. The purpose of the case is not to establish that these outcomes correspond to independent latent components of the human state. It is to show why one interaction history may require more than a single scalar description when its later behavioural consequences differ in evaluative direction. This qualification is particularly important because the AI’s systematic error can be represented as a shifted decision threshold on the underlying dark/light-cell ratio. A transmitted criterion could therefore improve classification on some case classes while worsening it on others. In addition, the published proportion tests treat multiple classification responses as independent although responses are nested within participants. We consequently use the published estimates descriptively as an illustration of multidirectional residual consequences and do not rely on their reported trial-level significance tests as new inferential evidence for P1.

4.3 Study 2: Policy-conditioned later unaided behaviour 

Cabitza and Vicente (2026a) studied 289 medical students in a simulated diagnostic task under different AI-support policies. The published report distinguishes AI advice alone, advice accompanied by confidence information, and a Feedback condition providing trial-by-trial information about the correctness of both the participant’s and the AI’s decisions. The authors interpret the resulting pattern as policy-dependent diagnostic learning. The released participant-level data allow a narrower framework-specific question to be tested directly: whether subsequent unaided behaviour differs across interaction policies. For P2, the focal outcome is each participant’s subsequent unaided accuracy on the 30 Phase 2 cases governed by the hidden diagnostic criterion. The central contrasts compare Feedback with Advice and with Advice+Confidence. The proposition does not require the behavioural difference to arise from a particular learning mechanism; it asks whether the observed residual outcome differs across delivery policies. An important measurement limitation is inherent to the design of the probe. Every hidden-criterion case has “positive” as its correct response. Higher accuracy on this restricted probe can therefore reflect either improved discrimination of the hidden cue or a more liberal tendency to respond “positive” when only one known criterion is present. The present analysis does not attempt to identify uniquely between those mechanisms. It treats the outcome as an observed post-interaction behavioural probe and interprets the between-policy contrasts as evidence of policy dependence at that level. Assignment to conditions used a pre-specified mapping from birth month rather than a random-number generator; consequently, causal interpretation requires the additional assumption that this assignment was as-if random with respect to the relevant outcomes.

# 5 Results and Analysis 

We now examine the four propositions, proposition by proposition, using the empirical mappings of Section 4. Each subsection specifies the trajectory contrast implied by the proposition and the observable probe of the corresponding human-state component, and then distinguishes three analytical levels: the result reported in the original publication, its representation in the framework, and the framework-specific contrast estimated from the released data where the original authors did not report it. Re-expressing a published result within a common theoretical space is not equivalent to estimating a new effect.

5.5 Cross-study synthesis 

The four studies should not be read as replications of one psychological effect. They differ in domain, intervention, state component, measurement scale, and temporal design. Their value lies instead in examining four structurally distinct implications of the same trajectory-based representation, with P1 serving as an illustration and P2–P4 as inferential contrasts. Table 8 summarises the empirical correspondence. Several conclusions follow from the joint pattern. First, residual consequences need not be scalar. The Vicente case illustrates how one prior interaction history can be associated with slightly higher observed accuracy on some case classes while transmitting a decision rule that substantially worsens performance on others. This does not require the two consequences to represent independent latent-state changes. Second, the relevant exposure cannot be reduced to binary AI access. In Cabitza and Vicente, subsequent unaided behaviour differs directly across support policies: Feedback exceeds both Advice and Advice+Confidence on the hidden-criterion accuracy probe, although the restricted outcome does not uniquely identify whether the difference reflects improved discrimination or a response-criterion shift. In Bastani et al., no difference from Control was detected on the paired unaided exam for the policy producing the largest assisted gain, and the nominal GPT Base reduction and Tutor–Base exam advantage do not survive small-sample cluster-robust inference. # 6 Discussion 

The analyses support a broader change in the evaluation boundary of Human–AI Interaction. The relevant question is not only what a human–AI configuration achieves while assistance is available, but also whether different interaction histories leave different human states afterwards. The implications are therefore primarily structural. The Residual Human States framework does not prescribe independence from AI, nor does it treat every post-interaction difference as learning, harm, or deskilling. It provides a framework for asking when later human capability matters, how interaction policy may shape that capability, which comparison trajectory is relevant, and how any resulting difference evolves over time. 

6.1 From AI performance to trajectory-based evaluation 

The Residual Human States framework adds a second evaluative object to HAI without replacing established measures of model capability, joint performance, trust, reliance, usability, or immediate task success: what AI enables now + what humans carry forward .

The second term matters when humans are expected to interpret, challenge, transfer, recover, or continue acting without equivalent assistance. In such settings, evaluating only contemporaneous performance leaves part of the coupled system’s longer-term performance envelope unobserved. This distinction also separates residual-state analysis from simple AI-withdrawal comparisons. Withdrawal can reveal what a person can do once assistance is removed, but a performance drop does not establish that prior AI use degraded the human. Functional dependence on a useful tool and trajectory-induced human change are different phenomena. A residual effect requires comparison with a relevant alternative trajectory: ∆Xres (Γ , Γc; τ ).

Accordingly, the framework does not privilege unaided performance as intrinsically superior. It asks whether alternative interaction histories produce meaningfully different later human states. 

6.2 Interaction policy as a trajectory-shaping variable 

The interaction policy Pt is not merely an interface choice. It determines when the human reasons, when AI intervenes, which errors or disagreements become visible, and whether feedback is processed as information for future action or consumed only as a current solution. Comparable AI capability can therefore generate different human trajectories when delivered through answer-first assistance, confidence cues, critique, progressive hints, or corrective feedback. The empirical evidence supports this distinction at the structural level. The diagnostic case shows that later unaided behaviour differs across assistance policies, although the available outcome cannot determine whether the difference reflects improved discrimination, a shifted response criterion, or both. The mathematics case likewise shows that policies can be clearly separated by assisted performance without an equally clear separation on the subsequent unaided probe. The robust conclusion is therefore not that one policy universally produces more learning, but that different delivery policies can be associated with different later outcomes, which makes them candidates for prospective manipulation as causal design variables.

6.3 Human agency and governable dependence 

Residual human-state analysis is compatible with useful offloading and deliberate delegation. Offloading can improve performance (Risko and Gilbert, 2016; Clark, 2025), reliance should be calibrated rather than eliminated (Lee and See, 2004; Eckhardt et al., 2025), and repeated dependence on AI need not be intrinsically maladaptive (Herrera, 2026; Mladin et al., 2026). The objective is therefore not maximal human independence, but an appropriate distribution of capability across the human–AI system. Which capabilities should remain human is role-dependent. Some functions may be delegated with little consequence; others must remain sufficiently available because humans are expected to interpret, contest, supervise, recover, or assume responsibility. A person may therefore remain formally “in the loop” while the practical capacity to exercise those functions changes over time. This motivates the idea of governable dependence : dependence that is observable, role-appropriate, and compatible with preservation or development of those human capacities that remain functionally necessary. The Residual Human States framework does not determine which capacities should be preserved; it makes their evolution an explicit empirical question. 

6.4 Limitations and future research 

The present evidence has clear inferential boundaries. None of the four studies was originally designed to validate the Residual Human States framework, so the empirical analyses provide structured secondary evidence rather than prospective validation of a complete latent-state model. The studies also differ in domain, outcome, intervention, and horizon; their results are structurally comparable but not metrically commensurable. Observable outcomes should therefore be interpreted as probes of selected human-state dimensions rather than direct measurements of the full state vector Xt. Because the four cases were purposively selected to illustrate the propositions, they do not indicate how prevalent these properties are in Human–AI Interaction research. The empirical depth is intentionally asymmetric. Study 1 is primarily illustrative: its multidirectional outcomes may arise from a common transmitted decision criterion and do not establish distinct latent-state changes. Study 2 provides stronger evidence of policy-dependent later behaviour, but its restricted accuracy probe cannot separate discrimination from response criterion, and causal interpretation depends on the plausibility of the birth-month assignment as effectively random. Because the two focal comparisons and the hidden-criterion probe were chosen knowing that the original article reported differences there, this analysis should be interpreted as a targeted secondary analysis rather than as a fully confirmatory test. Study 3 benefits from classroom-level allocation that the original authors treat as randomised but is constrained by only 44 unequally allocated clusters and limited within-teacher treatment variation; its interpretation therefore rests on small-sample robust inference rather than conventional CR1 significance. Moreover, its unaided outcome is measured minutes after assisted practice and should not be interpreted as evidence of durable retention. Study 4 provides the clearest evidence about temporal evolution, but longitudinal inference remains dependent on unverifiable assumptions about missingness despite the similarity of the main temporal contrasts across sensitivity analyses. Several substantive questions consequently remain open. First, the present cases identify residual differences more clearly than the mechanisms producing them. Future work should manipulate interaction trajectories prospectively while holding AI capability as constant as possible, allowing the causal effects of timing, feedback, disagreement, explanation, or reconstruction to be isolated. Second, later human outcomes should be observed across multiple horizons so that immediate transfer, delayed retention, attenuation, and recovery are not conflated. Third, domain-specific measurement should separate latent mechanisms where aggregate performance is ambiguous, including the distinction between discrimination and decision criterion in diagnostic settings. Fourth, comparing the same type of interaction policy across tasks and domains under a common design would make it possible to test whether residual effects depend on domain, a question that the present cross-study comparison cannot answer. A further priority is heterogeneity. The baseline human state X0 is likely to moderate the effects of assistance: expertise, prior knowledge, reliance propensity, and metacognitive calibration may change which interaction policy is beneficial and what form residual change takes. Repeated interaction also raises a deeper trajectory question: residual effects may accumulate, reverse, or become path-dependent as earlier AI exposure changes how later assistance is processed.

The next empirical stage should therefore move from documenting residual states to identifying the interaction mechanisms that generate them. The central design question becomes not simply whether AI should assist, but which assistance policy produces the desired balance between current human–AI performance and the human capabilities required afterwards . That question connects residual-state evaluation directly to the future design of adaptive Human–AI systems. 

# 7 Conclusions 

Human–AI Interaction should not be evaluated only while assistance is present. AI can improve current decisions, accelerate task completion, or increase assisted performance while also changing what the human will later know, notice, trust, contest, or be able to do. When future human judgment remains functionally relevant, these post-interaction consequences are part of the system being evaluated. The Residual Human States framework provides a common representation for this problem. It treats the human state produced by an interaction history as a distinct evaluative object, defined relative to a relevant alternative trajectory, assessed through domain-appropriate observable probes, and indexed by post-interaction horizon. The framework does not assume that AI assistance is intrinsically beneficial or harmful, nor that unaided performance should always be privileged. This perspective also changes the role of interaction design. The policy through which AI capability is delivered structures when humans reason, receive feedback, delegate, learn, and adapt. Where humans are expected to interpret, challenge, transfer, recover, or act without equivalent assistance, evaluation should therefore consider not only immediate human–AI performance but also the capabilities and dispositions carried forward. This does not require maximal independence from AI; it requires clarity about which human capacities remain functionally necessary and how alternative interaction trajectories affect them. The broader challenge for Human–AI Interaction is therefore not only to build systems that help humans perform better now, but to understand the trajectories through which those systems shape the humans who will act next. Evaluating interactive AI is incomplete whenever it measures what AI enables without also asking what its use leaves humans able, disposed, and prepared to do afterwards.
