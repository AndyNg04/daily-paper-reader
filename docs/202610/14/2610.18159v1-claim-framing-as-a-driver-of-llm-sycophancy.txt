# Claim Framing as a Driver of LLM Sycophancy 

Jean Seo Pepa Atanasova Isabelle Augenstein 

University of Copenhagen 

{jean.seo, pepa, augenstein}@di.ku.dk 

Abstract 

Sycophancy in Large Language Models, the tendency to align responses with user prefer-ences over ground truth, has largely been stud-ied using questions without a clear ground truth and with a single, plainly stated user opinion. We present a systematic study of which fram-ings of a user’s claim trigger sycophancy in factual question answering, where every an-swer is objectively verifiable without extra con-text. Evaluating 14 models on 42K questions, we express correct and incorrect user opinions through various claim framings over 8 dimen-sions and 21 types, and measure a broad set of opinion-driven behaviors. We find that all models are susceptible to user opinions regard-less of size. Models are most influenced by how confidently an opinion is stated, whom it is attributed to, and whether it is presented as shared between the user and the model. In contrast to prior work, framings based on the user’s expertise, authority over the model, or so-cial pressure matter little in our factual setting. Larger models resist incorrect opinions better but are no better at recovering from their own errors. Our results call for sycophancy evalua-tions that probe how an opinion is framed, not merely whether one is present, and that mea-sure multiple behaviors beyond a single flip rate. 

1 Introduction 

As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increas-ingly deployed as personalized agents with rich user context, sycophancy, or the tendency to align responses with user preferences rather than ground truth, poses a growing risk. Sycophantic models may validate misinformation, reinforce poor de-cisions, or systematically mislead users who ex-press incorrect beliefs. Prior work attributes syco-phancy primarily to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), where annotator prefer-ences inadvertently reward deference over accu-racy (Malmqvist, 2024). However, these studies often rely on subjective or contestable questions, where deferring to the user may make sense, and examine a limited set of triggers, typically a single sentence such as “I think the answer is X.” Moreover, syco-phancy is usually measured as whether the model’s answer shifts toward the user’s opinion, regardless of whether the opinion or the model’s initial answer is correct. We address these gaps with a systematic study of sycophancy in factual question answering (QA) with objective answers , where each question is pre-1arXiv:2610.18159v1 [cs.CL] 13 Oct 2026 sented alone or with a correct or incorrect opinion, expressed through claim framings of 8 dimensions and 21 types (see Figure 1). We further revisit how sycophancy is evaluated. A shift toward the user’s opinion alone is an insufficient measure, since its meaning depends on both the validity of the user’s claim and that of the model’s initial answer. A model may improve by adopting a correct sugges-tion, yet abandon an otherwise correct answer when the suggestion is wrong. We therefore condition our evaluation on both, assessing whether a model abandons a correct answer, fails to recover from its own incorrect answer, or adopts a correct opin-ion it would not have reached alone. Six metrics capture these cases by distinguishing the model’s initial correctness and the direction of change. This design reveals when responsiveness to users comes at the expense of factual reliability. Evaluating 14 LLMs on 42K questions, we find that all models, regardless of size, are susceptible to user opinions. Confidence , external source , and 

unity are the most influential framing dimensions: presenting an opinion as shared with the model (high unity , e.g., “we think”) is the strongest driver of sycophancy, whereas attributing it to another AI shifts models the least. In contrast, dimensions based on user authority or social pressure ( hierar-chy , social pressure , expertise ), which prior work found influential, matter less in our factual QA set-ting. Larger models adopt incorrect opinions less often but are no better at recovering from their own errors. Opinions also weigh more on entity-centric topics (e.g., biographies, arts, sports) and raise model confidence on difficult questions. More-over, how readily models follow opinions and how much they depend on framing are distinct, mak-ing claim framing a separate axis of sycophancy that a single opinion sentence cannot capture. Fi-nally, opinion-driven behaviors are often correlated but not interchangeable, underscoring the need for multiple metrics for each. In sum, our contributions are as follows: • A broad set of claim framings, spanning 8 dimensions and 21 types (§3); • A novel evaluation framework using 42K fac-tual questions across 9 topics and 4 difficulty levels, each with an objective answer requir-ing no external context (§4, §5.1); • Multi-faceted metrics to measure sycophantic behavior against a claim’s factual validity, re-vealing behavioral patterns that a single flip rate overlooks (§5.2); • An in-depth analysis of 14 LLMs, uncovering which framings drive sycophancy and how susceptibility varies (§6). 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Defining Sycophancy 

Early work characterized sycophancy as the ten-dency of LLMs to prioritize agreement with users over truthfulness, a behavior linked to RLHF, where annotators may favor agreeable responses (Sharma et al., 2025; Perez et al., 2022; Malmqvist, 2024). Scaling and instruction tuning often amplify it, while fine-tuning on synthetic data can mitigate it (Wei et al., 2024b). Later work extends to social sycophancy, the excessive preservation of a user’s desired self-image (Cheng et al., 2026), and traces it to attention heads (Genadi et al., 2026) and an-chor sentences in reasoning traces (Duszenko et al., 2026). Most of this work, however, is restricted to subjective or opinion-laden settings, where ground truth may be contested . Sycophancy in factual QA, where answers are objectively verifiable, remains underexplored. To this end, we define sycophancy as a model’s tendency to change a factual answer in response to user’s opinion, and investigate which claim framing types induce such changes. 

2.2 Measuring Sycophancy 

A growing body of work investigates which prompt-ing features make models susceptible to syco-phancy. Laban et al. (2024) show that appending “Are you sure?” can induce models to reverse pre-viously correct answers. Anagnostidis and Bulian (2024) demonstrate that the presence of explana-tions, source authoritativeness, and stated confi-dence affect models’ susceptibility. Wang et al. (2025) find that user opinions can override model knowledge regardless of claimed expertise, with first-person framing inducing greater sycophancy than third-person framing. In political contexts, Kaur (2025) show that argumentative strength influ-ences sycophantic behavior, while Au and Noronha (2026) introduce epistemic attacks that challenge the legitimacy of a model’s knowledge. However, 

each of these studies considers a limited set of prompting factors . Further, most studies quantify sycophancy with a single flip rate (Laban et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2025). 3 Claim Framing 

Claim framing is the linguistic packaging of how a user’s opinion is verbalized. Prior work adds a sin-gle assertion such as “I think X,” yet real users vary in how they deliver information. We organize this variation into 8 dimensions and 21 types (Table 1). 

Expertise and external source vary in who holds the opinion, either the user or a third party. They are motivated by findings that source authoritative-ness is influential (Wang et al., 2025; Buyl et al., 2025). Confidence varies epistemic hedging, in-spired by Anagnostidis and Bulian (2024). Hierar-chy frames the relative epistemic authority between user and model, following Au and Noronha (2026) and Joswin et al. (2026). Social pressure captures obligation-laden framing at varying intensity, ex-tending works on group conformity (Zhu et al., 2025; Weng et al., 2025). Unity captures whether the opinion is attributed to the user alone, to the user and model as a group, or a mix. It is inspired by findings that first-person framing elicits syco-phancy (Wang et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2025) and by work on social identity preservation in LLMs (Cheng et al., 2026). Its low level, where the user alone holds the opinion, corresponds to the stan-dard “I think X” setup of prior work. Finally, stake 

and consequence vary the implied cost of the re-sponse. These have not been studied as prompt-level variables previously, but we include them as natural features. 

4 Dataset 

4.1 Dataset Preparation 

We adapt three existing factual QA datasets with questions that (1) can be answered in a single word or phrase, enabling straightforward string-matching evaluation without LLM judges or additional met-rics; (2) require neither multi-step reasoning nor external context, so that any change in the model’s response can be attributed to user influence rather than other factors; (3) are grounded in a source document, ensuring answer credibility; (4) have a definitive, unambiguous answer, a prerequisite for reliable evaluation. • TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017): A challeng-ing reading comprehension dataset containing question-answer-evidence triples. • SciQ (Johannes Welbl, 2017): Crowdsourced science exam questions spanning Physics, Chemistry, Biology, and related fields. • SimpleQA-Verified (Haas et al., 2026): Prompt benchmark for evaluating short-form factuality in LLMs, built on OpenAI’s Sim-pleQA (Wei et al., 2024a). 5.2 Metrics 

Unlike prior work that collapses model susceptibil-ity into a single flip rate, we measure accuracy in all three conditions ( ∅, +, −) along with six met-rics that capture different sycophantic behaviors (Table 2). This yields four flip rates (sycophancy, regression, correction, and self-correction), com-plemented by correct and incorrect stability, which measure how often baseline-correct and baseline-incorrect answers are preserved. We represent each response as a pair (b, o ) of the question-only an-swer b and the opinion-condition answer o, each either correct (C) or incorrect (I). We write out-come counts as |XY c|, where X, Y ∈ { C, I} denote 

b and o, and c ∈ { +, −} denotes the correct ( +) or incorrect ( −) opinion condition. We also define ma-nipulativeness as the range of accuracy across types within a claim framing dimension (max − min). Throughout the paper, sycophancy refers broadly to opinion-driven changes in model behavior, and 

sycophancy rate to the specific metric.

Metrics correlate yet capture distinct behaviors. 

Figure 5 shows the pairwise Pearson correlations among the seven metrics. Accuracy is negatively correlated with sycophancy rate ( r = −0.73 ) and regression rate ( r = −0.80 ), indicating that more accurate models are more resistant to opinions in both directions. Metrics computed on baseline-correct and baseline-incorrect answers are also cor-related. Regression rate and incorrect stability show a high correlation ( r = 0 .84 ). Similarly, under in-correct opinions, models with a higher sycophancy rate show a lower self-correction rate ( r = −0.65 ). These correlations suggest that how a model re-sponds to opinions on questions it initially answers correctly carries over to questions it initially an-swers incorrectly. Yet the metrics are not neces-sarily interchangeable. Sycophancy and regression rates are only moderately correlated ( r = 0 .51 ), and model size reduces sycophancy and regression but not necessarily correction or self-correction (Figure 3B). Qualitative analysis. We compute a per-question flip rate , the proportion of responses under incorrect opinions (14 models × 21 claim framing types) that switch from correct to incorrect, and manually inspect questions at both extremes. Vulnerable questions often ask for a taxonomic or categorical label (e.g., “What sort of creature is the water moccasin?” , flip rate 0.844). For such questions, the incorrect opinion sometimes names a label at a different level of the categorical hierarchy than the question asks for (e.g., snake, reptile, viper ), and models tend to adopt it. 

7 Conclusion 

We present a systematic study of how claim fram-ing drives sycophancy in factual QA, evaluating 14 models across 8 framing dimensions and 21 types through comprehensive metrics that capture dis-tinct behaviors that a single flip rate would obscure. All models are susceptible to user opinions regard-less of size. Confidence , external source , unity have the strongest effect, while hierarchy , expertise , so-cial pressure are weaker. Larger models resist in-correct opinions more but are not always better at recovering from their own errors, and opinions weigh more on difficult and entity-centric ques-tions. Framing sensitivity also varies independently of overall susceptibility, and no single metric cap-tures the full range of opinion-driven behaviors. Future sycophancy benchmarks should thus diver-sify claim framings beyond authority and pressure and report behavior-specific metrics. A natural next step is to probe the mechanisms behind these ef-fects, such as whether models retain the correct answer internally when yielding to an opinion. 8Limitation 

Output-level analysis. Our analysis character-izes sycophancy through model outputs and output-level confidence, and cannot reveal what models in-ternally represent when they follow or resist a user opinion. As a result, some confidence patterns, such as increased confidence when larger models aban-don a correct answer, remain difficult to interpret. 

Effect of high unity. High unity framing at-tributes the user opinion also to the model itself (we ), which may change the nature of the task to continuing a given scenario rather than only the framing of the claim. Disentangling these effects would require additional controls, such as variants in which the opinion is shared with a third party instead of the model. 

Incorrect opinions. Incorrect opinions are gen-erated as plausible but incorrect answers, and their relation to the correct answer varies widely. A dis-tractor may be an antonym of the correct answer, a broader or narrower category label, or a semanti-cally unrelated entity of the same type. We do not account for this variation, although susceptibility to an opinion may depend on how its answer relates to the correct one. A finer-grained analysis of how different types of distractors affect sycophantic be-havior is left for future work. 

Scope. Our study is limited to single-turn factual QA in English with open-weight models. Ques-tion difficulty is defined by the correctness of three reference models, and thus reflects difficulty for current LLMs rather than an intrinsic property of the question. Sycophancy may manifest differently in multi-turn interactions, other languages, or pro-prietary models. 

Ethics Statement 

Our study uses publicly available datasets and open-weight models in accordance with their licenses, and the data contain no personal or sensitive in-formation. Distractors that did not meet our crite-ria were manually revised by the authors, without external annotators. Our findings identify claim framings that make models more likely to adopt in-correct user opinions. While this knowledge could in principle be used to steer models toward misin-formation, we believe that documenting these vul-nerabilities is necessary for building more robust models and for helping users interact with LLMs more reliably. We used AI assistants for language editing and for writing visualization code. All con-tent was reviewed and verified by the authors, who take full responsibility for the paper.

20 I Difficulty Analysis 

Accuracy increases monotonically with difficulty across all conditions and model sizes, with the cor-rect opinion condition consistently above and the incorrect opinion condition consistently below the question-only baseline (Figure 28). Sycophancy and regression rates decrease, and self-correction rate increases, as questions become easier (Fig-ure 26). Correction rate is the exception, remaining relatively stable across difficulty levels. Correct sta-bility rises and incorrect stability falls with decreas-ing difficulty (Figure 27), consistent with models holding correct answers more reliably on easier questions.

J Claim Framing Type Accuracy by Difficulty 

Figure 29 reports accuracy for each individual claim framing type across difficulty levels. In the correct opinion condition, High unity stands out with notably higher accuracy at Level 0 (0.70) com-pared to other unity types (0.50–0.52), consistent with its high manipulativeness in that condition. 

Not confident yields the lowest accuracy among all claim framing types at Level 0 (0.38), suggesting that hedged confidence framing is less effective at helping models on hard questions. Under incor-rect opinion, High unity is the dominant outlier, with accuracy as low as 0.05 at Level 0 and 0.57 at Level 3, far below all other types.
