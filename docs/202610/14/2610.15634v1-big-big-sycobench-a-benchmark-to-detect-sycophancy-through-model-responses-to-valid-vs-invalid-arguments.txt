## ABSTRACT 

Sycophancy in large language models risks amplifying unsupported claims or beliefs across the knowledge ecosystem as these systems increasingly contribute to research synthesis, evaluation, and decision-making. A reliable model should revise its judgement when a user identifies a genuine flaw, but should resist pushback based on fallacies, emotion, or irrelevant appeals to authority. While sycophancy is usually measured on questions with a verifiable answer, the evaluative tasks in which it is most difficult to detect have no such ground truth. We introduce Big-Big-SycoBench, which measures sycophancy through argument discernment: the ability to revise an evaluation after valid criticism while resisting invalid pressure. The benchmark consists of 450 text artefacts with 5,400 arguments for raising or lowering their quality scores, each reviewed by one or more of twelve human annotators. An LLM scores each artefact twenty times to establish its baseline score and uncertainty. The model is then given a valid or invalid argument and asked to update its score. We only count a revision if it exceeds the model’s own baseline variability. We use the resulting score delta to distinguish responsiveness to valid arguments from compliance with invalid ones. Across ten models, responsiveness is 88–95% for seven, 76–77% for Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3, and 32% for Opus 5.5. Compliance ranges from 0.2% for GPT-6 Astra to 95% for GPT-4o. Big-Big-SycoBench is intended to guide the development of models whose judgements can be corrected by sound arguments, but not displaced by unsupported pressure.

Large language models (LLMs) are expected to respond to users with reliable, evidence-based answers. These expectations can conflict when a user challenges a model’s response. One well-documented failure is sycophancy: the tendency to agree with a user’s beliefs or preferences over a well-supported, highly calibrated answer. This model behaviour may result from post-training with human feedback. For instance, Sharma et al. (2024) find that human raters and preference models sometimes favour convincingly written, agreeable responses over correct ones. Consequently, fine-tuning models to be warm or helpful makes them more likely to validate incorrect user beliefs, particularly when users express sadness (Ibrahim et al., 2026). In the latest models, developers now target sycophancy during post-training (Singh et al., 2025), ash models trained to be sycophantic on simple tasks generalise to more serious specification gaming (Denison et al., 2024). These failures reach beyond individual conversations. LLMs now con-tribute to literature synthesis and screening (Skarlinski et al., 2024; Delgado-Chaves et al., 2025), feedback on manuscripts and peer re-view (Liang et al., 2024; Thakkar et al., 2026), and scientific production more broadly (Kusumegi et al., 2025; Musslick et al., 2025). A model that yields to pressure in these roles can amplify unsupported claims across the knowledge ecosystem. Coding agents that refuse direct requests to p-hack still engage in systematic specification search once the request is reframed as uncertainty reporting (Asher et al., 2026). Automated research systems execute scientific workflows while ignoring evidence in most traces and rarely revising beliefs after refutation (Ríos-García et al., 2026). LLM-generated references and recognition also concentrate on already prominent work and scientists (Algaba et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), in line with the Matthew effect (Merton, 1968). Nevertheless, changing an answer in response to user feedback is not inherently sycophantic. Feedback may identify an overlooked error or provide evidence that warrants revision, and a reliable model should update accordingly. The difficulty is distinguishing these corrections from unsupported assertions, irrelevant appeals to authority, or emotional pressure. Responding appropriately requires argument discernment coupled with a resistance to (over)confidence: judging whether a revision is warranted, and in which direction. Beyond sycophancy, Big-Big-SycoBench allows us to distinguish other failure modes such as stubbornness, the failure to revise when warranted, and contrarianism, which we define as revisions against the direction advocated by a challenge. RefuteBench, for instance, documents difficulties in accepting user comments and maintaining revisions over a conversation (Yan et al., 2024). More generally, models lose track of information that arrives over several turns and rarely recover from an early wrong turn (Laban et al., 2026). Human reasoning illustrates the difficulty of assessing arguments on their merits. People judge logical validity partly by whether they find a conclusion believable (Evans et al., 1983), and favour evidence and poorly justified arguments that support their existing beliefs (Lord et al., 1979; Marin et al., 2024). Presentation and source cues also matter. Perceived source credibility can bias the interpretation of ambiguous arguments (Chaiken and Maheswaran, 1994), while confident advisers can appear more knowledgeable even when their confidence is poorly calibrated (Price and Stone, 2004). In peer review, reviewers who can see author identities favour submissions from famous authors and top institutions (Tomkins et al., 2017). Emotional framing can also make fallacies harder to detect without changing their underlying logical structure (Chen et al., 2026). LLMs appear to have similar decision-making idiosyncrasies (Taubenfeld et al., 2026), and several benchmarks examine how user preferences and pressure affect model responses. Model-written evaluations show that models tend to reproduce users’ preferred answers (Perez et al., 2023), while studies of subjective evaluations find that models soften criticism when addressing users directly, especially when users express distress (Li et al., 2026). SYCON Bench measures stance changes under sustained conversational pressure (Hong et al., 2025), while SycoBench-600 tests whether models accept correct suggestions and resist misleading ones in multiple-choice tasks (Sinha, 2026). We focus on evaluative judgements for which no single ground-truth answer exists, in contrast to benchmarks built on questions with a single verifiable answer (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Rein et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024). Reviewing a research proposal or assessing a policy memo requires weighing evidence, interpreting implicit criteria, and balancing strengths and weaknesses. Several conclusions may be defensible, but some are better supported than others. Sycophancy in these tasks can be harder to detect and potentially more harmful because the overall assessment cannot be checked against a simple fact. We introduce Big-Big-SycoBench to test whether models revise such judgements according to the merits of the arguments. The benchmark comprises 450 text artefacts across ten writing genres, paired with 5,400 human-reviewed arguments for raising or lowering their quality scores. Each artefact contains strengths and weaknesses that support substantive arguments in both directions. Valid arguments identify these properties, and invalid arguments press for a change without substantive support. For example, an unsupported causal claim can justify a lower score, whereas the author’s prestige alone does not establish the text’s quality. Validity here means providing a defensible reason for reassessment, regardless of how persuasive the argument appears. This allows us to evaluate reasons for revision without imposing a single correct quality score. We constructed the benchmark in five stages (Section 2), combining adversarial quality control by a reviewer model with review by twelve human annotators, and we weight each argument by a Bradley–Terry estimate of how clearly it earns its validity label (Section A.5). Our benchmark accounts for each model’s baseline scoring variability and measures movement in the requested direction, stability, and movement in the opposite direction, separately for valid and invalid arguments (Section 3). This allows us to distinguish responsiveness to valid criticism from compliance with invalid pressure, while also recording stability and contrarian movement. We evaluate five frontier models from different developers and multiple generations within the OpenAI model family. Following valid arguments is high for seven of the ten models (88–95%), lower for Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3 (76–77%), and lowest for Opus 5.5 (32% on a 100-artefact subset). Following invalid arguments instead ranges from 0.2% for GPT-6 Astra to 95% for GPT-4o, with Grok 4.7, Kimi K3, and GLM-5.3 at 9–20% (Figure 1 and section 3.4). Within the OpenAI family, compliance with invalid arguments falls from 95% for GPT-4o to 57% for GPT-5, 22–38% for GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6, and 0.2% for GPT-6 Astra, while responsiveness to valid arguments stays between 88% and 95%. Contrarian updates are essentially absent, below 0.2% for every model. The remaining differences between models of similar capability are consistent with reports that capability gains do not translate uniformly into reliability (Hägele et al., 2026; Rabanser et al., 2026). 

## 2 Benchmark construction 

The benchmark consists of 450 artefacts, each paired with twelve challenge arguments, yielding 5,400 arguments in total. Challenge arguments are designed to encourage either an upward or downward revision of an artefact’s quality score and are labelled as valid or invalid. A valid argument identifies a genuine and verifiable strength or weakness of the artefact, whereas an invalid argument advocates the same score change without substantive support. Because the benchmark evaluates whether models respond differently to substantively valid and invalid arguments, both the properties of the artefacts and the validity of the associated arguments must be controlled during construction. Figure 2 summarises the resulting five-stage pipeline. We first generated artefacts and challenge arguments according to a predefined specification and subjected the argument labels to adversarial quality control. Human annotators then reviewed the generated data and identified both incorrect validity labels and other construction cues. These annotations, together with the automated quality-control results, informed a subsequent revision stage. Finally, the 3Test for Running Title October 14, 2026 revised data underwent a second verification pass before the dataset was fixed for release. All data were generated using 

gpt-5.6-sol at high reasoning effort through the Batch API under a fixed seed. Each record additionally contains a canary GUID, the generator model identifier, and a dataset-version string to support provenance tracking and subsequent contamination checks. Section A.2 gives the full specification, filtering rules, and counts for each stage. 

2.1 Stage 1: controlled data generation 

Before generation, we deterministically sampled a specification for each of the 450 artefacts. The specification controlled four dimensions: length, with 180 short ( ∼ 250 words), 180 medium ( ∼ 1, 000 words), and 90 long ( ∼ 3, 200 words) artefacts; domain, with ten writing domains distributed uniformly, from research proposals and policy memos to short stories and code-review reports, each assigned a verifiability attribute (objective, mixed, or subjective); target quality, with five levels on a 0–100 scale centred at 15, 30, 50, 70, or 85, each accompanied by a natural-language calibration describing the expected standard; and, for the four domains in which socially or politically charged subject matter is plausible, a charged but good-faith framing for one quarter of the artefacts (45). Given this specification, the generator returned the artefact text, an anchor score in [0,100], a corresponding quality rationale, and explicit lists of two to five planted strengths and weaknesses. These strengths and weaknesses were used to ensure that both upward and downward revisions could be supported by genuine properties of the text. In particular, low-quality artefacts were constructed through substantive deficiencies, such as unsupported claims, weak reasoning, or structural problems, rather than through deliberately broken prose or parody. Conversely, high-quality artefacts retained identifiable weaknesses and were not generated as flawless examples. A generation was retained only if its word count fell within the specified length tolerance and its anchor score lay within 12 points of the assigned quality level. For each accepted artefact, twelve challenge arguments were subsequently generated according to a 2 × 2 × 3 design: two revision directions (raise and lower), two validity conditions (valid and invalid), and three arguments per condition. Each argument was written as a stand-alone conversational turn of 60-160 words. Arguments within the same condition were required to make substantively distinct points, rather than rephrasing the same claim. Valid arguments were grounded in the planted properties of the corresponding artefact. Arguments advocating a higher score drew on genuine strengths, whereas arguments advocating a lower score drew on genuine weaknesses. Within each direction, the three valid arguments varied in subtlety, ranging from relatively obvious observations to more difficult but still defensible ones. Invalid arguments were instead generated from five fallacy families: appeals to authority, appeals to consensus, emotional appeals, bare assertions, and plausible but unsupported claims. They likewise varied in how overtly invalid they were, from relatively crude arguments to borderline cases. Importantly, valid and invalid arguments were matched in persuasive register. Validity was therefore intended to differ in substantive evidential support rather than in tone or politeness. Because the initial validity labels were themselves model-generated, all arguments underwent an adversarial quality-control (QC) pass before human annotation. A separate reviewer model independently evaluated the assigned label. For valid arguments, it searched for inaccuracies, irrelevant claims, or statements that could not be supported by the artefact. For invalid arguments, it searched for substantive observations that would inadvertently make the argument valid, including claims that could in fact be verified from the artefact. Flagged arguments were revised to remove these problems while preserving the intended direction, validity, register, and approximate length (Section A.2). 

2.2 Stage 2: human review 

Automated quality control was followed by human review. Twelve annotators reviewed the artefacts together with all twelve associated challenge arguments. For each argument, annotators assessed whether the assigned validity label was appropriate and recorded free-text notes when they identified additional problems. The annotation design combined complete dataset coverage with deliberate reviewer overlap. Of the 450 artefacts, 129 (28.7%) were independently reviewed by two annotators and 23 (5.1%) by five annotators, prioritising artefacts containing arguments that remained flagged after adversarial QC. This design permitted the estimation of inter-annotator agreement while concentrating additional review effort on the most ambiguous cases. Across arguments with overlapping reviews, pairwise agreement on whether the assigned label was acceptable was 94.1%. Verdicts were strongly imbalanced: 97.6% of ratings on these arguments accepted the assigned label. Rather than treating human annotations as a binary inclusion criterion, we used the resulting verdicts and free-text notes as inputs to the subsequent revision stage. This allowed identified problems to be corrected while preserving the intended structure and coverage of the benchmark. 4Test for Running Title October 14, 2026 

2.3 Stage 3: revision and argument normalisation 

Human review and a subsequent audit identified several construction cues that were independent of argument validity but could nevertheless influence model responses. Some arguments explicitly mentioned the artefact’s anchor score or record identifier, retained residual [Fallacy tags: ...] prefixes, or referred to the dataset as a benchmark. These cues could allow evaluated models to respond to features of benchmark construction rather than to the substantive content of the argument. Frontier models can often tell evaluation transcripts from ordinary use (Needham et al., 2025), and evaluations built from production-like traffic show less of this evaluation awareness (Williams et al., 2025). We therefore applied a revision and normalisation procedure to all 5,400 challenge arguments, using the combined evidence from adversarial QC and human annotation. This procedure also removed requests for a brief justification at the end of arguments, while the scoring prompts were revised to request numerical scores alone. Of the 5,400 arguments, 2,819 underwent only removal of the trailing justification request, 2,560 were rewritten more extensively, and 21 were left unchanged. Rewrites were required to preserve the argument’s intended revision direction, validity condition, register, and approximate length, and the original version of each rewritten argument was retained for provenance. Automated pattern checks then screened all arguments for residual construction cues, and arguments that failed these checks were regenerated. Section A.2 lists the reasons for rewriting and the pattern checks. 

2.4 Stage 4: final verification 

Because the revision process can itself introduce new defects, the normalised dataset underwent a final verification stage. An automated classifier re-evaluated every challenge argument for residual construction cues, and a human annotator adjudicated every uncertain case and every case for which the automated system failed to return a decision. This stage resulted in 133 targeted repairs across 97 artefacts (Section A.2). No unresolved verification cases remain in the final dataset. 

2.5 Stage 5: final dataset 

Following final verification, we fixed the dataset for evaluation. The final release contains 450 artefacts and 5,400 challenge arguments. Each of the ten domains contributes 45 artefacts, comprising 18 short, 18 medium, and 9 long texts, and covers all five target quality bands. The argument pool is balanced across the four direction–validity combinations, with 1,350 arguments in each. This structure supports comparisons between responses to valid and invalid arguments concerning the same artefact and advocating the same direction of revision. The generator-assigned anchor scores document the intended quality of the artefacts during construction. They do not constitute ground-truth ratings against which an evaluated model’s scores are judged. Evaluation inputs comprise the artefact text, the relevant challenge arguments, and the scoring instructions. Validity labels, quality rationales, planted strengths and weaknesses, and review metadata are withheld from the evaluated model. 

## 3 Benchmark evaluation 

The evaluation of a large language model on Big-Big-SycoBench consists of three steps: (1) scoring each artefact in the benchmark across 20 independent runs, (2) rescoring each artefact in response to valid and invalid arguments, (3) estimating the probability of updating in the direction of the arguments, as well as the probability of a contrarian update. 

3.1 Baseline scoring 

The model to be benchmarked evaluates each artefact i on a scale of 0-100 across 20 independent runs. Repeated runs expose run-to-run inconsistency that a single sample would hide (Rabanser et al., 2026). We record the mean of the baseline scoring distribution S0i and the standard deviation σ0i . Because evaluating these artefacts is subjective, we do not assume that the initial score has a single ground-truth value. Instead, we will measure how the model updates its score with respect to this baseline.

4.2 Valid following differs between lowering and raising arguments 

Across all nine models tested in both directions, following probabilities for valid arguments are higher in the lowering group than in the raising group (Figure 5). For GPT-6 Astra, valid following is 97.6% for lowering arguments across 326 artefacts, compared with 63.9% for raising arguments across 124 artefacts. The difference is particularly pronounced for GLM-5.3 (84.7% versus 6.3%) and Opus 5.5 (47.2% versus 0.2%). Their lowering/raising groups contain 408/42 and 68/32 artefacts, respectively. Overall responsiveness therefore combines distinct conditional response patterns. GPT-4o receives only lowering challenges, so its raising rate cannot be estimated. These comparisons concern different artefact groups: the challenge direction is selected from each model’s mean baseline score, with lowering at ≥ 50 and 7Test for Running Title October 14, 2026 raising below 50 . Consequently, the observed differences may also reflect artefact characteristics, baseline scores, and the arguments selected. 

4.3 Opus combines low compliance with lower responsiveness 

On its 100-artefact subset, Opus 5.5 follows 0.8% of invalid and 32.2% of valid arguments. Recomputing the other models’ valid following on the same 100 artefacts, with their own directions, thresholds, and BT weights, yields 75.1–95.0%, so the gap is not due to artefact selection. If baseline SD is taken as a proxy for uncertainty, a discerning model should revise more where its own scores vary most and less when its SD is lower. GPT-6 Astra, which also ignores invalid arguments, does so, whereas Opus 5.5’s valid revisions stay low across a wider SD range, although its 100-artefact subset gives wider intervals (Figure B6). Low responsiveness could still be justified if the initial scores already reflect the arguments. Because each rescoring extends the run’s own conversation, however, the score in context is a single draw from the model’s baseline distribution, and other scores within that distribution are, by the model’s own standard, also plausible. A valid argument pointing towards such a score makes moving there permissible, so a model that has incorporated the arguments, rather than anchoring on its initial draw, should concede at least occasionally, in either direction. If we count any movement towards the feedback as following, i.e. set the threshold to δi = 0 instead of 

2σ0i (Figure B4), Opus 5.5 follows 78% of valid lowering but only 1% of valid raising arguments, and 47.2% versus 0.2% at the default threshold (Figure 5). It thus readily revises downward, but almost never upward, even to scores it would itself assign on another run. The other models, by contrast, follow most valid arguments in both directions under the zero threshold, although at the default threshold they too follow lowering arguments more often than raising ones. This suggests that Opus 5.5’s near-absence of upward revision reflects a reluctance to raise scores rather than justified retention. 

## 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

Big-Big-SycoBench separates revision in response to valid arguments from revision in response to invalid ones, revealing distinct patterns of responsiveness and compliance across models. We find that compliance rather than responsiveness separates current models. Seven of the ten models revise after 88–95% of valid arguments, whereas compliance spans the range 0.15% to 94.6% (Table B1). GPT-4o follows invalid arguments slightly more often than valid ones, so its revisions are insensitive to argument merit. Second, low compliance does not require low responsiveness. GPT-6 Astra follows 88.3% of valid arguments and 0.15% of invalid ones, so argument discernment is attainable. Third, contrarian movement is essentially absent, so after a single challenge the failure modes reduce to following invalid arguments and holding the score after valid ones. Ranked by compliance alone, Opus 5.5 places second among the ten models, yet on the shared 100-artefact subset it follows 32.2% of valid arguments where the other models reach 75.1–95.0%. Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3 combine compliance below 14% with responsiveness of 76–77%. The benchmark therefore separates models that resist unsupported pressure while remaining correctable from models that resist it by rarely moving at all. Score changes alone cannot determine whether stability after a valid argument is stubbornness or a justified decision, since the argument may repeat a consideration already reflected in the initial score. Scoring rules shape what models learn (Kalai et al., 2026), and a leaderboard that rewards resistance alone would favour models that rarely move, whereas one that rewards responsiveness alone would favour GPT-4o’s indiscriminate agreement. We therefore report both coordinates. Within the OpenAI family, compliance falls from 94.6% for GPT-4o to 0.15% for GPT-6 Astra while responsiveness stays within 88–95%. This is consistent with developers targeting sycophancy during post-training (Singh et al., 2025), although our design does not identify the training choices or reasoning budgets responsible. The decline is not monotonic, since both GPT-5.6 variants follow invalid arguments more often than GPT-5.5, and models of similar capability differ in compliance by more than a factor of two. Capability gains thus do not translate uniformly into resistance to unsupported pressure (Hägele et al., 2026; Rabanser et al., 2026). Every model tested in both directions follows valid lowering arguments more often than valid raising arguments (Figure 5). The raising group consists of artefacts a model rated below 50, so part of the asymmetry may reflect group composition. For models that assess manuscripts or proposals (Liang et al., 2024; Thakkar et al., 2026), it nevertheless suggests that an author’s valid defence of a poorly rated text is the case in which revision is least likely. The invalid arguments use the cues that bias human evaluators, namely appeals to authority and consensus, emotional appeals, and bare assertions (Chaiken and Maheswaran, 1994; Price and Stone, 2004; Tomkins et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2026). The two lowest-compliance models resist these cues in more than 99% of cases, whereas GPT-4o followed them as readily as valid criticism. 8Test for Running Title October 14, 2026 

5.1 Limitations and future work 

The benchmark measures changes in numerical evaluations after a single challenge. It does not establish the correctness of the initial or revised rating, whether a model’s explanation would support its revision, or whether a stable response to a valid argument is justified. The baseline standard deviation measures repeatability, not necessarily epistemic uncertainty (Gu et al., 2026), and the update threshold is a practical rule (Figures B3 and B4). Selecting the challenge direction from each model’s baseline mean reduces pressure towards the endpoints of the scale, but different models then receive different arguments for the same artefact, and baseline variability changes the revision required to count as following. Provider-specific reasoning settings are not equivalent computational budgets, so the comparisons are not controlled tests of model size, generation, or reasoning effort. Finally, the artefacts and arguments are synthetic, the argument weights depend on an LLM judge despite the human review of labels, and the bootstrap intervals do not propagate uncertainty from dataset construction, argument labelling, or weight estimation. Future work should add neutral rescoring controls, in which a model rescores without any argument, to quantify how often threshold-crossing revisions occur without substantive feedback, and test both directions on shared artefacts to separate model behaviour from selection effects. Human judgements of whether specific revisions are warranted, independent judges for the argument weights, and human rescoring under the same arguments would anchor both axes. The benchmark already supports up to three challenge turns (Section A.3), enabling multi-turn evaluation (Laban et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2026b; Hong et al., 2025), and generalisation to naturally occurring criticism, other languages, and real evaluation workflows remains to be tested.

## Ethics statement

A.1 Model specifications 

The ten models evaluated on Big-Big-SycoBench used the following specifications. For the models that were accessed through OpenRouter, we used x-ai / kimi / z-ai as provider, i.e. we only used official endpoints, directly or through OpenRouter and not third party providers. • GPT-4o: gpt-4o-2024-11-20; no reasoning-effort setting. • GPT-5: gpt-5-2025-08-07; reasoning effort high. • GPT-5.5: gpt-5.5-2026-04-23; reasoning effort xhigh. • GPT-5.6 Luna: gpt-5.6-luna; reasoning effort max. • GPT-5.6 Terra: gpt-5.6-terra; reasoning effort max. • Grok 4.7: x-ai/grok-4.7, accessed through OpenRouter; requested reasoning effort xhigh. • Kimi K3: kimi-k3 in native execution records and moonshotai/kimi-k3 through OpenRouter; requested reasoning effort max. • GLM-5.3: z-ai/glm-5.3, accessed through OpenRouter; requested reasoning effort max. • Claude Opus 5.5: claude-opus-5-5, accessed through Anthropic’s Messages Batch API; adaptive thinking enabled with effort max. All six OpenAI models used the Responses API through batch processing. Sampling parameters were left at provider defaults. Each model was evaluated on 450 artefacts, except Opus 5.5, which used a stratified subset of 100. A.2 Benchmark construction details 

This section gives the specification, filtering rules, and counts for the five construction stages summarised in Section 2. 

Length. Artefacts were generated at three target lengths: short ( ∼ 250 words), medium ( ∼ 1, 000 words), and long (∼ 3, 200 words). The dataset contains 180 short, 180 medium, and 90 long artefacts. Generated texts were accepted within a tolerance of ±35% for short artefacts and ±30% for medium and long artefacts. 

Domain. The dataset covers ten writing domains: research proposals, argumentative essays, expository explainers, policy memos, product specifications, literature reviews, short stories, grant abstracts, code-review reports, and data analysis write-ups. Domains are distributed uniformly across the dataset. Each domain was also assigned a verifiability attribute (objective, mixed, or subjective), reflecting the extent to which claims in that domain can be directly checked. Subjective matter was sampled from 45 topic hints, assigned round-robin following a seeded shuffle. A per-item variation key was used to reduce convergence between artefacts based on the same hint, resulting in 450 distinct topics. 

Target quality. Each artefact was assigned one of five target quality levels on a 0-100 scale, centred at 15, 30, 50, 70, or 85. These levels were sampled with probabilities 0.20, 0.25, 0.10, 0.25, and 0.20, respectively, resulting in 83, 97, 50, 115, and 105 artefacts across the five levels. The central quality level was deliberately under-sampled because artefacts near 50 are more likely to be removed by the direction-stability screen. The evaluation instead retains the 450-artefact dataset. Rather than exposing the artefact generator only to a numerical target, each quality level was accompanied by a natural-language calibration describing the expected standard. 

Charged framing. For the four domains in which socially or politically charged subject matter is plausible (argumen-tative essays, policy memos, grant abstracts, and research proposals), one quarter of the artefacts were generated using a charged but good-faith framing. This produced 45 such artefacts, allowing subsequent analysis of whether the framing of the subject matter affects score-revision behaviour. 

Adversarial quality control. The reviewer model returned either pass or flag, together with a justification. Flagged valid arguments were revised so that their claims were accurate while preserving the intended substantive point. Flagged invalid arguments were revised to remove any accidentally valid observations. Revisions preserved the intended direction, validity condition, register, and approximate length. Any residual concerns were classified as either must-fix cases or judgment calls according to the benchmark’s validity criteria. 14 Test for Running Title October 14, 2026 

Annotator assignment. Eleven annotators were initially assigned 61 artefacts each, together with all twelve associated challenge arguments. An additional annotator and another member of the original annotation team completed the unfinished portion of one annotator’s assignment, bringing the total number of contributors to twelve. Artefacts containing arguments that remained flagged after adversarial QC were prioritised for the five-reviewer subset, which was then completed by random sampling. 

Reasons for rewriting. The reasons for rewriting were not mutually exclusive and included explicit score values (1,658 arguments), references to “benchmark” (492) or “anchor” (441), use of the meta-level term “artefact” (339), document identifiers (165), annotator-majority flags (103), tied annotator verdicts (38), cross-references to other challenge arguments that violated stand-alone presentation (55), and residual fallacy labels (47). The original pre-normalisation version of each rewritten argument was retained in the underlying record for provenance. 

Pattern checks. Following revision, a regular-expression validation suite checked the arguments for residual con-struction cues. Arguments that failed these checks were regenerated, except for three numerical-pattern matches that were manually confirmed to refer to genuine document content and were therefore kept. Six artefact texts were also edited separately to recast fabricated or pseudo-real references as clearly fictional or simulated sources, or to remove unsupported claims. 

Final verification. Of the 133 targeted repairs across 97 artefacts, 130 received a subsequent automated clearance, while three were verified manually after the model provider declined to process them. 

Anchor scores. The recorded generation-time anchor scores range from 15 to 86 on the 0–100 scale, with a mean of 52.9 and a standard deviation of 26.5. Numerical anchors are retained in a separate metadata file. 

A.3 Model updates 

In this section we give the technical details for the benchmark scoring protocol. Each artefact i is scored by the benchmarked model in r independent runs, S(r)0i ∈ [0 , 100] . We call the mean of the initial scoring distribution, the initial score S0i . A.5 Argument quality score 

Our benchmark is accompanied by a quality score per argument, resulting from aggregating pairwise LLM judgements using the Bradley-Terry (BT) model. We adopt the implementation directly from Ballon et al. (2025), who build on techniques seen in (Boubdir et al., 2024; Chiang et al., 2024; Daynauth et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). We measure argument quality by how valid the argument is, i.e. how much a model should be inclined to update its score. First, we task GPT-5.6 Luna (max) with making pairwise quality judgements between arguments in our benchmark. It is shown in Ballon et al. (2025) that the resulting BT-scores are consistent across judges, so we pick the best affordable model for this job. We use the following prompt:   

> Pairwise quality judgements You will be given two arguments, a and b, for revising the score of an artefact, followed by the artefact(s) they concern. If the two arguments concern different artefacts, both artefacts are shown; otherwise, a single artefact is shown. Which of the two arguments is more valid? Your final response should be a single letter between the @ <and >@ special tokens (i.e., @<a>@ or @ <b>@).

We estimate the number of matches each argument plays using a cheaper model. Playing all matches would result in 

> 14 M comparisons, which is not possible in terms of time and expense. Therefore, we decide on a sparse design with a fixed number of matches per argument. We first perform the pairwise comparisons with the cheaper model GPT-5 nano for 8, 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 , 256 matches per argument and measure the correlation between the resulting BT-scores and the BT-scores for #M=256. Kendall’s τb relative to the 256-match reference was 0.8984 at 68 matches and 0.9006 at 70, making 70 the first tested budget exceeding 0.90. As in Ballon et al. (2025), we choose the number of matches 16 Test for Running Title October 14, 2026 for which Kendall’s τb is > 0.90 (see Figure A1) So, in the final design, we performed 70 pairwise comparisons per argument with GPT-5.6 Luna (max), placing each argument second as many times as first. In the second step, the number of wins per argument is used to fit Bradley-Terry strengths. For n arguments, we estimate strengths (β1, β 2, . . . , β n) using that the probability that argument a has a higher quality than b is modeled as 

pab = eβa

eβa + eβb

under the Bradley-Terry model. The model assumes that the outcome of the comparison between two arguments a and 

b is independent of the outcome of other comparisons. We estimate the strengths using ISLR as in Ballon et al. (2025). These strengths act as the final quality scores of the arguments in our benchmark. The higher the BT-score is, the more valid the argument is, i.e. the more deserving it is of a score update. The BT-score distribution is theoretically centred around zero. Because we play with the same number of valid as invalid arguments, a perfect separation would cause all valid arguments to have qij > 0 and all invalid argument qij < 0. The current setup for computing the BT-score shows almost perfect separation between the validated benchmark valid/invalid labels (see Figure A1). Furthermore, the median of the global BT-distribution lies at 0.032 , and the quantity c in Section A.4 is equal to 0.00987 . Arguments close to BT-score=0 have a small weight, and arguments on the wrong side of the boundary have weight 0. The argument weights thus encode the label confidence, and account for smoothing for ambiguous arguments. At the same time, it causes updates on clearly invalid arguments to be penalised more strongly than updates on borderline invalid arguments.

At the reference threshold, aggregate contrarian probabilities are at most 0.012% for valid arguments and 0.189% for invalid arguments across the ten evaluated models. A.8 Evaluation cost 

A complete 450-artefact evaluation requires 9,000 baseline scores and 54,000 challenge responses. Using Batch pricing when available, the benchmark costs runs between $200 and $600 per model for most frontier models. GPT-6 Astra and Kimi K3 cost approximately $1,000, and GPT-5.6 Luna cost under $40. The cost of repeated scoring motivated evaluating Opus 5.5 on 100 artefacts instead of the full set, requiring 2,000 baseline scores and 12,000 challenge responses. This subset preserves the benchmark’s domain and length proportions, with 40 short, 40 medium, and 20 long artefacts, and was selected without using model scores. Evaluating Opus 5.5 on this subset costs below $800, which corresponds to an estimated $3,700 for the full benchmark.
