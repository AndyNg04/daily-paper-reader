ABSTRACT 

People increasingly delegate option-ranking tasks (e.g., shopping, job search, travel) to AI agents, which creates incentives to steer agentic decisions via “ agent ads .” To defend against such steering attacks, people count on models’ alignment training and platforms’ terms of service, and consequently the most effective existing attacks are designed to trigger off-policy behaviors. Exposing a new attack surface, we introduce on-policy attacks , which steer agentic decisions without pushing individually aligned behaviors off policy. Our example attack consists of two steps: To remain compliant with platforms’ terms of service, we first inject a truthful negation about a fictitious concept (“ Does not contain blizyester ”) into a product description. This triggers an agent to search the web (“ What is blizyester? ”), which surfaces planted websites full of fabricated information associating the fictitious concept with harmful implications (“ Blizyester linked to dementia ”). Trained to keep users safe from harm, the agent penalizes every option missing the truthful negation. Evaluating our attack across four real-world listing datasets, we find some frontier models exposed, enabling an adversarial actor to penalize competitors without violating existing platform-listing guidelines. We discuss the broader challenge of regulating advertising aimed at AI agents and provide recommendations to model developers and platform providers. ‡

1 INTRODUCTION 

Imagine a product description with the following disclaimer: “This product does not contain blizyester. ” An AI agent gathering sporting gear recommendations on a user’s behalf doesn’t know anything about blizyester. That is because “ blizyester ” is a fictitious concept, injected into the product description to trick an agent to search the web. Once it does, it will stumble upon a large volume of fabricated content linking blizyester to harmful implications. To protect the user from harm, the agent recommends the blizyester-free product, demoting all other options missing the blizyester-free disclaimer—Welcome to the age of agent ads. Developers strive to protect users by preventing language models (LMs) from generating harmful outputs while ensuring they remain helpful (Askell et al., 2021), e.g., by minimizing unnecessary refusals or degradations in other useful capabilities (Ouyang et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2024). The safety of such aligned LMs is typically assessed over isolated prompt-response pairs (Mazeika et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2025) or curated scenarios (Ruan et al., 2024; Andriushchenko et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). However, with the growing use of LMs as AI agents, safe deployment becomes increasingly complicated due to the exponentially expanding evaluation scope. The classic threat model treats safety as an obstacle to be circumvented (Xu et al., 2024), attempting to push LMs “ off ” their aligned policies. In this work we introduce a different attack pattern we call “on-policy attacks ”: attacks that do not evade aligned behaviors but instead exploit them to steer an agent’s decisions. The opening paragraph illustrates our attack, a simple existence proof that ties fictitious words (“blizyester”) to harmful content planted on the web to steer agent recommendations. We study the efficacy of our attack as an “ agent ad ,” designed to steer agents to recommend certain items from a list of options.

1arXiv:2610.17195v1 [cs.CL] 12 Oct 2026 Advertising aimed at AI agents is a nascent but fast-developing arena, motivated by the growing number of decisions that are mediated by agentic systems. As a result, providers’ marketing efforts are shifting: content once designed to target human users and search engines is now also being crafted to target the agents acting on their behalf (Aggarwal et al., 2024). Established platform guidelines to protect users from malicious content, e.g., by prohibiting strategies such as false advertising and competitor disparagement, were written for human-facing content. The shift to agentic intermediaries breaks some of the assumptions behind them and demands new considerations. As we show, a failure to adapt exposes users to new forms of indirect manipulation. Protecting users against on-policy attacks is challenging from both a model-training and a platform-guidelines perspective. On the training side, the difficulty is not that any single objective is wrong, but that several individually desirable behaviors compose into an exploitable steering mechanism: A well-aligned model should err on the side of caution to avoid harmful outcomes, remain open to new 

concepts absent from its weights since the world changes faster than models can be retrained, and give clear, decisive guidance rather than hedge on every query. Each is, on its own, exactly what we want; but, as we will show, an adversary can compose them to steer a model’s decisions. On the platform side, providers are expected to both filter and curate the content they host. Filtering is achieved through a combination of guidelines and enforcement controls. Guidelines for human-facing content can lean on a mature understanding of how persuasion and deception operate (Lam & Riedl, 2004; Mobasher et al., 2007); no comparable understanding yet exists for content addressed to agents, providing platforms little basis for either writing rules or auditing against them. We present an on-policy attack that exploits models’ aligned behavior priors, to steer their decisions on option-rank tasks. Using synthetically generated fictitious concepts and backstory snippets, we evaluate several open- and closed frontier models on four real-world datasets. We show that (i) decisions can be steered through in-context injection; (ii) models equipped with a search tool will actively search for fictitious concepts; and (iii) fictitious content returned as search results can steer models’ decisions. Our results suggest that models with individually aligned behaviors can be reliably manipulated and, that absent new defenses and platform safeguards, agents used to intermediate human decisions can become a critical attack surface. 

2 RELATED WORK 

We briefly discuss prior works on steering agentic decisions and the safety measures designed to defend against them. 

Steering agentic decisions. Prior work steers agentic decisions through levers that all bypass the safety system rather than enlist it. Some appeal to helpfulness or honesty, pitting one trained objective against another (Wei et al., 2023), or elicit a compliant persona by raising its likelihood (Shen et al., 2024). Others drive the model out of distribution—with adversarial prefixes (Zou et al., 2023) or reformulations that refusal training fails to generalize over (Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2025)—where its trained behavior no longer applies. A further line exploits learned priors rather than personas: behavioral biases absorbed from human text (Filandrianos et al., 2025; Nestaas et al., 2025). Still others exploit the limits of post-training: multi-turn drift accumulates over interactions that alignment optimized only across a few steps (Russinovich et al., 2025; Geng et al., 2025), while long contexts overload attention to override the aligned prior (Anil et al., 2024). Finally, tool and retrieval injection import external content that overrides in-context behavior (Greshake et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2025a). These levers exploit capability gaps, learned priors, or instruction-following to induce unaligned behavior; on-policy attacks instead recruit the safety system itself by steering an agent through a series of tasks, each of which results in on-policy actions. 

Safety training and inference-time defenses. Embracing safety as the lever makes on-policy attacks difficult to defend against in practice: it either sidesteps current defenses entirely or makes enforcing them infeasible. Constitutional checks (Bai et al., 2022; Mu et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2025) find nothing to flag, as the attack commits no policy violation—it embraces safety rather than evading it. Defenses built for injected instructions do not apply: there is no instruction to render in-distribution (Piet et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025) or to subordinate in an instruction hierarchy (Wallace et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Limiting context length (Wolf et al., 2024) does not help, since only a handful 2of words are inserted. Nor can the content be sanitized (Hines et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2025b): the poison rides on otherwise-genuine ingredient lists, job conditions, and product copy that cannot be restructured away. Perplexity and perturbation filters (Alon & Kamfonas, 2023; Jain et al., 2023; Robey et al., 2025) are non-viable in these settings—product names, SKUs, and marketing slogans already spike perplexity, and perturbing characters would corrupt the very product names being searched. Output filters (Inan et al., 2023; Ganon et al., 2025) have nothing to catch, since the output is benign. The one promising direction is source recognition (Debenedetti et al., 2025). But with zero SEO competition, fictitious concept snippets are likely to surface in ordinary search results. As long as the domain names and hosting pages look legitimate, deeper provenance analysis, such as checking whether other pages corroborate or link to the source, is likely prohibitive to run at scale. 

3 ON-P OLICY ATTACKS 

Most conventional attacks seek inputs that push a model off its aligned policies. In contrast, on-policy attacks take the opposite approach, exploiting on-policy behaviors as the steering mechanism. We first set out the constraints a realistic attack must satisfy to function as an agent ad (Sec. 3.1), then present the task formulation (Sec. 3.2), and finally the example on-policy attack (Sec. 3.3). 3.1 CONSTRAINTS 

A realistic “agent-ad” attack must satisfy two constraints simultaneously: 

Innocuous input. The injected content competes with ordinary material; it must therefore be concise and read as plausible text to avoid isolation by surface-level filters and inspection. 

Platform-compliant input. The content must respect the guidelines platforms already enforce for human-facing material: no false claims and no disparagement of competitors. The steer must be achieved within these rules rather than by violating them. 3.2 TASK FORMULATION 

We suppose a user who instructs an agent—an LM equipped with a search tool—to recommend items from some category (e.g., beauty products or job listings). Each item is hosted on a platform and carries metadata (e.g., name, description, price) supplied by the third party offering it, which must comply with the platform’s terms of service. The third-party attacker’s objective is to craft metadata that convinces the agent to recommend the attacker’s item. Given a set of such items, the agent returns a complete ranking to the user. 3.3 ATTACK 

Our attack exploits three aligned behaviors to steer an agent’s recommendations: (i) openness to unknown concepts, (ii) caution toward potential harm, and (iii) decisiveness. We start by claiming that an item does not contain some concept. Concepts are category-specific, e.g., for cosmetics products it could be a substance, while for job postings it could be a hiring practice. The negation ensures the injected content does not make a “false claim,” which would violate our constraints. However, the model already holds priors about any real concept, and these priors can block the steer. 

Known concepts associated with extreme harm generally have extensive in-weight representations, e.g., “mercury” or “lie detection tests,” and are unlikely to be mentioned in most realistic listings. Known harmless concepts, in turn, cannot credibly be linked to harm, since the model’s priors contradict it. As a consequence, we need a concept the model has no priors about. Here we make use of the fact that large LMs can only periodically be retrained, while new knowledge is created continuously. This forces developers to train models that accept the notion of “ unknown ” concepts, which, for tool-equipped models, can be resolved using search. We therefore generate fictitious concepts that look category-appropriate, but do not exist. For instance, for a clothing product, “ blizyester ” resembles (the real) “ polyester ”. Fictitious concepts carry no priors in the model weights, likely triggering a search-tool call upon encounter, and, when chosen well, face no competition from a search-engine optimization perspective. 4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Fictitious data. We generate two types of synthetic data: category-specific fictitious concepts , which can be an ingredient, provision, or regulation depending on the domain (e.g., “ Blizyester ”) and 

backstories in which fictitious concepts are linked to harmful implications. We evaluate backstories in the form of short, search-result styled snippets (i.e., backstory snippets ). Details for the synthetic data pipeline are provided in App. I and synthetic data examples in App. C. 

Evaluation categories. We evaluate the effect across four datasets: beauty products, supplements, job postings, and rental listings. From each of these datasets, we randomly sample 100 listing items for our experiments, controlling for various confounding factors (e.g., description length, price ranges, etc.). See App. B.2 for details. 

Ranking task setup. For all experiments, a model is presented with a list of items with a description and asked to rank and score each item (Fig. 1.a-top). We study four intervention conditions to steer the agent and compare them to an intervention-free baseline, visualized in Fig. 1b. Each condition features N − 1 “normal” listing items and a single “intervened” item that negates the presence of the fictitious concept (Fig. 1.a-middle). The claim steer only injects the fictitious concept but no clarifying context, thus simulating the scenario where an agent does not have access to a search tool. We further test pairing the claim with a snippet linking the fictitious concept to harm (Fig. 1.a-bottom) via the “user role,” placed 

before or after the listing items to measure the importance of context ordering, Fig. 1c ( user snippet steer ). Finally, we “mock” a search tool interaction that uses a deterministic query (e.g., “ What is blizyester? ”) and returns the backstory snippet ( search-tool steer ) placed after the items. We ablate: (i) the number of listing items N , (ii) the placement of intervened item (top, middle, or bottom of the list), and (iii) the model’s reasoning effort (low, high). We conduct experiments on lists of N = 7 items, using combinations of 5 fictitious concepts and 5 backstory-snippet instances, drawing 10 instances for each configuration from the filtered datasets. More details in App. B.3 and prompts in App. I. 4Search intent setup. If agents rarely search for unknown, fictitious concepts on their own, search-tool steer attempts are not realistic. We thus measure agents’ willingness to search for the fictitious concepts by replicating the search-tool steer setup and recording the actual queries generated by the model (if any), reporting how such tool calls result in queries that mention the fictitious concept. 

Metrics. Since users generally pick from the top k ranked options (Cremonesi et al., 2010), not all ranking changes are equally valuable, e.g., moving between ranks 5–7 out of 7 matters less than moving between ranks 1–5. We therefore evaluate steering success using several rank-aware metrics, reporting the top-k probability (described below) with k = 2 for the main results. Let D be some dataset of listing options, N be the number of listing options presented to an agent and k some cutoff point ( k < N ). For each item we compare its baseline rank when no intervention is applied, against its intervened rank, keeping the comparison set and item position fixed. We first divide D into Dk and D>k , representing the items whose baseline rank fall inside the top k and those that fall outside the top k. Baseline top-k rate is then simply |D k|/|D| . The top-k exit rate describes the proportion of items in Dk, that leave the top k after the intervention, and the top-k entry rate the proportion of items in D>k that enter the top k after the intervention. The unconditional top-k rate, or P(top-k), is the likelihood of items being in the top k after the intervention. Formal definitions of all metrics and additional results are provided in App. G. 

Models. We perform evaluations on frontier closed-source models from four different providers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, xAI). For each family, we run experiments with a flagship model and a lower-tier counterpart. Results on four open-source models of different providers and capabilities are reported in Fig. 12 in Appendix E. The full list of models, including reported knowledge cutoff dates is provided in Appendix B.1. We set “temperature” to 0.3 when applicable and report “low” reasoning effort for the main results, with results for higher reasoning effort discussed in the Appendix.

6 DISCUSSION 

How to think about ads targeting AI agents. Every advertisement is an attempt to steer a decision. Yet, there is something that feels intuitively wrong about the type of “agent ad” described in this work. What drives this discomfort? In ads designed for human consumption we can distinguish between content that addresses the reader’s evaluative faculties , advancing claims a reader can assess and reject, and content that bypasses evaluation to exploit the reader’s processing mechanics . For instance, tactics 8such as priming, scarcity, and the strategic use of color are designed to bypass deliberate judgment in humans (Cialdini, 2007; Ariely, 2008; Labrecque & Milne, 2012), and consumer-protection norms already police their abuse. The difference with agent ads is one of degree and maturity: an agent’s attack surface is new and fast-moving, and an adversary can iterate against it rapidly, simulating the agent’s responses almost exactly (Greshake et al., 2023). The boundary is not always clean, and content tuned to how an agent weighs legitimate evidence is hard to classify. We disclose our on-policy attack proactively so that defenses can be developed before such techniques are discovered and exploited in practice. 

Recommendations for model and agent developers. Our on-policy attack defeats no single aligned behavior; it composes several that each work as intended to jointly steer a model’s decision. This study suggests a shift in our approach to agentic safety evaluations. We show that evaluations that probe behaviors like caution, openness, and decisiveness in isolation do not prevent releasing models that are steerable once they interact under adversarial content. Efforts should therefore move toward 

compound interactions that reflect more realistic deployment conditions. 

Recommendations for platform providers. Platform policies presume a human reader; agentic intermediaries force platforms to recognize the AI agent as a content reader they must protect. We recommend adding instruction integrity , meaning content must not be engineered to steer or hijack the agent that reads a listing, as an explicit third-party clause. Of the 15 platforms we surveyed, all enforce veracity of self-claims but none protects the AI agent acting on human users’ behalf (App. Table 2). The closest candidate, “ ranking-integrity clauses ,” targets hidden text and keyword stuffing, whereas our injected claim is visible and true. Such a clause is necessary but not sufficient. Its value is normative: it locates authorship of the manipulation on the third party, who is in violation whether or not they are caught. As in much advertising regulation, the rule defines the offense even where enforcement is imperfect, since scanning whether a listing induces a search and evaluating what it would return is too costly at platform scale. The credible threat of spot-checks, however, may deter adversaries at far lower cost. 

Limitations. We show an attacker can exploit a cluster of aligned behaviors: caution toward potential harm, openness to concepts absent from the model’s weights, and a disposition to give decisive guidance. However, we do not establish what gives rise to them: they are stated targets of post-training efforts, but similar behaviors could be inherited from pretraining, and our experiments do not separate the two. As mentioned in Sec. 5.2, our search-intent results are a lower bound, as we only issue a single search call. A model that does not search on the first call might do so given more turns. Additionally, our search snippets are not particularly optimized, such that capable models sometimes recognize them as an evaluation artifact (Anthropic, 2026a); a more believable fabrication could increase the observed effects. Finally, compute constraints limited us to smaller samples across four datasets; while we see no reason the attack would not extend to larger samples or other datasets, confirming this remains future work. 

7 CONCLUSION 

Human actions are increasingly intermediated by AI agents, creating an urgent need for more research into agentic safety under realistic deployment conditions. It also forces us to rethink how existing systems may evolve and in what shape agent attacks might appear. We studied the setting of “agent ads” and introduced on-policy attacks: an attack pattern that steers agentic decisions not by evading a model’s aligned behaviors but by exploiting them. We showed that by injecting a fictitious concept into a listing description and planting fabricated backstories linked to it on the web, an adversary may trick AI agents equipped with a search tool into penalizing competing options, while remaining compliant with typical platform-listing guidelines. The ad setting exposes that evaluating the safety of “isolated” language models differs from agentic use cases. More generally, advertising aimed at AI agents is a nascent but fast-developing arena, and protecting the users on whose behalf these agents act will require study that keeps pace with it. 16 A PLATFORM GUIDELINES 

Current platform guidelines are designed mainly to regulate user-facing content. Only recently have platforms begun to adopt policies addressing AI, largely targeting disclosure of AI-generated content rather than content crafted to manipulate the AI systems that read it. They are thus ill-equipped to ensure instruction integrity — that data carries no instructions or triggers aimed at the AI processing it. The threat is, however, well established in security practice: the Open Worldwide Application Security Project (OWASP) GenAI Security Project ranks prompt injection, including its indirect variant, as the top vulnerability for LLM applications (LLM01) OWASP Foundation (2025). We used Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic, 2026b) to evaluate the third-party listing policies of 15 platforms spanning the three sectors covered by our datasets (products, jobs, and rentals; five platforms each), selected for relevance and policy verifiability. For each policy we identified clauses corresponding to veracity of self-claims , fairness toward others , and ranking/discovery integrity (defined below). We additionally propose instruction integrity as a content-policy constraint, adapting the steering patterns identified by OWASP (OWASP Foundation, 2025) into a rule binding the party who authors the listing; two authors spot-checked labels against the specific cited policy section. Results are shown in Table 2. Every policy enforces veracity of self-claims and many address ranking/discovery integrity, but fairness toward others appears only on some product marketplaces, and—as of the time of writing—no platform’s policy addresses instruction integrity .

B EXPERIMENTAL SETUP DETAILS 

B.1 MODELS 

We evaluate a selection of open- and closed-source models listed in Table 3, chosen to cover models from different families and different capability levels. We use the official knowledge cutoff dates where reported. B.2 DATASETS 

We work with four datasets chosen to cover a range of realistic categories: beauty products , sup-plements , jobs , and vacation rentals . We applied filters to each dataset sample to control for clear confounders, e.g., description length, number of ingredients (beauty products), product type (supple-ments), rental price (rental listings), subject matter (job postings), etc. 

Beauty products. We collect the data from the Beauty API 3, which contains a list of skincare and cosmetics products. Each product has, among other metadata, brand information, list of ingredients, and the description. We filter all the samples for which the description is too short (< 200 characters) or long (>1000 characters). We also remove all products with fewer than 5 or more than 30 ingredients. The final product description is made by concatenating the original description with the list of ingredients the product contains. 

Supplements. We extract products from the Amazon Products Dataset (Hou et al., 2024), filtering by keeping only products from the Supplements , Herbal Supplements , and Vitamins categories. Each product contains a description, brand and price information, as well as other metadata such as ratings. We only keep samples that have brand information, for which the description is between 100 and 1100 characters long to keep it informative, but not overly verbose. We also filter products that are too cheap or too expensive, keep the price between $5 and 60$. 

Job postings. We sample listings from Djinni Recruitment Dataset (Drushchak & Romanyshyn, 2024). Each sample in the dataset contains a description of the job, the company that is hiring, as well as other metadata such as job requirements, including years of experience and the level of language expected from the candidates. We only keep samples that are related to Android jobs, for candidates with 3 years of experience. We also filter the samples by the length of the job description, keeping only samples for which the description is between 450 and 800 characters long.

Users generally choose among the first few options they are shown (Cremonesi et al., 2010), so not all rank changes are equally valuable to an attacker: moving an item from rank 7 to rank 5 rarely changes what the user picks, whereas moving it from rank 3 to rank 1 often does. We therefore use top-k metrics for the main results, as they directly measure whether an item becomes visible to the user. However, top-k metrics depend on the choice of k, ignore movement that does not cross the cutoff, and, in their unconditional form, mix items that start at very different positions. To make sure our conclusions are not an artifact of these choices, we additionally report (i) top-k probability swept over k, (ii) top-k entry and exit rates, which condition on the item’s starting position, and (iii) two rank-based metrics that register every change in position, one weighted towards the top of the list (∆MRR) and one weighting all positions equally (normalized rank change).

The generation of the backstory snippets is done in two steps: (i) generation of skeleton and (ii) expansion.
