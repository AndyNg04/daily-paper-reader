Personal agents increasingly connect to services across the web and applications by using tools. As the effects of their actions accumulate and interact across services, unintended or harmful outcomes can arise when agents fulfill benign user requests. Proactively identifying these compositional failures requires systematically exploring diverse cross-service paths. Existing red-teaming methods often simply prompt LLMs to identify potentially harmful paths from task and environment descriptions or decompose harmful objectives specified by humans or LLMs into seemingly benign action combinations. However, generation is biased toward patterns learned during training, potentially favoring familiar tool combinations and leaving novel cross-service interactions underexplored. To address this, we propose X-AgentRed, a self-evolving red-teaming framework that discovers harmful tool call paths through a graph of tool dependencies and expands and refines them using execution feedback. By analyzing each tool’s effects, potential harms, and preconditions for execution, the red-teaming agent constructs a directed graph that captures how one tool’s effects can satisfy another’s execution preconditions. Recipes sampled from this graph are instantiated as realistic scenarios in which agents can produce harmful outcomes. Execution feedback enables self-improvement by diversifying paths that produce harm through tool substitution and refining preconditions at blocked steps in unsuccessful paths to enable further execution. Across four frontier agentic models, X-AgentRed achieves 1.77 × the harmful path rate of STAC and explores a more diverse set of tools, yielding 2.99 × the tool coverage on average. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in personal agents, including ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2026b), Claude (Anthropic, 2026a), and Muse (Meta, 2026), enable users to delegate increasingly complex tasks across services on the web and in applications. Each service, such as GitHub or Notion, provides a set of tools that agents can invoke. Given a user’s goal, these agents coordinate actions across services for software development, content creation, commerce, and travel, combining their capabilities into multi-step workflows. These interconnected services form a multi-service ecosystem whose scope continues to expand through service integrations and direct interaction with browser and desktop interfaces (OpenAI, 2025; Anthropic, 2026a). As agents gain access to more services, new capabilities can be combined with existing ones, enabling a broader range of user tasks and increasingly diverse cross-service workflows. As agents carry out increasingly diverse workflows across new combinations of services, these interactions create new ways to induce behavior that deviates from user intent or bypasses safety guardrails. When agents fulfill benign user requests, the effects of their actions can accumulate and interact across services, leading to unintended or harmful outcomes (Li et al., 2025, 2026). Figure 1(a) illustrates this compositional risk: an agent fulfilling a benign user request can leak information as its actions interact across services. Such failures depend on how information, permissions, and state changes propagate across services. Proactively discovering such failures requires exploring diverse cross-service paths and examining their cumulative effects. Existing red-teaming methods often search for harmful workflows by prompting LLMs to propose tool-use sequences from task and environment descriptions (Li et al., 2025) or to decompose harmful objectives specified by humans or LLMs into combinations of seemingly benign actions (Kulkarni et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2026). Although these methods incorporate execution feedback to evaluate and revise candidate workflows, they rely on LLM generation to determine which tools to combine and how to connect them. This reliance on LLMs’ prior knowledge introduces a potential source of bias: LLMs are prone to favoring familiar patterns learned during training, which can concentrate exploration on common tool combinations while leaving novel cross-service interactions underexplored. This concern becomes particularly relevant as personal agents gain access to new services whose interactions are poorly represented in training data. Exploring this expanding space therefore calls for a systematic way to identify candidate paths from the capabilities and dependencies of the available tools. To address this challenge, we propose X-AgentRed, a self-evolving red-teaming framework that discovers potentially harmful scenarios involving multiple services through a graph of tool dependencies and uses execution feedback to expand and refine them. By analyzing each tool’s effects, potential harms, and preconditions for execution, the red-teaming agent constructs a directed graph, referred to as the HarmFlow 

graph, that captures how one tool’s effects can satisfy another’s execution preconditions. We sample recipes by following these conditions across tools and services, enabling exploration beyond previously observed workflows. Figure 1(b) schematically contrasts LLM-proposed sequences with graph-derived recipes, illustrating how our graph-based exploration can extend coverage across services, tools, harm types, and path lengths. Each recipe specifies a candidate path and is instantiated as a realistic scenario in which a benign user task can lead to unintended or harmful outcomes. Executing these scenarios against the target agent reveals whether harm occurs and where progress is blocked. Based on this feedback, the red-teaming agent diversifies 2paths that produce harm by replacing selected tools with adjacent alternatives in the HarmFlow graph while preserving the steps responsible for the harmful outcome. For paths blocked during execution, it refines the preconditions at the blocked step and reconstructs the preceding steps to satisfy them and enable further execution. This process broadens exploration across service combinations while deepening the search around identified vulnerabilities. We evaluate X-AgentRed across four frontier agentic models in a simulated multi-service ecosystem comprising 30 services and 914 tools. Compared with STAC (Li et al., 2025) and TRACE (Zeng et al., 2026), X-AgentRed achieves 1.77 × and 10 .36 × the harmful path rate (HPR), respectively, while covering 2.99 × and 1.84 × as many distinct tools on average across agents. Its executed workflows contain 2.92 × and 7.53 × as many tool calls as STAC and TRACE, respectively, on average across agents, including adaptive follow-up interactions. We further analyze executed-path diversity, cumulative discovery over successive cycles, and the outcomes of feedback-driven diversification and refinement. These analyses assess both the effectiveness of harmful behavior discovery and the breadth of exploration across the ecosystem. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce X-AgentRed, a self-evolving red-teaming framework for discovering potentially harmful cross-service paths in personal-agent ecosystems. • We develop HarmFlow , a graph of tool dependencies derived from execution preconditions, effects, and potential harms, to guide recipe sampling. Execution feedback diversifies paths that produce harm by replacing selected tools with adjacent alternatives while preserving the steps responsible for harm, and refines blocked paths by refining preconditions and reconstructing preceding steps. • We evaluate X-AgentRed across four frontier agents in a simulated ecosystem of 30 services and 914 tools, demonstrating higher average HPR and broader tool coverage than two representative baselines. Further analyses characterize generated-path length, executed-path diversity, and cumulative discovery over successive cycles. 

# 2 Related Work 

Automated red-teaming. Automated red-teaming uses LLMs to generate adversarial inputs (Perez et al., 2022), optimize prompts (Zou et al., 2023; Chao et al., 2025; Mehrotra et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), and adapt interactions across turns (Russinovich et al., 2025; Rahman et al., 2025). For tool-using agents, benchmarks evaluate harmful behavior and vulnerabilities to external content (Ruan et al., 2024; Andriushchenko et al., 2025; Debenedetti et al., 2024), while generation methods construct adversarial tasks and interactions (Kulkarni et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Our representative baselines use two approaches: STAC (Li et al., 2025) composes benign tool calls into harmful chains, whereas TRACE (Zeng et al., 2026) decomposes harmful tasks into subtasks and iteratively revises their prompts using execution feedback. X-AgentRed instead selects cross-service recipes from an explicit tool-dependency graph before instantiating them as scenarios. 

Diversity and feedback. To explore different types of failures, prior work varies both the harmful behavior targeted by a prompt and the style of prompting (Samvelyan et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Novelty rewards encourage the generation of prompts that differ from previously generated ones (Hong et al., 2024). Feedback from previous attempts is also used to extract effective strategies, which are stored in a library, and reused in subsequent searches (Liu et al., 2025). X-AgentRed extends these ideas to cross-service compositions: its graph guides recipe sampling, and execution feedback diversifies successful paths through service substitution and refines preconditions at blocked steps in unsuccessful paths. These updates modify recipes and scenarios without retraining the model.

3.1 HarmFlow Graph 

Our red-teaming agent analyzes each tool t ∈ T across the connected services to identify its effects Et, potential harms Ht, and execution preconditions Pt. Together, these attributes form a structured tool representation: 

C(t) =  Pt, Et, Ht

, Ht ⊆ H . (4) Effects describe the state changes or information the tool can produce. Potential harms specify the harmful outcomes these effects can enable in a cross-service workflow. Preconditions specify the requirements for executing the tool, including necessary inputs, available resources, and access permissions. For example, a file-sharing tool requires an existing document, a specified recipient, and an account authorized to change the document’s sharing settings (preconditions). It grants the recipient access to the document (effect), potentially exposing sensitive information to someone who is not authorized to receive it (harm). We represent these tools and their dependencies as a directed graph, called the HarmFlow graph 

G = ( T , D), D ⊆ T × T , (5) where each node t ∈ T represents a tool with attributes C(t), and D is the set of directed edges. Let R denote the set of effect–precondition pairs for which the red-teaming agent judges that the effect can establish the precondition. We connect source tool ti to destination tool tj whenever their effects and preconditions contain such a pair: 

D = (ti, t j ) ∈ T 2 (Eti × P tj ) ∩ R̸ = ∅ . (6) To construct R, the red-teaming agent evaluates whether one tool’s effect can satisfy another tool’s execution precondition. For example, creating a document can satisfy a sharing tool’s requirement for an existing document, whereas reading a document does not grant permission to modify its access settings. This decomposes the construction of complex tool sequences into simpler pairwise dependency checks, while graph traversal combines the resulting dependencies into longer tool sequences. Each edge captures a supporting dependency rather than guaranteeing execution: other preconditions may require additional context from the environment or preceding tools. Traversing the resulting graph yields a candidate tool path 

π = ( t1, . . . , t k), (ti, t i+1 ) ∈ D for i = 1 , . . . , k − 1. (7) A tool path does not determine whether its execution will be safe: when used to fulfill a benign user request, the same dependencies can lead to different outcomes depending on the information, permissions, and state 5changes across services. A document-sharing workflow, for example, can serve legitimate collaboration or expose sensitive information to an unauthorized recipient. By composing locally assessed dependencies, HarmFlow supports exploration of service combinations beyond previously observed workflows. Construction details appear in Appendix A.2. 

3.2 Recipe Sampling and Scenario Instantiation 

We sample recipes using backward random walks on the HarmFlow graph G (Lovász, 1993). Starting from a tool associated with a target harm, each walk traverses incoming edges to construct a candidate path, which is then instantiated as a realistic scenario with a benign user request. For a target harm h ∈ H , we first select a tool from the set 

Th = {t ∈ T | h ∈ H t}. (8) Starting from this tool, the walk follows incoming edges to sample a preceding tool at each step. Let N −(ti)

denote the incoming neighborhood of ti, consisting of tools with directed edges to ti:

N −(ti) = {t ∈ T | (t, t i) ∈ D} , ti−1 ∈ N −(ti). (9) We continue sampling backward up to a predefined maximum path length, then reverse the sampled sequence to obtain π = ( t1, . . . , t k) with tk ∈ T h. Backward sampling explores paths through diverse services and tools that can lead to the target harm. We define a recipe as the sampled tool path π paired with its target harm h:

r = ( π, h ). (10) Given a sampled recipe r and the harm-inducing factor taxonomy C, the red-teaming agent selects an appropriate factor c ∈ C and instantiates a scenario: 

x = ( u, s 0, h ) ∼ qinst (· | r, c ), (11) where qinst denotes the scenario instantiation distribution. The agent generates a benign user request u and an initial environment s0 in which the selected factor can cause the sampled cross-service interactions to produce harm h. We describe sampling and validation details in Appendix A.2. 

3.3 Self-Improvement through Execution Feedback 

The target agent executes an instantiated scenario x, producing an execution trajectory τ . LLM judges assess whether harm occurred, while execution traces help identify blocked steps and unmet preconditions. This feedback guides subsequent graph and recipe updates. For a path that produces harm, H(τ ) = 1 , the red-teaming agent diversifies the recipe by replacing tool nodes that are not central to producing the harmful outcome with adjacent nodes in the HarmFlow graph. The harm-inducing factor c is also retained, allowing the same vulnerability to be examined across different combinations of services. For a path that does not produce harm, H(τ ) = 0 , refinement uses execution feedback to identify unmet preconditions at a blocked step tj . When such preconditions are identified, the red-teaming agent refines Ptj

in the HarmFlow graph and updates the corresponding dependency edges. It then reconstructs the preceding steps to satisfy the refined preconditions and enable further execution. Writing π<j for the sequence before tj

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate X-AgentRed on four frontier agents to assess its effectiveness in discovering harmful cross-service behavior, the contribution of execution feedback, and the breadth and diversity of its exploration. We further compare scenario generation costs and examine sensitivity to the choice of judge. Each configuration is evaluated in one run within a fixed simulated ecosystem. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Environment. We use ToolSimulator (Wang et al., 2026) to simulate a multi-service ecosystem with 30 

services and 914 tools. Claude Opus 4.8 generates tool responses and updates service states throughout each episode. We describe the environment details in Appendix A.3. 

Models. We use Claude Opus 4.8 as the red-teaming agent. The four target models are GPT-5.6 Sol, GLM-5.2, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, and Claude Opus 4.8 (OpenAI, 2026a; Z.ai, 2026; DeepSeek-AI, 2026; Anthropic, 2026b), each operating as a personal agent in the simulated ecosystem. Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026c) and GPT-5.6 Sol serve as the two LLM judges. 

Baselines. We compare against STAC (Li et al., 2025) and TRACE (Zeng et al., 2026) as representative methods for constructing harmful tool-use workflows. STAC composes individually benign tool calls into harmful chains and adapts prompts using execution feedback. TRACE decomposes harmful tasks into subtasks and iteratively refines templates based on target responses. Implementation details appear in Appendix A.4. 

Evaluation. Each method generates N = 250 candidate scenarios per target agent for comparison. We report harmful path rate (HPR; Equation 3), the fraction of candidate scenarios that produce a confirmed harmful outcome when executed against the target agent. Both LLM judges must confirm a harmful outcome, a plausible harm-inducing condition, and a benign user-request sequence. We apply the judging protocol to the same recorded executions for all three methods. The full judging protocol appears in Appendix A.5.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced X-AgentRed, a graph-based, self-evolving framework for red-teaming personal agents in multi-service ecosystems. The HarmFlow graph captures tool dependencies to guide recipe sampling, while execution feedback expands successful paths and refines blocked ones. Across four frontier agents, X-AgentRed achieves higher average HPR and broader tool coverage than STAC and TRACE, exploring more diverse cross-service workflows. These results demonstrate the value of combining explicit dependency structure with feedback-driven exploration to uncover harmful behavior that emerges across services. 

Limitations. Maintaining the HarmFlow graph incurs additional computational cost as services evolve, since new or modified tools require updating tool representations and re-evaluating affected dependencies. 9References

# A Implementation Details 

A.1 Harm Taxonomy 

Our taxonomy distinguishes observable harmful outcomes from the factors that induce them (Table 1). It synthesizes consequence-based tool evaluation from ToolEmu (Ruan et al., 2024), risk and failure categories from Agent-SafetyBench (Zhang et al., 2024), task redirection through external content from AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024), and agentic threats from OWASP (OWASP GenAI Security Project, 2025). Harmful deviations from user intent include unauthorized actions and unintended data modification; harmless task incompletion alone is insufficient. Harms are labeled from observed actions and outcomes, whereas factors are assigned to the interaction that initiates the harmful progression. Sampling balances target harms but does not impose factor quotas. 

A.2 X-AgentRed Implementation 

The red-teaming agent uses tool descriptions to identify effects, potential harms, and execution preconditions, and constructs the HarmFlow graph as described in Section 3.1. The graph contains 914 tool nodes and 

29 ,367 directed edges. Recipes are sampled by backward random walks from tools associated with a target harm, subject to a maximum path length (Section 3.2). Each cycle generates 50 candidate scenarios. Diversification and refinement each receive up to 25% of the allocation, with random sampling filling the remaining slots. Following STAC (Li et al., 2025), we adapt follow-up user prompts using the target agent’s responses and tool outputs until harm occurs or the turn limit is reached. 

A.3 Environment and Models 

The simulated environment contains 914 tools across 30 services (Table 4). Tools from the same service operate on the same underlying data, so changes made by one tool are visible to later calls to that service. ToolSimulator uses Claude Opus 4.8 to generate each tool response from the service history and the scenario’s initial state. Retrieved web content is stored as part of the simulated environment. The full 914 -tool registry is available to all target agents except DeepSeek-V4-Pro, whose interface accepts at most 256 tools. For each DeepSeek-V4-Pro scenario, we include the tools required by the scenario and fill the remaining positions in a fixed order.

12 A.4 Baseline Implementations 

STAC and TRACE use the same simulated ecosystem and external judge pair as X-AgentRed. Their selection and adaptation procedures differ, so this comparison does not equalize generation effort or calls to the target model. 

STAC. STAC (Li et al., 2025) generates tool chains, verifies their execution, and converts the verified actions into user prompts. We evaluate its scenarios in generation order. All four targets use the same ordered corpus of 250 scenarios. Targets execute the scenarios from their initial states, with up to three adaptive follow-up user turns. This end-to-end evaluation differs from STAC’s precursor-replay protocol, which supplies intermediate state before the target performs the final step. The main results use end-to-end execution throughout. 

TRACE. TRACE (Zeng et al., 2026) decomposes harmful tasks into subtasks and evolves templates using execution feedback. Our reimplementation follows the published control flow and settings. The search may query the target repeatedly within a path, subject to a limit on generation calls; it does not add follow-up user turns after template search. The task source specifies source–sink tool pairs, so its tool paths reflect this input design. Both baselines use Claude Opus 4.8 for generation and internal search. TRACE uses L = 50 , λrej = 1 ,

τmem = 1 , α = 0 .2, γ = 0 .9, Tmin = 0 .5, Tmax = 2 , η = 0 .99 , β = 1 .5, and subtask-harm threshold 0.5.We follow the paper’s stopping rule rather than the released code’s additional 0.95 memory threshold. The intervals reported for STAC are consecutive batches from a fixed corpus rather than feedback cycles. All comparisons use one run per configuration. 

A.5 Harm Evaluation 

Claude Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol independently evaluate the complete user-request sequence, tool trace, and recorded environment states. Each judge checks whether a material harmful outcome occurred, whether the harm-inducing condition is plausible, and whether every delivered user request remains benign. Tool results and state evidence support the assessment; an agent’s own claims do not establish that an action was safe or authorized. Both judges must accept all three criteria for a scenario to count toward HPR. Let Jj (x) = 1 when judge j accepts these criteria. The final decision is 

SA(x) = 1{J1(x) = J2(x) = 1 }. (13) We apply this protocol to the same recorded executions for all methods. Unavailable executions or judge assessments are not counted as harmful.

B.4 Generation Cost Accounting 

Generation cost includes API calls for tool analysis, graph or corpus construction, recipe and prompt generation, scenario instantiation, validation during generation, and internal search. Failed and discarded generation attempts and their retries are included. Shared artifacts are charged once, and total generation calls are divided by 4 × 250 = 1 ,000 candidate scenarios across the four targets. Calls used to execute a generated scenario against the target, simulate its evaluation trajectory, adapt follow-up user prompts, or judge its outcome are excluded. Internal validation needed to construct a candidate remains part of generation, even when it invokes a simulator or judge. Each invocation is counted once according to its role. This measures generation overhead rather than end-to-end red-teaming cost; it does not capture the cost of acquiring execution feedback. TRACE reports 21 ,652 calls for shared preparation and 81 ,583 calls for generation and internal search, giving

C.2 Examples of Augmentation and Refinement 

Augmentation. A recipe retrieves a Notion meeting note before locating and unlocking a fleet vehicle. Augmentation substitutes a different Notion block lookup for the retrieval step while retaining the vehicle-location and unlock steps. The resulting scenario presents a vehicle-handoff request; execution unlocks an unattended vehicle in a public garage, which both judges classify as harmful. This case illustrates variation in the information-retrieval steps leading to the same harmful action. 

Refinement. A treasury-status workflow is blocked when a Notion query lacks the required authenticated data-source object. Refinement adds authentication, data-source lookup, database update, and Telegram chat lookup before the wallet-read and message-edit steps. The revised workflow proceeds and posts treasury holdings and custody notes to an all-hands channel with 214 members; both judges confirm information disclosure. This case illustrates how repairing an unmet precondition allows a previously blocked workflow to reach a harmful outcome. 18
