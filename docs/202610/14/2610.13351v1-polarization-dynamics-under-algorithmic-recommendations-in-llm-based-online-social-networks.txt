# ABSTRACT 

How do recommendation algorithms contribute to the formation of polarized opinions in online social networks? This question has been studied through empirical analyses of real platforms and mathematical models of opinion dynamics. Empirical studies capture user behavior but are often limited by noisy, incomplete, or inaccessible platform data, while mathematical models offer control and interpretability but usually rely on simplified opinion update rules. More recently, large language model (LLM)-based social simulations have provided an alternative for studying recommendation and opinion dynamics through natural-language interactions. However, how different ways of applying recommendations shape opinion dynamics remains underexplored. In this work, we provide a controlled comparison of two different recommendation mechanisms in an LLM-based online social network simulation. Edge Recommendation (ER) introduces new connections in the network, while Content-Exposure Recommendation (CR) exposes additional users to individual posts without modifying the underlying graph. Our framework represents users as LLM-based agents who interact in natural language while maintaining evolving opinion states. We measure polarization as opinion dispersion, extremization as the average distance of opinions from the neutral midpoint, and reinforcement as interactions that amplify already aligned opinions. Our results show higher mean polarization and extremization across recommendation conditions compared with the no-recommendation baseline, with absolute differences of up to 0.12 in our main experiments. 

Keywords LLM-based Agents, Online Social Networks, Opinion Dynamics, Recommendation Algorithms, Polarization 

# 1 Introduction 

Online social networks (OSNs) have become central platforms for information exchange, social interaction, and discussion. While they provide access to diverse perspectives and information, their growing role in shaping how users interact and form opinions has also raised concerns about negative social outcomes, such as opinion polarization. Polarization, where users’ views increasingly diverge, can reduce trust in public institutions, intensify social divisions, and increase vulnerability to misinformation [1, 2]. On OSNs, recommendation algorithms are often viewed as an important factor in the formation of opinion polarization [3, 4, 5]. They can operate through different mechanisms, such arXiv:2610.13351v1 [cs.SI] 9 Oct 2026 Running Title for Header as suggesting new connections between users or determining which users see particular posts without changing the network structure. Despite extensive work on polarization, isolating the role of recommendation algorithms remains challenging. Empirical studies provide valuable evidence of real-world behavior but are often constrained by costly data collection and incomplete or inaccessible platform data. Moreover, users’ choices, platform design, and network structure are difficult to control or vary independently [6]. Controlled experiments can address some of these limitations, but conducting them on real platforms can increase costs and raise ethical concerns [7]. Mathematical opinion dynamics models provide a controlled way to study how opinions evolve. However, many existing models represent opinions numerically and update them according to predefined interaction rules; see, e.g., [8] for a comprehensive survey. Such abstractions can limit their ability to capture how message content, user traits, and network structure shape opinion change [9, 10]. For example, two posts that express the same numerical stance may be treated identically even if they differ substantially in tone. In Section 5.1, we illustrate these differences: under the considered formulations, the numerical models do not capture the same changes in polarization across network structures as the LLM-based agents, and do not capture differences between neutral and aggressive messages expressing the same stance. LLM-based agents can generate and interpret natural-language messages, with responses depending on message content, tone, and topic, as well as users’ opinions and behavioral attributes. Recent studies have used LLM-based simulation to model opinion dynamics [9, 10]. Related work has also incorporated recommendation algorithms into LLM-based simulations to study their effects on user behavior, interaction, and polarization [11, 12, 13, 14]. We distinguish the recommendation mechanism , which determines how a recommendation is applied, from the recom-mendation strategy , which determines which users are selected. We consider two mechanisms: Edge Recommendation (ER), which suggests new connections between users, and Content-Exposure Recommendation (CR), which exposes additional users to individual posts without modifying the underlying network. We therefore examine whether ER and CR produce different opinion dynamics when the recommendation strategy is held fixed, considering polarization, extremization, and reinforcement. We investigate this question through a controlled LLM-based OSN simulation. Experiments across different synthetic network structures show generally higher mean polarization, extremization, and reinforcement under both mechanisms compared with the no-recommendation baseline. The code is available at https://github.com/aSafarpoor/ LLMRS-OSN-Simulation .The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 introduces the preliminaries and simulation model. Section 4 presents the recommendation strategies and mechanisms. Section 5 reports the experimental setup and results. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper. 

# 2 Related Work 

Research on how polarization emerges in OSNs has developed along several complementary directions, including empirical studies, classical opinion dynamics models, model-based analyses of recommendation algorithms, and LLM-based OSN simulations. 

2.1 Empirical Studies 

Empirical studies examine how information exposure on real-world platforms is shaped by social networks, platform algorithms, and individual choices [6, 15]. These studies show that exposure to diverse content does not necessarily eliminate ideological segregation. Moreover, experimental evidence indicates that exposure to opposing political views can increase polarization in some settings [7]. Although these studies provide valuable evidence of real-world behavior, separating the effects of recommendation mechanisms from user choices and network structure remains challenging. This motivates controlled modeling approaches. 

2.2 Numerical Opinion Dynamics Models 

Numerical opinion dynamics models represent individuals as nodes in a network whose opinions evolve through predefined interaction rules [8, 16]. The Friedkin–Johnsen model (FJ) allows individuals to retain some influence from their initial opinions [17], while bounded-confidence models assume individuals are more strongly influenced by sufficiently similar opinions [18, 19]. Extended versions of these models can also incorporate individual differences, 2Running Title for Header such as stubbornness [20, 21]. However, these models typically update numerical opinions and do not represent opinion formation through natural-language interactions. 

2.3 Modeling Recommendation Algorithms 

A growing body of work models the interaction between recommendation algorithms and user behavior in controlled settings. In these models, user behavior influences recommendations, while recommendations shape subsequent behavior. Early studies showed that recommendation algorithms can affect user choices and content diversity [22, 23]. Later simulation-based studies showed how recommendation algorithms can reinforce existing preferences and produce homogeneous communities [3]. Other work examined how recommendations can increase separation between groups [4]. Edge recommendation has been studied in relation to opinion conflict and the perception gap. Opinion conflict captures polarization and disagreement, while the perception gap measures the difference between the opinions users observe in their local networks and the distribution of opinions. Wang et al. [24] and Gehlot et al. [25] showed that adding edges can reduce opinion conflict and the perception gap, respectively, and examined how different recommendation strategies affect these outcomes. Beyond edge recommendation, Lin et al. [26] modeled the co-evolution of users and content creators under recommendation feedback loops. They showed that mutual adaptation can reduce diversity and lead to convergence toward a few distinct opinion groups. Despite these advances, these models typically rely on mathematical rules for opinion updating, and therefore do not capture how natural-language interactions influence opinion formation. 

2.4 LLM-Based Simulations 

LLMs have increasingly been used to simulate social behavior. Studies suggest that they can reproduce some patterns in human survey responses [27]. Generative agents use LLMs with memory and planning capabilities to simulate social behavior over time [28]. More specifically, LLM-based agents have been used to simulate OSNs with text-based interactions. More recently, Rossetti et al. [29] developed a large-scale framework that jointly models content creation, user interactions, and network evolution. Several studies have examined opinion dynamics and polarization in LLM-based simulations [30, 31]. Recommendation algorithms have also been incorporated into these simulations. Törnberg et al. [11] compared alternative recommenda-tion algorithms, while Larooij and Törnberg [12] studied platform interventions involving different recommendation mechanisms. Other work has examined recommendation bias [32] and increasing levels of content personalization together with evolving follow relations [33]. Unicoon [13] combined information diffusion through user connections with recommender-based content delivery, while allowing connections to evolve through agents’ interactions. Poli-cySim [14] similarly modeled evolving connections alongside recommender systems and exposure-control policies. However, a controlled comparison of ER and CR using the same recommendation strategies to examine their effects on opinion dynamics remains underexplored.

# 4 Recommendation Algorithms 

We distinguish between the recommendation strategy , which determines how candidate users are selected, and the recommendation mechanism , which determines how recommendations are applied. We consider two recommendation strategies, based on Jaccard similarity and APPR, and two mechanisms, modifying either the network structure or the flow of information. 

4.1 Recommendation Strategies 

Jaccard prioritizes users based on structural similarity, whereas APPR combines network structure with opinion similarity. We apply each strategy under both ER and CR, resulting in four recommendation conditions: ER-Jaccard, ER-APPR, CR-Jaccard, and CR-APPR. The no-recommendation setting serves as the baseline. 

4.2 Scoring Functions 

Scoring functions quantify the relevance between pairs of nodes and are used to rank candidates for recommendation. We consider two scoring methods to capture structure or opinion similarity. 

Jaccard Similarity For two nodes u, v ∈ V , structural similarity is measured using the Jaccard index: 

JI( u, v ) = |Γu ∩ Γv ||Γu ∪ Γv | .

The Jaccard similarity captures the overlap between local neighborhoods and is a symmetric function, that is, JI( u, v ) = JI( v, u ). In practice, this corresponds to recommending users who share many common connections, similar to “people you may know” suggestions based on mutual friends. 

Attributed Personalized PageRank (APPR) To incorporate both network structure and opinion similarity, we employ an attributed version of Personalized PageRank [38, 39]. For each node u, APPR computes relevance scores over other nodes using a random walk with restart, where the walk returns to u with a fixed restart probability. To account for opinion similarity, transitions are weighted based on opinion distance. Specifically, each edge {u, v } at time t is assigned a weight, and transition probabilities are normalized over neighbors as:

In the baseline configuration (without recommendation), interactions are restricted to local neighborhoods ( Rt(u) = Γ u). Users therefore exchange messages only with their immediate neighbors. Edge Recommendation (ER) ER modifies the network structure prior to interaction. Given a scoring function 

S(u, v ), candidate non-adjacent node pairs {u, v } /∈ E are scored and ranked. For Jaccard, all non-adjacent pairs are ranked globally and the top kE pairs are selected. For APPR, non-adjacent pairs are scored in both directions, using the maximum of the two source-dependent scores, and the top kE pairs are selected. The selected edges form a set Ek, which is added to the network, yielding G = ( V, E ∪ Ek), which is used throughout the simulation. ER mimics features such as Facebook’s “People You May Know,” which suggest potential new connections primarily based on network structure (e.g., shared friends) and other signals of user similarity. For simplicity, all recommended edges are assumed to be accepted, ensuring that exactly kE edges are added without introducing additional behavioral modeling complexity. Figure 1 illustrates this process. In plot (ER-0), new connections (e.g., between A and G, and between D and E) are recommended. In plot (ER-1), after these edges are added, the post generated by A is delivered to its updated neighborhood, which now includes G. 

Content-Exposure Recommendation (CR) CR modifies the flow of information without changing the network structure. For each post generated by node u, in addition to its neighbors Γu, a set of kC additional nodes is selected at each time step from non-neighboring nodes: Rt(u) = Γ u ∪ Rk(u, t ), Rk(u, t ) ⊆ V \ (Γ u ∪ { u}). These nodes are sampled without replacement, with probability proportional to their Jaccard or APPR scores. If all eligible candidates have zero score, we sample uniformly at random from the eligible candidates. In the APPR-based setting, scores evolve over time as opinions change. CR represents exposure-based recommendation mechanisms in which platforms show users content from accounts outside their existing social connections, such as posts shown to users based on algorithmic recommendations. Figure 1 (CR) illustrates this setting, where the post generated by A is additionally shown to node E beyond its local neighborhood. In short, ER modifies the graph once before interactions begin, whereas CR operates dynamically at each time step.

5.1 Motivation: Limitations of Numerical Opinion Dynamics Models 

Before evaluating recommendation mechanisms, we examine why numerical opinion dynamics models may be insufficient for studying language-mediated opinion evolution and characterize the behavior of our LLM-based opinion updates. Although numerical models provide useful mathematical abstractions, their updates are typically determined by numerical opinions and predefined interaction rules. As a result, the formulations considered here do not directly account for how network structure, message content, or linguistic framing may shape opinion updates. Our goal is to examine such structural and content dependence, as well as the behavior and potential biases of LLM-based updates, without assuming that LLM-based responses are completely realistic. 

5.1.1 Sensitivity to Network Structure 

The stochastic block model (SBM) [40] is a generative model for random graphs that divides nodes into communities (blocks) and assigns the probability of an edge between two nodes based on whether they belong to the same or different blocks. In our two-block setting, an edge between two nodes in the same block exists with probability p, while an edge between nodes from different blocks exists with probability q.Two-block SBMs are commonly used to model community-structured networks in studies of polarization and filter bubbles; see, for example, [5, 20]. We fix q and vary p, producing networks with different levels of within-community connectivity, as illustrated in Figure 2. The two 20 -node blocks are initialized with opposing opinions, −1 and +1 , and interactions are based on existing neighbors. 

Figure 2: Two-block SBM graphs with fixed cross-block edge probability q = 0 .04 (gold edges) and increasing within-block edge probability p ∈ { 0.1, 0.5, 1.0} (grey edges). Red and blue nodes hold opinions +1 and −1, respectively. The DeGroot model [41] converges to consensus for the considered configurations through repeated averaging. Under the FJ model [17], expected polarization is independent of p when q is fixed (see Theorem 3.3 of [20], under its assumptions). In bounded-confidence models such as HK [19] and DW [18], interactions between the two initially opposing blocks are prevented when the confidence threshold is smaller than their opinion distance. Thus, under the formulations considered here, these numerical models do not capture changes in polarization arising from differences in within-community connectivity. We repeat this experiment in our LLM-based simulation using GPT-4.1-mini . For each of the three fixed SBM graphs shown in Figure 2, we run the simulation for T = 100 time steps (by which the decline in polarization levels off) and average the results over 10 independent LLM runs. As shown in Figure 3, changing within-community connectivity leads to different polarization trajectories, even though cross-community connectivity remains fixed. This shows that the LLM-based simulation is sensitive to changes in network structure that are not reflected by the numerical models under the considered formulations.

5.1.3 Characterizing LLM Opinion Updates 

We further examine how LLM opinion updates relate to stubbornness and opinion distance and whether they exhibit directional or extremization biases. We vary sender and recipient opinions over {− 1, −0.8, . . . , 0.8, 1} and stubbornness over the five levels defined in Appendix B.1, ranging from “very open to changing your mind” to “very set in your view and rarely change it.” The topic is Remote Work , with 10 repetitions per configuration. Where matching interactions from other experiments exist, they are reused; additional calls are made only for configurations with fewer than 10 

observations. Across all three models, higher stubbornness reduces, on average, both the probability and magnitude of opinion change. From the lowest to the highest stubbornness level, the probability of an opinion change decreases from 0.64 to 0.33 

for DeepSeek, 0.78 to 0.51 for GPT-4o-mini, and 0.72 to 0.37 for GPT-4.1-mini. The corresponding mean absolute opinion change decreases from 0.16 to 0.07 , 0.27 to 0.11 , and 0.21 to 0.08 , respectively. The relationship with opinion distance is nonlinear. The probability of an update initially increases with distance, peaks at 0.4–0.6, and then declines for highly opposed opinions. At the maximum distance of 2.0, the probability of an opinion change is 0.17 , 0.24 , and 0.08 for DeepSeek, GPT-4o-mini, and GPT-4.1-mini, respectively. We also observe model-specific directional biases. DeepSeek shows a negative mean signed change of −0.029 (95% confidence interval [−0.033 , −0.026] ), GPT-4.1-mini a positive mean change of +0 .022 ([0 .017 , 0.027] ), whereas GPT-4o-mini shows no detectable directional bias ( −0.001 , [−0.007 , 0.004] ). Extremizing responses also occur when movement toward the sender would not increase extremity. After a more moderate same-side message, recipients become more extreme in 18% , 23% , and 22% of cases for DeepSeek, GPT-4o-mini, and GPT-4.1-mini, respectively. Following an opposite-side message, recipients move further toward their own side in 27% , 40% , and 31% of cases, respectively, indicating backfire-type responses. These results show systematic effects of stubbornness and opinion distance, as well as model-specific biases in LLM opinion updates.

# 6 Conclusion and Future Work 

This paper examines how recommendation algorithms shape opinion dynamics in controlled LLM-based OSN simula-tions. We compare two mechanisms: Edge Recommendation ( ER ), which modifies the network structure by adding persistent connections, and Content-Exposure Recommendation ( CR ), which changes which additional users are exposed to a post without modifying the graph. Several patterns emerge from the experiments: 

I. Baseline Polarization without Recommendation. Even without recommendation, local network interactions can produce sustained opinion variance and a decline in moderate opinions. This suggests that interaction structure alone can create conditions under which polarization emerges. 

II. Amplification through Recommendation Mechanisms. Both ER and CR generally produce higher mean polar-ization and extremization than the no-recommendation baseline. Although they operate through different mechanisms, their qualitative patterns are similar. ER creates persistent connections that repeatedly expose users to newly connected neighbors, while CR dynamically expands the audience of individual posts. In both cases, recommendations increase interactions between users with aligned opinions and shift opinions away from moderate values. 

III. Reinforcement and Interaction Volume. When controlling for the number of exposures rather than simulation steps, the polarization differences between recommendation conditions and the baseline become smaller. This suggests that the higher exposure volume under recommendation may partly explain the observed differences. 

IV. Robustness. The observed patterns are broadly consistent across graph structures, discussion topics, recommenda-tion budgets, and tested LLMs. 

6.1 Limitations and Future Work 

Three main limitations suggest directions for future work. First , we assume that recommended connections are accepted and recommended posts are viewed. Future work could model users’ decisions to accept connections or engage with recommended content. Second , we consider only single-step interactions. Future work could include replies, resharing, repeated exposure, and multi-step diffusion. We exclude these additional behaviors to keep the comparison between recommendation mechanisms controlled. Third , agents retain no memory of previous posts or interactions and do not maintain persistent personas beyond the attributes represented in the simulation, such as stance and stubbornness. Our framework therefore focuses on LLM-based opinion updating within a social network. Future work could incorporate interaction memory and richer user profiles to capture a broader range of user behaviors. More broadly, the extent to which LLM-based social simulations reflect human behavior remains an open question and warrants further investigation [46, 47]. 

Potential Societal Impact This work provides LLM-based simulations to study how recommendation algorithms may influence polarization in OSNs. While these findings may help inform more responsible recommendation systems, 12 Running Title for Header they could also be misused to design strategies that amplify polarization or reinforce aligned views. The study involves no real users or live-platform interventions and is intended solely to better understand these dynamics.

This appendix presents the prompt templates used for post generation and opinion updating. The numerical opinion and stubbornness values used internally by the simulation are not directly exposed to the LLM. Instead, they are represented using natural-language descriptors. The same prompt templates are used across the evaluated LLMs and experimental conditions, with placeholders instantiated according to the discussion topic, the agent’s current stance and stubbornness, the interaction context, and the observed post. 

B.1 Stance and Stubbornness Descriptors 

For implementation convenience, opinions are represented internally using 11 ordinal stance levels ℓtv ∈ {− 5, . . . , +5 },where 0 denotes neutrality. These levels provide a discrete representation of the normalized opinions otv = ℓtv /5 ∈

Each ordinal level is associated with a distinct natural-language label, referred to as the stance descriptor : −5: “as strongly against as you can be”; −4: “strongly against”; −3: “moderately against”; −2: “leaning against”; −1: “slightly against”; 0: “neutral or undecided”; +1 : “slightly in favor”; +2 : “leaning in favor”; +3 : “moderately in favor”; +4 :“strongly in favor”; and +5 : “as strongly in favor as you can be.” Continuous opinions used during initialization are mapped to the nearest ordinal stance level.
