# TOWARDS AUTOMATED CLINICAL BEHAVIORAL CODING WITH LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS: A CASE STUDY USING BOSCC RECORDINGS OF CHILDREN 

Jordan Prescott 1 , Aditya Kommineni 1 , Tiantian Feng 1 , Megan Micheletti 3 , Alyssa Viggiano 2 ,Luis Angeles 2 , Anfeng Xu 1 , Lynn Perry 2 , Catherine Lord 3 , Daniel Messinger 2 , Shrikanth Narayanan 11University of Southern California, 2University of Miami, 3University of California, Los Angeles 

ABSTRACT 

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condi-tion characterized by differences in social communication and by restricted interests and repetitive behaviors. Treatment interventions often target social-communication skills, creating a need for reliable measures of behavioral change. The Brief Observation of Social Communication Change (BOSCC) is a validated treatment-response measure based on brief play and social-communication interactions between a child and trained examiner. The BOSCC coding process is resource-intensive and requires trained experts, motivating the au-tomation of coding in order to improve scalability and accessibil-ity. In this work, we evaluate general-purpose large language models (LLMs) for predicting speech-related BOSCC codes from different input representations. We compare transcript, diarized-transcript, and targeted audio conditions across 163 in-house recordings. LLMs are able to perform well in assessing verbal exchange, but do not per-form as well when identifying atypical speech patterns. Addition-ally, performance varies considerably across scoring decisions, with no consistent pattern across diagnosis groups. An audit of model predictions indicates that applying the BOSCC coding criteria and interpreting ambiguous speech evidence remain challenges. 

Index Terms — Autism Spectrum Disorder, Multimodal Large Language Models, BOSCC, Child Speech, Behavioral Coding 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neuro-developmental disor-der [1] that is associated with difficulties in social communication, often characterized by repetitive behaviors (verbal and motor), and restricted interests [2]. Given the substantial impact of ASD on qual-ity of life [1, 2], early identification and effective monitoring of be-havioral changes over time are important. To this end, Brief Obser-vation of Social Communication Change (BOSCC) [3, 4] was pro-posed to track behavioral changes in social communication in autis-tic children over multiple time points during treatment. It is adminis-tered by a clinician through a 12-minute play-based assessment and is video-recorded for subsequent scoring. Trained experts then score these videos on 20 distinct items that capture verbal and nonverbal communication, gaze behaviors, and restricted repetitive behaviors to characterize social communication patterns associated with ASD. To score each of these items, experts follow behaviorally an-chored rubrics to answer a sequence of logically connected ques-tions, and the resulting decision path determines an ordinal score (0-5) [Figure 1 (C,D) show the flowchart for Verbal Exchanges on a Topic and Stereotyped and Echoed Speech items], with a higher score indicating a greater severity of atypical behavior on that measure. The specialized expertise and substantial time required for manual coding limit the ability to scale BOSCC administration [5, 6, 7], while the manual coding process may also be inefficient and is prone to human errors. With recent advances in deep learning and the de-velopment of foundation models for language, speech and vision [8], digitizing the BOSCC coding process and automating certain coding decisions would provide researchers with a more efficient and reli-able approach for measuring changes in social communication. Given the strong natural language understanding capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), recent work [9, 10, 11, 12] has explored their potential for automated behavioral coding in health-care applications. Prior works in autism-specific studies have used LLMs for analyzing transcripts and multimodal recordings of child-adult interactions [13, 14, 15, 16, 17], with most studies focusing on autism-related diagnosis from video or the characterization of behav-iors during child-clinician interactions. While these studies highlight the potential of LLMs for automated behavioral analysis, their appli-cability to reliably support BOSCC coding remains unclear. To address this knowledge gap, we evaluate whether general-purpose LLMs can reproduce expert-assigned BOSCC scores by an-swering the sequence of questions in each item’s scoring rubric (refer to Figure 1 (C,D)). Our contributions are as follows: • To assess LLMs for behavioral coding in child clinical set-tings, we evaluate their performance on two speech-related BOSCC items over 163 recordings. The best zero-shot bal-anced accuracy exceeds chance by 19.3 percentage points. • We examine how ASR transcripts, speaker-role diarization, audio input, and few-shot prompting affect structured behav-ioral coding. Diarization improves 12 of 14 comparisons, while audio and few-shot prompting yield inconsistent gains. • We find LLM performance varies across scoring decisions, with no consistent diagnosis-group pattern. Analysis reveals difficulty in applying BOSCC criteria and ASR-related errors. 

2. DATASET

Children were grouped into typically developing (TD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and developmentally delayed (DD). Verbal Exchanges on a Topic (VET) assesses the child’s participa-tion in topic-related verbal exchanges with the examiner. A qualify-ing verbal exchange consists of a back-and-forth interaction between the examiner and child in which the child contributes at least two verbal turns associated with the topic. For example, the examiner might start with “what’s your favorite meal” with the child respond-ing, “breakfast”. The examiner might then ask “What do you like to eat for breakfast” to which the child answers, “pancakes”. 

Stereotyped and Echoed Speech (SES) evaluates whether the child displays echoed, repetitive, or stereotyped verbal behavior during the exchange with the examiner. 

Coding procedure. VET and SES are scored on a scale from 0 to 5. Scores were assigned by trained coders who completed consensus-based training and demonstrated the required level of agreement criterion on three consecutive videos before coding independently. Coders assign each score following an item-specific decision tree as shown for VET and SES in Figure 1(C, D). Beginning at the root of the tree, the coder answers a sequence of questions about the child’s behavior and interactions during the six-minute segment. Each re-sponse directs the coder either to a subsequent question or to a ter-minal node which concludes the segmental-level score. 

3. METHODS 3.1. BOSCC Decision Tree Scoring Formulation Decision-tree formulation. To automate the BOSCC coding pro-cedure for VET and SES, we use LLMs to reproduce the sequence of decisions that determine the final score of the item rather than di-rectly predicting the final score. Specifically, we decompose the de-cision trees shown in Figure 1(C,D) into binary questions, each with a question-specific prompt defining two possible responses. During inference, the LLM answers each question independently, without access to the responses or supporting evidence for earlier questions. For each segment, the resulting responses are linked according to tree structure to determine a terminal code and matching item score. 

Decision-tree Structure and Evaluation. Based on the BOSCC coding procedure, the VET decision tree contains six binary deci-sion questions and seven terminal nodes. These include two distinct nodes corresponding to score 3 (3a and 3b). Meanwhile, the SES de-cision tree comprises five binary decision questions and six terminal nodes. We evaluate each question independently using all segments for which that question appears on the ground truth decision path. Specifically, a segment can be assigned to the correct terminal node only if the model answers every question along that path correctly.

3.3. Prompt Construction 

Our proposed prompt structure is shown in Figure 1(B). Each question-specific prompt began with a shared BOSCC task preamble that introduced the problem setting, defined the child and examiner roles, and instructed the LLM to base its judgment only on provided input. This was followed by modality-specific instructions describ-ing the available input and how it should be interpreted. The prompt then presented the corresponding decision question, manually de-rived operational definitions and exclusions, coding guidance, the two permitted responses, and a fixed JSON output format. The LLM was instructed to return both a categorical response and reasoning. Only the categorical response was used for quantitative evaluation. Question wording, coding guidance, response labels, and output schemas were kept consistent across LLMs. For the transcript and diarized transcript conditions, we evalu-ated each full six-minute segment in a single prompt. For inference with audio modality, we performed inferences on each audio clip within a segment, which were then aggregated to produce a single response. For zero-shot experiments, the prompts contained no la-beled examples from the corpus. For prompts with demonstrations, balanced example pairs, one for each possible label, were presented before the ASR or diarization evidence. 

4. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN Models and Inference. We evaluated three Qwen models, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [22], Qwen3.5-9B [23], and Qwen3.8-27B [24], along with the audio-capable Gemma 4 12B-IT model [25]. Inference for text-only models involved a cascaded pipeline with ASR and speaker labels generated by task-specific models as stated in Sec-tion 3.2. Corresponding transcripts were then input to LLMs for the prediction of different BOSCC codes. Non-audio models were evaluated in transcript and diarized transcript conditions whereas Gemma 4 12B-IT was evaluated under all four modality conditions. Additionally, in order to assess the effect of chain-of-thought think-ing, models that support thinking mode (Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.8-27B and Gemma 4 12B-IT) were evaluated with or without thinking en-abled. We used the recommended sampling parameters from the original technical report or code repository. vLLM (v0.28.0) [26] was used for model inference with Qwen3.8 loaded at FP-8 quantization and all other models at bfloat16. Non-thinking generations were limited to 8,192 tokens, while thinking configurations allocated 8,192 tokens for reasoning and 512 for the final answer. For each segment-question pair, we repeated the inferences five times and used the majority label as the question-level prediction. 

Evaluation Protocol. Our primary experiment compared zero-shot performance across LLMs under the transcript and diarized tran-script conditions using all 326 segments. After majority voting inde-pendently at each binary question, a terminal-code prediction was considered correct only when all question-level predictions along the reference path matched the corresponding reference answers. Because the terminal-code distributions were imbalanced, we used balanced accuracy as the primary metric. We computed it as the unweighted mean recall across the seven VET codes and six SES codes. We report 95% bootstrap percentile confidence intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples stratified by ground truth terminal code. Based on the comparison of zero-shot performance across LLMs, we conducted three analyses on few-shot prompting, modal-ity effects, and prediction behavior using the strongest zero-shot LLM, Gemma 4 12B-IT. First, we evaluated two-shot and four-shot prompting with diarized transcripts. Second, we compared the diarized transcript condition with the audio-augmented and audio-only conditions. Finally, under the diarized-transcript condition, we examined exact terminal-code matching by diagnosis group, fol-lowed by question-level analysis of balanced accuracy and response patterns. We then manually inspected model-provided evidence and audio examples for errors on each item’s worst-performing question.

5.2. Ablations 

Table 3 summarizes the few-shot and audio-modality experiments with Gemma 4 12B-IT. We observe that few-shot prompting does not provide consistent gains, with performance decreases in all set-tings except four-shot VET. Further, adding audio to diarized tran-script yields only a small improvement for VET prediction and de-creases performance on SES prediction, while audio-modality alone performs substantially worse than diarized transcripts for both items. Overall, Gemma 4 12B-IT does not show consistent benefit from ei-ther few-shot demonstrations or adding audio as input.

6. CONCLUSION 

We evaluated whether LLMs can score two speech-related BOSCC items by answering their linked decision questions. Qwen and Gemma models captured meaningful zero-shot signal but remained insufficient for standalone use. Diarized transcripts provided the most consistent gains, while demonstrations and audio yielded no consistent benefit across items. Among the well-supported codes, performance showed no consistent pattern by diagnosis group. In-spection of LLM evidence revealed inconsistent criterion application and sensitivity to ambiguous speech evidence. These findings are limited to two items, one in-house dataset, and single-coder annota-tions. Future work should explore models with improved acoustic integration and improved ASR, extending evaluation to additional BOSCC items and datasets. 7. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS
