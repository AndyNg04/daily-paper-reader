ABSTRACT 

Multiplayer social dilemmas on hypergraphs show a variety of emergent phenomena, which has been attributed to group interactions. Here we test whether pairwise dynamics can reproduce them as well. We derive the mesoscopic Langevin equation of a testbed model and find the operator content of absorbing phase transitions: a directed-percolation line, a tricritical point, and a first-order region whose spinodal is the explosive transition. In our analysis, three-player games only set the values of the Landau coefficients. We construct a strictly pairwise model with short-lived memory, in which every event involves at most two entities, that reproduces these coefficients term by term and has exactly the same mean-field fixed points. Simulations confirm that the two dynamics share mean-field critical and tricritical scaling and the size of the critical mass, and that a purely three-player game on a two-dimensional triangular lattice lies in the directed-percolation class. For these games, the explosive transition and the critical mass therefore do not require group interactions, and the critical behavior is that of the pairwise theory. 

> Keywords: higher-order interactions, evolutionary game theory, social dilemmas, absorbing phase transitions, directed percolation

1 INTRODUCTION 

How cooperation survives among self-interested agents is a long-standing problem in evolutionary game theory [1, 2, 3]. Its statistical-physics formulation places players on the nodes of a network and lets strategies spread by imitation. Spatial structure then changes the fate of cooperators [4, 5, 6]. In the last few years, hypergraphs and simplicial complexes have been used to describe interactions among more than two units [7, 8]. Multiplayer games [9, 10] have accordingly been placed on higher-order structures [11]. In particular, Civilini et al. [12] showed that, above a critical fraction of three-player games, the Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD) undergoes an explosive transition to a bistable state. In that state a cooperative phase coexists with full defection, and a critical mass of initial cooperators is required to reach it. This was interpreted as a novel explanation, rooted in higher-order interactions, for the survival of cooperation [12]. Here we ask a different question: do higher-order games define new collective behavior at large scales? Universality suggests caution. Very different microscopic rules often lead to the same collective behavior, because coarse-graining erases most of what distinguishes them [13, 14, 15]. Whether group interactions

nevertheless change this picture has recently been examined for several canonical models: Ising spins, contagion, voter dynamics, and phase oscillators on hypergraphs [16, 17, 18, 19]. In each case the group terms modify thresholds, bifurcations, and mechanisms, but not the large-scale behavior, unless they bring in a new symmetry or conservation law [20]. For instance, simplicial contagion maps onto the Langevin theory of the quadratic contact process, where simplicial terms merely renormalize pairwise coefficients [17]. Here we show that the same holds for evolutionary games. First, the mesoscopic description of higher-order social dilemmas has exactly the operator content of standard absorbing-state field theories. Second, its phase diagram consists of a directed-percolation (DP) line, a tricritical DP (TDP) point, and a first-order region, with the explosive transition as the spinodal of the latter. Third, a strictly pairwise microscopic dynamics with finite-lived memory produces the same field theory. Fourth, simulations of higher-order and pairwise dynamics give the same critical behavior: the pairwise network in mean field, and the ordinary pairwise game in two dimensions.

3 A PAIRWISE REALIZATION WITH MEMORY 

The analysis above suggests that three-player games enter the collective dynamics only through the values of g2 and g3. If so, any microscopic dynamics that generates the same coefficients should display the same phase diagram, even without group interactions. In a three-player game, the payoff of a player depends simultaneously on two co-players, but a similar dependence can arise from a sequence of pairwise encounters, provided players keep a short-lived memory of them. We make this explicit by constructing a microscopic dynamics in which every event involves at most two entities and that generates the same Landau coefficients as the higher-order game. 4 NUMERICAL TESTS 

We compare both models by simulating the higher-order game and its pairwise counterparts, first in well-mixed populations and on random hypergraphs, where mean-field exponents are exact, and then on a two-dimensional lattice. Details about the numerical simulations are given in the Supplementary Material. 

4.1 Mean field 

At S = 0 we consider well-mixed populations of N players, 10 ≤ N ≤ 5 × 10 5. For the game, the Fermi dynamics reduces to a birth-death process for the number of cooperators, and its quasistationary (QS) state [40, 41] is computed exactly from the principal eigenvector of the generator, without sampling. The pairwise reactions of Eq. (7) are simulated in a well-mixed population (equivalently, an annealed homogeneous network) with mass-action rates, using the Gillespie algorithm [42] and the QS reservoir method [41]. Figures 2A,B show that the pairwise game and the higher-order game with δ = 0 .05 scale as 

ρQS ∼ N −1/2 and τ ∼ N 1/2, the mean-field DP behavior. At the tricritical point both dynamics cross over to ρQS ∼ N −1/3 and τ ∼ N 2/3, as expected for mean-field TDP [33]. Both laws follow from balancing a drift −gρ m with a demographic noise of variance ρ/N , which gives ρQS ∼ N −1/m and τ ∼ N (m−1) /m ,with m = 2 on the DP line and m = 3 at the tricritical point [40, 33]. The pairwise case reproduces not only the exponents but also the amplitudes of the game, with no free parameter, since its rates are fixed term by term by the Landau coefficients of Eq. (4). Figures 2C,D turn to the first-order region at S = −0.1. Here we simulate the higher-order game agent by agent on random hypergraphs built as in [12], with 2- and 3-hyperedges placed uniformly at random, 

⟨k⟩ = 20 , and a new hypergraph for each run. The pairwise reactions are simulated, as above, in a well-mixed population of the same size. The active peaks of the QS distributions of both dynamics follow the mean-field branch ρ+(δ). The onset of the active state is explosive, with ρ+ = 1 /2 at the spinodal and 

ρ+ ≃ 0.70 at δ = 0 .4. The explosive onset is therefore reproduced without any three-body event. The same holds for the critical mass. Starting from a random configuration with a fraction ρ0 of cooperators, the probability Pact of reaching the active state jumps from 0 to 1 at ρ1/2 ≃ ρ−(δ), the unstable branch of Figure 2C. The width of the jump scales as N −1/2, as expected from demographic noise at a saddle point, and the curves of the hypergraph game and of the pairwise network collapse onto one another and onto the parameter-free mean-field prediction (Figure 2D). Unlike the exponents, the critical mass is a nonuniversal quantity, set by the location of the saddle ρ− and thus by the bare coefficients. Its agreement between the two dynamics is therefore a direct test of the ad-hoc mapping of Eq. (4), rather than a consequence of universality.

5 DISCUSSION 

Higher-order social dilemmas enrich the set of microscopic mechanisms that produce facilitation. Indeed, their mesoscopic dynamics is the Langevin theory of absorbing phase transitions with one field and one absorbing state. Its phase diagram is the textbook one: a DP line, a TDP point, and a first-order region. Microscopic arity only enters through the bare coefficients of Eq. (4), i.e., through nonuniversal quantities such as thresholds, spinodals, and the size of the critical mass. All of this is consistent with a general principle: arity is not a universality label [20]. Known exceptions point in the same direction. In the pair-contact process and in parity-conserving branching walks, the order of the reaction matters because it creates additional absorbing states or a conservation law [14, 15]. Likewise, memory with an infinite lifetime, such as permanent immunization, is an additional slow field and changes the class, from DP to dynamic percolation [46, 32]. The pairwise equivalence above relies on memory that decays at a finite rate. The same pattern has emerged for other canonical models placed on hypergraphs [20]. In the Ising model, three-spin terms that reward aligned hyperedges are exact sums of pairwise bonds, and irreducible four-spin terms shift the critical point without changing the mean-field exponents [16]. In simplicial contagion, 2-simplices add a quadratic term to the Langevin equation of the contact process [47, 17], a term that pairwise facilitation, thresholds, or memory also produce [37, 29]. In opinion dynamics, linear group rules reduce to pairwise dynamics on weighted networks [39, 18], whereas nonlinear rules, such as those of the q-voter model, change the collective behavior because they break the conservation of the average opinion [39, 48, 31, 49]. In synchronization, triadic phase interactions among globally coupled oscillators act as a second-harmonic force, and the two-cluster states and explosive transitions they produce also occur in pairwise models [19, 50, 51, 52]. Higher-order social dilemmas are one more instance of this pattern. Two consequences follow. First, bistability, a finite critical mass, or an explosive onset of cooperation are not signatures of irreducible group interactions. They identify a nonlinear facilitation mechanism [34], but not its microscopic origin, since a sequence of pairwise encounters with memory produces the same field theory [Eq. (7)] [53, 54]. Distinguishing the two requires microscopic information, for instance interventions that separate simultaneous exposure from accumulated memory, or subleading features of the fluctuations. Second, a higher-order game defines genuinely new collective behavior only if its group terms generate a feature that survives coarse graining, such as a new symmetry, a conservation law, an additional slow field, or a long-range coupling. Otherwise, as here, it remains a legitimate and often convenient microscopic choice, and the physics at large scales is fixed by the pairwise field theory.  

> 9Villegas and Meloni Higher-order social dilemmas

These results contribute to an ongoing discussion on when higher-order representations are needed [20, 55, 56, 57], and on how the mechanism of a system should be distinguished from its collective behavior. In that language, the present results concern behavior: higher-order and pairwise social dilemmas differ in mechanism, yet share the same large-scale physics.

4.2 Triangular lattice 

Players occupy an L × L triangular lattice with periodic boundaries. In the pairwise game every edge hosts a 2-game; in the higher-order game every elementary triangle hosts a 3-game ( G = 1 /4, W = 1 /2). Players imitate nearest neighbors with the Fermi rule applied to total payoffs ( T = 1 .1, wtot = 1 ), with continuous-time random-sequential updates. Spreading runs start from a single cooperator [6] on lattices with L = 1024 , 2048 , and 4096 , up to t = 10 4, 3 × 10 4, and 10 5, respectively, so that no cluster reaches the boundary ( ∼ 10 7 runs per value of S). The critical point is the value of S at which the late-time curvature of ln P and ln N versus ln t vanishes, with ln P and ln N interpolated in S between five simulated values; it is the same for the three sizes. Exponents are fitted over the last two decades at Sc, and their uncertainties are the changes for Sc ± 10 −5. At L = 4096 the hyperscaling combination θ + 2 δs holds for both cases.
