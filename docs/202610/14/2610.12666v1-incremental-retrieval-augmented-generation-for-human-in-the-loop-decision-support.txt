Abstract 

We introduce iRAG, a human-in-the-loop decision-support system combining Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) with Hybrid Decision-Making. Unlike conventional RAG sys-tems, relying on static Knowledge Bases (KBs), our proposal addresses settings where the KB is initially empty and is progressively populated through human decisions. The system stores previ-ous decisions as retrievable precedents and moves from silent observation to evidence-grounded contestation and, finally, to autonomous handling with abstention. Against an independent baseline, iRAG matches final-decision error under 10% drift without retrieval decay and reduces it under 40% drift across all configurations. Retrieval decay improves performance on changed knowledge while increasing error on stable knowledge, evidencing a stability–plasticity trade-off rather than a general advantage. Human oversight is an architectural provision of the workflow; its usability with real human users remains to be evaluated. 

Keywords: Large Language Models, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Hybrid Decision-Making, Skeptical Learning, Learning-to-Defer, Concept Drift 

# 1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on large text corpora to generate contextually appro-priate natural language. When embedded in a larger software system to invoke tools, maintain a state, and/or act on behalf of a user, an LLM constitutes the core of an agent [26]. The behaviour of an LLM-based agent depends on its training data and, at inference time, on the context it receives: the user’s input, any externally retrieved information, and a system prompt defining the agent’s role and constraints [28]. Therefore, a frozen LLM can be adapted to different tasks without retraining by defining its context appropriately. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) exploits this property systematically. Rather than relying on knowledge encoded during training, an LLM agent retrieves relevant passages from an external Knowledge Base (KB) at query time, and includes them in its context to produce a suitable response [5], grounding LLM outputs in verifiable, domain-specific evidence. However, standard RAG formulations assume a static KB, compiled offline before deployment [10], but the knowledge could be built incrementally through the decisions, progressively populating an initially empty KB through human deliberations. 1arXiv:2610.12666v1 [cs.HC] 8 Oct 2026 The coordination of human and machine decisions over time has been extensively studied in the Machine Learning literature under the umbrella of Hybrid Decision-Making (HDM) [20], with two main paradigms: either the machine is the primary decision-maker, deferring to the human when necessary (like Learning-to-Defer, LtD ) [8], or the human makes decisions, and they are as-sisted by a model which has learned from their past behaviour (like Skeptical Learning , SL) [29,30]. 

Bridget [19] unifies the two paradigms by moving between Human-in-Command and Machine-in-Command phases. The transitions are governed by Fading Empirical Accuracy (FEA), a temporally weighted variant of the Empirical Accuracy from SL, i.e., the agreement between the human an-notator and the machine. By discounting older decisions, FEA provides an estimate of the model reliability. The original paper only proposes this idea; to the best of our knowledge, neither FEA nor the phase transition has been tested empirically. Moreover, HDM systems have dealt with 

concept drift , which can also affect a RAG system built around an incremental, dynamic KB. When decision criteria evolve, older documents may misrepresent the current criteria, silently degrading the quality of RAG-grounded generations. A static formulation of RAG is therefore ill-suited to domains where the KB must be built incrementally, and where the currency of retrieved evidence determines decision quality. In this work, we propose iRAG, an LLM-based system bridging RAG and HDM to support human experts when the KB must be built from their own deliberations. The system is named after both its incremental nature, and the close relationship it has with its user(s). Our proposal rests on two main ideas. First, the KB is not a pre-compiled corpus, but an incremental repository of previous human decisions , structured to retrieve semantically relevant precedents for similar cases. Second, the relationship between the system and the user evolves over time . At first, unless iRAG abstains, its decisions are compared with the human ones to update the system’s FEA. Once the FEA exceeds a threshold, the system moves from a silent observer to an active interlocutor , presenting disagreements to the user (who retains the final decision) with evidence-grounded explanations retrieved from the KB. If FEA further improves, iRAG can move from a SL-based paradigm, in which it challenges the human, to a LtD-inspired one, in which it takes autonomous decisions and consults the human only when uncertain. Effectively, iRAG empirically tests the concept proposed by [19]. The contributions of this paper are as follows: • We formalise incremental deliberative RAG , i.e., RAG over a KB that grows as a direct con-sequence of the decisions supported by the system. • We define the system behaviour, from silent observation to SL-based contestation and, finally, to LtD-inspired autonomy. • We evaluate iRAG against an independent Controller-free RAG-with-defer rollout. Our claims are deliberately system-level: we study iRAG under controlled decision-maker and acceptance policies without real human involvement, following previous controlled evaluations with simulated decision-makers or oracle-mediated in the SL [3, 17, 29] and LtD [18, 21, 23] literatures. The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 surveys related work across RAG and HDM systems. Section 3 formalises the problem setting and the proposed architecture. Section 4 presents the experimental evaluation. Section 5 concludes with a discussion of limitations and future directions. 22 Related Work 

In this section, we review the literature on Hybrid Decision-Making (HDM) and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). Related topics are also discussed. 

HDM Systems. Hybrid Decision-Making refers to systems where final decisions result, en-tirely or in part, from the collaboration between a human decision-maker and a Machine Learning model [25]. HDM is especially relevant in high-stakes domains where full automation may be neither safe nor desirable. The literature identifies two broad families, corresponding to Learning-to-Abstain 

and Learning Together : machine-driven HDM, where the model makes most decisions and defers to the human under specific conditions, and human-driven HDM, where the human retains the primary role and the machine proposes corrections or improvements [20]. The representative machine-driven paradigm is Learning-to-Defer (LtD) [14,16,18], an extension of Learning-to-Reject [4, 31]. Instead of simply abstaining when uncertain, the model routes am-biguous instances to a human supervisor. A limitation of machine-driven HDM is that the human is consulted at the machine’s discretion and consequently plays a largely passive role. [2] addresses this by providing natural-language suggestions from an integrated LLM to guide the human towards a predicted answer while preserving their final say. Skeptical Learning (SL) belongs to the human-driven paradigm [3, 17, 22, 29, 30]. The human is the primary decision-maker, while the model learns incrementally from their decisions and acts as a surrogate . When its prediction differs from the human decision, it computes a skeptical score ,combining epistemic uncertainty and prediction confidence. If it exceeds a threshold, the model presents its prediction as an alternative that the human can accept or reject; the surrogate is then updated using the final human decision. In this way, it can identify inconsistent or erroneous decisions and help the human remain consistent over time. All SL variants are affected by early-decision bias : the surrogate is influenced by early decisions and assumes that the user acts in good faith. SL implementations differ in how the surrogate itself is updated. [29] retrains the model from scratch after each interaction, whereas [3, 17] employ Incremental Learning (IL) [24] which updates the model with new samples rather than retraining it. Systems that learn incrementally deal with 

concept drift : a change in the underlying data distribution over time [12,15]. As such, while exposed to new data, IL models need to maintain a good stability-plasticity trade-off , where stability refers to the ability to retain previous knowledge while learning something new, and the plasticity to modify existing information in light of new data [9, 13]. 

RAG Architecture. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) was introduced by [10] as a gen-eral architecture for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks. Instead of relying only on knowledge encoded in the parameters of a LLM model, a RAG system employs embedding similarity to retrieve passages from an external KB at inference time and includes them in the generation context. This allows the KB to be updated without retraining the model and grounds its outputs in verifiable evidence [5]. The original formulation treats the KB as static: the document index is built offline and remains fixed during training and inference [10]. Subsequent work has attempted to improve the retrieval mechanism, e.g., with reranking techniques re-ordering retrieved documents, thus improving the relevance of the passages passed to the main LLM [1, 7, 27]. For example, RePAQ [11] retrieves from a large QA-pair memory by matching the embedded input question against stored question embeddings and returning the answer associated with the nearest question. TG-RAG [6] deals with 

updating the KB, by introducing temporal graph structures to handle time-sensitive knowledge, enabling incremental updates as new documents arrive. These approaches share the assumption that the KB grows by ingesting external document streams, such as updated domain corpora. iRAG departs from this assumption: its KB grows from 

human deliberations , represented as structured records of decisions made over time. 3Figure 1: iRAG’s three states. Transition between them is governed by Fading Empirical Accuracy .

3.1 Comparison with HDM systems 

Algorithm 1 summarises the complete interaction loop of iRAG. As mentioned, the system shares its principles with established HDM approaches, particularly Skeptical Learning, Learning-to-Defer, and the framework proposed in [19], but implements adaptation through an evolving retrieval con-text. Whereas HDM approaches based on retraining or Incremental Learning update the predictive model using newly observed decisions, iRAG keeps the parameters of its LLM fixed and appends each input and its final decision to an external KB. Retrieved records then inform subsequent decisions without additional model training. The use of natural-language decisions, rather than a limited set of decision labels defined a priori ,also changes how agreement and deferral are implemented. iRAG employs an auxiliary model to assess whether a proposed answer covers the final human decision, making its reliability estimate dependent on the quality of this semantic assessment. Abstention serves a distinct purpose, allowing the model to defer when the evidence retrieved from the KB is insufficient. These mechanisms preserve key HDM principles while introducing dependencies on retrieval quality and auxiliary-model judgements. The distinction between iRAG and HDM systems is summarised in Table 1. Both adapt sequen-tially to newly observed decisions, but they differ in what is updated and in how past observations remain available to the system. # 4 Evaluation 

iRAG was evaluated on a synthetic benchmark simulating customer-support tickets for a CRM-like product undergoing quarterly releases, with simulated human profiles. This evaluation does not substitute for a human-participant study, which we defer to future work. The experiment separates the effects of retrieval and FEA decay on adaptation, authority allocation, and final-decision accuracy. The code with instructions to replicate the experiments is available on: https: //github.com/FedericoMz/iRAG .

Dataset. We created a synthetic dataset representing four quarters Q1, . . . , Q 4 of product evolution. Each quarter contains 500 tickets across five topics ( Billing, Integrations, Permissions, Reporting , and Onboarding ) and tagged as easy, normal, or hard. A deterministic pipeline (available for audit in the project repo) generated the product documentation, the tickets, a gold answer consistent with the documentation of the corresponding quarter, and a perturbed answer for each 7human profile. The first quarter contains no drift and is shared by both dataset variants. In Q2–

Q4, either 50 (10%) or 200 (40%) tickets are near-duplicates of earlier tickets whose correct answer changed after a release. These tickets form the drift subset ; the others the stable subset . The two variants contain, respectively, 150 and 600 drift tickets, among the 2, 000 nominal tickets. 

Human Profiles. We simulate three decision-makers by perturbing the gold answers, following the methodology used in prior HDM evaluations [17, 18, 21]: the CEO , who is always correct; the 

Domain Expert (DE), reliable within one declared topic; and the Intern , reliable on easy tickets and systematically defaults to the previous quarter’s answer on drift tickets. Hard tickets and a randomly selected 50% of out-of-domain normal tickets are assigned to the CEO; in-domain easy and normal tickets are given to the DE; out-of-domain easy tickets are answered by the Intern; and the remaining out-of-domain normal tickets are assigned to the DE acting outside their expertise, set as Billing . The random assignment is resampled independently in each repetition. Following the original SL evaluation, where a related oracle-mediated resolution was used [29], in SC a suggestion is accepted if it covers the gold answer (as checked by an auxiliary LLM call). This is an experimental control policy, simulating cooperative users who accept a disputed proposal if judged as correct. 

Experimental Protocol. Quarters are processed in order to preserve drift, while tickets within each quarter are shuffled. Every run begins with a trusted bootstrap: all Q1 tickets are assigned to the CEO and iRAG is locked in SO while constructing the KB and updating FEA. Normal state transitions and informed-mixture assignment begin in Q2. The first 100 tickets of every subsequent quarter are also assigned to the CEO, and state transitions are suspended until this review window ends. If a quarter begins in DS, iRAG first returns to SC. This periodic review provides recent trusted decisions, potentially including drift tickets because the within-quarter order is random, and mitigates the early-decision bias of SL-based systems. We independently set λRAG and λFEA 

either to 0.99861 (D) or to 1 (ND), yielding four decay combinations at each drift level. The decaying value gives retrieval a half-life of approximately one quarter (500 KB insertions) and FEA a half-life of approximately 500 reliability observations. This factorial design separates retrieval plasticity from adaptation of the reliability estimate. We set θsem = 0 .7, K = 5 , (α, β, γ ) = (0 .70 , 0.55 , 0.80) , and 

N = 30 . Both M and MA use Amazon Nova 2 Lite through Amazon Bedrock with temperature zero, chosen for reproducibility of the decision policy; sensitivity to sampling temperature is left to future work 1. Questions were embedded with qwen3-embedding:4b through Ollama. Each configuration is repeated ten times using the same initial seed and independent within-quarter permutations. 

Evaluation Measures. The final-decision error is the proportion of final answers not covering the gold answer. The human-only baseline is the counterfactual error obtained by always retaining the initially assigned human answer. Our Controller-free RAG-with-defer baseline is instead exe-cuted as an independent end-to-end simulation. It follows the same bootstrap and review protocol: the CEO finalizes all Q1 tickets and the first 100 tickets of each later quarter. On every remaining ticket, M uses the same retrieval and abstention rules as iRAG; a non-abstaining answer is final, whereas an abstention defers to the informed-mixture-assigned human. The baseline constructs its own KB exclusively from its own final decisions, uses non-decaying retrieval ( λRAG = 1 ), and has no SO/SC/DS controller or FEA. We run it with the same ten seeds; consequently, one independent baseline is shared by all four iRAG configurations within each drift dataset. We report final-decision error separately for stable and drift tickets. The abstention rate is computed conditionally on the tickets processed in SO, SC, or DS. 4.1 Contribution of RAG Decay 

Figure 2 reports the FEA and cumulative-error trajectories for the complete factorial design. During the CEO-controlled Q1, iRAG’s final-decision and human-only error remain zero by construction while FEA accumulates evidence about model behaviour. The initially wide FEA bands narrow as observations accumulate. From Q2 onward, the configurations diverge under normal assignment and state transitions; the local changes around later quarter boundaries reflect the injection of recent CEO-reviewed decisions before transitions resume. The independently rolled-out Controller-free RAG-with-defer trajectory is repeated across the four panels for the corresponding drift dataset, rather than recomputed from each iRAG trajectory. Against the principal incremental-RAG benchmark ( λRAG = 1 ), retrieval decay produces the stability–plasticity trade-off. Holding FEA decay fixed, it lowers drift-ticket error by 7.87 –8.87 points under 10% drift (paired 95% CIs [−11 .10 , −4.63] and [−12 .16 , −5.57] ) and by 2.63 –3.78 points under 40% drift ( [−4.00 , −1.27] and [−5.29 , −2.28] ). All four drift-ticket improvements remain significant after Holm correction. Retrieval decay simultaneously raises stable-ticket error by 2.31 –2.62 and 

1.29 –1.85 points, respectively, with all four paired CIs above zero. The mechanism is consistent with the scoring rule: down-weighting older precedents reduces interference from superseded decisions, but also weakens still-valid evidence when no change has occurred. The aggregate result depends on how many tickets can benefit from this plasticity. The 10% dataset contains 150 drift and 1,850 stable tickets. Per repetition, retrieval decay therefore pre-vents approximately 12 –13 drift errors but introduces approximately 43 –49 stable errors, increasing aggregate error by 1.48 –1.84 points. The 40% dataset contains 600 drift and 1,400 stable tickets: approximately 16 –23 avoided drift errors are offset by 18 –26 additional stable errors, leaving only 

0.11 –0.16 points of aggregate difference, with both CIs including zero. Thus, the smaller percentage-point improvement on individual drift tickets at 40% drift has greater system-level relevance because 10 it applies to four times as many changed tickets. The incremental-RAG benchmark retains the lowest mean aggregate error, whereas retrieval decay consistently provides stronger resistance to concept drift. Retrieval decay is therefore not universally superior, but a controllable exchange of stability for plasticity whose utility grows with the prevalence and cost of change. 

4.2 Contribution of FEA Decay 

FEA decay has a different, primarily operational effect. It does not directly alter which precedents are retrieved; instead, it reduces the influence of older agreement observations on the reliability estimate controlling state transitions. Holding retrieval decay fixed, FEA decay increases the model-finalized proportion by 5.24 –6.89 points under 10% drift and 5.98 –8.38 points under 40% drift, with every paired CI above zero. Over 2,000 tickets, this corresponds to approximately 105 –138 additional model-finalized decisions per repetition under 10% drift and 120 –168 under 40% drift. FEA decay should therefore be interpreted primarily as a change in the timing and extent of delegation, rather than as a direct improvement to retrieval quality. The accuracy consequence depends on the environment. Under 10% drift, FEA decay changes final-decision error by only 0.24 –0.60 points and both CIs include zero. Under 40% drift, it increases error by 0.85 points in the incremental-RAG benchmark (95% CI [0 .21 , 1.49] ) and by 0.90 points with retrieval decay ( [0 .19 , 1.61] ), although only the former remains significant after within-contrast Holm correction. This is consistent with an authority-exposure effect: earlier or longer residence in DS makes a larger share of model errors final, even though the decay operation itself does not directly change the retrieved evidence. The final states make the threshold sensitivity visible. All 10% repetitions finish in DS. Under 40% drift, both retrieval-decay conditions finish in DS in all ten repetitions, compared with one of ten for FEA decay alone and none for the fully non-decaying benchmark. Yet the terminal mean FEA values occupy a narrow range around the predefined γ = 0 .80 . Small continuous differences can therefore produce large differences in the discrete DS/SC count, and the final state alone does not reveal how long a run spent under autonomous authority. We consequently treat final-state frequencies as threshold-dependent operational outcomes and rely on continuous FEA, error, and model-finalization rates as the principal evidence. This motivates future sensitivity analysis of α,

β, and γ rather than interpreting the observed state split as a threshold-independent property of the system. 

4.3 Overall Contribution of iRAG 

Controller-free RAG-with-defer supplies a fully independent comparison. Its non-decaying KB is updated from its own decisions, so the comparison includes the downstream effect of authority allocation on later retrieval rather than replaying iRAG’s realised trajectory. It obtains 5.50 ±0.62 %final-decision error under 10% drift and 14 .03 ±0.73 % under 40% drift. Because this baseline always uses λRAG = 1 , the same dataset-level value is compared with all four iRAG configurations; only the R:ND columns hold the retrieval-decay setting fixed. Under 10% drift, Controller-free RAG-with-defer is numerically best. The two R:ND iRAG settings obtain 5.52 –5.75 %, and their paired differences from the baseline are small ( 0.02 –0.25 

points) with both 95% CIs crossing zero. With retrieval decay, iRAG’s aggregate error increases to 6.99 –7.59 %: its drift-ticket error is lower than the baseline ( 25 .20 –26 .20 % versus 31 .67 %), but its stable-ticket error is higher ( 5.51 –6.08 % versus 3.38 %). Thus, at low drift, iRAG matches the simpler baseline without retrieval decay, while temporal plasticity incurs the stability cost identified above. 11 The comparison reverses under 40% drift. Every iRAG configuration scores 9.51 –10 .52 %, reduc-ing error by 3.52 –4.53 points relative to Controller-free RAG-with-defer; all paired 95% CIs have lower bounds of at least 2.92 points, and all ten matched seeds favour iRAG in every configura-tion. Here iRAG improves both stable-ticket error ( 2.65 –5.02 % versus 7.84 %) and drift-ticket error (22 .87 –27 .12 % versus 28 .47 %). It also allows the model to finalize only 26 .12 –35 .80 % of tickets, compared with 59 .98 % for the controller-free baseline, showing that the gain coincides with more selective autonomy rather than more model exposure. Taken together, the results bound iRAG’s contribution more precisely. The controller does not yield an unconditional advantage: under mild drift, an independent non-decaying RAG-with-defer policy is already competitive. Under stronger drift, however, iRAG’s state-dependent allocation of authority produces a substantial end-to-end improvement over both that baseline and the human-only policy. The factorial design then separates the remaining mechanisms: retrieval decay governs the stability–plasticity trade-off, while FEA decay governs the speed and extent of delegation. Per-run metrics, counts, statistical tests, plots, and seeds are provided in the project repository. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We have presented iRAG, an incremental deliberative RAG system in which the knowledge base is constructed directly from the decisions supported by the system. iRAG operationalises a progressive collaboration between humans and LLMs: it first observes human decisions, then contests unsup-ported disagreements, and finally assumes conditional autonomy. In doing so, it combines an au-ditable, continuously updated RAG memory with explicit transitions between human-in-command and machine-in-command operation. The experiments provide the first empirical evaluation of FEA-driven transitions. Against inde-pendently rolled-out Controller-free RAG-with-defer, iRAG is statistically indistinguishable in the mild-drift, non-decaying setting and clearly more accurate under stronger drift. This is achieved by granting the model less final authority, establishing a synergy between the system and the human user(s) (defined as a co-evolutionary relationship in [17]). The complete factorial design separates the two forms of temporal decay: retrieval decay produces the stability–plasticity trade-off, con-sistently improving changed knowledge while sacrificing some stable accuracy, whereas FEA decay chiefly increases the amount of delegated authority. Under stronger drift, the retrieval benefit almost entirely offsets its stability cost and enables every retrieval-decay repetition to return to autonomous operation. iRAG therefore provides a concrete, auditable mechanism for managing adaptation and authority in an incremental RAG system while retaining human oversight, model abstention, and periodic reassessment. By design, iRAG also inherits assumptions from the HDM systems it combines. From SL, it inherits reliance on human decisions, and therefore early-decision bias (the reason for the CEO boot-strap and periodic trusted review in our experiments), as well as an empirical accuracy measured through agreement rather than objective correctness. From LtD, the Deferring Surrogate state in-herits the risk of over-reliance on the machine and selective feedback during autonomous operation. From Bridget , it inherits user-specified, potentially arbitrary thresholds for state transitions, whose sensitivity is visible in the final-state results. These are explicit design assumptions inherited from the original paradigms and limit the conclusions that can be drawn from the present evaluation. Future work can mitigate them through learned or task-calibrated thresholds, broader and indepen-dent reliability checks, and adaptive human-review schedules. Moreover, as mentioned, M and MA

share the same underlying model family; although our audit (see fn. 1) found no evidence of blanket self-preference, a fully independent auxiliary judge would strengthen this validation further. Addi-12 tionally, our experiments assign a substantial share of tickets to the oracle decision-maker; future works should vary the quantity, to establish how iRAG behaves with a fallible bootstrap expert or narrower review windows. This study establishes the feasibility of the architecture under controlled conditions and provides a reproducible basis for its human-centred validation. As the next step, we are planning a workshop with human participants to evaluate the transition and contestation mechanisms in practice and to begin the participatory design of the iRAG interface. The workshop will examine how users understand system states, inspect retrieved evidence, respond to contested decisions, and take back or delegate control.
