Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate strong individual task performance, yet still struggle to collaborate with users over extended, multi-turn interactions, for example by attempting solutions before understanding the user’s needs or by failing to revise mistaken assumptions after receiving feedback. We propose Collaborative Theory of Mind ( CToM ), a framework that addresses these challenges by explicitly training LLMs to anticipate user responses and to use those predictions to improve their own behavior. 

CToM drafts a response, predicts how the user will respond, and uses this prediction to guide internal revision before presenting a response. During training, the user’s reply to each draft serves as the target for predicting the next turn, and we train the assistant to minimize the negative log-likelihood of that reply. We combine this prediction objective with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) to optimize both task performance and interaction efficiency. Across mathematical reasoning, coding, and writing tasks, CToM 

achieves higher collaborative task performance than all baselines; for example, on a collaborative mathematical reasoning task, it improves final accuracy from 74.1% to 79.6% compared with naive GRPO. Ablations show that predicting user responses also makes collaboration more efficient: under the same reward, adding prediction supervision reduces the number of user replies by 8% on coding and 17% on writing while improving task performance. Behavioral analyses of interaction trajectories show that user-response predictions become more accurate after training, and that more accurate predictions are associated with larger improvements in collaborative task performance through self-revision.

## 1 Introduction 

Today’s large language models (LLMs) demonstrate strong capabilities in mathematical reasoning, code generation, and writing, motivating their use as assistants for increasingly complex tasks. In practice, many of these tasks require multiple exchanges in which users and assistants clarify requirements, develop solutions, and respond to feedback. For example, a user drafting a project proposal may refine its scope, intended audience, and priorities while reviewing successive drafts. Strong performance on individual tasks, however, does not reliably translate into effective collabo-ration. Effective collaboration requires reasoning about the person one is interacting with. In human collaboration, people rely on theory of mind —understanding what others know, want, and intend— to anticipate how they will act and react [13]. This ability makes a measurable difference: groups whose members score higher on a theory-of-mind test perform better on a broad category of tasks, even when they communicate only through text [3]. A collaborator preparing a draft, for instance, can consider what the recipient may find unclear with their goals and address those issues before sharing it. This perspective motivates a concrete training principle for AI assistants: learning to anticipate how a user would respond can help an assistant identify and address shortcomings in its own response before sharing it with the user. Inspired by this, we propose Collaborative Theory of Mind (CToM) , which trains an assistant to predict the user’s next reply to its own response and to act on that prediction (Figure 1b). As shown in Figure 2, the assistant drafts a response, predicts the user’s feedback on it, and revises the draft in an internal loop until it expects the user to be satisfied or reaches its revision budget; only then does it show the response. During training, the user’s real reply to each draft supervises the prediction for that draft. We train the prediction with a supervised loss and the task behavior with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) [19]. CToM differs from prior work in two ways. First, prior work on theory of mind in LLMs primarily evaluates this ability through explicit reasoning about users’ beliefs and profiles [8, 16]; we train assistants to develop this ability by anticipating user responses, with the goal of improving collaboration. Second, prior methods use simulated future conversations to reward collaborative outcomes [22] or revise drafts using the model’s own critiques [12]. In CToM , we explicitly train the assistant to predict how the user would respond and revise from that perspective, encouraging the assistant to better understand the user while learning to accomplish the task. We evaluate CToM on collaborative mathematical reasoning, coding, and writing tasks, training a Qwen3-8B assistant [23] with a Qwen3-32B user simulator. CToM improves the task score over the strongest baseline by 7.5%, 4.4%, and 6.5% on math, coding, and writing, respectively. The analyses yield three further findings. First, predicting user responses improves both task performance and efficiency. Ablating prediction supervision and the effort penalty separately, we 

2find that prediction supervision raises the task score in every comparison, and that CToM , which uses both, requires the fewest user input effort among these variants. Second, predictions of user replies become more accurate after training, and these improved predictions help the assistant revise responses more effectively, both within the internal revision loop and across conversation turns. Third, forcing models to ask more clarification questions does not automatically improve performance; encouraging the assistant to ask more questions lowers its task score in every domain. Our contributions are as follows: • We propose CToM , a training framework that makes predicting the user’s next reply an explicit learning objective and uses these predictions in an internal revision loop, so that the assistant corrects its responses before the user sees them. • We evaluate CToM on mathematical reasoning, coding, and writing, where it outperforms all baselines in task performance. Through ablations, we show that predicting user responses also reduces the effort users spend, measured by the number and length of their replies. • We show that our training objective teaches the assistant to anticipate user replies and use these predictions to revise responses more effectively. The objective also yields interesting interaction strategies. For example, the learned policy outperforms one explicitly encouraged to ask more clarifying questions, despite having no direct training signal for that. 

## 2 Related Work 

Interactive Evaluation Human interaction is seldom one-shot. Underspecification is a natural aspect of human conversation [6]. Naturally, over the course of human-LLM interaction, user intent, goal, and task completion are revealed. Pre-LLM literature on dialogue systems argues that considering both task outcomes and the interaction is instrumental [21]. Moreover, task success does not necessarily translate to improved human-LLM interaction [11]. Recent work in multi-turn evaluation has highlighted that LLM performance degrades when user intents get clearer over multiple turns [9]. Their analysis shows that LLMs tend to make premature assumptions. Overreliance on these early assumptions is a key failure mode as the model fails to incorporate information from late interaction in a conversation episode. These works motivate that human-LLM interaction needs to be evaluated both in terms of interaction quality and final task performance [7]. 

Collaborative LLMs A growing body of research on collaborative language models investigates how effective LLMs are as collaborators and how they can be trained to be better collaborators. CoAuthor analyzes detailed traces of human-AI writing sessions and highlights that notions of good collaboration are grounded in a combination of ownership and productivity [10]. Davidson et al. [2] identify that models with strong individual performance can perform substantially worse when coordinating between multiple agents. Shao [18] proposes a collaboration layer where AI can serve as a proactive collaborator. Complementing the system design aspect, CollabLLM focuses on training models to be proactive collaborators. It uses simulated interactions and multiturn-aware rewards to train models to uncover user intent and make suggestions that improve subsequent outcomes [22]. Similarly Sun et al. [20] trains a model to be proactive and personalized beyond task support. In contrast to specific rewards for different aspects of collaboration, we propose a general purpose methodology that infers user intent and revises its output proactively. 

Theory of Mind LLMs have been shown to exhibit Theory of Mind , i.e., the models are able to infer users’ mental states without explicit verbalization. Specialized networks or agents can learn to infer agents’ characteristics and mental states from behavioral observations [14, 15]. On the other hand, Symmetric Machine Theory of Mind studies such modeling in environments where agents can both communicate and act [17]. FANToM extends evaluation to conversational contexts 

> 3

with unequal access to information, revealing difficulties in consistently tracking participants’ beliefs [8]. ToM-SWE maintains a model of user goals, constraints, and preferences to guide agents [24]. Ruan et al. [16] evaluate and train agents to detect and resolve discrepancies between users’ beliefs and the task environment. Furthermore, Gong et al. [4] show that ToM can show improved performance in static benchmarks, however, such improvement may not translate to dynamic environments. RL training on models in collaborative tasks may help with implicit theory of mind capabilities [17], which provides a formative ground for our method on collaborative theory of mind.

## 4 Method: Collaborative Theory of Mind (CTOM) 

In a collaboration, much of the cost of a flawed response falls on the user, who must read it, notice the problem, and spend a turn explaining it. Many such corrections are predictable from the conversation so far: a misread constraint, an overlooked requirement, a format the user already asked for. An assistant that anticipates the user’s reply to a candidate response can fix these problems before the user sees them, saving both a correction turn and the effort of reading a flawed answer. When the assistant lacks information only the user has, however, predicting the reply means guessing what it does not know, and revising on that guess would make it overconfident rather than correct; the right move is to ask. 

CToM builds both behaviors into a single policy (Figure 2) that chooses among three actions each round (Section 4.1). Ask sends a clarifying question. Draft runs a hidden loop that drafts, predicts the user’s reply, and revises until the prediction signals satisfaction, then shows the final draft. Commit submits the last shown draft. During training, each prediction is supervised by the simulated user’s reply to that draft: the actual reply for the shown draft and a counterfactual reply for each discarded one (Section 4.2). The policy is optimized with GRPO on a combined reward of task success and a penalty on user effort (Sections 4.3–4.5).

5.1.2 Baselines and Ablations 

We compare CToM with two baselines; all prompts are provided in Appendix C. Prompting Base evaluates the untrained Qwen3-8B with a minimal interactive prompt (Prompts 5–7): the assistant responds each round directly, with no planning, action selection, prediction, or revision. 

Naive GRPO fine-tunes the same model with GRPO using this minimal prompt and the reward in Eq. (4). Because it generates a single response per round, we raise its per-response cap so that its total generation budget per round equals CToM ’s combined caps for drafts, predictions, and revisions. We also ablate the two training signals of CToM . The ablations keep CToM ’s prompt (Prompts 1–4), which instructs the assistant to plan, choose an action, and draft, predict, and revise, as well as its revision cap and all other settings. CToM w/o effort sets weffort = 0 in Eq. (4), CToM w/o prediction sets λpred = 0 in Eq. (6), and CToM task-only removes both, training on the task reward alone.

Their low turn and token counts reflect this collapsed interaction rather than efficient collaboration, so we compare user effort only among CToM and its ablations, which share the draft–predict–revise loop. 

5.3 User Effort 

Visible assistant output does not show how much the user must contribute. Table 2 therefore counts the user’s replies after the opening request and their total length in tokens. A conversation that ends at the first turn requires no input from the user, so we only count conversations that need at least one round of user feedback. We use these conversations to measure the user’s input effort. 

CToM requires the least user input on coding and writing. Compared with CToM task-only, it needs 22% fewer replies on coding and 19% fewer on writing, and 17% fewer user tokens in both, the lowest of the four variants. On math, the three variants that use at least one signal are close (2.23–2.28 replies, 85.7–92.8 tokens), and all need less input than CToM task-only. 

Predicting user responses improves efficiency. Under the same reward, adding prediction supervi-sion ( CToM w/o prediction to CToM ) reduces user replies by 8% on coding and 17% on writing and user tokens by 7% and 18%, while also raising the task score. This shows that accurate user prediction can reduce the user’s input effort by finding and fixing potential problems before the user raises them (Figure 3b and c). 

5.4 Behavioral Analysis 

Does CToM learn to predict user responses? We measure how closely each prediction matches the user’s actual reply, using word-level F1 between the predicted and observed replies, for the 

CToM and CToM w/o prediction models in Table 1. Every trained CToM checkpoint exceeds the untrained model in all three domains (Figure 3a). CToM w/o prediction, trained with the same reward but without prediction supervision, improves much less or not at all: its final F1 is 14.0, 12.8, and 15.8 on math, coding, and writing, compared with 19.7, 43.8, and 35.8 for 

CToM . Learning to predict the user thus comes from the prediction objective rather than from reinforcement learning alone. F1 levels differ across domains and are not directly comparable, since the replies differ in form. Math feedback typically addresses errors in a specific multi-step solution, which is likely harder to anticipate than feedback on coding and writing drafts. Do user predictions help internal revision? We measure self-revision gain as the mean score change from the first to the shown draft in revised rounds, with no user feedback in between. Figure 3b compares math runs trained by CToM vs. CToM w/o prediction, at revision caps of one and three. With the prediction loss, the gain is larger at both caps, and both variants gain more with the higher cap. Revision thus improves answers before the user sees them, and the improvement is larger when the assistant is trained to predict feedback. 

How does CToM collaborate on coding? We examine how each method recovers from a first program that fails at least one unit test. Since a question produces no code, we align conversations at the first turn that contains a program rather than at turn 1 (Figure 3c). Prompting Base and Naive GRPO never revise: their conversations end after the first program, so their scores stay at 20.9 and 30.0. CToM and the two ablations in Figure 3c keep working after the failure, and CToM recovers faster and further: after one more turn it reaches 38.4, compared with 33.8 for CToM w/o prediction, and it ends at 46.2 compared with 40.5. The methods that use the draft–predict–revise prompt thus repair a failing program with the user’s feedback, whereas the two baselines never change it. The larger recovery of CToM than CToM w/o prediction suggests that learning to predict the user helps not only its internal revisions (Figure 3b) but also its revisions across turns. 

How does CToM use actions? Figure 4(a) shows how often CToM chooses each action. Without regularization, Ask almost disappears after training on math (0.0%) and coding (2.9%) but remains common on writing (32.7%). The tasks explain the difference. A math problem is fully stated, and a coding request fixes the function signature, leaving only a few behaviors to infer. A writing request, by contrast, leaves most of the target document unspecified, and feedback on a long draft reveals the missing requirements more slowly than a direct question. To test whether the policy has simply stopped exploring, we add an entropy bonus on the action-selection token, the single character that encodes Ask , Draft , or Commit (Prompt 4). This keeps Ask frequent in all three domains, but the regularized runs reach a lower task score at their selected checkpoints (Figure 4(b)). A higher Ask rate thus does not by itself improve collaboration; the unregularized policy’s action choices already suit each domain.

Across math, coding, and writing tasks, our findings support a central principle: learning to anticipate user feedback helps assistants revise more effectively, reduces the input users must provide, and leads to useful interaction strategies. The most important next step is a study with human users. Our experiments use a simulated user, so it remains to be tested whether an assistant can accurately predict how real users will respond, whether CToM keeps its task performance with them, and whether people prefer working with it in real-world settings. More broadly, theory of mind can take many forms, and predicting the user’s next reply is only one of them. CToM mainly serves task completion: it helps the assistant catch problems in a draft before the user does. Other forms of user modeling, for example, modeling users’ personalities and preferences, could improve human-AI collaboration by making the assistant feel more friendly, personalized and attentive to the user. Given a model of the user, how the assistant should draw on it also needs research. CToM 

explicitly revises a draft whenever its predicted user reply signals dissatisfaction, but this may not be the best way to use the prediction for every task. CToM also applies its user model only to the Draft action, while decisions such as whether to ask, what to ask, and when to commit could benefit from it as well. How to incorporate theory of mind into every decision the assistant makes remains an open question for future work.

A conversation’s score is the mean F1 over its pairs, and the reported value is the mean over eligible conversations (Eq. (10)), multiplied by 100. Table 7 lists the values plotted in Figure 3. The two pairing conventions produce different denominators, so levels are comparable within a run but not across domains. C.1 Assistant Prompts for CTOM and Its Ablations 

CToM and its three ablations in Table 1 use the same system prompt within each domain (Section 4.1). Each turn opens with a plan and an action decision. During rollouts, the user simulator never sees plans, internal drafts, or predicted replies; it receives only the shown draft, the question after Ask , or, after Commit , the last shown draft (preceded by I WANT TO ANSWER: on coding and writing).

Prompt
