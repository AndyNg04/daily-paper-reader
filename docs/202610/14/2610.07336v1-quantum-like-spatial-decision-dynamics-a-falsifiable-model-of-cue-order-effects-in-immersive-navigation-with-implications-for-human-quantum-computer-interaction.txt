Abstract 

The sequence in which a person encounters spatial evidence can change a later choice, yet an order effect alone does not identify a quantum-like cognitive structure. We introduce Quantum-Like Spatial Decision Dynamics (QSDD), a falsifiable state-space account of embodied decision making in which cue exposures are completely positive trace-preserving maps, intermediate judgments are quantum instruments, and route commitment is an operationally defined mea-surement. We distinguish this formal claim from any assertion that cognition is microscopically quantum. We then specify a minimal binary model with a fixed decision basis, noncommuting cue rotations, a fixed symmetry-breaking initial azimuth, dephasing, and lapse; its five fitted parameters must generalize across environments rather than being refit to individual conditions. A reproducible simulation study evaluates recovery and model discrimination under both QSDD and seven-parameter classical logistic ground truths. Across 40 replicates per design cell, QSDD was preferred on held-out environments in 85% of QSDD-generated datasets at 240 indepen-dent observations per environment-order cell and 95% at 480, but in 0% of classically generated datasets at every tested sample size. The simulation also exposes weak identification of the lapse parameter and is explicitly a design analysis, not human evidence. We provide a preregistra-ble virtual-reality experiment, telemetry schema, classical comparison set, and failure criteria. Finally, we show how the same process-measurement logic can be transferred to human inspec-tion and debugging of quantum circuits. QSDD is therefore offered as a constrained model to be defeated or supported by data, and as a methodologically continuous route from immersive interaction research to human–quantum computer interaction. 

Keywords: quantum cognition; spatial decision making; virtual reality; cue-order effects; model comparison; behavioral telemetry; human–quantum computer interaction

# 1 Introduction 

Spatial decisions are rarely made from a neutral vantage point. A navigator turns the head, ap-proaches a landmark, opens a map, hears a directional cue, hesitates, backtracks, and eventually crosses a boundary that expresses a choice. Each act changes what information is available and may change the state from which the next judgment is made. The order of observations is therefore not merely a record of a pre-existing decision. It can be constitutive of the decision. Immersive environments offer unusual experimental leverage over this process. They preserve con-tinuous, embodied interaction while allowing precise control of the scene, cue timing, viewpoint, locomotion, and consequences. They also permit dense measurement of gaze, head and hand mo-tion, path geometry, pauses, selections, and revisits. Virtual reality (VR) has accordingly become an important instrument for spatial-cognition research, although transfer from virtual to physical navigation must be evaluated rather than presumed [9, 11, 29]. Most computational accounts of spatial choice remain classical. Bayesian models represent belief revision, Markov and partially observable Markov decision processes represent state transition and action, and sequential-sampling models represent noisy evidence accumulation toward a boundary. These are powerful baselines. Yet some human judgments display context dependence, question-order effects, and departures from simple total-probability decompositions for which quantum prob-ability has provided compact formal accounts [8, 18–20, 26, 31]. Quantum cognition employs the mathematics of quantum theory as a probability calculus for cognition; it does not, by itself, claim that cognitive activity is implemented by a physically quantum brain [7, 15, 30]. An order effect, however, is not uniquely quantum. A classical model with memory, recency, nonlin-ear state transition, or condition-specific parameters can also produce sequence sensitivity. The sci-entific burden is therefore stronger: QSDD must impose a reusable geometry, make cross-condition predictions with frozen operators, survive parameter-recovery tests, and outperform strong classical alternatives on held-out participants and environments. Merely fitting an interference term after observing the data is not evidence. This paper addresses that burden in three steps. First, it develops a consistent operator account in which cue exposures are channels and registered judgments are instruments. Second, it reduces that account to a five-parameter binary model that can be estimated and falsified. Third, it reports a two-ground-truth simulation showing when model comparison can and cannot distinguish that model from a more flexible classical alternative. The resulting protocol is then carried into an immersive navigation experiment. The work also has a domain-specific transfer target. Quantum computing increasingly requires interfaces through which learners and practitioners compose, inspect, compare, and debug quantum programs [2, 3, 16]. QTerra treats a variational quantum circuit as an immersive work object and records how users inspect, revisit, manipulate, and reason through its representations [5]. QSDD can therefore be tested first in controlled spatial decisions and later in circuit-inspection tasks without claiming a new quantum algorithm or a microscopic theory of mind. The paper makes five contributions: 1. It separates physical measurement, quantum-like cognitive modeling, and interactional observa-tion. 2. # 2 Observation and Collapse: An Epistemic Boundary 

2.1 Physical measurement is not a settled theory of consciousness 

In textbook quantum mechanics, a state may be represented by a vector or density operator, unitary evolution preserves superposition, and measurement supplies probabilities for outcomes. Interpre-tations disagree about what the formalism says exists and whether an apparent collapse is physical, epistemic, relational, emergent through decoherence, or unnecessary. Historical proposals connect-ing mind and measurement make consciousness-centered collapse a legitimate topic in the philosophy of physics, but not an established empirical premise for cognitive science [23, 25, 28]. Nothing in the framework below depends on a conscious observer physically selecting one branch of a microscopic wave function. The connection is structural rather than ontological: a context-sensitive state is transformed by an intervention, and a registered outcome changes the state relevant to subsequent predictions. 

2.2 Three claims that must not be conflated 

This separation is more than rhetorical caution. If a quantum-like model succeeds, it supports a probabilistic structure for the task; it does not establish neural quantum coherence. If it fails, that failure bears on the model and task, not on quantum mechanics. The proposal is therefore compat-ible with multiple physical interpretations and with ordinary neurocomputational implementation. 

2.3 Collapse as an operational term 

We use decision resolution for the event at which a task records one alternative: crossing into a corridor, selecting a waypoint, grasping an object, or confirming a route. “Collapse” may be used as shorthand for the corresponding conditional state update, but it carries no claim of instantaneous, irreversible, or conscious physical collapse. Human commitment can be graded, delayed, revised, or reopened. # 3 Background 

3.1 Spatial decision making is embodied and context dependent 

Spatial navigation integrates egocentric and allocentric information, landmarks, remembered routes, goals, and self-motion cues. These sources need not be simultaneously available or mutually consis-tent. Immersive experiments show that more than one spatial reference frame can support working memory after self-movement, with gaze providing a behavioral signature of which representation is recruited [9]. Multisensory input can also change navigation strategies and performance rather than simply add redundant evidence [13]. Such findings motivate a state representation in which cue sequence and viewpoint may change the operative decision basis. VR provides both ecological richness and experimental control [12], but it is not transparent. Lo-comotion technique, field of view, display latency, embodiment, simulator familiarity, and missing vestibular or proprioceptive cues can alter behavior. Recent evidence that neural representations can differ between virtual and physical navigation reinforces the need to treat immersion as a modeled context rather than a neutral delivery channel [29]. 

3.2 Immersive interaction produces process evidence 

Immersive analytics treats movement, spatial arrangement, manipulation, and viewpoint as compo-nents of sensemaking rather than interface overhead alone [10, 17]. Work on embodied provenance shows that interaction histories can help reconstruct how an analysis unfolded [32]. Gaze and head motion can predict navigational difficulty or the need for aid [1], but any single channel is ambigu-ous: gaze may indicate interest, confusion, salience, or motor preparation. QSDD therefore uses multimodal sequences and task events, not gaze alone, to constrain a latent-state model. QTerra applies this process orientation to quantum-circuit inspection by capturing gaze, movement, selection, parameter manipulation, cost-surface inspection, and revisitation [5]. QSDD inherits the same methodological commitment: final accuracy is insufficient to distinguish how a person arrived at a decision. 4 of 24 Quantum-Like Spatial Decision Dynamics Basu 

3.3 Quantum cognition as a probabilistic modeling program 

Classical probability places events in a common Boolean algebra and typically assumes a joint distribution for relevant variables. Quantum probability represents events with subspaces or positive operators on a Hilbert space. Incompatible questions need not share a single joint distribution, and sequential measurements may not commute. This structure has been used to model order effects, conjunction and disjunction errors, violations of the sure-thing principle, and dynamic judgment [8, 18, 19, 26, 30]. The strongest evidence for a quantum-like account is not that a post hoc interference term fits data. A flexible classical model can often do the same. Strong evidence requires an independently motivated state space, constrained operators, parameter recovery, novel prediction, and comparison with plausible classical models on held-out data [20]. The QQ equality is one example of a structural prediction derived from a quantum question-order model and tested across many surveys [26]. QSDD adopts this predictive standard. Context dependence must also be distinguished from contextuality in its stronger technical sense. A distribution can change across contexts because of direct influences without violating every non-contextual representation. Contextuality-by-Default provides one framework for separating those possibilities, and behavioral demonstrations require experimental systems designed for that analysis [4]. The present paper predicts context-dependent and potentially noncommuting updates; it does not claim to have demonstrated contextuality. A dedicated cyclic design would be required for that stronger claim. Quantum cognition also offers a computational path: cognitive models expressed in Hilbert space can be compiled into actual quantum circuits without implying that the biological brain uses qubits [27]. This makes it possible to move in stages from quantum-like behavioral modeling, to circuit representations of those models, and eventually to experiments on quantum simulators or hard-ware. The empirical HCI program and the computational quantum program can therefore converge without being conflated. 

3.4 Human–quantum computer interaction as a transfer domain 

Human–quantum computer interaction concerns the people, representations, tools, and practices through which quantum computation becomes usable [2]. Recent interface work has moved beyond agendas to concrete techniques for circuit composition, machine inspection, circuit comparison, optimization review, and result analysis [16]. Human-centered quantum software engineering simi-larly emphasizes practice, responsibility, and cooperative support [3]. These developments motivate QTerra and the later transfer study in this paper, but they do not validate QSDD. The empiri-cal connection must be earned by testing whether a model first constrained in navigation predicts behavior in quantum-circuit tasks.

6.2 What the simulation establishes—and what it does not 

Under the selected QSDD ground truth, the win rate was 0.75 at N = 40 , 0.85 at N = 240 , and 0.95 at N = 480 , where the 5th percentile of ∆ELPD was positive. Under the classical ground 11 of 24 Quantum-Like Spatial Decision Dynamics Basu truth, QSDD was never preferred in the 200 simulated datasets spanning all five sample sizes. The comparison procedure therefore did not manufacture a QSDD advantage merely because the quantum-like model was included. The corrected signed-cue geometry also shows that N = 240 is less decisive than the earlier symmetry-restricted parameterization suggested. Median normalized parameter RMSE under QSDD ground truth was 0.129 at N = 40 and 0.106 at N = 480 . At N = 480 , the medians for (θ0, ω L, ω M , κ ) were (1 .351 , 1.119 , 0.960 , 0.855) , close to their generating values. The lapse median was 0.035, equal to the generating value to the reported precision, but its 5th–95th percentile interval remained wide at [0 , 0.106] . This identifies a design weakness: confirmatory estimation should either constrain lapse using independent catch trials or omit it when such trials are unavailable. The result does not show that people follow QSDD, and N = 480 is not a universal sample-size prescription. The simulated effect sizes, lack of participant heterogeneity, balanced cells, fixed initial azimuth, and known cue-strength scale are optimistic relative to a real VR study. Pilot-based design simulation must therefore replace these illustrative values before recruitment. The value of the present result is narrower but important: the repaired model is executable, both cue signs affect prediction, its central parameters are recoverable under stated conditions, and the same analysis can reject it when the ground truth is classical. 

# 7 From Immersive Telemetry to Model Evidence 

Telemetry provides observations xt generated conditionally by a latent decision state and the task context: 

xt ∼ p(xt | ρt, c t, η ), (28) where η contains participant- and device-level parameters. The framework does not label a gaze fixation as a measurement merely because it was recorded. An event is assigned theoretical status by experimental design and model specification. A practical telemetry schema should synchronize: trial and condition identifiers; cue onset and offset; head, hand, and body poses; gaze ray and validity; scene-object intersections; map or panel events; movement speed and angular velocity; path coordinates; decision-boundary crossings; rever-sals; response time; confidence; and task outcome. Sampling rates should be recorded rather than assumed, and derived features should preserve a link to raw timestamps.

8.2 Task and apparatus 

Participants navigate a family of matched virtual environments containing repeated decision nodes. At each critical node, two cue families provide route-relevant evidence: • Landmark cue ( L): an egocentric, scene-embedded object or sign associated with a route. • Map cue ( M ): an allocentric overhead representation indicating layout or goal relation. The environment is designed so that cue reliability is learned in practice trials and manipulated in the experiment. Critical trials include congruent cues, conflicting cues, single-cue trials, and unresolved/no-query trials. The same geometry is mirrored and texture-varied to reduce memoriza-tion. A head-mounted display with eye tracking records gaze, head pose, hand pose, and locomotion. A matched desktop condition may be included as a secondary factor, but the core quantum-like test is within immersive VR. 8.9 Threats to inference 

Several apparent quantum-like signatures have mundane explanations. Unequal cue duration can mimic order effects; a confidence question can create the effect it measures; gaze-contingent rendering can couple measurement and stimulus; scene salience can masquerade as preference; repeated trials can induce reinforcement learning; and simulator discomfort can alter search behavior. The study should therefore use matched exposure, manipulation checks, neutral probe wording, calibration trials, counterbalancing, and hierarchical participant effects. A no-probe group is essential for estimating probe reactivity. 

# 9 Transfer to Human–Quantum Computer Interaction 

The navigation experiment isolates the cognitive model from quantum-domain knowledge and inter-face novelty. A separate transfer test can then ask whether the constrained process model generalizes to a genuinely quantum-computing task. QTerra provides one such testbed by treating a variational circuit as an immersive work object [5]. A user may inspect circuit structure, approach a gate, ma-nipulate a parameter, view a local cost-landscape slice, revisit an ansatz layer, and then decide where an error lies. The alternatives are no longer literal left and right turns but competing debugging hypotheses or next actions. For example, define C as circuit-structure inspection, S as cost-surface inspection, and D as an error-localization decision. QSDD predicts that 

P(D | C → S) may differ from P(D | S → C) (31) when the two representations induce incompatible judgment contexts. QTerra’s telemetry can test whether the order affects not only the final answer but also subsequent revisits, parameter edits, 16 of 24 Quantum-Like Spatial Decision Dynamics Basu and confidence. This extension closes a distinctive loop: humans reason about quantum circuits, while quantum-probability models are evaluated as accounts of how that reasoning unfolds. The transfer test should preserve the inferential discipline of the navigation study. Circuit and sur-face views must be matched for exposure and information; representation order must be randomized; quantum expertise must be measured; and one operator family must predict held-out circuits. The primary outcome is not whether immersion is engaging, but whether representation order changes error localization, subsequent revisits, parameter edits, and confidence in the constrained pattern predicted by QSDD. The transfer has three analytically separate layers: 1. Object layer: quantum circuits, parameters, measurements, and optimization landscapes be-come objects of human inspection and action. 2. Cognitive layer: QSDD models how representation order, observation, and interaction may transform the user’s decision state. 3. Infrastructure layer: telemetry, replay, provenance, and privacy-preserving computation make the reasoning process measurable and governable. Together, these layers define a testable contribution to human-centered quantum computing. They also identify where collaboration is required: quantum specialists validate task semantics, math-ematical psychologists audit identifiability, and HCI researchers evaluate the representation and workflow.

# 11 Privacy, Ethics, and Reproducibility 

Immersive telemetry can be identifying. Head and hand trajectories, gaze patterns, reaction times, and navigation strategies may function as behavioral signatures. Data minimization should begin at study design: collect only channels required by a hypothesis; separate identity from trial data; document sampling rates and transformations; restrict raw replay access; and state retention and sharing policies during consent. Where cross-institutional or cloud analysis is necessary, aggregate release, differential privacy, se-cure enclaves, or encrypted computation may reduce exposure. A quantum-resilient homomorphic architecture is a long-term systems direction for analytics over sensitive motion and gaze data, but its cost, leakage model, and utility must be evaluated rather than assumed [6]. Privacy technology does not replace informed consent or governance. Reproducibility requires more than releasing a Unity scene. A complete research object should include environment versions, cue randomization seeds, coordinate conventions, event schema, gaze-validity rules, preprocessing code, preregistration, model code, synthetic data, and a de-identified 18 of 24 Quantum-Like Spatial Decision Dynamics Basu feature-level benchmark where raw traces cannot be shared. Exact headset, runtime, locomotion method, frame rate, and eye-tracker calibration procedure should be reported.

# 12 Philosophical Interpretation 

QSDD makes a modest but consequential philosophical move. It rejects the picture in which an observer passively extracts a fixed preference from an unchanged internal state. In an immersive environment, observation is an action situated in a perception-action loop. The route a person approaches determines the next view; the map they open changes what relations become salient; the question asked may alter subsequent judgment; and commitment changes the space of available actions. This does not entail that reality is created by attention. It entails that the decision object is partly constructed through context-sensitive interaction. The quantum-like formalism is useful when the available judgments cannot be treated as simultaneous readings from one stable joint distribution without loss of predictive structure. The term “superposition” should likewise be read carefully. It does not mean that a person secretly chooses every route in parallel. It means that, relative to a specified measurement basis, the model represents a state that is not yet an eigenstate of the eventual decision. “Collapse” does not mean that consciousness changes a physical world. It denotes the conditional update associated with a registered outcome. “Interference” does not mean waves in neural tissue. It denotes a departure from classical additive decomposition under a constrained probabilistic model. These translations preserve the intellectual force of the original philosophical fascination while relo-cating it to a research program that can fail. If cue order does not matter, if a classical latent-state model predicts better, or if the quantum-like parameters do not generalize, QSDD should be rejected or restricted. 

# 13 Limitations and Failure Criteria 

First, Hilbert-space models are expressive and can overfit. Geometric elegance is not evidence. The fixed-axis model reduces this risk, but richer QSDD variants will require explicit gauge constraints, recovery analysis, held-out prediction, and stronger classical baselines. Second, behavioral equivalence is common. A quantum-like model and a classical model with memory can produce similar distributions. Noncommutativity is not exclusive to quantum models. The practical goal is not to prove a uniquely quantum ontology, but to determine whether the Hilbert-space model provides a more compact and general account under specified constraints. 19 of 24 Quantum-Like Spatial Decision Dynamics Basu Third, the simulation is conditional on its generating parameters, fixed initial azimuth, fixed cue-strength scale, independent Bernoulli observations, and simplified population structure. It demon-strates signed-cue observability and executability and exposes weak lapse identification; it does not establish real-world power or empirical support. A pilot-informed hierarchical simulation must precede the human study. Fourth, telemetry is not transparent access to thought. Gaze, hesitation, and backtracking are multiply determined. Latent-state interpretations must survive manipulation checks and alternative emission models. Fifth, spatial decisions can be continuous and high-dimensional. Discretizing them into left/right alternatives may discard geometry. Extensions should use higher-dimensional spaces, continuous POVMs, or hybrid state-space models while preserving identifiability. Sixth, VR is a context in its own right. Results may not generalize to physical navigation, desktop interfaces, or expert scientific work. Cross-medium replication is therefore part of the research program, not an afterthought. Finally, nothing in a successful QSDD experiment would establish consciousness-caused physical wave-function collapse. The following outcomes would count against the proposed framework: no replicable sequence effects under controlled exposure; failure of interference parameters to pre-dict held-out conditions; poor parameter recovery; instability across equivalent task encodings; or equal/better generalization by simpler classical models. 

# 14 Future Work 

The empirical program proceeds through four dependent tests: 1. Pilot identification. Estimate realistic cue strengths, participant heterogeneity, lapse, trial dependence, and tracking loss; rerun the design simulation before recruitment. 2. Controlled immersive evidence. Test frozen cue-order predictions, protocol-qualified inter-ference, progressive commitment, and reopening in a preregistered spatial task. 3. Telemetry-informed replication. Integrate gaze, pose, and path emissions only after the choice model is identified; compare QSDD against classical sequential models on held-out par-ticipants and environments. 4. Quantum-domain transfer. Evaluate circuit-inspection and debugging tasks with quantum learners and practitioners while preserving the same operator and measurement constraints. Each stage is independently falsifiable. Failure to recover parameters halts interpretation; failure against classical baselines restricts or rejects QSDD; and failure to transfer into circuit tasks limits the human–quantum interaction claim without invalidating a navigation-specific result. Future work should also examine adaptive interfaces. If a model detects sustained unresolved competition, a system could offer a cue or alternative representation. Such intervention creates a closed loop and must be evaluated carefully: an interface that predicts uncertainty also changes it. Randomized micro-interventions can estimate this causal effect while avoiding the circular claim that a model is accurate because the interface forces behavior to match it. 20 of 24 Quantum-Like Spatial Decision Dynamics Basu 

# 15 Conclusion 

QSDD converts a broad intuition about observation, context, and commitment into a model that can fail. Cue exposures are channels, registered judgments are instruments, route choice is an operational measurement, and a fixed sign-sensitive five-parameter geometry must predict new envi-ronments. The two-ground-truth simulation establishes that this repaired model is executable and distinguishable from a flexible classical comparator under stated conditions, while also revealing that lapse is weakly identified and that reliable discrimination may require more observations than the symmetry-restricted formulation suggested. It does not substitute for human evidence. The decisive test is therefore empirical and comparative. A preregistered immersive study must recover the model, predict held-out order-contrast surfaces, survive classical latent-state and sequential-sampling alternatives, and respect the protocol assumptions required for interference-like claims. If those tests fail, QSDD should be restricted or rejected. Human–quantum computer interaction supplies a consequential transfer domain. People must learn quantum concepts, inspect circuit representations, debug programs, interpret probabilistic outputs, and decide through imperfect tools. Methods from immersive interaction, visualization, behavioral telemetry, and empirical HCI can address that human layer directly. The resulting contribution is neither a theory of quantum consciousness nor a claim of hardware novelty; it is a falsifiable account of how representation order and embodied observation may shape decisions within spatial and quantum information environments.
