Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) support social simulation,
but faithfully reproducing real users requires grounding both
structured platform decisions and language in sparse, noisy
behavioral evidence. We introduce PsyMemBehav, which
maps psychology-informed profiles and retrieved memories
to target-first platform behavior through two-level identity-to-
action reasoning. To support this approach with comprehen-
sive behavioral traces, we construct a dataset PsyMemBehav-
bench from 5.57M observed behaviors of 5,862 real users (ap-
proximately 950 per user) across Reddit, Bluesky, and Twitter.
Each recorded engagement is modeled as target selection fol-
lowed by Comment, Like, or Repost and optional response
generation. PsyMemBehav combines 41-dimensional user de-
scriptors, top-5 BM25 memories, reverse-CoT supervision,
and rubric-guided DPO over complete reasoning-action tra-
jectories. Experiments show that PsyMemBehav 27B ranks
first in 11 of 12 reported individual-level metrics, improv-
ing average response quality over GPT-5.4 from 54.28 to
59.68 (9.95% relative), and obtains the lowest group-level
MAE (0.081) and MSE (0.009) among simulated systems.
These results support evidence-grounded user modeling for
observed-thread continuation; the benchmark remains condi-
tional on recorded engagement and does not model no-action
decisions. 1

Introduction
Online public opinion develops through repeated user inter-
actions: people comment, like, repost, choose whom to ad-
dress, and express stances within evolving discussion threads.
When aggregated, these micro-level decisions shape observ-
able stance distributions, interaction networks, attention con-
centration, and information diffusion. Simulating this process
offers a controlled setting for studying how public opinion
may evolve under different discussion contexts (Hu et al.
2024; Liu et al. 2025; Jeon et al. 2025; Cheng et al. 2025).
LLM agents offer a flexible mechanism for such simulations,
but the credibility of aggregate results depends in part on
whether individual behaviors are grounded in heterogeneous
real-user evidence (Argyle et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2024). Flu-
ent yet generic or uniformly active agents can distort action
frequencies, target selection, and stance composition when

Conventional character role-play usually starts from an ex-
plicit persona and evaluates whether generated dialogue re-
mains persona-consistent (Xu et al. 2026b; Xiao et al. 2026;
Cheng et al. 2026). Social-media user simulation poses a
different task: it infers observable platform behavior from a
user’s partial and noisy behavioral history together with the
current thread context. The prediction covers both textual re-
sponses and structured decisions about target selection and
the subsequent action type. We formulate the task as predict-
ing a user’s observed engagement in a given post–comment

context, including the target, subsequent action type, and op-
tional textual response. We normalize observed interactions
into Comment, Like, and Repost2. The logs record posi-
tive interactions but not impression-level exposure. Accord-
ingly, the benchmark is conditional on a recorded engage-
ment event, contains no explicit no-action label, and cannot
distinguish unseen threads from threads that a user viewed
but ignored. Within this scope, the task predicts the target,
subsequent action, and optional text.
Existing LLM-based social simulation systems have ex-
plored information diffusion, online conversation, and social
media event modeling (Park et al. 2023; Gao et al. 2024; Hu
et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025; Jeon et al. 2025; Ji et al. 2026).
These studies show that LLM agents can generate natural-
language interactions and support controlled simulation of
social processes. However, they often rely on coarse per-
sonas, demographic attributes, or manually specified agent
descriptions, and are less focused on predicting a concrete
user’s platform behavior from long-horizon behavioral his-
tories and situated thread context.
To address this, we introduce PsyMemBehav, a structured
framework that predicts social media behavior by integrat-
ing user profiles, longitudinal memories, and thread context.
The framework maps this evidence to behavior through two
sequential steps: identity activation, which selects relevant
traits and memories for the current thread, and action de-
cision, which identifies the target and predicts the subse-
quent action and response. Furthermore, we align the com-
plete reasoning-action trajectories via rubric-guided prefer-
ence optimization. This ensures the simulator optimizes not
just for response fluency, but also for action fidelity, memory
grounding, and behavioral consistency.
To evaluate this, we construct a large-scale dataset
PsyMemBehav-bench, containing 5.57M behaviors from
5,862 users across Reddit, Bluesky, and Twitter. Each sample
of PsyMemBehav-bench integrates 41 psychology-informed
descriptors, retrieved history, context, and observed behav-
ior. This enables the joint evaluation of structured decision-
making and text generation, moving beyond traditional text-
only role-playing.
We evaluate PsyMemBehav at the individual level by
matching held-out user interactions and at the thread level by
comparing aggregate comment-section statistics in observed
post contexts. In summary, our contributions are as follows:
1) We propose PsyMemBehav, a novel framework that
models conditional social-media behavior through a two-
stage structured decision process: identity activation and ac-
tion decision. By dynamically integrating 41-dimensional
psychology-informed user profiles and retrieved longitudi-
nal memories with thread context, our approach moves be-
yond flat text generation to produce grounded, context-aware
behavioral predictions.
2) We introduce a tailored alignment strategy combining
Reverse-CoT supervision and rubric-guided DPO to opti-
mize complete reasoning-action trajectories. This ensures

Related Work
2.1
Social-Media Account Simulation
LLM-based agents have been used to simulate human-like
behavior in role-playing, interactive environments, and so-
cial settings (Park et al. 2023; Argyle et al. 2023; Gao et al.
2024). Prior work shows that LLMs can maintain personas,
imitate conversational styles, and produce role-consistent re-
sponses (Shao et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Tu et al. 2024).
In parallel, user modeling studies show that digital traces
reveal stable attributes such as personality, preferences, de-
mographics, and behavioral tendencies (Kosinski, Stillwell,
and Graepel 2013; Youyou, Kosinski, and Stillwell 2015;
Hovy and Søgaard 2015), motivating personalized behavior
generation from historical traces.
Social-media account simulation, however, goes beyond
generic persona imitation. Users do not only generate text,
but also select interaction targets within a thread and choose
platform-specific actions such as replying, quoting, repost-
ing, liking, or ignoring. Although recent work explores LLM
agents for interactive social behavior and social simulation
(Ren et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024), existing approaches often
focus on generic personas, aggregate populations, or domain-
specific simulated users rather than real accounts grounded
in historical traces and platform-specific action spaces. Our
work addresses this gap by modeling real accounts with psy-
chological traits, platform-specific behavioral traits, retrieved
memory, and thread context.
2.2
Rubric-Guided Alignment for
Social-Behavior Simulation
Preference learning aligns LLMs with desired behaviors
through human or AI feedback and direct preference opti-
mization (Ouyang et al. 2022; Bai et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2023;
Rafailov et al. 2023), typically targeting general objectives
such as helpfulness, harmlessness, or instruction following.
Social-media simulation instead requires faithfulness to user
profiles, grounding in memory, platform-appropriate actions,
thread-context coherence, and social plausibility.
LLM-as-a-judge methods enable evaluation with explicit
criteria rather than a single overall score (Zheng et al. 2023;
Liu et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2024). Building on this idea,
we construct rubric-guided preference pairs over complete
reasoning-action trajectories, aligning both intermediate rea-


soning and resulting platform behavior rather than only final
textual responses.
2.3
Group-Level Thread Simulation
Agent-based modeling studies how individual behaviors pro-
duce collective phenomena such as diffusion, coordination,
polarization, and network formation (Epstein 1999; Macy
and Willer 2002; Lazer et al. 2009). LLM agents extend
this tradition with language-based reasoning and context-
sensitive interaction policies (Park et al. 2023; Gao et al.
2024), while introducing challenges such as unstable iden-
tities, unrealistic information access, and weak behavioral
grounding.
Recent work argues that social simulation should be eval-
uated beyond single-turn plausibility, including interaction
dynamics, stance distributions, network structures, conver-
sational diversity, and thread-level realism (Zhou et al. 2024;
Zhang et al. 2025a). We therefore first improve account-level
identity-to-action modeling through psychology-informed
reasoning and rubric-guided DPO, and then evaluate whether
these improvements transfer to group-level thread simula-
tions.

Methodology
3.1
Problem Formulation and Overview
We formulate social-media user simulation as a time-
anchored multi-action prediction problem. For a user u at
time t, the simulator observes
xt = (Pu, Mu,t, Tt, Ct),
where Pu is the user profile, Mu,t is retrieved behavioral
memory before time t, Tt is the current thread context, and
Ct is the set of candidate targets, i.e. the visible post or
comments the user may interact with at t. The simulator
predicts a complete platform behavior
yt = (ct, at, rt),
where ct ∈Ct is the selected target, at ∈A the subsequent
action, and rt the generated comment when required. We
normalize the heterogeneous behaviors in raw logs into three
visible actions, A = {Comment, Like, Repost}: textual en-
gagement (comments, replies) maps to Comment, reaction-
only behavior to Like, and resharing (including quote re-
posts) to Repost; original posts and past interactions serve
as evidence rather than prediction labels.
This requires more than fluent response generation: con-
ditional on a recorded engagement event, the simulator must
first select a target, then choose Comment, Like, or Re-
post and generate text when required. Because behavior is
context-dependent, a plausible comment can still be wrong
if the user would target a different item or act differently.
We address this with two design choices. First, two-level
identity-to-action reasoning situates the user in the current
thread before deciding the platform behavior (§3.2). Second,
two-stage trajectory training optimizes complete reasoning-
action trajectories rather than only final responses, through
reverse-CoT supervision and rubric-guided preference opti-
mization (§3.3). Figure 2 gives an overview.

Stage 2: Rubric-guided trajectory DPO.
SFT yields
well-formed trajectories but does not say which of several
fluent ones is the more faithful simulation. We therefore
optimize preferences over complete trajectories. The rubric
itself is induced rather than hand-specified. We sample tra-
jectory pairs and ask a judge to write free-form critiques
explaining why one trajectory is a less faithful simulation
than the other; an LLM then consolidates the full set of cri-
tiques into ten criteria that together account for the observed
weaknesses, which the human annotators verify for coverage
and non-redundancy. Grounding the evaluation axes in the
weaknesses simulators actually exhibit, rather than in a pri-
ori intuitions, makes the rubric an explanatory cover of the
failures rather than an arbitrary checklist. Once induced, the
rubric is frozen and applied uniformly during preference op-
timization. For each input we compare a trajectory rolled out

and evidence-to-action coherence; full definitions in the ap-

4
Experimental Setup
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed PsyMemBehav
framework, we conduct experiments on the social-media user
simulation task defined in Section 3. The model first selects
the target, then predicts Comment, Like, or Repost and any
required comment text.
4.1
Base Models
We conduct experiments on two backbone models: Qwen3.5-
9B and Qwen3.5-27B. The 9B model is used to examine
whether the proposed framework remains effective under a
smaller simulator, while the 27B model serves as the stronger
backbone for our main experiments. Both models are trained
under the same two-stage pipeline, resulting in two models:
PsyMemBehav 9B and PsyMemBehav 27B.
4.2
Baselines
We compare PsyMemBehav 9B and PsyMemBehav 27B
against the following baselines: GPT-5.4 (OpenAI 2026),
Gemini-2.5-Pro (Comanici et al. 2025), Claude-Sonnet-4.6
(Anthropic 2025), Qwen3.7-Max (Yang et al. 2025), and
DeepSeek-V4-Pro (Xu et al. 2026a). Additionally, Qwen3.5-
9B and Qwen3.5-27B (Yang et al. 2025) are included as
open-weight baselines. All models receive the same full user
profile, BM25-retrieved pre-event memories, thread context,
and candidate targets. They use the unified end-to-end prompt
in the appendix, which selects a target before predicting the
action and branch-specific text; gold target and action labels
are withheld.
4.3
Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate the models at both individual and group levels.
Individual-level evaluation.
We report two behavior-level
metrics. Target Accuracy measures whether the model se-
lects the correct interaction target among the candidate posts
and comments. Action Accuracy measures whether the
model chooses the correct platform action—Comment, Like,
or Repost—for the selected target.
For generated comments, we use Gemini-3-Flash as an au-
tomated judge to score seven quality dimensions: semantic
alignment, intent alignment, emotional consistency, stance
consistency, style fidelity, persona memory, and naturalness.
We report both individual dimension scores and their aver-
age. The complete scoring prompt appears in the appendix.
Group-level simulation.
To evaluate multi-user thread
continuation, we place profiled simulators in the same ob-
served thread seed and roll out their target-first actions.
Following prior work that evaluates collective simulations
through interaction, stance, and influence structure (Zhou
et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025), we report four complementary
metrics. Reply Reciprocity is the share of interacting dyads
with mutual directed replies, a standard property of directed
networks (Garlaschelli and Loffredo 2004). Stance deviation
is the ℓ1 distance between simulated and real Pro, Anti, and
Neutral distributions, using the conventional favor/again-
st/neither stance formulation (Mohammad et al. 2016). Top1
KOL is the reply share received by the most-addressed user,
operationalizing key-opinion-leader concentration (Katz and
Lazarsfeld 1955); Top5 PageRank is the normalized PageR-
ank mass of the five highest-ranked users (Brin and Page
1998). MAE and MSE summarize absolute and squared de-
viations across thread-level statistics. Detailed formulas are
in the appendix.
4.4
Dataset and Data Split
We construct PsyMemBehav-bench from Reddit, Bluesky,
and Twitter through official APIs. It contains 5.57M behav-
iors from 5,862 real users (approximately 950 per user), with
usernames replaced by randomized hash identifiers. Platform
events are normalized as Comment, Like, or Repost. Each
instance combines a two-group profile, five BM25-retrieved
pre-event memories, thread context, and candidate targets
with the observed target, action, and response. A user-disjoint
chronological split assigns 5,000 users to training and 862
to validation and testing; held-out users are unseen, and all
profile and memory evidence predates prediction. Collec-
tion favors accounts with rich observable histories. Thus,
the scale denotes longitudinal density rather than platform-
wide representativeness and may overrepresent active users
while underrepresenting low-activity, private, or new users.
Among closely related studies reporting real-user counts,
FineRob uses 1,866 users and 78.6K records (approximately
126 histories per user), whereas MOSAIC uses 204 real
users and 30 posts and GA-S3 models group agents over 30
events. PsyMemBehav therefore provides larger and denser
longitudinal evidence for user-level target–action–response
modeling, not a claim of platform-wide coverage (see the
appendix).

Individual-Level Results

5.2
Ablation Study
Table 3 separates training ablations from component abla-
tions on Qwen3.5-9B. R-CoT/CoT-SFT is supervised fine-
tuning on accepted reverse-CoT traces without DPO, whereas
R-DPO is rubric-guided trajectory optimization.
Naive CoT prompting degrades performance, and output-
only SFT improves response quality without improving ac-
tion prediction. R-CoT instead improves target accuracy, ac-
tion accuracy, and profile fidelity; adding R-DPO further im-
proves most behavior and response metrics. Relative to Base,
the complete R-CoT+R-DPO pipeline raises target accuracy
from 38.79 to 48.22, action accuracy from 51.61 to 59.52,
and average response quality from 35.60 to 48.78.
The two stages contribute differently. Relative to Base,
R-CoT adds 7.85 points in target accuracy, 2.00 in action
accuracy, 7.77 in average response quality, 3.81 in Psych fi-
delity, and 5.54 in Trait fidelity. Adding R-DPO contributes
a further 1.58, 5.91, 5.41, and 0.75 points to target accu-
racy, action accuracy, response quality, and Psych fidelity,
while Trait fidelity decreases by 1.36. Thus, reverse-CoT ac-
counts for most target and trait gains; DPO mainly strength-
ens action selection and response quality despite a small
trait-preservation trade-off.
Removing either profile group degrades performance, con-
firming that general psychological and platform-specific ev-
idence are complementary. Removing identity activation
mainly reduces response quality, whereas removing action
decision produces the lowest target and action accuracy, sup-
porting their respective roles in evidence selection and target-
first behavior prediction.
The component losses also reveal specialization. Remov-
ing identity activation causes the largest drop in average re-
sponse quality (−4.60) and Psych fidelity (−5.15), whereas
removing action decision causes the largest losses in target
accuracy (−6.02) and action accuracy (−3.14). Platform-
specific traits contribute more to target accuracy (−4.32)
and Trait fidelity (−4.34) than general traits (−2.87 and
−1.10), while general traits contribute more to response qual-
ity (−3.71 versus −2.24). This pattern matches the intended
division of labor among evidence and reasoning components.
5.3
Group-Level Thread Simulation
Table 4 compares PsyMemBehav 27B with strong general
models on continuations of the same observed thread con-
texts. PsyMemBehav 27B obtains the lowest stance devi-
ation (0.155), MAE (0.081), and MSE (0.009), while its
reciprocity (0.121) is closest to the real value (0.043). Its
lower reciprocity error also avoids the over-interactive group-
chat pattern produced by general-purpose agents. Relative to
GPT-5.4, PsyMemBehav reduces stance deviation by 11.4%,
MAE by 18.2%, and MSE by 35.7%. GPT-5 and GPT-5.4 re-
main closer on Top1 KOL and Top5 PageRank, respectively,
so PsyMemBehav does not lead every structural statistic. In
direct pairwise evaluation, PsyMemBehav is preferred over
GPT-5.4 in 64.0% of thread continuations versus 34.0%,
with 2.0% ties (Appendix). These results concern continu-
ation within observed threads, not platform-wide social dy-
namics.
PsyMemBehav’s absolute reciprocity gap from the real
threads is 0.078, compared with 0.139 for GPT-5.4, a 43.9%
reduction. For influence concentration, however, its Top1
KOL gap is 0.019 versus 0.013 for GPT-5, and its Top5
PageRank gap is 0.072 versus 0.067 for GPT-5.4. The
strongest improvements therefore concern interaction reci-
procity, stance composition, and aggregate fidelity, while
centralization remains model-dependent.
6
Conclusion and Limitations
We present PsyMemBehav, combining 41-dimensional user
descriptors, BM25 memories, target-first reasoning, reverse-
CoT supervision, and rubric-guided DPO. PsyMemBe-
hav 27B achieves the strongest individual results and
lowest aggregate deviation in observed-thread continua-
tion, supporting evidence-grounded user models for micro-
level public-opinion simulation. Limitations include missing
exposure/no-action labels, LLM-inferred rather than self-
reported profiles, answer-conditioned reasoning traces, and
aggregate rather than per-instance human validation. Future
work should address these issues across broader settings.

Evaluation Rubric and DPO Preference
Construction
We use a ten-criterion rubric to evaluate simulated user be-
haviors and construct DPO preference pairs. The rubric cov-
ers four aspects: action-level fidelity, user-profile alignment,
content quality, and format/reasoning validity. This design
reflects the fact that social-media simulation is not only a
text generation problem: the model must first select a valid
interaction target, then choose how to interact with that tar-
get, and only then produce a response if the selected action
requires visible text.
Action-level fidelity includes action accuracy, interaction-
intensity calibration, and target/thread grounding. User-
profile alignment includes persona consistency, memory ac-
tivation discipline, and stance consistency. Content quality
includes response naturalness and specificity. Format/reason-
ing validity includes output format validity and CoT plausi-
bility.
The DPO judge receives the correct high-level action la-
bel but not the gold visible text. This prevents the judge from
rewarding exact surface matching and encourages evalua-
tion of target grounding, action fidelity, persona consistency,
memory discipline, stance, naturalness, specificity, format
validity, and reasoning plausibility. The weighted score is

D.4
Human–Gemini Evaluation Agreement
For the human evaluation, we selected 30 anonymized user
IDs and evaluated five behavior instances per ID, yield-
ing 150 annotated instances. Human evaluators and Gemini
scored the same outputs under the same seven-dimensional
rubric. Table 2 in the main paper compares their dimension-
level means. The human and Gemini overall means are 54.40
and 55.46, respectively, a human-minus-Gemini difference
of −1.06 points. Humans score semantic alignment, style
fidelity, and naturalness slightly higher, whereas Gemini
scores intent, emotion, stance, and profile/memory consis-
tency higher. This dimension-mean comparison assesses ag-
gregate calibration; it neither establishes per-instance agree-
ment nor replaces a formal human reliability analysis.
The largest absolute gaps occur for stance (4.20 points) and
emotion (3.60 points), while all other dimensions differ by
at most 3.73 points. Because the signs vary, Gemini does not
show a simple global tendency to be uniformly more lenient
or stringent. The dimension-specific gaps therefore motivate
reporting human evaluation together with LLM-judge results
and cross-judge robustness.

Our use of large-scale does not imply platform-wide cover-
age. Instead, it refers to the joint scale of real-user coverage,
total behavioral volume, and the density of longitudinal his-
tories available for user modeling. We therefore character-
ize the resource more precisely as a large-scale, longitudi-
nal, user-centered dataset. Table 8 compares PsyMemBehav-
bench with closely related work along three dimensions: the

available for modeling each user, and the granularity of the
prediction or simulation target.
Here, real-user coverage denotes the number of distinct
real users whose observed behavioral evidence is used to
construct or evaluate the simulator. Behavioral evidence cap-

The group-level evaluation places multiple profiled simula-

continuations.
Reply Reciprocity (Rec.).
Reciprocity is a standard
directed-network measure of mutual linkage (Garlaschelli
and Loffredo 2004). We compute the fraction of interacting
user pairs (u, v) for which both reply directions occur:
Rec. = |{(u, v) : u →v ∧v →u}|
|{(u, v) : u →v ∨v →u}|.
Overly high reciprocity indicates an unrealistic group-chat
pattern rather than broadcast discussion.

Following the conven-
tional favor/against/neither formulation of stance (Moham-
mad et al. 2016), each comment is annotated as Pro, Anti,
or Neutral. We measure the ℓ1 distance between simulated
