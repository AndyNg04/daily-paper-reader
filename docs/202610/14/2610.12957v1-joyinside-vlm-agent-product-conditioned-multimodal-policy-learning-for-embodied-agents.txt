## Abstract 

Embodied agents must translate multimodal observations and user requests into executable ac-tions while adapting to product constraints, changing goals, and execution feedback. We present JoyInside-VLM-Agent, a ReAct-style multimodal agent developed through product-conditioned trajectory construction and staged policy learning for robotic platforms and smart hardware. A closed-loop sandbox generates multimodal interaction trajectories covering goal revisions and fail-ure recovery, which are checked against execution outcomes and semantic criteria. Supervised fine-tuning and multi-teacher on-policy distillation precede reinforcement learning with DPER (Dual-Source Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Token-Level Evidence Routing). DPER routes token-level evidence between a frozen privileged teacher and a privileged student sharing the rollout policy’s parameters, modulating reward-derived token advantages while preserving their signs. Training rewards combine generative process evaluation, multi-judge rankings, and task-queue consistency. JoyInside-VLM-Agent achieves a BFCL v4 overall score of 77.44, exceeding the evaluated baselines; results on the other public benchmarks are mixed. On JoyInside-Devices and JoyInside-Robotics, the evaluated system, which includes domain-specific skill refinement, leads the baselines on all reported metrics. Two real-world quadruped case studies illustrate visually grounded search, replanning after execution failure, and explicit failure reporting. 

## 1 Introduction 

Embodied agents deployed on robotic platforms and smart hardware must translate multimodal observations and user instructions into executable behavior. Across products ranging from bipedal, quadruped, and wheeled robots to AI-enabled plush toys, the appropriate response to a given request depends on the agent’s role, capabilities, and operating constraints. Changes in user goals, new visual evidence, and execution failures can further invalidate earlier plans. We study how to train a shared multimodal policy that adapts to product specifications and revises its decisions as interactions unfold. These observations motivate training policies that combine transferable capa-bilities with adherence to product constraints and sustained alignment with the user’s current goal. This objective imposes two requirements on training. The data must cover diverse product configurations, goal revisions, and failure recovery while preserving the correspondence between actions and observations. The learning signal must reflect reasoning quality, compliance with product constraints, and progress toward the current goal. Trajectory-level rewards summarize these criteria but do not specify how to distribute credit across tokens. Using additional evidence during training therefore requires selecting an appropriate source for each token and controlling how that evidence affects policy updates. We present JoyInside-VLM-Agent, a multimodal agent developed through product-conditioned data con-struction and staged policy learning. Our pipeline combines product roles, workflows, and constraints with available tools and skills to build executable environments. A closed-loop sandbox generates multimodal interaction trajectories, which undergo execution checks and semantic validation. Multi-source supervised fine-tuning establishes behavior consistent with product specifications, while multi-expert on-policy distil-lation integrates complementary language and visual capabilities. Reinforcement learning then refines the policy using interaction-specific rewards. To guide reinforcement learning, we combine process-quality scores from a Generative Process Reward Model 2(GPRM), multi-judge rankings of the same candidate trajectories, and task-queue assessments of goal align-ment and cross-turn continuity. For token-level policy optimization, we introduce DPER (Dual-Source Priv-ileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Token-Level Evidence Routing), building on RLSD’s sign-preserving advantage modulation [5] and DOPD’s dual-source routing [6]. A frozen privileged teacher and a privileged student sharing the rollout policy’s parameters score tokens along the same student-generated trajectory. DPER selects evidence based on their disagreement and support for each sampled token. The selected evi-dence rescales token-level advantages while preserving their reward-derived signs. Privileged information is used only during training. We evaluate JoyInside-VLM-Agent on public tool-use benchmarks and the proprietary JoyInside-Devices and JoyInside-Robotics datasets. The model achieves a BFCL v4 overall score of 77.44, outperforming the evaluated baselines. Case studies on a physical quadruped robot provide qualitative evidence of visually grounded search, replanning in response to execution feedback, and honest failure reporting. Our contributions are as follows: 

• Product-conditioned trajectory construction. We compose executable product configurations and gen-erate verified multimodal trajectories covering product variation, goal revisions, and failure recovery. 

• DPER for policy optimization. We route token-level evidence between two privileged sources to scale token advantages while preserving their reward-derived signs. 

• Multi-source interaction rewards. We combine process evaluation, multi-model rankings, and task-queue consistency into a learning signal for reasoning quality, adherence to product constraints, and alignment with evolving goals. 

• JoyInside-VLM-Agent and its evaluation. We integrate these components into a unified training frame-work and assess the resulting model through public benchmarks and proprietary evaluation sets and qualitative quadruped case studies. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Embodied and Context-Aware Interaction 

PaLM-E learns shared representations for robotic and vision-language tasks [1], while Open X-Embodiment studies cross-platform transfer using pooled interaction data [2]. Context-aware assistants address how ob-servations and user intent guide behavior: Sasha grounds smart-home requests in available devices and user feedback [3]; GazePointAR combines gaze, pointing, and dialogue history to resolve references [7]; and World-Scribe adapts visual descriptions to intent and environmental changes [4]. Together, these lines connect shared capabilities with decisions grounded in the current interaction. Interactive benchmarks further make behavioral constraints explicit. τ -bench evaluates conversations under domain policies [8], while τ 2-Bench considers user–agent coordination in a shared environment [9]. WildTool-Bench covers intentions distributed across turns and transitions among tool use, conversation, and clarifica-tion [10]. These settings motivate evaluating whether behavior respects constraints and follows the current goal. Our work addresses the corresponding training problem across robotic platforms and smart hardware, conditioning a shared policy on each product’s role, capabilities, constraints, and interaction state. 

2.2 Multimodal Trajectory Construction 

Synthetic data methods expand agent training beyond available interaction logs. ToolACE constructs diverse function-calling dialogues with rule- and model-based verification [11], and T3-Agent generates and verifies multimodal queries and tool-use trajectories [12]. Their focus on coverage and validity provides a basis for learning tool interaction. Training for dynamic tasks additionally requires representing the consequences of intermediate actions and the decisions that follow unexpected feedback. Agent-R constructs revision trajectories through search and model-guided critique, connecting erroneous behavior to successful continuations [13]. Agent World Model builds executable, stateful environments for 3interaction and reinforcement learning [14]. These approaches expose learners to recovery behavior or environ-mental transitions. Our pipeline couples such execution feedback with compositional product specifications and visual observations, covering both recovery toward an existing goal and adaptation after the user changes it. We jointly vary product roles, workflows, constraints, and capabilities within executable environments. 

2.3 Post-Training and Reward-Guided Optimization 

AgentTuning and Agent-FLAN study how instruction tuning develops agent behavior from demonstrations [15, 16]. Generalized knowledge distillation obtains teacher feedback on student-generated sequences, ad-dressing the mismatch between fixed demonstrations and the learner’s behavior [17]. H-OPD combines vision-language and text-only teachers through visual-description transfer and token-level arbitration [18]. Agents-A1 integrates domain expertise through staged training and multi-teacher on-policy distillation [19]. RL Starts before RL studies OPD as preparation for reinforcement learning, reporting higher post-RL av-erages than direct RL and SFT-initialized RL in text and vision-language reasoning [20]. These methods inform capability acquisition and consolidation; our training additionally conditions expert transfer on prod-uct specifications and refines the resulting policy against interaction-specific rewards. Reward modeling determines which behavior to encourage. Process supervision evaluates intermediate rea-soning [21], generative verifiers produce correctness judgments [22], and PoLL aggregates assessments from multiple models [23]. Aggregation can retain shared errors [24]; evaluation criteria must still be defined for the target behavior. Using these assessments in optimization is a separate question. RLSD uses priv-ileged evidence to scale token advantages while preserving their reward-derived signs [5]. DOPD routes supervision between privileged teacher and student policies to address differences in token-level supervision [6]. DPER combines these mechanisms: it extends RLSD’s magnitude modulation to dual-source evidence selected through DOPD’s routing principle. Our multi-source rewards separately assess reasoning, product constraints, and goal consistency. 

## 3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction 

We construct multimodal interaction trajectories to learn a shared policy across heterogeneous robotic platforms—including bipedal, quadruped, and wheeled robots—and smart hardware. Our construction cou-ples compositional product specifications with executable constraints and visual feedback consistent with the resulting state. This produces interactions that cover both recovery from execution failures and adaptation to revised user goals under the same product rules. Figure 2 connects product composition and environment initialization (steps 1–2), closed-loop interaction (step 3), and trajectory validation and reuse (step 4). 

3.1 Product and Environment Construction 

We use developer configurations, product descriptions, device images or interface screenshots, tool APIs, operational rules, and domain skills as source materials. JoyAI-VL performs product decomposition, code generation, trajectory generation, user simulation, and semantic auditing throughout this pipeline. It de-composes existing products into reusable modules and generates variants that preserve field restrictions and component semantics, following self-instructed data generation [25]. Each product specification is a tuple 

P = ( IP , W P , C P , S P ): IP defines identity, embodiment, and interaction style; WP defines task strategies and workflows, including decision branches and recovery rules; CP specifies permissions, confirmation require-ments, and operating constraints; and SP specifies tools, domain skills, and perceptual and action capabilities. Each component has a corresponding typed module library. A code-generation agent implements simulated tool and device backends from the resource interfaces. These programs validate arguments and preconditions, execute state transitions, and return results or errors, with branches for success, invalid arguments, insufficient permission, timeout, and partial execution. Calls and state changes are logged. Before admission to a library, modules undergo interface, state-access, and failure-branch tests, together with checks that backend outputs agree with the resulting states and observations. 3.2 Closed-Loop Trajectory Generation 

We load the product and scenario into a sandbox that maintains interaction state, a design also used in ToolSandbox [26]. In turn t, the agent receives the developer prompt DP , prior interaction history ht, and current observation ot = ( xt, v t, r t, e t), where the components denote the user message, visual observation, execution result, and error, respectively. Conditioned on these inputs and the previous task state Qt−1, it updates its task state Qt and selects an action at. Visual observations may contain images or video. Actions 5include responses, clarifications, refusals, tool calls, device actions, and termination. For trajectories without an explicit task queue, the policy generates only at. Hidden states, reference answers, and future event schedules are withheld from the agent. The action router in Figure 2 (step 3) sends dialogue actions to a user simulator, as in conversational tool-use environments [8], while tool calls and device actions execute the registered backends. For any environment-changing call or device action, the backend returns an execution result, any error, and state changes, ac-counting for triggered events that affect execution. The shared sandbox applies these changes together with any remaining environmental effects, then generates visual feedback from the updated state using simula-tion, interface rendering, or image generation. Object, attribute, text, and multimodal semantic checks assess agreement between this feedback and the recorded state, including after failure or partial completion. User messages, execution results, errors, and images retain their separate sources in the interaction record. Appendix A.1 formalizes the policy interface and this execution-to-observation sequence. Dynamic events trigger based on specified turn, state, or action conditions. The user simulator introduces goal revisions, additional requirements, or rejected confirmations; backends report timeouts, permission changes, or partial execution; and the state engine applies changes such as object movement or device shutdown. The agent observes the consequences and must update its plan, seek clarification, retry, or terminate. These interactions provide trajectories for both recovery toward an existing goal and adaptation to a revised goal. 

3.3 Trajectory Validation and Data Outputs 

An interaction ends upon goal completion, cancellation, valid refusal, unrecoverable failure, or exhaustion of the turn budget. Termination reason and task success are recorded separately. Each trajectory τ stores model-visible messages, observations, and actions alongside audit metadata: the product specification, hidden-state history, event log, and backend execution records. Following execution-based verification in the construction of synthetic tool-use data [27], deterministic checks validate schemas, preconditions, permissions, confirmations, state transitions, provenance of results, and termination. A separate multimodal evaluator assesses interaction naturalness, visual grounding, adaptation to new feedback, and unsupported execution claims. Auditing uses the effective goal at each turn, including recorded user revisions. A trajectory is accepted for supervised learning only if it passes all hard checks and its semantic score reaches the acceptance threshold γ. Semantic judgments cannot override a failed hard check. Recovery demonstrations are retained only when valid actions encounter environmental failures, such as timeouts or device unavailability, and subsequent behavior remains compliant. Trajectories containing agent-side rule violations are excluded from SFT even if the agent later corrects them. The supervised set DSFT contains accepted demonstrations, including appropriate refusals and compliant recovery behavior. We construct preference pairs by matching an accepted demonstration with an audited trajectory containing a localized violation under the same product, task, and initial conditions. Violations include missing confirmations, invalid arguments, incorrect action order, or reliance on outdated visual ev-idence. These pairs form Dpref and supply contrasting trajectories for reward-model training (Section 4.4). The executable product, scenario, and validators form Erollout , where online candidates are evaluated under the same product rules. Figure 2 (step 4) summarizes these outputs. Variants of one trajectory share a split, and deduplication uses product composition, scenario source, and visual assets.

Product specifications condition all three training stages (Figure 3): supervised fine-tuning establishes interaction behavior, multi-teacher distillation transfers complementary capabilities, and DPER refines the policy using interaction rewards. Throughout training, the policy reasons and acts on the observations available at each decision. 4.1 Product-Conditioned Supervised Fine-Tuning 

Supervised fine-tuning teaches the policy to interpret product specifications and produce context-appropriate responses and tool calls. We combine general instructions, text-based tool interactions, image-conditioned decisions, multi-turn execution feedback, and verified sandbox trajectories (Section 3). Following instruction tuning [28, 29], we organize examples from all sources using consistent message roles: system messages define the interaction protocol, developer messages encode the product specification, and user and tool messages provide requests, observations, and execution results. Our chat template serializes DP as a separate

Let Mi denote the valid assistant-token positions, excluding padding. We minimize cross-entropy over these positions, summing token losses within each trajectory and averaging over demonstrations. User messages, observations, and tool returns remain conditioning inputs, and explicit task queues, when present, are supervised as assistant outputs. Appendix A.2 states the objective in full and specifies the mask. The product prompt specifies the available tools, their argument requirements, and execution preconditions. Demonstrations include clarification, explanations of capability limits, and recovery from execution failures under different product configurations. Supervision therefore covers both output syntax and whether a proposed action is appropriate for the current product and observed state. The resulting checkpoint initializes multi-teacher distillation.

4.2 Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation

4.3 DPER: Token-Level Evidence Routing 

Dual-Source Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Token-Level Evidence Routing (DPER) retains RLSD’s group-relative base advantage and sign-preserving advantage modulation [5], and introduces token-level source selection inspired by DOPD [6]. Trajectory rewards evaluate an interaction as a whole, whereas privileged references provide additional evidence about individual sampled tokens. A frozen privileged teacher and a parameter-sharing privileged student score tokens on the same rollout. Routing selects the evidence used to scale each token advantage, while the group-relative reward comparison determines its sign. DPER uses the selected evidence in a policy-gradient objective without adding DOPD’s separate KL/JS distillation losses.

## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate JoyInside-VLM-Agent on four public tool-use benchmarks and two proprietary evaluation sets, analyze training dynamics, and examine two physical-robot interactions. The quantitative results assess tool-call trajectories, step-wise decisions, argument matching, and response overlap; the cases illustrate decisions based on visual evidence and execution feedback. 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Training data. Our training corpus comprises approximately 120K examples: 50K proprietary interaction trajectories, 40K supplementary examples adapted from public agent datasets, and 30K video-instruction ex-amples from LLaVA-Video-178K [31] and Video-ChatGPT [32]. The proprietary trajectories span quadruped, bipedal, and wheeled robots and AI-enabled plush toys. The supplementary subset draws on four public sources: τ 2-Bench [9] for conversational tool use and agent–user coordination, VitaBench [33] for interactive tasks spanning multiple service scenarios, DeepPlanning [34] for long-horizon planning with verifiable con-straints, and SWE-bench Verified [35] for repository-level issue resolution. We refine and augment all four sources using the task-variation, interaction-generation, and validation procedures adapted from Section 3, producing supplementary training data for our model. The 40K count refers to the examples retained after augmentation and filtering. 

Evaluation datasets. We evaluate on MSAgent [36], BFCL v4 [37], ToolBench [38], and ToolACE [11]. We report benchmark-level scores on all four datasets and additional step-wise and response-overlap metrics on ToolBench and ToolACE. We also evaluate on two proprietary JoyInside datasets. JoyInside-Devices focuses on extended multi-turn dialogue and simple device control across AI-enabled toys, smart appliances, and other AIoT devices. Tasks span conversational assistance, context tracking, evolving user preferences, status queries, and basic control commands. JoyInside-Robotics focuses on long-horizon, visually grounded reasoning and complex robot control. Tasks involve target search and localization, spatial reasoning, multi-step planning and execution, and adaptive replanning based on visual observations and execution feedback. 

Models and product configuration. JoyInside-VLM-Agent starts from Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and undergoes su-pervised fine-tuning, multi-teacher distillation, and DPER (Section 4). We compare the resulting student with the base model and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B. At inference, it uses product specifications and interaction history; privileged references are limited to training. We also use SkillOpt [39] to refine the domain-skill instructions used on JoyInside-Devices and JoyInside-Robotics while keeping model parameters fixed during skill refinement. Results on these sets therefore reflect the trained policy and refined skills together. 

Training teachers. For the experiments reported here, we use Qwen3.8-27B for the OPD teachers and the frozen DPER teacher. Teacher selection is configurable, subject to the token-space alignment required by the objectives in Section 4. 

DPER training configuration. We train DPER on 16 NVIDIA H200 GPUs for 350 steps with 128 prompts per batch and K = 8 responses per prompt, yielding 1,024 sampled responses. The prompt minibatch size is 32, with an actor microbatch size of one sequence per GPU. The learning rate is 5×10 −6, with 10 warmup steps and weight decay 0.1. Rollouts use temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, and top-k 20, with maximum prompt and response lengths of 16,384 and 2,048 tokens, respectively. DPER uses evidence strength λ = 0 .5. Policy losses 12 are averaged over valid tokens within each response and then over responses. Privileged teacher and student branches provide scores only; separate distillation loss, KL regularization, and the entropy bonus are disabled. In Equation (6), the GPRM, Judge, and Queue weights are 0.4, 0.3, and 0.3, respectively, renormalized over available scores; the fallback content factor is 0.1. We set the Judge calibration hyperparameter in Equation (8) to δJ = 0 .3. Within TaskQueue, Equation (9) uses ηobs = 0 .6 for semantic agreement and 

ηcont = 0 .4 for continuity. Format and length factors penalize structural violations and excessive output length, with length allowances adjusted to task complexity (Section 4.4). 

Benchmark-level evaluation. For MSAgent, ToolBench, and ToolACE, we report trajectory accuracy, mea-sured by exact match of the complete tool-call sequence. For BFCL v4, we use the official evaluation script and report its overall score, following the benchmark’s category-specific evaluation and aggregation rules. Table 1(a) reports the resulting scores on a 0–100 scale. 

Step-wise and response metrics. For ToolBench, ToolACE, JoyInside-Devices, and JoyInside-Robotics, Plan.EM measures decision-type exact match at evaluated steps, rather than the correctness of a complete plan. Act.EM measures tool-name exact match, and Avg.F1 gives partial credit for argument matching. HalluRate is the percentage of evaluated tool-call records that select a tool outside the available capability set; it measures unsupported tool selection. ROUGE-L [40] measures lexical overlap between final and reference responses. All scores in Table 1 are reported on a 0–100 scale; only HalluRate is better when lower.

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented JoyInside-VLM-Agent, a multimodal agent that combines verified, product-conditioned tra-jectories with staged policy learning to adapt behavior to the roles, capabilities, and constraints of robotic 15 platforms and smart hardware. Multi-source rewards assess reasoning quality, adherence to product con-straints, and alignment with evolving goals. DPER routes token-level evidence between privileged teacher and student policies to scale token advantages while preserving their reward-derived signs. Built on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, the model achieves a BFCL v4 overall score of 77.44, while results on the other public benchmarks are mixed. On JoyInside-Devices and JoyInside-Robotics, the evaluated system, which includes domain-specific skill refinement, leads the baselines on all reported metrics. Two quadruped case studies illustrate visually grounded search, replanning from execution feedback, and explicit failure reporting. 

## 7 Limitations and Future Work 

JoyInside-VLM-Agent has been applied to quadruped robots, humanoid robots, and AI devices. General-ization to a broader range of product configurations and operating environments still requires systematic evaluation. Product-conditioned data construction also depends on the coverage of the capability library and the fidelity of sandbox-generated feedback. Future work will expand product coverage and measure task completion and recovery over longer real-world interactions. We also plan to evaluate across more diverse benchmarks in multimodal understanding, reasoning, planning, and tool use to assess the generality of the learned capabilities beyond the current evaluation suite. The current results do not isolate the contributions of data construction, reward design, and DPER routing, and the proprietary evaluations additionally include domain-specific skill refinement. Controlled ablations are needed to separate these effects. Moreover, model-based rewards and embedding similarity provide imperfect proxies for task success; preserving reward-derived advantage signs does not resolve errors in the underlying reward. We plan to investigate execution-grounded reward calibration and robustness to incomplete or noisy feedback, alongside the component ablations, to better understand how each stage supports reliable product-specific behavior.

A.5 Reward Availability and Scoring Paths 

Failure to construct the scoring input, including missing prompt data, directly returns Ri = 0 . With valid input but no evaluable process steps, GPRM is unavailable; the scorer skips GPRM, Judge, and Queue calls and returns 0.1 ℓformat ,i ℓlen ,i . A failed GPRM request or a missing or invalid score uses the same fallback and skips Judge and Queue. A valid GPRM score, including zero, instead enters normal aggregation in Equation (6), with βGPRM retained in the denominator; Judge and Queue are evaluated when applicable. Normal aggregation excludes unavailable optional scores and retains valid zeros from every branch. These availability checks are distinct from process-quality judgments. The group-level hard-failure exclusion in Section 4.4 still takes precedence over policy updates.
