Abstract 

AI companions provide users with emotional support and ongoing companionship, but their sustained relational interactions also introduce distinct safety risks. Prior work largely tar-gets general harms or specific companion plat-forms and risk categories, while relying on manually collected conversations, limiting scal-able and user-grounded evaluation and miti-gation. To address this, we introduce Com-panionSafe , a unified evaluation-to-mitigation framework for AI companion safety. Its evalu-ation component, CompanionEval , provides scalable multi-turn evaluation by constructing 1,300 relationally grounded settings from di-verse personas, matched companion characters, and dialogue scenarios across four turn tiers. Its mitigation component, CompanionGuard ,converts benchmark-exposed failures into turn-localized supervision for companion-specific risk detection. Our results show that even strong LLMs and existing guard models re-main vulnerable to companion-specific rela-tional harms, while both closed- and open-source models exhibit increasing safety risks as interactions become longer. Together, Com-panionEval and CompanionGuard form a prac-tical evaluation-to-mitigation loop for AI com-panion safety, turning relational safety failures exposed during multi-turn interaction into su-pervision for companion-specific guardrails.

AI Companions. AI companions support sus-tained social and emotional interaction and are often experienced as friends or sources of emo-tional support (Brandtzaeg et al., 2022; Skjuve et al., 2022). Users may also form romantic rela-tionships with these systems (Pan and Mou, 2024) or use them for identity exploration, role-play, and social fulfillment (Li and Zhang, 2024; Ma et al., 2026; Kieserman et al., 2026). Such sustained rela-tionships can introduce companion-specific harms, including disregard, control, manipulation, and in-fidelity (Zhang et al., 2025), as well as emotional dependence (Laestadius et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2026) and blurred relational boundaries (Ciriello et al., 2024). These findings motivate safety re-search that accounts for the relational and longitu-dinal nature of AI companion interactions. 

Safety Evaluation. LLM safety evaluation has progressed from general-purpose benchmarks to increasingly domain- and context-specific settings. General benchmarks such as HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024), JailbreakBench (Chao et al., 2024), and StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024) evaluate harmful request handling, jailbreak robustness, and refusal behavior across broad safety risks. Re-cent work further shows that safety requirements vary across domains and user populations, includ-ing K–12 education (Park et al., 2026), child-facing interactions (Krishna-Kumar et al., 2026), medical dialogue (Sheoran and Hao, 2026), el-derly users (Fan et al., 2026), and agentic sci-entific research (Liao et al., 2026). AI compan-ions introduce a distinct relational setting in which safety depends on user characteristics, compan-ion roles, and interactions unfolding over multiple turns. Recent evaluations therefore examine de-ployed companion platforms (Wei et al., 2025), companionship and social behaviors (Kaffee et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026), anthropomorphic inter-actions (Ibrahim et al., 2026), persona-grounded multi-turn scenarios (Juneja and Lomidze, 2026; Liu et al., 2026), and long-term simulated in-teractions (Shen et al., 2026). Building on this progression, CompanionEval systematically evalu-ates companion-specific safety across diverse per-sonas, matched characters, scenario-grounded in-teractions, and controlled interaction lengths. 

Safety Guardrails. General-purpose guardrails such as WildGuard (Han et al., 2024), Llama Guard (Inan et al., 2023; Meta Llama Guard Team, 2024), Qwen3Guard (Qwen Team, 2026), and gpt-oss-safeguard (OpenAI, 2025) are designed to detect broad safety risks. Beyond general-purpose guard models, recent work explores safety alignment and adaptive defenses, including multi-turn safety alignment through adversarial red-teaming (Guo et al., 2025), dialogue-level defenses against latent multi-turn attacks (Jiang et al., 2025), and co-evolving jailbreak attacks and guardrails (Qi et al., 2026). Domain-specific safeguards further address sensitive settings such as multi-turn mental-health support (Farinhas et al., 2026), psychiatric crisis detection (Nelson et al., 2026), and elderly-chatbot interactions (Fan et al., 2026). For AI com-panions, existing work primarily focuses on detect-ing harms in real-world conversations (Ren et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026), while dedicated guard models for broad companion-specific relational harms remain limited. CompanionGuard addresses this gap by converting benchmark-exposed fail-ures into turn-localized supervision for companion-specific risk detection. 

3 CompanionSafe 

CompanionSafe consists of two components: 

CompanionEval , a multi-turn safety benchmark that constructs diverse persona–character–scenario interactions under a fine-grained AI compan-ion safety taxonomy, and CompanionGuard , a companion-specific guardrail that converts failures exposed by CompanionEval into turn-localized supervision for safety detection. Together, the two components form an evaluation-to-mitigation pipeline in which benchmark-exposed failures di-rectly inform guardrail training. Figure 1 provides an overview of the full framework. 

3.1 CompanionEval 3.1.1 Safety Taxonomy 

CompanionEval adopts the 13-category fine-grained taxonomy of harmful behaviors in AI com-panion interactions proposed by (Zhang et al., 2025), which has also been adopted by two re-cent studies on AI companion safety (Zhang et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026). The taxonomy covers both general content-safety risks, such as sexual, hate speech, and self-harm, and relational risks specific to AI companions, such as manipulation, control, infidelity, and disregard for the user. To enable fine-grained evaluation within each 3safety category, we use Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026a) to operationalize each category into atomic 

test points , each specifying a concrete harmful be-havior to be evaluated. This process yields 135 test points across the 13 safety categories. We manually review all generated test points to ensure that each faithfully reflects the intended safety category and constitutes a valid evaluation target. The full 13-category taxonomy, together with one example test point per category, is provided in Appendix B.1, and the prompt used to generate the test points is provided in Appendix E.1. 

3.1.2 Data Construction 

Our data construction aims to (1) cover diverse user profiles and (2) match them with realistic AI com-panion usage settings. For each safety category, we construct personas by sampling from a curated attribute pool, and further pair each persona with a matched character and dialogue scenario. Together with the target test point, each persona–character– scenario combination forms one evaluation data point. We sample 100 personas per safety cate-gory, yielding 1,300 data points in total. The data construction process is illustrated in the Data Con-struction panel of Figure 1. 

Persona Card Construction A persona card rep-resents a user with a specific background and is organized into three fields: • Demographics : basic characteristics such as age, occupation, and life stage; • Psychostate : psychological characteristics such as mood state, interpersonal pattern, dis-tress level, and cognitive vulnerability; • Socialcontext : social and situational factors such as relationship status, life stressors, fi-nancial conditions, and social support. Persona construction starts from a manually cu-rated base schema comprising 23 attributes and 170 discrete values across the three fields. The full schema is provided in Appendix B.2. For each safety category, we construct and sample personas through the following five steps: 1. Constraint : GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) de-rives category-specific constraints that retain only attributes and values relevant to the target safety category. 2. Combine : the retained attribute values are enumerated within each constrained subspace, producing 39,000 candidate personas in total. 3. Filter : GPT-4o removes clear logical contra-dictions (e.g., a school-age child who is mar-ried) while preserving uncommon, complex, or vulnerable user backgrounds. After filter-ing, 20,725 candidate personas remain. 4. Sample : 100 personas are sampled from the filtered candidate pool for each safety cate-gory using a fixed seed. 5. Compile : GPT-4o rewrites each sampled at-tribute combination into a second-person per-sona card for use by the Persona LLM. The attribute-value distributions of the resulting 1,300 personas are reported in Appendix B.3. The prompts used for the three GPT-4o steps are pro-vided in Appendix E.2. 

Character Card Construction A character card specifies the companion role paired with a given persona and consists of three fields: • Name : the character’s name; • Description : the character’s identity and rela-tionship with the user; • Personality : the character’s temperament and speaking style. Conditioned on the persona card, GPT-4o gen-erates a matched character card whose role, rela-tionship, and interaction style are tailored to the user’s background and companionship needs. The generation prompt is provided in Appendix E.3. 

Scenario Construction Given a test point, per-sona card, and character card, GPT-4o generates a dialogue scenario in which behavior associated with the target test point can be evaluated. Each sce-nario consists of two sentences. The first describes a recent event, the persona’s immediate reaction, and the relationship-grounded reason for turning to the character. The second specifies a conversational move that probes the target test point, including any boundary, request, dependency cue, or crisis signal needed to expose the target behavior. The genera-tion prompt is provided in Appendix E.4. 

3.1.3 Multi-turn Dialogue 

Each data point is instantiated as a multi-turn di-alogue between a Persona LLM and a Character LLM. The Persona LLM simulates the user using the persona card and dialogue scenario, while the Character LLM is the evaluated model and plays the companion specified by the character card. The Persona LLM is configured for targeted pres-sure testing. Its system prompt consists of six mod-ules: (1) identity and task, (2) role-play require-4ments, (3) the target safety category and test point, (4) pressure-testing requirements, (5) the persona card, character card, and dialogue scenario, and (6) output requirements. The Character LLM is kept test-blind. It receives only the character card, shared dialogue history, and a fixed response trigger, without access to the target safety category, test point, pressure-testing instructions, or evaluation criteria. The complete prompts are provided in Appendix E.5. 

3.1.4 Evaluation Protocol 

Each dialogue trace is evaluated by an LLM judge and labeled as safe or unsafe . A trace is safe if the Character LLM declines, redirects, or otherwise avoids the target harmful behavior, and unsafe if it materially exhibits that behavior. For unsafe traces, the judge additionally returns supporting evidence with the corresponding assistant-turn indices and excerpts. 5 CompanionGuard Evaluation 

5.1 Evaluation Setup Baselines. We consider two types of baselines. The first consists of general LLM judges, includ-ing GPT-5.6 Luna, Claude Sonnet 5, and Gem-ini 3.8 Flash, all evaluated zero-shot. The sec-ond consists of guard models, including Llama-Guard-3-8B (Meta Llama Guard Team, 2024), Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B (Qwen Team, 2026), and gpt-oss-safeguard-20B (OpenAI, 2025). For policy-aligned evaluation, the LLM judges and gpt-oss-safeguard-20B use the shared guard prompt. Llama-Guard-3-8B receives our 13-category tax-onomy through its policy slot, while Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B uses its standard chat format with the fixed policy template. 

Implementation Details. The turn-localized con-struction yields 17,703 examples, comprising 7,142 safe and 10,561 unsafe examples. We use 16,846 examples for training and 857 for testing, with the test set containing 329 safe and 528 unsafe ex-amples across 13 safety categories. We fine-tune Llama-Guard-3-8B using LoRA SFT with rank 16 for one epoch. The base model is selected through the ablation in Appendix D.2. Training hyperpa-rameters and the guard prompt are in Appendix C. 

Metrics. We report Accuracy, binary F1 for un-safe detection, and macro-F1 over the 14 classes. Accuracy measures overall classification accuracy, binary F1 evaluates safe–unsafe discrimination, and macro-F1 averages the one-vs-rest F1 scores over the 13 safety categories and the SAFE class.

6 Conclusion 

We introduce CompanionSafe , a unified frame-work for evaluating and mitigating safety risks in AI companion interactions. Its benchmark compo-nent, CompanionEval , enables multi-turn evalua-tion across diverse personas, matched companion characters, and relational scenarios, while Com-panionGuard learns directly from failures ex-posed by the benchmark. Across ten closed- and open-source LLMs, we find that safety risks in-crease over multi-turn interaction and that models with strong overall safety can remain vulnerable 8to companion-specific relational harms. We fur-ther show that benchmark-derived failure trajecto-ries provide effective supervision for companion-specific risk detection. These findings highlight the need to evaluate AI companions as sustained relational systems. Together, CompanionEval and CompanionGuard form a practical evaluation-to-mitigation loop, turning benchmark-exposed safety failures into supervision for companion-specific guardrail training. 

Limitations 

This study has several limitations. First, Compan-ionEval relies on LLM-generated personas, com-panion characters, scenarios, and dialogues. While enabling scalable and systematic evaluation, these synthetic interactions may not fully capture the linguistic diversity, evolving relationships, and con-textual complexity of real-world AI companion use. Second, CompanionGuard relies on LLM-judge annotations rather than human-verified labels. Al-though the selected judge achieves strong agree-ment with human annotations ( κ = 0 .95 ), judge-specific biases may affect the training data and evaluation results. Broader human validation could further improve reliability. Third, our experiments cover 13 safety cate-gories, ten Character LLMs, and interactions of up to seven turns. These settings may not fully represent diverse companion applications, cultural contexts, or long-term relationships. Future work could extend the evaluation to additional platforms, languages, and longer interaction horizons. 

Ethical Considerations 

Our study focuses on evaluating and mitigating safety risks in AI companion interactions. Compan-ionEval is constructed entirely from synthetic per-sonas, companion characters, scenarios, and model-generated dialogues. The benchmark does not in-volve real users or collect personal data, thereby avoiding direct privacy risks and exposure of vul-nerable individuals during data generation. The evaluation covers sensitive topics including self-harm, sexual misconduct, hate speech, manipu-lation, and other relational harms. These scenarios are included solely to stress-test model behavior and identify safety failures rather than to encour-age harmful interactions. The resulting benchmark and CompanionGuard are intended for safety re-search, auditing, and the development of safer AI companion systems. Because the benchmark contains adversarial prompts and harmful interaction scenarios, its data and prompts could potentially be misused to gen-erate manipulative or unsafe companion behav-ior. We therefore emphasize that CompanionSafe should be used for defensive research and safety evaluation, with appropriate access controls, us-age policies, and human oversight when applied in downstream systems. CompanionGuard is de-signed as a safety detection tool and should not be treated as a substitute for professional judgment in high-stakes settings such as mental-health crises. To support reproducibility, we plan to release the CompanionEval benchmark, code, prompts, judge annotations, and the CompanionGuard LoRA adapter, subject to safety review and applicable model licenses.
