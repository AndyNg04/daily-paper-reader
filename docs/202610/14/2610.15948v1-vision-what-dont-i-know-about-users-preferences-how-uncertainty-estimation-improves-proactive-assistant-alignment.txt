## Abstract 

Proactive Smart Assistants (PSAs) increasingly use general knowl-edge, learned patterns from group-level data, patterns from individ-ual user’s data, and recent sensor observations to anticipate which action a user may prefer. Existing systems typically select actions from predicted user preference likelihoods, but these likelihoods alone do not reveal how reliable the prediction is, which evidence supports it, or why ambiguity remains. In this vision paper, we argue that uncertainty estimation can provide this missing infor-mation and substantially change appropriate downstream behavior. Using the example of a home assistant, we introduce the questions that uncertainty estimation aims to answer, i.e. how sure is the assistant about the predicted preference likelihood, whether the confidence come from group-level assumptions or individual’s data, and whether likelihood ambiguity reflect the user’s intrinsic pref-erence variability or insufficient data for the particular situation. We further introduce uncertainty estimation applications in neigh-boring domains, such as human trajectory prediction, personalized activity recognition, information-collection, and model-based con-trol. Finally, we draw on insights from neighboring domains to envision how future uncertainty-aware proactive assistants may address key challenges in proactive interaction.

## 2 Applied Uncertainty Estimation In Neighboring Domains 

Several neighboring domains face a problem similar to that of proac-tive smart assistants: predicting human behavior from noisy, partial, and context-dependent observations. Research on pedestrian and vehicle trajectory forecasting has particularly emphasized the mul-timodality of human behavior. Social GAN [15] generates multiple socially plausible future trajectories instead of committing to a single deterministic prediction. Trajectron++ [27] further demon-strates that probabilistic human-behavior prediction can integrate heterogeneous evidence, including past trajectories, agent interac-tions, semantic maps, and the planned behavior of the observing system. Driving behavior prediction methods such as MultiPath 

[10] separates uncertainty over discrete behavioral modes from un-certainty within each mode by predicting probabilities over anchor trajectories together with a continuous distribution around each anchor. Beyond uncertainty associated with an individual, Tang et al. [28] introduce collaborative uncertainty to capture additional uncertainty induced by interactions and correlations among multi-ple agents. These works demonstrate the importance of predicting a distribution over possible human behaviors; however, such a dis-tribution does not necessarily indicate how reliably its probabilities have been estimated. Other work explicitly models different sources of uncertainty. Most closely related to our proposed evidence-source analysis, Itk-ina and Kochenderfer [21] attribute trajectory-prediction uncer-tainty to interpretable input concepts, including past agent behav-ior, road structure, and social context. An et al. [4] propose the Compressed Data Representation Model to disentangle aleatoric uncertainty arising from inherent system randomness from epis-temic uncertainty arising from insufficient data. Together, these works suggest that a PSA should not report only an aggregate confi-dence value: it should identify both which evidence sources support a prediction and whether the remaining uncertainty reflects gen-uine variability or insufficient knowledge about the individual and current context. Human-motion prediction and wearable activity recognition fur-ther distinguish uncertainty caused by limited knowledge from variability inherent in observed human behavior. Xu et al. [32] ex-plicitly separate epistemic uncertainty, which indicates unfamiliar observations or insufficient model knowledge, from heteroscedastic aleatoric uncertainty associated with variation in possible human motions. In wearable activity recognition, Akbari and Jafari [1] use epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty to identify informative exam-ples for personalizing a population-trained model to a new user. 

Huo et al. [20] use uncertainty quantification to identify unknown contexts in mobile activity recognition, recognizing that behavior observed in one situation may not generalize reliably to a new context. These results are relevant to PSAs initialized from group-level data: uncertainty can indicate that a prediction is supported primarily by a population model and that additional individual or situation-specific evidence is needed. Uncertainty estimation can influence downstream information gathering and action selection. Uncertainty can directly constrain downstream actions: CLUE [3, 12] incorporates epistemic uncer-tainty into model-predictive control by filtering highly uncertain action trajectories, favoring actions with both high utility and How Uncertainty Estimation Improves Proactive Assistant Alignment UbiComp Companion ’26, October 11–15, 2026, Shanghai, China 

confidence, and reverting to a reliable fallback controller when no sufficiently safe action is available. Ardywibowo et al. [5] use activity-prediction entropy to adaptively select sensor inputs while balancing uncertainty reduction against sensing costs. TIME [19] similarly uses periods in which the system is not mission-critical to select exploratory actions that balance expected information gain against cost. Dixit et al. [13] demonstrate how adaptively cal-ibrated uncertainty sets for human or agent trajectories can be passed directly to a downstream planner, allowing system behavior to respond to changes in prediction reliability. Analogously, a PSA could use the source and type of its preference uncertainty to decide whether to initiate a reversible action, continue observing, elicit the user’s preference, or remain silent. Finally, Learning Progress Monitoring [18] shows that exploration based on raw prediction error can become fixated on irreducible ran-domness; instead, its method rewards improvements in the learned model, prioritizing observations from which the agent can still learn. Following that intuition, a PSA should gather additional evidence when its uncertainty is reducible, but should not repeatedly query or stay silent and observe the user when the remaining ambiguity reflects genuine preference variability. 

## 3 Envisioning Future Uncertainty-Aware Proactive Assistants 

The neighboring domains reviewed above suggest a broader vi-sion for how uncertainty could shape future proactive assistants. Rather than treating uncertainty as an additional confidence value attached to a predicted action, future PSAs may use it to decide how to participate in an interaction: whether to act, suggest, ask, wait, or remain silent. This vision extends longstanding ideas in mixed-initiative interaction, in which intelligent systems consider the costs of acting, interrupting, and requesting clarification [17], as well as human–AI interaction guidelines that encourage systems to limit automation when uncertain and support efficient correc-tion [2]. Uncertainty estimation could make these principles more actionable by revealing not only that an assistant is uncertain, but also what remains unknown and whether further interaction is likely to resolve it. 

Initiative that adapts to the source of uncertainty. Work on selective prediction and uncertainty-aware control often frames uncertainty as a choice between acting and deferring [3, 7, 13]. Future PSAs could support a richer range of responses. An assistant might directly perform a low-risk action, offer a suggestion without executing it, ask a clarifying question, continue observing, select a reversible alternative, or remain silent. Importantly, these responses need not depend only on the pre-dicted preference likelihood. Two actions with the same predicted likelihood may warrant different forms of initiative when one pre-diction is supported by extensive individual history and the other is inferred mainly from population-level patterns. A home assis-tant, for example, might automatically adjust lighting when it has repeatedly observed the user’s preference in the same context, but merely suggest the adjustment when encountering that situation for the first time. In this way, uncertainty-aware initiative could help PSAs remain useful without treating every plausible prediction as sufficient justification for intervention.

Active learning research shows that uncertainty can help systems identify observations that are particularly useful for personalization [1, 5]. In future PSAs, this idea could influence not only whether the assistant asks a question, but also the content of that question. Different knowledge gaps may naturally lead to different forms of interaction. When a prediction relies mainly on a population prior, the assistant might ask about a general preference: ‘Do you usually want the blinds closed when the afternoon sun reaches your desk?” When the user’s general preference is already known but the current situation is ambiguous, it might instead ask a contextual question: ‘Are you working on the screen right now?” If the uncertainty comes from a temporarily unreliable sensor, waiting for another observation may be more appropriate than interrupting the user. Such assistants could make preference elicitation feel less like re-peated confirmation and more like a purposeful conversation. They might also allow users to choose how and when personalization occurs, for example by setting an asking budget, postponing ques-tions, or identifying situations in which the assistant should not interrupt. The value of information would therefore be considered together with the cost of obtaining it from the user. 

Recognition that not every preference must become pre-dictable. Human-motion prediction and uncertainty-aware learn-ing distinguish uncertainty caused by insufficient knowledge from variability that is inherent in the observed behavior [4, 32]. A fu-ture PSA could similarly recognize that inconsistent choices do not always indicate that more personalization is needed. A user may genuinely have no fixed preference between two actions, may consider several alternatives equally acceptable, or UbiComp Companion ’26, October 11–15, 2026, Shanghai, China Zhiyu An, Ziyi Xuan, Yu Yang, and Wan Du 

may decide based on factors unavailable to the assistant. In such cases, repeatedly asking questions or collecting additional data may not produce a more stable prediction. An uncertainty-aware assistant could instead recognize that the remaining ambiguity has persisted despite observation. It might stop trying to infer a single preferred action, rotate among acceptable options, choose the most reversible alternative, or ask only when the consequences are substantial. 

Learning Progress Monitoring [18] similarly suggests that explo-ration should focus on uncertainty that is still being reduced rather than on prediction errors that remain irreducible. Applied to PSAs, this principle could help prevent personalization from becoming an endless effort to eliminate human variability. A future assistant might therefore know not only when it needs to learn more, but also when it has learned that no single stable preference exists. 

Explanations grounded in evidence provenance. Uncer-tainty attribution in trajectory prediction demonstrates that a sys-tem can identify which input sources contribute to a prediction [21]. Future PSAs could use this capability to provide explanations that describe the basis of personalization rather than presenting a generic confidence score. Instead of saying ‘I am 72% confident,” an assistant might explain, ‘I suggested this because you accepted it on the last three workdays,” ‘I have not observed what you prefer when guests are present,” or ‘This suggestion is currently based mostly on patterns from other users.” These explanations reveal what the assis-tant believes it has learned and make incorrect assumptions easier to identify. They may also expose when a recommendation depends too heavily on group-level generalizations or on observations that the user does not consider relevant. Such explanations could be paired with lightweight opportunities for correction, including ‘not true for me,” ‘only when I am alone,” or “do not use this information.” Uncertainty would then become an entry point for inspecting and repairing the assistant’s model of the user. This may be more meaningful than displaying confidence alone, particularly because uncertainty displays and AI explanations do not automatically produce appropriate trust and may sometimes increase reliance on incorrect suggestions [6–8]. 

Multi-user assistants that distinguish uncertainty from disagreement. Trajectory forecasting has introduced collaborative uncertainty to describe uncertainty arising from interactions among multiple people [28]. Shared PSAs face a related challenge. An assistant may be uncertain because it does not know one person’s preference, because it cannot determine who is present, or because it knows that different users prefer incompatible outcomes. These situations may appear similar at the prediction level but call for substantially different responses. Future multi-user PSAs could represent each affected person’s preference separately from the procedure used to reconcile those preferences. When one person’s preference is missing, the assistant might ask that person directly. When the users’ preferences conflict, it might surface the disagreement, suggest a reversible compromise, or follow a previously agreed household rule. When the assistant is uncertain about who will be affected, it might avoid acting until the relevant users are identified. This distinction could also help prevent shared assistants from treating the most frequently observed user as representative of the entire group. Existing work on collaborative assistants and interac-tive negotiation suggests that assistants can support group decisions rather than silently resolving them [24, 31, 36]. An uncertainty-aware PSA could therefore serve not only as a predictor of collective preferences, but also as a mediator that makes missing representa-tion and genuine disagreement visible. 

Uncertainty that evolves through long-term interaction. 

The uncertainty of a PSA is unlikely to remain fixed. It may decrease as the assistant observes recurring routines, increase when the user enters an unfamiliar situation, and change when preferences shift over time. A future uncertainty-aware assistant could therefore treat personalization as an ongoing interaction rather than a one-time learning phase. Its behavior might become more autonomous in familiar, low-risk situations while remaining cautious in new or consequential contexts. It could recognize when an established pattern is no longer reliable and temporarily return to suggesting or asking rather than acting. It might also remember which questions have already been answered, avoiding repeated requests unless there is evidence that the user’s preference has changed. This longitudinal perspective broadens the evaluation of uncer-tainty aware PSAs beyond predictive calibration alone. The quality of such an assistant would also be reflected in the number and tim-ing of its interruptions, the consequences of incorrect actions, the ease with which users can repair its assumptions, its improvement after feedback, and its treatment of changing or variable preferences. Users’ understanding of when the assistant knows their preferences and when it is extrapolating would be similarly important. These concerns connect uncertainty estimation directly to established HCI questions surrounding agency, appropriateness, trust calibration, and user–system gaps [23, 25]. Together, these possibilities envision uncertainty estimation as an interaction resource rather than solely a modeling technique. A future uncertainty-aware PSA would not merely attach confidence to its predictions. It would adapt its initiative, seek information selectively, recognize irreducible preference variability, reveal the evidence behind its assumptions, and distinguish missing knowl-edge from interpersonal disagreement. Such capabilities could sup-port proactive assistance that is simultaneously more personalized, more understandable, and more respectful of the people whose lives it seeks to assist.
