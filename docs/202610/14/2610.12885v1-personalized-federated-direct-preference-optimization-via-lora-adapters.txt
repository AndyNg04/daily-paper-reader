Abstract 

Federated Direct Preference Optimization (FedDPO) enables privacy-preserving alignment of language models across dis-tributed agents without centralizing sensitive preference data. However, existing FedDPO analysis assumes all clients con-verge to a single shared policy, which is fundamentally at odds with the heterogeneous preference distributions present in practice. We propose Personalized FedDPO (pFedDPO) ,in which each client maintains a small private LoRA adapter atop a shared global base model. In pFedDPO, the base model is synchronized via FedAvg each round, while LoRA adapters remain local and capture client-specific preference patterns. We prove that this decomposition replaces the gradient diver-sity κ2 in the FedDPO bound with a residual heterogeneity 

κ2eff , which is no larger than κ2 when adapters absorb client-specific directions, at the cost of an additive adapter-bias term that vanishes as adapters converge. Experiments on preference datasets show that pFedDPO consistently attains a lower gra-dient norm than vanilla FedDPO while remaining competitive in training loss and per-client reward accuracy. 

Introduction 

Aligning language models (LMs) (Wang et al. 2023) with human preferences is increasingly performed via Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al. 2023), which avoids explicit reward modeling by optimizing a classification-style loss over (chosen/rejected) response pairs. As AI systems proliferate to distributed and privacy-sensitive environments, e.g., healthcare devices (Nguyen et al. 2022), personal assistants (Sa’ad, Felemban, and Tab-bakh 2025), institutional silos (Huang et al. 2023), prefer-ence data is naturally fragmented across users who cannot share their data due to regulatory constraints (HIPAA (Moore and Frye 2019), GDPR (Li, Yu, and He 2019)). This moti-vates distributed DPO, and in particular the federated and de-centralized variants recently analyzed theoretically in (Jiang 2026). A critical assumption for FedDPO in (Jiang 2026) is that all N clients are driven toward a common global policy. This is appropriate when preference distributions are homo-geneous, but unrealistic in practice: a medical professional

in an ICU and a casual user evaluating restaurant suggestions have genuinely different preferences that should not be aver-aged away. The standard FedDPO convergence bound con-tains an irreducible floor proportional to gradient diversity (κ2), which captures exactly this inter-client heterogeneity and cannot be reduced by communicating more or training longer, for a fixed step size. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and LoRA (Hu et al. 2022) in particular, offers a natural solution: decompose each client’s model into a shared global base and a small private adapter. The base model learns the shared structure of prefer-ences across all agents and is aggregated via FedAvg (Li et al. 2019), while the adapter captures client-specific deviations and is never communicated. This decomposition is the key contribution of this work, differing from the method in (Zhu et al. 2026). We make the following contributions: We propose pFed-DPO, a personalized federated DPO algorithm in which each client maintains a LoRA adapter atop a shared base model updated via FedAvg. We prove a convergence bound for pFed-DPO in which κ2 is replaced by κ2eff at the cost of an explicit adapter-bias term Cε ϕ, so the bound is tighter than vanilla FedDPO whenever the heterogeneity reduction outweighs this bias (Theorem 1). On two preference datasets, pFed-DPO consistently attains a lower gradient norm than vanilla FedDPO with competitive loss and per-client accuracy.

Experiments 

We evaluate on Stanford SHP (Esfandiari et al. 2021) (main result) and Anthropic hh-rlhf (Bai et al. 2022) (in Appendix), each with N = 5 agents holding 120 disjoint non-IID pref-erence pairs. The backbone is DistilGPT-2 ( ≈82M param-eters). LoRA targets the attention projection (“ c_attn ”) with default rank r = 8 and α = 16 (the scaling parame-ter). We compare three methods: Centralized DPO (pooled data), Vanilla FedDPO (full model aggregation, S = 5 ), and pFedDPO (base + private LoRA, S = 5 ). All methods use AdamW ( η = 10 −4), β = 0 .2, gradient clipping at 1.0, batch size 4, E = 3 local steps, and R = 80 communication rounds. A full list is in Appendix.

As shown in Figure 5(a), pFedDPO consistently achieves a lower gradi-ent norm than vanilla FedDPO across all 80 rounds, with the gap largest in the later rounds as adapters accumulate client-specific knowledge. Figure 5(b) shows the empirical κ2 mea-sured every 10 rounds on pFedDPO’s base model gradients. Starting from ∼1500 at round 0, κ2 drops sharply to ∼350 by round 10 and continues to decrease gradually to approxi-mately 150–200 by round 70. Related Work 

DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023) and its extensions (Amini, Vieira, and Cotterell 2024; Park et al. 2024; Xiao et al. 2024) fo-cus on the centralized, single-model setting. FedDPO and DecDPO (decentralized DPO) were proposed and analyzed in (Jiang 2026), establishing the first convergence guaran-tees for preference optimization in distributed settings, where prior work focuses primarily on learning (Saadati et al. 2024, 2025; Esfandiari et al. 2021) and more recently agentic AI systems (Rillo et al. 2026). Our work extends this by ad-dressing the personalization gap they identify as a limitation. Existing federated DPO approaches (Spadea and Seneviratne 2025; Zhu et al. 2026) do not address this theoretical gap: they either treat all clients symmetrically without personal-ization (Spadea and Seneviratne 2025) or introduce person-alization empirically without bounding how it improves con-vergence (Zhu et al. 2026). Personalized federated learning with LoRA has been studied in (Yi et al. 2023; Qi et al. 2024; Song et al. 2026; Gu et al. 2026; Lee et al. 2026), but not in the context of preference optimization, where the trajectory-level DPO objective introduces additional variance and het-erogeneity that require explicit analysis. We refer interested readers to Appendix for more related work and include a methodological comparison table. 

Conclusion 

We propose pFedDPO, a personalized federated DPO frame-work where each client trains a private LoRA adapter on top of a shared global base model. The base model is synchro-nized across clients each round, while adapters remain local, preserving personalization without increasing communica-tion compared with standard FedDPO. Our analysis shows this design better handles client heterogeneity while intro-ducing only a small bias that diminishes over training. Em-pirically, pFedDPO consistently lowers the gradient norm relative to vanilla FedDPO while keeping preference accu-racy competitive across all clients.

FedDPO (Jiang 2026) establishes the first convergence guar-antees for distributed DPO under full and partial partici-pation, staleness, and decentralized topologies, but targets a single shared policy and does not support personalization. Its bound contains an irreducible κ2 floor that our work directly reduces. Spadea and Seneviratne (Spadea and Seneviratne 2025) compare Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO) and DPO in a federated LLM fine-tuning setting using Alpaca-7B. They show that KTO outperforms DPO across MT-Bench-1, Vicuna, and AdvBench benchmarks, particularly under re-distributed (non-paired) data where DPO cannot be applied. Their work is purely empirical with no convergence analysis, and does not address personalization: all clients train toward a single global model. The key contribution is demonstrating KTO’s resilience to non-IID data due to its single-response feedback requirement, which sidesteps the paired-response constraint of DPO. FedPDPO (Zhu et al. 2026) uses a globally shared LoRA adapter with a personalized client-specific LLM head, a client-specific explicit reward head, and a bottleneck adapter to balance global and local features. Its theory is described as establishing a probabilistic foundation and soundness. It transmits shared LoRA updates of dimension O(r(m + n)) 

per client, achieving up to 4.80% average accuracy improve-ment. However, its adapters are globally shared rather than private, and its theory does not provide a rate-explicit bound relating personalization to κ2. pFedDPO makes the opposite trade-off: full d-dimensional base updates (same volume as vanilla FedDPO) in exchange for fully private adapters and an explicit convergence guarantee. The key differentiator of pFedDPO is therefore with a rate-explicit convergence bound that makes the effect of person-alization explicit. Unlike FedPDPO, pFedDPO never trans-mits adapter weights, keeping all personalization parameters on-device, at a higher upload volume than FedPDPO. Un-like Spadea and Seneviratne’s work (Spadea and Seneviratne 2025), pFedDPO explicitly personalizes to each client’s pref-erence distribution. Please see Table 1 for methodological comparison. 

Discussion on pFedDPO 

Figure 1 shows the specific detail of how pFedDPO works schematically. A closely related line of work (Cho et al. 2024; Saadati et al. 2025; Kuo et al. 2024; Sun et al. 2024; Yi et al. 2023; Bian et al. 2025, 2026; Xie et al. 2026) applies parameter-efficient federated fine-tuning by training LoRA adapters locally and periodically aggregating or averaging those adapters across clients. In contrast, pFedDPO inten-tionally keeps LoRA adapters private and only synchronizes the shared base model . This design is motivated by the nature of preference alignment, where client heterogeneity reflects genuine differences in user values, style, or alignment objec-tives rather than noise to be averaged away. When LoRA adapters are shared globally, they must si-multaneously encode both: 

• task-level adaptation of the shared model, and 

• client-specific preference information. Under heterogeneous preference distributions, these objec-tives can conflict. Averaging adapters across clients may therefore dilute personalized signals, causing preference in-terference : updates that improve alignment for one client can harm another. This is especially pronounced in DPO, where gradients are driven by pairwise preference comparisons and are highly sensitive to local preference distributions. pFed-DPO avoids this by separating these roles: 

• the base model captures globally transferable alignment knowledge shared across clients; 

• the private LoRA adapter captures client-specific residual preferences. This decomposition makes the global model responsible for learning common alignment structure, while the adapters ab-sorb personalization. As a result, the server never aggregates incompatible preference-specific parameters. A second advantage is privacy. LoRA adapters trained on preference data can encode highly sensitive information about user behavior, style, or preference distributions. Up-loading them, even though lightweight, may leak personal-ization signals. Keeping adapters local provides a stronger privacy boundary than methods that transmit adapters to the server. From an optimization perspective, private adapters also reduce effective client drift. Since some heterogeneity is absorbed locally, the shared model sees less conflicting gradient signal during aggregation, which leads to more sta-ble federated optimization. This is reflected in our analysis through a smaller effective heterogeneity term relative to vanilla FedDPO (Jiang 2026). Finally, communication re-mains unchanged relative to standard FedDPO: only updates to the shared base model are transmitted. Compared with adapter-sharing methods, pFedDPO therefore offers a better tradeoff between: 

• global coordination (via shared base model), 

• local personalization (via private adapters), 

• privacy preservation, and 

• robustness to preference heterogeneity.

Intuition 

By (3), each client’s adapter is a private, low-rank shift of the shared weights. In vanilla FedDPO, every client pushes the shared base in its own direction, and these directions partially cancel under FedAvg, leaving the diversity κ2.In pFedDPO, each client first moves its private shift until the loss can no longer be reduced within the adapter’s tangent space (Lemma 1). Whatever part of the client’s disagreement with the population can be expressed there is resolved locally and never reaches the server. What the client uploads is the remainder: the part of its gradient orthogonal to that space, plus non-adapted parameters. The adapter thus acts as a client-specific pre-filter on the base gradient. The filter removes heterogeneity only where it can express it: inside the adapted modules ( κ2

> A

) and inside its rank budget ( κ2

> ∥

). It removes it cleanly only when clients’ filters are roughly aligned and the curvature does not leak the shift back into the orthogonal complement (small δ).

Datasets. Stanford Human Preferences (SHP) (Ethayarajh, Choi, and Swayamdipta 2021) contains about 385K pairs of human-written Reddit comments answering the same post, drawn from 18 subject-area subreddits; comment A is la-beled preferred over B if it received a higher score despite being posted later, so labels reflect the collective votes of Reddit users rather than designated annotators. Anthropic hh-rlhf (Bai et al. 2022) contains multi-turn human–assistant dialogues in which a crowdworker chose the more helpful (helpfulness subsets) or less harmful (harmlessness subset) of two model responses; the chosen and rejected dialogues share the conversation history and differ in the final assistant turn. For each dataset we form N = 5 agents with 120 dis-joint pairs each. Sequences are truncated to 64 tokens, and reward accuracy is computed on training pairs of each agent. 

SHP Table. Table 3 summarizes round-80 results on SHP. Centralized DPO has the highest gradient norm and low-est accuracy, reflecting its smaller per-round gradient work in this (non-compute-matched) regime. Vanilla FedDPO at-tains the lowest training loss (0.05) and highest reward ac-curacy (0.82). pFedDPO attains the lowest gradient norm (0.02), with training loss (0.06) and accuracy (0.80) close to vanilla FedDPO. Its measured base-gradient diversity falls from ∼1500 to ∼150–200. As discussed in the above on measuring κ2 and κ2eff , this trajectory alone does not sepa-rate the adapter effect from training progress.

Limitations 

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The theoretical analysis inherits the log-linear softmax policy assumptions of (Jiang 2026). Extending the bounds to transformer archi-tectures requires controlling Fisher information spectra and is left for future work. Theorem 11 further assumes that each local loss is μϕ-PL in the adapter and that the minimizing low-rank shift is unique, which are strong for bilinear LoRA parameterizations, and κ2eff ≤ κ2 is established only to first order under a shared-subspace condition. The constant C in the adapter-bias term Cε ϕ is not available in closed form in terms of E, η, and local curvature; such an expression would let practitioners choose E so that the bound is guaranteed to improve on vanilla FedDPO. pFedDPO does not reduce communication relative to vanilla FedDPO as both transmit 

d-dimensional base updates, whereas FedPDPO (Zhu et al. 2026) transmits only shared low-rank modules, which may be preferable under tight bandwidth. A variant that transmits low-rank updates while keeping adapters private is an open direction. Empirically, we use DistilGPT-2 ( ≈82M parame-ters), 120 randomly partitioned pairs per agent, a single seed for ablations, and report accuracy on training pairs; scaling to 7B+ models, domain-based partitions, held-out evaluation, and multiple seeds are important future work. The rank ab-lations show that the best rank is dataset-dependent. Finally, pFedDPO assumes synchronous updates, and extending the asynchronous staleness analysis of ((Jiang 2026), Thm. 5.4) to private adapters warrants dedicated analysis. 

Ethics Statement 

Federated DPO, including pFedDPO, is designed to enable preference-based alignment of language models in privacy-preserving settings where raw data cannot be centralized. We identify the following ethical dimensions. 

Privacy and data minimization. pFedDPO transmits only base model parameter updates, not raw preference data or LoRA adapters. This avoids sharing raw data and adapter weights, although full base-model updates are higher-dimensional than the low-rank updates shared by adapter-sharing methods and may leak more information. However, model updates can still leak information about local data through gradient inversion attacks. Combining pFedDPO with differential privacy mechanisms is a natural extension that we do not implement here due to the interaction between DP noise and the gradient diversity bound, which warrants dedicated analysis. 

Preference heterogeneity and value plurality. A central motivation for personalization is that different users have genuinely different preferences, and a single global policy may suppress minority preferences by averaging toward the majority. pFedDPO mitigates this by allowing each client’s adapter to capture deviations from the shared base, poten-tially preserving value diversity. At the same time, private adapters could also encode harmful or biased preferences that would be moderated by global averaging. Adversarial clients and Byzantine robustness. pFedDPO, like FedDPO, is vulnerable to clients that submit mali-cious base model updates to steer the global model. Private adapters cannot be inspected by the server, which may en-able model poisoning through the base model update while concealing the intent in the local adapter. Robust aggregation methods should be considered in adversarial settings. 

Scope of application. Our experiments use two open aca-demic preference datasets (hh-rlhf, SHP) for research valida-tion. Deployment of pFedDPO in production systems, par-ticularly in high-stakes domains such as healthcare or legal advice, requires additional safeguards including human over-sight of alignment targets, regular evaluation against safety benchmarks, and mechanisms for clients to audit or contest the preference optimization process.
