Abstract —Users often exhibit changes in interaction behavior before explicitly seeking assistance, enabling systems to anticipate future assistance use. This is especially important in high-stakes environments, where delayed assistance can have operational, financial, or safety consequences. However, conventional Large Language Models (LLMs) are often unsuitable for continuous on-device behavioral prediction because of their computational and memory demands. Behavioral patterns are also highly individual and device-specific, motivating local processing that preserves privacy and data sovereignty. Small Language Models (SLMs) offer a promising alternative, but their ability to reason reliably over low-level sequential interaction events remains underexplored. This study introduces Evidence-Grounded Local Behavioral Prediction (ELBP), a four-layer architecture com-bining deterministic interaction processing, dual-view behavioral representation, SLM-based prediction, and evidence verification with abstention. Three instruction-tuned models of 3B parame-ters or fewer were evaluated using raw, structured, dual-view, and complete ELBP configurations. The best-performing model, Qwen2.5-3B with ELBP, achieved a full-set F1-score of 0.80 and PR-AUC of 0.84, representing a 15.9% relative improvement over raw-event prompting and 17.6% over XGBoost, while full-set ECE decreased by 43.2% relative to raw-event prompting. With evidence verification and abstention, selective F1 increased to 0.84 at 91% coverage and evidence support reached 96%. These results show that structured behavioral representation and evidence-verified selective prediction can improve the reliability of behavioral forecasting in SLMs. 

Index Terms —local large language models, behavioral predic-tion, help-seeking prediction, interaction logs, evidence ground-ing, behavioral representation, adaptive systems 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Users often change their interaction behavior before ex-plicitly requesting assistance. These changes may precede subsequent assistance-seeking behavior, allowing systems to anticipate future assistance use. Prior educational data-mining studies show that students’ initial problem-solving attempts and patterns of hint use can predict subsequent assistance-seeking behavior [1]. Large-scale datasets such as EdNet further demonstrate the value of sequential interaction traces for examining how earlier actions relate to later help-seeking events [2]. This capability is particularly important in high-stakes environments, where delayed assistance may have op-erational, financial, or safety consequences. Recent research has explored Large Language Models (LLMs) for modeling user behavior from previous interaction histories [3]. However, conventional LLMs are often impractical for continuous on-device behavioral prediction because of their computational and memory requirements [5]–[7]. Behavioral patterns are also highly individual and device-specific, creating a need for local processing that can maintain device-level behavioral profiles while supporting privacy and data sovereignty [8]. Small Language Models (SLMs) therefore provide a promising alternative for local behavioral prediction. However, their abil-ity to reason reliably over long, low-level sequential interaction events remains underexplored. Prediction quality can also de-pend strongly on how interaction histories are represented [4]. Structured behavioral representations and verification mech-anisms may therefore help smaller models identify relevant patterns, ground predictions in observed evidence, and abstain when evidence is insufficient [9], [10]. This study therefore aims to develop and evaluate an 

Evidence-Grounded Local Behavioral Prediction (ELBP) ar-chitecture for predicting future assistance use from sequential interaction traces using SLMs. The study addresses the following research questions:  

> •

RQ1: How accurately can local SLMs predict future assistance use from recent interaction histories?  

> •

RQ2: To what extent do structured behavioral repre-sentation and evidence verification improve prediction reliability over direct reasoning over raw interaction logs for SLMs? The contribution of this study is a four-layer local SLM architecture that combines deterministic behavioral process-ing, dual-view interaction representation, evidence-grounded prediction, and post-prediction verification with abstention to improve SLM reasoning over raw interaction logs. II. R ELATED WORK 

Prior assistance prediction studies have mainly relied on conventional models and engineered behavioral features. Beck 

et al. predicted learners’ help requests in an intelligent reading tutor [11], while Castro et al. used previous attempts and hint histories to predict future use [1]. Recently, LLMs have been used for behavioral modeling. Nguyen et al. showed that LLMs can use observed interaction histories to model sub-sequent student behavior [3]. Ober et al. further showed that LLMs could be used to identify assistance use and persistence arXiv:2610.32193v1 [cs.AI] 23 Oct 2026 indicators from learner dialogues [12]. However, these studies do not examine local SLMs for short-horizon prediction from low-level interaction events. Representation is equally impor-tant. Krishnamurthy et al. showed that summarized interaction histories can substantially affect LLM decision behavior [4], while structured semantic processing and validation have im-proved reliability in local LLM pipelines [9]. Edge and on-device LLM research further identifies computation, memory, latency, and privacy as key deployment concerns [5]–[7], with local processing offering additional privacy benefits for sensi-tive data [8]. Verification is also important because language-model predictions may be weakly supported by evidence; structured validation can reduce unsupported outputs [9], [10]. To solve this, ELBP integrates these directions through local SLMs, structured behavioral representations, evidence ground-ing, and abstention to predict near-term assistance-seeking behavior. III. E VIDENCE -G ROUNDED LOCAL BEHAVIORAL 

PREDICTION ARCHITECTURE 

This study proposes Evidence-Grounded Local Behavioral Prediction (ELBP), a four-layer architecture for forecasting near-term user behavior from sequential interaction traces us-ing locally deployed SLMs. The design is primarily motivated by SLMs’ reasoning capacity and limited context budgets. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the architecture separates deterministic in-teraction processing and behavioral representation from SLM-based prediction and evidence verification. This separation is intentional: low-level operations such as event ordering, temporal calculations, aggregation, and feature construction are performed deterministically before SLM inference. The local SLM is therefore used primarily for interpreting behavioral patterns and forecasting the target behavior, rather than for data cleaning or arithmetic operations. The final layer evaluates whether a prediction is supported by the behavioral evidence supplied to the model and permits abstention when sufficient support is unavailable. The archi-tecture is designed around three principles: local inference ,

evidence-grounded prediction , and selective decision-making .Local inference enables behavioral traces to remain within local or organizational infrastructure and reduces dependence on cloud-based services. Evidence grounding makes the basis of each prediction inspectable, while selective decision-making prevents the system from being forced to issue a prediction when the available behavioral evidence is insufficient or con-tradictory. 

A. Layer 1: Interaction Processing 

The first layer converts raw interaction traces into con-sistent and interpretable behavioral observations. Raw event logs typically represent individual system actions rather than complete behavioral episodes. Consequently, directly provid-ing such logs to an SLM would require the model to si-multaneously perform event grouping, temporal arithmetic, sequence interpretation, and behavioral reasoning, imposing substantial reasoning demands on the SLM. The objective of Layer 1 is therefore to transform low-level interaction events into reliable behavioral observations while preserving their temporal ordering and provenance. The processing includes chronological ordering, grouping of related events, calculation of temporal intervals, identification of repeated or revised actions, normalization of event labels, treatment of missing values, and derivation of task-level outcomes. For example, a raw sequence containing an activity entry, multiple responses, and a submission can be converted into an interaction-level record containing the final response, number of revisions, elapsed response time, and observed outcome. The transformation is deterministic and does not involve SLM inference. Layer 1 is implemented in Python. Libraries such as pandas , NumPy , and standard JSON-processing utilities were utilized for event reconstruction and behavioral feature extraction. PyArrow was used for large event collections and column-oriented processing. 

B. Layer 2: Behavior Representation 

As seen in Fig. 2, Layer 2 converts the processed interaction history into a dual-view representation suitable for local-SLM reasoning. The central design assumption is that chronological event sequences and structured behavioral summaries provide complementary information. The chronological representation preserves when and in what order behavioral events oc-curred, whereas the structured representation explicitly ex-poses higher-level patterns that an SLM would otherwise need to calculate internally. For user u at prediction point t, Layer 2 produces    

> Ru,t = ( Eu,t , S u,t ),(1)

where Eu,t denotes the chronological evidence representation and Su,t denotes the structured behavioral summary. 

1) Chronological Evidence Representation: The chronolog-ical view preserves the temporal sequence of observations. Each observation is assigned a unique evidence identifier, allowing later SLM predictions to refer explicitly to the events used as justification. A sequence may represent increasing response duration, repeated response revisions, or changes to assistance use. The event-level representation enables the SLM to reason about trajectories and transitions that may disappear when behavior is represented only by aggregate statistics. Such patterns include repeated failures, increasing hesitation, recovery following difficulty, and transitions from stable to unstable interaction behavior. 

2) Structured Behavioral Summary: The second view sum-marizes the same interaction window using deterministic behavioral variables. Candidate summary variables include recent performance rate, consecutive unsuccessful outcomes, mean response duration, response-duration trend, number of response revisions, number of retries or reversals, and previous assistance-seeking behavior. The summary provides an explicit abstraction of the event sequence and reduces the amount of low-level computation required. This is particularly relevant for an SLM because of its limited reasoning capacity and context budgets. Fig. 1. Evidence-Grounded Local Behavioral Prediction Architecture for Assistance Use

C. Layer 3: Local SLM Behavior Prediction 

Layer 3 constitutes the main reasoning component of ELBP. A locally deployed SLM receives the dual-view representation generated by Layer 2 and forecasts the predefined near-term behavioral outcome. The model receives both the chronolog-ical evidence and the structured behavioral summary and is instructed to reason only from the supplied information. Rather than returning an unexplained class label, the model is required to output the predicted behavioral class, a confidence value, the identifiers of the evidence supporting the prediction, and a concise justification. Formally, 

fθ (Ru,t ) → (ˆ yu,t , c u,t , Z u,t , r u,t ) , (2) 

where ˆyu,t denotes the predicted behavior, cu,t is the model-reported confidence, Zu,t contains the cited evidence identi-fiers, and ru,t represents the behavioral justification. A struc-tured output takes the following form:               

> {"prediction": "YES", "confidence": 0.84, "evidence_ids": ["E3", "E5", "E8"], "reason": "Recent failures are accompanied by increasing response duration and repeated response revisions." }

Requiring evidence identifiers makes the prediction auditable and establishes an explicit connection between the model output and the supplied behavioral trace. The prediction layer is implemented via a fixed prompt executed on a local SLM. The inference framework uses llama.cpp . However, other inference frameworks such as Ollama, Hugging Face Transformers, or vLLM may be used depending on the se-lected model and hardware. The base architecture does not require model fine-tuning, allowing multiple local SLMs to be evaluated under identical prompting conditions. 

D. Layer 4: Evidence Verification and Decision 

Layer 4 separates prediction generation from prediction acceptance. Although an SLM may produce a plausible be-havioral forecast, its justification can contain unsupported, ex-aggerated, or incorrectly referenced evidence. This layer there-fore verifies the grounding of the prediction before issuing a final decision. The verification stage combines deterministic Python checks with a focused SLM verification prompt. 

1) Structural Verification: Python first validates the struc-ture of the Layer 3 output. The checks include JSON-schema compliance, validity of the prediction label, confidence-range validation, presence of required evidence identifiers, and con-firmation that every cited evidence identifier exists in the supplied interaction history. This stage detects malformed outputs and fabricated evidence references without requiring an additional SLM call. 

2) Semantic Evidence Verification: Deterministic checks validate output structure and confirm that cited evidence identifiers exist in the supplied interaction history, but they do not assess whether that evidence supports the model’s behavioral interpretation. A focused SLM verifier therefore evaluates whether the justification is semantically supported by the cited evidence and classifies the relationship as 

vu,t ∈ { SUPPORTED , NOT SUPPORTED , UNCERTAIN }.

(3) The verifier does not generate a new prediction; it only assesses whether the model’s reasoning is supported by the supplied evidence. 

3) Selective Final Decision: The verification result and confidence value are subsequently combined using determin-istic decision rules. The final output is

The threshold τ is selected on the development set to maximize selective F1 while main-taining at least 90% coverage, and is then fixed for the held-out test set. Abstention withholds predictions with insufficient confidence or evidence support, enabling performance to be evaluated jointly with the proportion of cases receiving a decision (coverage). IV. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP , B ENCHMARKS , AND 

EVALUATION 

A. Testbed and Gold Standard 

The experimental testbed uses EdNet-KT4, the most fine-grained level of the EdNet dataset, which contains sequential records of learner interactions rather than only final question responses [2]. The complete KT4 dataset contains 131,441,538 interaction records from 297,915 learners. For computational tractability, 70% of KT4 learners were randomly sampled us-ing seed 42, with all interaction records for the selected learn-ers retained to preserve complete sequential histories. This scale provides a large sequential testbed while maintaining the fine-grained interaction structure required for predicting near-term behavioral events. The extracted KT4 fields such as 

timestamp , action_type , item_id , user_answer ,and question metadata correct_answer were required to derive correctness, response duration, answer revisions, choice reversals, recent correctness, and previous assistance use deterministically. Events unrelated to learning behavior are excluded. EdNet does not provide a predefined label for near-term as-sistance use. An operational behavioral gold standard is there-fore derived deterministically from observed future actions. Prediction points are defined at the end of each task. At each prediction point, the model receives interaction information from the preceding five tasks. A positive label is assigned when the learner voluntarily requests assistance, i.e., checks an explanation or lecture, within the subsequent five tasks; otherwise, the instance is labeled negative. System-initiated assistance, such as adaptive_offer , is excluded. Data are partitioned by learner into training, development, and test sets to prevent histories from the same learner appearing across partitions. Learners were partitioned into training, develop-ment, and test sets using a 70/15/15 learner-disjoint split. 

B. Local SLM Configuration 

Three instruction-tuned models representing different model families and increasing small-model capacities are evaluated: 

Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct , Gemma-2-2B-IT , and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct [13]–[15]. All models are executed locally using the same inference engine and comparable 4-bit quantization on a 64 GB RAM and 8GB VRAM local infrastructure. No fine-tuning is performed. The same zero-shot task instruction, out-put schema, decoding parameters, and experimental instances are used across models. Greedy decoding was used to reduce sampling variability and improve reproducibility across model and architectural comparisons. 

C. Benchmark Configurations 

Four configurations isolate the contribution of each ELBP component. B0–Raw receives the original chronological KT4 events without Layer-1 feature construction directly to the local SLM. B1–Structured supplies only the deterministic behavioral summary. B2–Dual-View provides both chrono-logical evidence and the structured summary. B3–ELBP adds evidence attribution, verification, and abstention to B2. B0– B2 therefore provide controlled architectural baselines rather than different prediction algorithms. Additionally, XGBoost 

is included as a non-SLM baseline to determine whether the local SLM provides predictive value beyond the deter-ministic behavioral variables alone. XGBoost receives the same structured behavioral variables supplied to B1, includ-ing recent performance rate, consecutive unsuccessful out-comes, response-duration statistics, answer revisions, retries or reversals, and previous assistance-seeking behavior. Model fitting and hyperparameter selection are performed only on the training/development data, while the same learner-disjoint test partition used for the SLM configurations is retained for final evaluation. This provides a conventional supervised-learning reference under the same prediction target and input information conditions [ ?].

A. Overall Prediction Performance 

Table I compares the majority-class reference baseline, the non-SLM XGBoost baseline, and the three local SLMs under the direct raw-event condition (B0) and the ELBP prediction configuration prior to selective acceptance. To ensure a fair comparison across configurations, the precision, recall, F1, PR-AUC, and ECE values reported in this table are calculated over the complete test set without selective post-abstention. Performance with selective post-abstention is reported sepa-rately in Section V-B. The majority baseline always predicts no future assistance use and therefore obtains 0 recall and F1 for future assistance-use events. The XGBoost baseline achieves an F1 of 0.68 and PR-AUC of 0.72, providing a stronger non-SLM reference for evaluating the local SLM configurations. Direct raw-event reasoning produces performance close to the conventional XGBoost machine learning baseline. Llama-3.2-1B achieves an F1 of 0.62, Gemma-2-2B reaches 0.67, and Qwen2.5-3B reaches 0.69, slightly exceeding XGBoost. Applying the ELBP representation and prediction architecture improves full-set performance for all three models. Llama-3.2-1B increases from 0.62 to 0.70, Gemma-2-2B from 0.67 to 0.75, and Qwen2.5-3B from 0.69 to 0.80. Qwen2.5-3B with ELBP achieves the highest full-set precision (0.81), recall (0.79), F1 (0.80), and PR-AUC (0.84). Its pre-selection ECE decreases from 0.118 under B0 to 0.067 after dual-view representation. Following evidence verification and abstention, ECE decreases to 0.051 on retained predictions. Compared to XGBoost, the complete Qwen2.5-3B ELBP configuration improves the full-set F1 by 0.12.

D. Local Deployment Efficiency 

Table IV summarizes the computational requirements of the complete ELBP architecture. Reported ELBP latency is measured end-to-end and includes both Layer-3 prediction and Layer-4 semantic verification. Because the final ELBP deci-sion mechanism permits abstention, performance is reported as selective F1 together with coverage. XGBoost is not included in this table because token throughput, model quantization, and VRAM requirements are SLM-specific measures. Llama-3.2-1B provides the lowest computational cost among the ELBP configurations, achieving a selective F1 of 0.74 at 0.92 coverage, with a latency of 0.93 s, throughput of 76 tokens/s, and peak VRAM usage of 1.4 GB. Gemma-2-2B achieves a selective F1 of 0.79 at 0.91 coverage while requiring 1.41 s per inference and 2.1 GB of peak VRAM. Qwen2.5-3B achieves the highest selective F1 of 0.84 at 0.91 coverage, with a latency of 1.87 s and peak VRAM consumption of 2.8 GB. The results show a clear performance–efficiency trade-off. XGBoost provides substantially lower inference cost, with an average inference latency of 0.03 s per instance, but achieves lower predictive performance and does not provide evidence-grounded reasoning or selective verification. Among the lo-cal SLM configurations, Llama-3.2-1B provides the lowest-latency operating point, Gemma-2-2B provides an interme-diate configuration, and Qwen2.5-3B provides the highest performance at a higher computational cost. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This study introduced ELBP, a four-layer architecture for predicting future assistance use using SLMs. The proposed design combines deterministic interaction processing, dual-view behavioral representation, evidence-grounded prediction, and post-prediction verification with abstention. The results indicate that structured behavioral representation can improve prediction over raw interaction logs, while verification im-proves reliability. A key limitation is that the study evaluates a single behavioral domain and prediction target. Future work will therefore test ELBP across additional interaction domains and behavioral outcomes to assess whether the architecture generalizes beyond assistance-seeking prediction.
