Ask to Evolve: A Unified Harness for Lifelong
Embodied Navigation with Hybrid Action Space
and Persistent Memory
Kai Sheng, Liuyi Wang†, Jinlong Li, Haojie Dai, Chengju Liu, Qijun Chen†
School of Electronic and Information Engineering, Tongji University, Shanghai, China
Giving embodied agents the freedom to act autonomously, initiate interactions, and understand their sur-
roundings through experience is a step toward bringing them to life in the physical world. Realizing this vision
requires action, interaction, and memory to work together, so that each encounter can inform current decisions
and contribute experience for future tasks. We present EvoLife, a unified navigation harness that enables a
multimodal embodied agent to choose how to move, when to ask, and what experience to carry forward. Its
hybrid action space supports waypoint navigation, local discrete adjustments, pixel-based navigation, and
object-directed navigation. Active interaction allows the agent to seek clarification or assistance when it
encounters ambiguity or difficulty, while persistent spatial memory binds observations and dialogue to places,
retaining useful experience to guide subsequent visits. We evaluate eight benchmarks across four task families:
vision-language navigation, object navigation, interactive instance navigation, and continuous multi-task navi-
gation. With an average of only 1.87 active interactions per episode, our method achieves a first-visit success
rate (SR) of 100% across four 100-episode sets spanning vision-language navigation and object navigation.
Complete-sequence SR reaches 98% on 50 LHPR-VLN sequences and 94% on 50 GOAT-Bench sequences.
In the R2R revisit setting, success weighted by path length (SPL) increases from 82.23% to 93.31% with
retained experience. Multi-day physical navigation further demonstrates exploration, proactive help-seeking,
and revisiting remembered places. Together, these results advance the vision of bringing embodied agents to
life through autonomous action, proactive interaction, and the accumulation and reuse of experience.

Introduction
Lifelong embodied navigation calls for agents that can take initiative in how they act, interact, and accumulate
experience. An agent should be able to choose how to pursue a goal according to its environment and task
requirements, ask questions when it encounters ambiguity, seek assistance when progress becomes difficult,
and carry what it learns into future tasks. These capabilities give concrete meaning to the vision of bringing
embodied agents to life in the physical world. Through sustained engagement with their surroundings, agents
can connect what they perceive with what they do, use interaction to develop their understanding, and build
experience that remains useful beyond a single encounter.
People navigating unfamiliar environments naturally ask for clarification, request directions, and seek help
when needed. Embodied agents need the freedom to take the same initiative. A question can clarify an
instruction, identify a useful continuation, or obtain assistance with a physical obstacle. The value of that
interaction can also extend beyond the immediate task: an answer about a place, together with the observations
and actions that follow, can become experience for a later visit. This motivates the central idea of Ask to
Evolve: an agent actively asks to improve its understanding, acts on the resulting information, and retains
useful experience to guide future behavior.
Embodied navigation provides concrete settings for studying this process.
Vision-language navigation
connects linguistic instructions with visual observations and navigation decisions, including movement through
continuous environments [2, 3, 4, 12]. Interactive navigation introduces dialogue as a source of clarification and
assistance [13, 14, 15], while iterative and lifelong navigation make earlier encounters relevant to subsequent
tasks [16, 11]. Systematic real-world evaluations have documented a substantial gap between simulated and
physical navigation performance [17, 18]. This gap highlights the importance of coordinating perception,
decision-making, and execution, together with the ability to obtain assistance and reuse experience in real
environments.
Existing methods offer useful foundations. Topological planners connect visual waypoints with continuous
control [19], and language-model navigators integrate observations, reasoning, and actions through execution
interfaces [20, 21]. Memory-based systems retain observations and behavior, while navigation harnesses
preserve maps and task records across sessions [22, 23, 24]. These advances motivate a unified systems
question: how can an embodied agent keep multiple ways of moving, actively requested assistance,
and remembered experience available within the same decision loop? The connection matters
because an interaction may change the next movement, revise the interpretation of a place, and contribute
knowledge that is useful during a subsequent task.
We introduce EvoLife, a unified harness for lifelong embodied navigation with a hybrid action space, active
interaction, and persistent spatial memory. Its action space supports four complementary modes: waypoint
navigation toward a selected spatial location, local discrete adjustments for fine control, pixel-based navigation
toward a selected image location, and object-directed navigation toward an identified target. These modes
give the agent flexibility in expressing its movement intention. It can select a spatial waypoint, make a short
adjustment, indicate a visible location, or approach an object, according to the task and available observations.
Execution returns the actual outcome, allowing the agent to inspect its progress and choose its next action.
Fig. 2 presents the overall framework.
Active interaction is part of this ongoing decision process. The agent chooses when to ask and formulates
its question using the current task, observations, and relevant memory. It can request clarification when an
instruction is ambiguous, ask for guidance when the continuation is uncertain, or seek practical assistance
when the environment prevents progress. After receiving a response, the agent decides how to act and uses
subsequent observations and execution feedback to assess the outcome. Interaction therefore gives the agent
an active means of obtaining information and assistance while remaining connected to movement and the
experience generated through execution.
Persistent spatial memory allows these experiences to accumulate across tasks. Place records bind locations to
descriptions, observations, historical images, and dialogue. Information acquired through an interaction can
update a place description, support a later navigation decision, or help interpret a familiar environment under
changed conditions. New observations can, in turn, prompt revisions to existing records. This continuous
accumulation and refinement of spatial knowledge allows the agent’s experience to evolve across successive
2


tasks. By creating, retrieving, updating, and reusing historical memories, the agent can not only acquire new
knowledge from current encounters but also leverage evolving experience to guide future decisions.
We evaluate EvoLife on eight navigation benchmarks across four task families: vision-language navigation [2, 3,
4], object navigation [5, 6, 7], interactive instance navigation [8, 9], and continuous multi-task navigation [10, 11].
The main study includes six sets of 100 single-task episodes and two sets of 50 continuous sequences. With an
average of only 1.87 active interactions per episode, our method achieves a success rate (SR) of 100% across
four 100-episode sets spanning vision-language navigation and object navigation. Complete-sequence SR
reaches 98% on LHPR-VLN and 94% on GOAT-Bench. In the R2R revisit setting, success weighted by path
length (SPL) increases from 82.23% to 93.31% with retained experience. Ablation analyses further examine
interaction and experience reuse, while multi-day physical experiments demonstrate exploration, proactive
help-seeking, and revisiting remembered places.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. Hybrid action space. We propose EvoLife, which enables an embodied agent to select among four
navigation modes and adapt subsequent decisions based on execution feedback.
2. Active interaction. We integrate proactive interaction into the navigation loop, allowing the agent to
autonomously decide when to ask, generate clarification or assistance requests, and incorporate responses
into navigation decisions.
3. Persistent memory. We develop a dynamic long-term memory mechanism that associates observations,
descriptions, and dialogue with spatial locations, enabling explicit memory updates and continuous
experience accumulation across tasks.
Together, extensive benchmark evaluations and multi-day physical experiments advance the vision of bringing
embodied agents to life through autonomous action, proactive interaction, and accumulated experience that
guides future encounters.

Related Work
2.1
Embodied Navigation
Embodied navigation connects visual perception and language understanding with executable movement [25, 26].
R2R established instruction-guided indoor navigation, RxR expanded instruction diversity through detailed
multilingual descriptions, and continuous VLN extended execution beyond discrete viewpoint graphs [2, 3, 4].
Surveys and systematic real-world evaluations organize VLN through action and model paradigms and
identify challenges in transferring navigation capabilities from simulation to physical environments [17]. These
developments make the connection between decision-making and execution a central issue in embodied
navigation.
Existing approaches organize this connection in different ways. ETPNav combines online waypoint topology,
cross-modal planning, and obstacle-aware execution [19]. NavGPT and NavGPT-3 explore explicit reasoning
and interfaces connecting reasoning, action, and runtime execution [20, 21].
NaVid, StreamVLN, and
NaVILA connect visual history and language with executable behavior [27, 28, 29]. P2DNav uses panoramic
direction selection and downview grounding, NavJev introduces action-specific visual compression, and CLASH
coordinates reactive and reflective planning [30, 31, 32]. Correctable landmark discovery and disentangled
navigation reasoning provide further mechanisms for interpreting observations and selecting actions [33, 34, 35].
These studies highlight the importance of how movement intentions are expressed and executed. EvoLife
integrates waypoint navigation, local discrete adjustments, pixel-based navigation, and object-directed
navigation into a unified interface. The agent selects among these modes, inspects execution feedback, and
obtains additional information through interaction and memory, connecting action selection with the evidence
available for subsequent decisions.
3


2.2
Interactive Navigation
Interactive navigation enables embodied agents to obtain information and assistance through dialogue. CVDN
connects navigation with dialogue history collected through interaction with an informed guide [13]. HANNA
studies agents that actively request language and visual assistance [14], while ORION integrates thinking,
acting, and asking to incorporate feedback into object navigation [15]. These approaches treat interaction as
an action that can influence how an agent interprets its task and proceeds through the environment.
Interaction is particularly useful when the available instruction or observation is insufficient to identify the
intended goal. VL-LN and CoIN study dialogue for underspecified goals and target disambiguation [8, 9].
GG-Nav and ProCompNav further investigate interactive navigation and goal resolution [36, 37]. Talk2Escape
and AVERT-VLN also examine external assistance in navigation [38, 39]. Across these settings, useful
interaction requires the agent to decide when help is needed, formulate a relevant request, and incorporate the
response into subsequent action. Questions therefore connect the agent’s current uncertainty with information
that supports continued navigation.
EvoLife integrates this initiative into the unified navigation loop. The agent can ask for clarification, request
guidance, or seek assistance with an environmental difficulty, then use the response and subsequent observations
to decide how to proceed. Information acquired through interaction also becomes part of persistent place
records, remaining available beyond the current exchange. This connection between asking, acting, and
retaining experience is central to Ask to Evolve.
2.3
Spatial Memory and Experience Evolution
Spatial memory associates observations and semantic information with locations that an embodied agent can
revisit. Visual language maps connect semantic queries with spatial locations, while object-centric graphs
represent relations among detected entities [40, 41]. Three-dimensional scene memory supports selective
retrieval of observations for embodied tasks [42]. These representations provide spatial context for interpreting
current observations and selecting actions. Their use over time also involves retrieving relevant content and
revising stored information when new evidence becomes available.
Lifelong navigation explicitly considers experience reuse across tasks. GOAT-Bench evaluates sequential
category, description, and image goals, while Iterative VLN studies continued navigation using retained experi-
ence [11, 16]. Memoir combines viewpoint-level observation and behavior memory with query imagination [22].
NavHarness preserves navigation state, maps, and task knowledge across sessions, while HarnessVLN connects
planning support with persistent spatial and event representations [23, 24]. These approaches consider
both what an agent remembers and how its decision interface makes that memory available for subsequent
navigation.
EvoLife focuses on how autonomous action and proactive interaction jointly contribute to persistent spatial
experience. Place records connect locations with descriptions, observations, historical images, and dialogue.
Information obtained through a question can revise the interpretation of a place, while subsequent movement
and observation provide further evidence for updating the record. The agent can inspect historical evidence,
revisit remembered locations, and update memory as its understanding changes.

For waypoint navigation, let Ct denote geometric candidates generated from observed geometry and
refreshed using the latest RGB-D observations. The agent assesses these candidates using the available
views and selects an index for navigate_candidate. The backend then plans through observed free space and
executes the resulting path continuously. Navigation to a remembered place uses the same waypoint execution
mechanism, as described in Sec. 3.5.
Local discrete adjustments are provided by step, which supports forward movement and left or right turns.
In the simulated interface, step(1) requests a forward movement of 0.25 m, while step(2) and step(3) turn
left and right by 15◦, respectively. These actions support local exploration, orientation changes, and fine
adjustment after a longer navigation request, with observations and feedback returned for the next decision.
Pixel-based navigation allows the agent to select a floor location directly in an image through navigate_pixel.
Its arguments identify the source view, normalized pixel coordinates, and image version. The backend uses
RGB-D evidence and the corresponding camera transform to obtain a ground waypoint, checks its reachability
through observed free space, and executes navigation toward it. This mode connects a visually selected
location with continuous movement without requiring selection from the candidate set Ct.
For object-directed navigation, the agent first identifies the target in the current RGB observations and uses
localize_object to select a source view and specify a bounding box or image point for RGB-D localization. The
backend filters valid depth measurements within the selected box or a local neighborhood of the selected point,
extracts a depth-consistent component, and back-projects the retained pixels to estimate object geometry.
The returned object identifier links this estimate to its supporting 3D points and visual overlays. The agent
can use verify_object to reproject the estimate into current views and inspect depth-consistency evidence. The
navigate_object tool then accepts the object identifier, a stand-off distance, and optional facing and inspection
settings. The backend searches for reachable approach locations in observed free space and plans a path to a
selected location. The agent examines the returned views and execution feedback to assess the approach and
decide whether further movement or adjustment is needed. The agent remains responsible for identifying the
intended target from visual evidence and deciding when to stop.
Navigation requests can specify an inspection distance or execution time. Control returns at the requested
checkpoint, or upon arrival, collision, lack of progress, or budget exhaustion. The agent then selects its next
tool using the actual returned state. Navigation requests perform movement, while task submission remains a
stop decision.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the guide receives the navigation instruction, current visual observations, target
information, and historical dialogue. Unlike the navigator, the guide additionally has access to privileged
spatial information, including the current pose, target location, shortest navigable path, and relevant map
information. Visual representations of the reference route are also provided to facilitate spatial reasoning and
natural-language guidance.
Rather than directly exposing target coordinates, complete reference paths, or private maps to the navigator,
the guide translates this information into natural-language responses using relative directions, visible landmarks,
and spatial relationships, resembling how humans provide directions in unfamiliar environments. For instance-
level navigation tasks such as VL-LN and CoIN, the navigator initially receives only the target category,
whereas the guide has access to the specific target instance, allowing it to resolve instance ambiguity through
interaction.
The generated response rt is returned through the tool feedback zt and becomes available for subsequent
decisions. The agent can use the guidance to clarify the intended target, determine a navigation direction,
or resolve difficulties encountered during execution. It then selects its next action and verifies the outcome
through new observations and execution feedback. In physical deployment, interaction may also involve direct
human assistance, such as opening a closed door, after which the agent observes the changed environment and
resumes navigation.
Interaction also contributes to persistent experience. The question, response, and associated spatial context
can be retained as place-grounded memory, allowing acquired guidance to be retrieved and reused during
subsequent navigation tasks. In this way, active interaction supports both immediate navigation decisions and
the accumulation of experience for future encounters.

3.5
Persistent Memory
Persistent memory enables spatial experience to be retained across task boundaries and reused during
subsequent navigation tasks. Rather than resetting acquired knowledge after each task, the agent preserves
observations, executed motion, and dialogue associated with spatial locations, allowing previous encounters to
inform future decisions. Observed geometry Gt supports planning, executed routes Rt preserve movement
history, and retained dialogue Dt provides interaction evidence.

accumulated experience around named locations that the agent can inspect, revise, and revisit.

The remember_place tool creates a record at the actual current pose or updates an existing place identifier,
preserving timestamped visual evidence alongside its description. Place descriptions may already accompany
current observations. When further retrieval is useful, search_memory matches stored names, descriptions,
and object labels; an empty query lists available places. The inspect_place tool retrieves historical four-view
images and place details, explicitly distinguished from current observations. The agent selects these operations
according to its information needs, without a fixed retrieval order.
Given an available place identifier, navigate_place plans toward its recorded location using current observed
geometry. The destination can remain active during local inspection or avoidance, after which resume_-
navigation replans from the actual current pose. Both tools use the waypoint execution mechanism, allowing
current observations and motion feedback to guide continued navigation.
Geometry, executed routes, and spatially associated dialogue are recorded automatically, while semantic place
records are maintained selectively by the agent. New observations and answers can enrich a description,
correct a mistaken interpretation, or motivate retiring an obsolete record. For example, a clarification can
establish which room an entrance leads to, while a later visit can provide additional visual landmarks or reveal
changed access conditions. Crucially, these records remain available beyond individual tasks, allowing the
agent to retrieve and reuse spatial knowledge accumulated during earlier encounters. During revisits, retained
descriptions, historical images, and dialogue help distinguish similar locations and guide navigation toward
remembered places. New observations and execution feedback can then verify, refine, or correct existing
records. Through this continuous process of retention, reuse, and revision, spatial experience progressively
evolves across tasks, enabling future navigation to build on earlier exploration and interaction rather than
repeatedly acquiring the same information.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup and Protocols
Tasks and Benchmarks.
Table 2 summarizes the evaluation protocols. R2R-CE and RxR-CE evaluate route
following in continuous environments [2, 3, 4]. HM3D v2 and HM3D-OVON use object-category goals in

Experimental Details.
Simulation and local tool execution use a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell
GPU. The simulation environment is built upon Habitat [43]. The navigator receives four RGB views,
depth-derived observed geometry, and the current pose provided by the simulator. For the main experiments,
both the navigator and the guide use GPT-6 Astra [44] with the ultra reasoning setting. They operate in
independent sessions with separate contexts. The guide privately accesses route- and goal-related information,
while the navigator receives only the natural-language replies generated by the guide, with a maximum of
ten interactions per subtask. We additionally evaluate Qwen3.8-Max (xhigh) [45] and DeepSeek V4.1-Flash
(max) [46]. In each configuration, the navigator and guide use the same backbone in separate sessions, while
retaining the shared navigation harness and tool interfaces.
Measures.
Success rate (SR) requires the agent to stop within the task’s accepted success region. Oracle
success rate (OSR) measures whether the navigation trajectory ever reaches the success region, while navigation
error (NE) measures the final distance to the goal. Success weighted by path length (SPL) jointly evaluates
task success and travel efficiency [47]. Normalized dynamic time warping (nDTW) further evaluates the
correspondence between the executed trajectory and the reference path [48]. Trajectory length (TL) measures
the total distance traveled by the agent along its executed trajectory, expressed in meters. For VL-LN and
CoIN, we additionally report the Number of Questions (NQ), defined as the average number of agent-initiated
question–answer exchanges per episode. For LHPR-VLN, we retain the official evaluation metrics [10]. Success
Rate (SR) measures whether all subtasks are completed successfully in the required order, while Independent
Success Rate (ISR) measures individual subtask success. Conditional Success Rate (CSR) accounts for
dependencies between consecutive subtasks, assigning greater credit when both the current and preceding
subtasks succeed. CSR weighted by Ground Truth (CGT) further weights these contributions by the relative
ground-truth path lengths of the subtasks. GOAT distinguishes subtask success rate (s-SR) from complete-
sequence success rate (e-SR). R2R and RxR use a 3 m destination threshold. For success criteria based on
geodesic distance to accepted target viewpoints, the thresholds are 0.25 m for OVON, VL-LN, CoIN, and
GOAT, and 0.1 m for HM3D v2. LHPR stages use a 1 m threshold.

more tool decisions, resulting in substantially longer execution times.
For active interaction, the three models average 1.66, 1.59, and 3.21 questions per episode, respectively.
DeepSeek V4.1-Flash requests assistance considerably more frequently than the other two models, indicating
a greater reliance on external interaction for information and guidance during navigation. Considering only
navigator-side API-equivalent costs, the average cost per episode is $1.06, $0.46, and $0.03, respectively.
Overall, GPT-6 Astra achieves the best performance in navigation success, path efficiency, and execution
time, while DeepSeek V4.1-Flash maintains a high success rate at the lowest inference cost.
4.5
Navigation Benefits Beyond Arrival Confirmation
To investigate whether the benefits of active interaction extend beyond arrival confirmation, we conduct a
further analysis that preserves other interactions but directly executes STOP upon the first arrival-confirmation-
related request. This setting eliminates subsequent navigation adjustments guided by arrival-confirmation
feedback, allowing us to examine the contributions of earlier interactions, such as directional guidance and
target clarification.
As shown in Table 7, our method still achieves strong navigation performance. Specifically, it obtains 90.0% SR
and 74.0% SPL on R2R-CE, and 95.0% SR and 71.6% SPL on RxR-CE. Compared with the non-interactive
R2R baseline (86.0% SR and 63.7% SPL), these results still demonstrate clear improvements.
On object navigation benchmarks, our method achieves 69.0% SR and 51.1% SPL on HM3D-OVON, and
89.0% SR and 57.5% SPL on HM3D v2. These results demonstrate that the guidance provided by active
interaction during navigation is also highly effective. Meanwhile, the larger performance gaps on object
navigation tasks highlight the value of further navigation adjustments under more stringent success criteria.

stopping decisions rather than incorrect semantic target recognition.

is evaluated as a failure.

5
Discussion and Limitations
In this work, EvoLife integrates hybrid actions, active interaction, and persistent memory into a unified
embodied navigation framework, enabling agents to adaptively select movement strategies based on envi-
ronmental conditions, actively seek external guidance, and reuse historical experience in subsequent tasks.
Experimental results across eight navigation benchmarks demonstrate the applicability of our framework to
vision-language navigation, object navigation, and multi-stage continuous navigation. Comparisons across
different decision backbones further demonstrate the adaptability of the unified tool interface, while revealing
distinct action selection strategies among models. In particular, GPT-6 Astra tends to favor waypoint naviga-
tion for longer-distance movement, whereas other models rely more frequently on local actions, highlighting
the importance of appropriate action selection strategies for improving navigation efficiency.
Active interaction not only helps agents confirm arrival at a destination but also provides directional guidance,
target clarification, and environmental assistance during navigation. Our analysis beyond arrival confirmation
shows that the agent maintains strong navigation performance even without subsequent adjustments based on
arrival-confirmation feedback, indicating that the benefits of active interaction extend beyond final success
verification. Meanwhile, persistent memory enables the agent to associate interaction-derived information
with spatial locations and reuse it in subsequent tasks. Memory-based revisit experiments demonstrate that
the agent can maintain high success rates without additional interaction while reducing navigation steps
and tool decisions. Real-world experiments further illustrate the agent’s ability to restore historical maps,
revisit known locations across days, and handle changes in door states through active interaction. These
findings highlight the complementary roles of active interaction and long-term memory: interaction acquires
new information, while memory allows that information to remain useful in subsequent tasks.
Despite the promising results, our method still faces limitations in real-time performance and operational cost.
The navigation process involves multiple rounds of model inference, tool execution, and active interaction,
resulting in decision latency and API costs that limit its applicability to long-term real-world robotic tasks.
Future work will focus on optimizing decision efficiency and interaction strategies to reduce system latency and
operational costs while maintaining navigation performance, advancing the framework toward more efficient
and sustainable lifelong embodied navigation.
6
Conclusion
In this paper, we presented EvoLife, a unified framework for lifelong embodied navigation that integrates
hybrid actions, active interaction, and persistent memory. By enabling agents to adaptively select navigation
actions, actively seek external guidance, and retain spatially grounded experience, our framework supports
efficient navigation and knowledge reuse across tasks. Extensive experiments on eight navigation benchmarks
demonstrate strong performance across vision-language navigation, object navigation, and multi-stage continu-
ous navigation. Further analyses highlight the effectiveness of active interaction and the benefits of persistent
memory in improving navigation efficiency during revisits. Real-world experiments additionally demonstrate
the framework’s ability to reuse historical experience across days and adapt to dynamic environmental changes.
These results establish a promising foundation for embodied agents capable of continuously accumulating and
reusing experience in lifelong navigation.
