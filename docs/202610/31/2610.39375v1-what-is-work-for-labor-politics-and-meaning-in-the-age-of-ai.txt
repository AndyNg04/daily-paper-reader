# What is Work For? 

## Labor, Politics, and Meaning in the Age of AI ∗

Julian Jacobs 

University of Oxford and Google DeepMind 

October 30, 2026 

Abstract 

This paper investigates the role of work in providing a source of meaning, purpose, community, and other non-monetary benefits. Using an original cross-national survey of 8,234 working-age adults across the United Kingdom, United States, Denmark, and India, I study the factors that motivate people in their work, with an eye toward the potential future AI economic transformation. I find that 81% of respondents report being satisfied with their job, and a majority of people across all four countries are strongly motivated by the non-monetary components of their employment. Using an original “Work Values” Index, I show that one third of working respondents in our sample are motivated intrinsically by their job—valuing their role for its own sake. Nearly one quarter of respondents are driven by social and community-related aspects of their employment. A further 17% are driven by a feeling of social status. Just over one quarter of workers are motivated by the transactional financial benefits of their job. Although a majority of people would choose to leave their role if offered their current salary as a guaranteed income, one fifth of workers would choose to stay in their job even if offered up to 120% of their salary. Workers who are intrinsically motivated by their job were disproportionately likely to refuse a basic income at any substitution level. Groups attached to their job for non-monetary reasons were also more likely to pursue volunteering or work-like activities if they received guaranteed income substitution but could no longer perform their current role. Crucially, these non-monetary work motivations are evenly distributed across the income distribution and between white- and blue-collar workers. I argue that these findings have implications for managing the AI transition, understanding attitudes to AI, and making sense of policy preferences. Guaranteeing a basic income may indeed be necessary for managing any future AI labor shock, yet it could ultimately fail to compensate workers for any loss of the non-monetary benefits they derive from work. 

Keywords: Technological change, artificial intelligence, meaning of work, social status, reservation wage, political backlash, populist attitudes.

## 1 Introduction 

How much do people value the work that they do every day? In standard neoclassical economic theory, labor and leisure are trade-offs, with employment treated purely as a source of disutility —something people would prefer to do less of if given an option (see Adam Smith’s “toil and trouble,” Smith, 1998; Lazear, 2018). Yet a diversity of literature in economic sociology and behavioral philosophy suggests that labor provides non-monetary benefits, which include a source of personal identity, structure, communal belonging, and social status (Durkheim, 1984; Marshall, 1920; Spencer, 2015; Valentino, 2021; Korinek and Juelfs, 2022). Indeed, subjective perceptions of social belonging are often entangled with self-perceptions of value, prestige, and importance within the workplace (Blau and Duncan, 1967; Ridgeway and Walker, 1995; Lamont, 1992; Valentino, 2021). This paper explores the value that people place on their employment, with a particular focus on the AI economic transition. It is well established that technological automation can impose short- and medium-run challenges for some workers, who may experience hardship due to under-employment, stagnating or lower real wages, and redundant skill-sets (Autor, 2015; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2017, 2020). Yet when a technology dispenses with some forms of work, it also may create a loss of the non-monetary value that a person’s employment offered—for instance, social opportunities or a feeling of purpose. Recent digital automation played a role in rendering some middle-wage occupations obsolete, potentially shifting some displaced workers into roles that were more precarious and less linked to their prior skill-sets (Autor et al., 2008; Acemoglu and Autor, 2011; Oesch, 2013; Kalleberg, 2009). Social scientists have recently theorized that this labor dislocation can—in addition to imposing economic hardship—erode workers’ sense of intrinsic purpose and usher in feelings of status anxiety or “ontological insecurity,” whereby a person feels aggrieved at the loss of meaning and social standing their job afforded them (Giddens, 1991; Cassar and Meier, 2018; Kurer, 2020; Metten and Bayerlein, 2023). Political scientists, in particular, have used versions of this theory to explain digitalization’s role in kindling populist spirit across developed economies (Gingrich, 2019; Kurer and Gallego, 2019; Kurer, 2020; Jacobs, 2024). The role of the non-pecuniary 1 value of work in shaping the AI economic transition has, to date, been an under-explored topic of empirical research. Indeed, if a person attaches significant weight to their work, outside of financial considerations, income transfers alone (e.g. through basic incomes) may ultimately fail to replace what a person loses if AI automates their job. Reckoning with this question is particularly important today, as advanced AI systems diffuse throughout economic activities, creating the potential for entirely new areas of labor substitution (Jacobs and Imas, 2026). In particular, this research makes five core contributions to existing literature. First, across all occupations, 81% of respondents reported being satisfied with their jobs, and I show that a majority of workers are attached to their employment for non-monetary reasons. Specifically, I have developed a four-category Work Values Profile classification, which shows whether a worker is primarily motivated by transactional, intrinsic, community, or status-related considerations. relationship with their employment, valuing it for pay and benefits. By contrast, the data shows that one-third of workers in our sample carry an Intrinsic motivation, whereby they report obtaining a sense of purpose, learning, or a feeling of societal contribution from their work. Meanwhile, nearly one-quarter (23%) of respondents have a Community orientation, reporting that they value their employment because it brings them a sense of social belonging or an opportunity for socializing. Furthermore, 17% of respondents are Status-Driven , saying they value their employment because it offers them recognition and prestige. An especially important observation—with implications for how policy responses to technological change ought to be designed—is that these Work Values Profiles cut very evenly across occupation categories and income levels. In other words, a person’s likelihood of valuing their work for non-monetary reasons is not obviously moderated by how much a person makes or what they do. Second, I show that most people would choose to voluntarily leave their job if they were offered a full guaranteed income replacement. Yet a sizable fraction—20% of workers—would choose to stay in their job even if offered up to 120% as guaranteed income. This share stays roughly consistent across the income distribution, at 21% for low-income, 18% for middle-income, and 22% for high-income workers. The share is slightly higher for “white-collar” workers (21%) than for “blue-collar” workers (17%). Each of these divergences is highly statistically significant ( p < 0.01) in multivariate regression models controlling for income, occupation, demographic observables, and country fixed effects. Fourth, I show that perceptions of AI’s economic impacts may play a role in moderating people’s work attachment, including the wage that they would require to stop working. Specifi-cally, if someone believes that AI is more likely to automate their job, their demanded wage replacement shrinks across all Work Values Profiles, at a statistically significant two to four percentage points per standard deviation of perceived threat ( p < 0.01). These results are robust to a battery of controls on observables; however, I note that the model cannot infer the directionality of this relationship (i.e. more pessimistic people might demand lower wages, while also believing AI will automate their job). Nonetheless, this research finds a relationship between perceptions of AI’s threat and demanded replacement wages. I note, in particular, that across Work Values Profiles, the slope of the downward shift is significantly steeper among Intrinsic 

and Community oriented workers than among Transactional workers. Future research, with a causal identification approach, can help elucidate this relationship further. Finally, I directly engage with contemporary political economy literature, exploring a theo-retical puzzle about why technological automation has historically been associated with rising support for anti-establishment right-wing parties even when welfare transfers are available. Section 2 reviews the literature on technological change, the non-monetary value of work, and political backlash to economic shocks, while Section 3 formalizes a theoretical framework for understanding the non-pecuniary value of labor, including with an eye toward understanding socio-political behavior. Altogether, this paper argues that work continues to provide a significant source of value to people’s lives outside of pure economic considerations. The presence of this non-monetary dimension can be seen across income groups and occupational categories, suggesting far more complex sets of social, psychological, and individual reasons for valuing work. If AI does indeed usher in a significant shock to the labor market that displaces many workers, this research paper suggests that supplementing incomes (e.g. through basic income programs) may be a necessary 

but insufficient mechanism to support workers. An effective societal response may need to reckon with the simultaneous shocks to both income and the deeply-embedded social, cultural, political, and normative value that work provides. 

## 2 Literature 

Technological change is instrumental in helping social scientists understand economic history, and modern capitalism. The evidence is clear that productivity-boosting innovation has been vital to sustained economic growth, as well as in shaping global and domestic distributions of power (Autor, 2015). Over the past 40 years, major technology shocks have amalgamated with financialization (Epstein, 2005; Krippner, 2005; Ahlquist and Ansell, 2017; Wiedemann, 2021), trade exposure (Autor et al., 2013; Pierce and Schott, 2016), and institutional liberalization (Blyth, 2013; Western and Rosenfeld, 2011) to shift the global balance of economic power. Labor, Politics, and Meaning in the Age of AI 5Given the magnitude of technology’s economic effects, it is perhaps unsurprising that it has also consistently ushered in anxieties over the potential impacts of new innovations on labor automation (Mokyr et al., 2015). During the early Industrial Revolution, Charlotte Brontë (1849, p. 50) observed how newly introduced textile machinery in northern England “threw thousands out of work, and left them without legitimate means of sustaining life.” David Ricardo likewise argued “the substitution of machinery for human labour is often very injurious to the interests of the class of labourers” (Kurz, 1984), while Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels warned that rapid mechanization risked deepening working-class alienation and immiseration (Engels, 2009). By 1927, U.S. Secretary of Labor James Davis asked whether extraordinary industrial mechanization merely “dispenses with [workers]” (The New York Times, 1927). Jobs have, of course, persisted. And so mainstream social science often dismissed fears about automation as the “Luddite fallacy,” arguing that productivity-boosting innovation ultimately creates more better-paying employment than it destroys over the long run. Yet an extensive body of modern literature shows that technological change—including modern digitalization and digital automation—has produced sharp divisions between labor market winners and losers (Alvaredo et al., 2018; Atkinson, 2015; Milanovic, 2016; Piketty, 2014). These effects appear to have emerged through at least two mechanisms. First, macroeconomic evidence suggests that prior moments of technological progress have compressed labor’s aggregate share of national income, while capital’s share of income has grown (Allen, 2009; Elsby et al., 2013; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2017). Meanwhile, technology has previously shifted demand for skills in the labor market. Skill-biased technological change (SBTC) defines the phenomenon where new innovations shift which skillsets are rewarded by markets. Previously, workers whose cognitive skills were complemented by digital technologies saw rising real wages, while workers in routine middle-skill occupations often faced wage stagnation and declining employment shares (Autor et al., 2008; Acemoglu and Autor, 2011; Goos et al., 2014). 2

The corresponding political implications of these labor market effects have been a central focus of comparative political economy. Scholars have previously argued that the rise of right-wing populism and anti-establishment voting across developed democracies represents an “economic backlash” driven by the experience of falling behind for many workers (Evans and Tilley, 2017; Im et al., 2019; Gingrich, 2019; Kurer and Gallego, 2019; Schöll and Kurer, 2024; Bicchi et al., 2024). Yet the exact mechanics of this backlash present a theoretical puzzle. Standard theoretical frameworks assume that voters respond primarily to material economic incentives. If technological displacement inflicts material damage on workers’ livelihoods, affected individuals will likely demand greater government spending and support left-wing pro-redistribution parties, according to these theoretical assumptions (Margalit, 2012; Swank and Betz, 2003). This puzzle is central to this paper’s theoretical contribution. Specifically, I look into the role of the non-pecuniary value of work in explaining the mismatch between pro-redistribution material incentives on the one hand and real-world right-wing populist electoral behavior on the other. Prior literature shows that work is unsurprisingly a multi-dimensional social concept, offering much more to workers than a simple exchange of leisure hours for wages. Within sociology and psychology, for instance, employment is often regarded as a source of personal identity, temporal structure, purpose, and social status (Durkheim, 1984; Marshall, 1920; Spencer, 2015; Korinek and Juelfs, 2022). Yet a recurring question within this literature concerns how each of these dimensions operates. In practice, social status or purpose, for instance, might be features of a comparatively small portion of the labor market, or else they might be present across broad swathes of workers. Moreover, there is a question about what these non-monetary components mean in practice, given their inherently subjective and relativistic nature (Singh-Manoux et al., 2005; Miyakawa et al., 2012; Ridgeway and Walker, 1995; Blau and Duncan, 1967). One articulation of this concept is that modern labor markets comprise a heterarchy of prestige, whereby multiple competing and subjective hierarchies of status, prestige, and morality define individuals’ self-perception of occupational worth (Valentino, 2021; Boutyline and Vaisey, 2017). For instance, industrial craft workers, skilled tradespeople, and administrative personnel often possess deep occupational pride in their “blue-collar” work or “producerist ethic,” which prizes tangible skills, self-reliance, and hard work (Bonikowski and DiMaggio, 2016; Lamont, 1992). These hierarchies are often “sticky” across generations (Hodge et al., 1964; Bourdieu, 1984). With this framework in mind, we can better understand why rapid technological change— such as that which might soon be ushered in by AI—can be disruptive, beyond pure economic considerations. Indeed, productivity-boosting technological change that alters demand for skills can shift established heterarchies. When automation dispenses with occupations, many displaced workers lose both their income and the non-pecuniary value of their prior job (Oesch, 2013; Goos et al., 2014). In the political economy literature, one prominent explanation concerns status anxiety : a form of ontological insecurity whereby an individual might perceive an erosion of their place in the social order (Giddens, 1991; Kinnvall, 2004; Metten and Bayerlein, 2023; Kurer, 2020). Equally fundamental, however, is the loss of intrinsic meaning and craft or professional purpose derived from the act of working itself—a reward that, unlike external status or material consumption, cannot be carried into a different role or replaced by passive cash transfers (Ryan What is Work For? Labor, Politics, and Meaning in the Age of AI 7and Deci, 2000; Cassar and Meier, 2018). According to this theory, material income and social status can shift inversely and decouple (Gidron and Hall, 2017; Kurer, 2020; Vlandas, 2020). A worker whose job is changed by AI might maintain their employment and receive compensatory government welfare transfers while simultaneously experiencing significant feelings of eroded status, and the loss of the non-pecuniary value of their work. Social psychological literature suggests that the resulting threats to social standing can manifest in higher rates of authoritarianism, nostalgia for a perceived golden age, and heightened out-group hostility (Lipset, 1959; Tajfel and Turner, 1979; Riek et al., 2006; Küpper et al., 2010). And so, when technological shocks threaten work, status anxiety can exert a rightward pressure, toward parties with anti-establishment and nostalgic rhetoric. This force appears to compete with—and can triumph over—the simultaneous leftward pull ushered in by material economic insecurity. 

## 3 Theory 

I now formalize this theory, distinguishing between the broader concept of the non-monetary (non-pecuniary) value of work (NW ) and three conceptually distinct non-monetary sub-dimensions of employment. These include intrinsic meaning (IW ), communal workplace belonging (CW ), and social status (SW ). Building on prior literature, intrinsic utility (IW ) captures rewards derived from the act of working itself—including personal purpose and fulfillment and societal contribution (Durkheim, 1984; Ryan and Deci, 2000; Cassar and Meier, 2018). When work provides intrinsic value, it does so for its own sake because the worker finds their role inherently meaningful. Communal workplace utility (CW ), meanwhile, captures the value of work in the form of social integration, relational belonging, and daily camaraderie with coworkers and clients (Durkheim, 1984; Lorenzini and Giugni, 2012). Finally, a measure of status utility 

(SW ) captures a person’s perception that their occupation provides them with “social status,” prestige, and public recognition from groups in society (Weber, 1978; Tajfel and Turner, 1979; Gidron and Hall, 2017; Valentino, 2021). Shayo (2009) suggests that individuals weight both material consumption and the relative status of the groups with which they identify when making employment decisions. For instance, a worker may shift toward alternative identities or entrepreneurial pursuits when their immediate economic role fails to satisfy subjective perceptions of high social standing. Across all three non-monetary dimensions, commitment to a current job depends on the value a worker gets from their occupation ( IW , C W , S W ) compared to what they would experience if they were not working in that role ( IN W , C N W , S N W ). Since intrinsic utility ( IW ) is generated directly through active participation in one’s occupation, an intrinsically attached worker cannot simply replicate it via passive cash transfers ( IW > I N W ). It is with this theory that I seek to test three primary empirical hypotheses across our four-country survey dataset. First, I hypothesize ( Hypothesis 1 ) that in direct comparisons with transactionally motivated people, workers who place a significant importance on the non-monetary value of labor (including intrinsic, community, or status-related components) will require higher wages to voluntarily exit employment. Second ( Hypothesis 2 ), workers who value their employment particularly due to status, intrinsic, or communal motivations will pursue work-like or prosocial activities (such as seeking new employment, entrepreneurship, volunteering, or caregiving, rather than pure leisure) in scenarios where they receive income transfers but are no longer able to work in their current role. ## 4 Data, Design, and the Non-Monetary Value of Work 

I now proceed by discussing how this paper will test its hypotheses about the role of the non-pecuniary value of work in shaping labor market attachment and socio-political attitudes. To study these questions, I conducted an original cross-national survey of 8,234 working-age adults across four high- and emerging-income democracies. These countries are Denmark (N = 2 , 032), India ( N = 2 , 072), the United Kingdom ( N = 2 , 052), and the United States (N = 2 , 078), respectively. This survey was fielded in June and July of 2026, in partnership with More in Common (a polling organization), which used national quota sampling on age, gender, educational attainment, region, and other observables to generate a representative sample of each country’s adult working population. 4

The four countries were selected because they represent divergent economic contexts and market structures. We might accordingly expect divergences in the ways in which people value their work—and respond to AI—to be partially conditioned by these national contexts. The United Kingdom, for starters, is a mature hybrid post-industrial service economy, in which the public is generally familiar with AI systems—two thirds of British adults, for example, say they have used AI chatbots. Yet it is also a country marked by high levels of economic pessimism, which is apparent in the British public’s general sentiments about AI’s impacts. Public attitudes toward AI’s impact on jobs sit at −36, while just 14% of Britons expect AI’s economic impacts to benefit ordinary citizens. The United States, meanwhile, is a highly dynamic liberal economy, with flexible labor markets and high average wages, but significant levels of inequality and a long-standing socio-cultural skepticism of state interference with free enterprise. As we will see later, Americans are disproportionately likely to have a transactional relationship with their employment, relative to other countries. And they are also more likely to emphasize individual self-reliance over public insurance or safety nets. Denmark is a coordinated social democracy, and home to its famous “flexicurity” system, which amalgamates an ease of hiring and firing with generous support for active labor market policies and safety nets, which are meant to provide a cushion for workers who experience job loss. Danish workers reported the highest job satisfaction rate among countries in this study, with 82% indicating they are satisfied or very satisfied. They also hold the lowest fear of personal AI job replacement (+24 net positive expectations regarding AI’s macroeconomic impact). Finally, India is a developing economy with expanding digital services and strong global demand for its software, IT, and digital outsourcing services. An important note is that, due to internet coverage and literacy constraints, online survey sampling in India captures a disproportionately educated, urban, and “white-collar” segment of the country’s working population. ## 7 Discussion and Limitations 

The analyses I have presented in this paper offer evidence meant to advance our understanding of what motivates non-monetary work attachment, how strong this pull is, and what its implications may be for the age of AI. I now evaluate our empirical results against the three primary hypotheses formalized in Section 3. First, Hypothesis 1 —that workers who place significant importance on the non-monetary value they derive from their labor will require higher reservation wages to voluntarily exit employment than transactionally motivated workers—appears to receive support, particularly for intrinsically -motivated workers, though not across all non-monetary profiles uniformly. Indeed, in this paper’s modelling, individuals with an Intrinsic Profile exhibit a 10-percentage-point higher raw median reservation wage (80% vs. 70%) and require a statistically significant covariate-adjusted wage replacement premium of 1 .76 to 1 .92 percentage points in our most conservative estimates and at 5 .77 percentage points ( p < 0.01) in less conservative estimates. One in four Intrinsic workers (23% vs. 14% of Transactional workers, p < 0.01) say they would refuse to leave their jobs at any price up to 120% of current salary. Community-oriented workers ( βC CW ) similarly exhibit a higher extensive-margin refusal rate than Transactional workers (17% vs. 14% , p = 0 .03). By contrast, Status-Driven workers ( βS SW ) do not demand a higher reservation wage to leave their current roles, requiring a 60% median wage replacement rate—the lowest threshold of any profile. Although belief in the non-pecuniary value of work is very common and appears to play a role in moderating occupational attachment, the precise expression of that non-pecuniary value can vary greatly, depending on the reasons a worker values their job outside of financial considerations. Second, Hypothesis 2 —that workers motivated by status, intrinsic, or communal values will pursue work-like or prosocial activities rather than pure leisure when receiving guaranteed income transfers outside their current job—again appears supported across all three non-monetary profiles and may help explain the Status-driven pattern in Hypothesis 1 . When survey respondents were asked how they would allocate their time if they could no longer work and instead received a guaranteed basic income, nearly four-fifths (79%) of Transactional workers said they would pursue leisure activities (rest, hobbies, and travel). By contrast, Status-Driven (55%), Community (46%), and Intrinsic (46%) workers were significantly more likely than Transactional workers (39% , p < 0.01) to seek new paid work opportunities or start a business. Intrinsic (57%) and Community (51%) workers were also the most likely of any group to dedicate their time to unpaid volunteering or caregiving (vs. 45% of Status-Driven and 46% of Transactional workers, 36 Julian Jacobs 

p < 0.01). For Status-Driven workers, a low reservation threshold in their current job may reflect a willingness to use guaranteed income partly to finance upward career mobility or entrepreneurship ( SN W ≥ SW ), rather than detachment from work itself. Third, Hypothesis 3 directly engages with a puzzle in political economy literature, supposing that workers who value the non-monetary components of their job will express greater concern about AI-related employment impacts and react more strongly in their labor-market and political attitudes when facing high displacement threat. This hypothesis receives potential support in both stated labor-market behavior and in political attitudes, though more research is needed to validate this finding. This paper shows that Status -oriented and Community -oriented workers report the highest level of concern about the threat of AI replacing their job (Figure 3). Moreover, a perception of AI’s replacement threat was associated with a steeper decline in respondents’ reported reservation wages among Community and Intrinsically -motivated workers than among Transactional workers. When I looked at the relationship between perceptions of AI’s job impacts and political attitudes (with a particular focus on the UK) (Table 6), we find that Transactional workers express more negative baseline views of AI’s macro-political impact. Yet when we isolate for belief that AI will cause adverse effects on both a person’s job and their industry’s job quality and job counts (Figure 9, Panel A, and Appendix Table A5), we find clearer potential evidence to support Hypothesis 3 . Specifically, I show that Intrinsic , Community , and Status -driven workers exhibit a significantly steeper negative political reaction than Transactional workers. Several important methodological limitations warrant careful discussion. The key limitation of this study is its reliance on cross-sectional observational survey data, which cannot credibly establish causal relationships between perceptions of AI displacement threats, work values, and political attitudes. In particular, the Work Values constructed in this paper may themselves be endogenous and downstream of job quality, among other factors. Workers in rewarding, autonomous jobs may naturally prioritize intrinsic fulfillment, whereas workers in routinized or precarious roles may defensively adopt a transactional orientation toward work. Yet two empirical checks in Section 5 should help temper some of this concern. When we control directly for standardized job satisfaction, we still find a distinct Intrinsic reservation wage premium intact. Meanwhile, controlling for respondents’ perceived confidence in finding an equally good outside job strengthens the Intrinsic premium. Conceptually, therefore, Work Values Profiles are best interpreted not as fixed psychological traits, but instead as a worker’s realized relationship within their current job—capturing part of what they stand to lose if that role is automated. Still, it remains possible that other unobservables also shape these relationships. Likewise, it is plausible that politically alienated or generally pessimistic individuals selectively perceive greater personal AI job threat, rather than AI threats causing political alienation. We cannot credibly determine the direction of this relationship within this paper’s modeling. Similarly, workers who already hold anti-establishment views may retrospectively rationalize their political attitudes by emphasizing technology-related grievances. Additionally, although the profile validation What is Work For? Labor, Politics, and Meaning in the Age of AI 37 analysis in Section 4 (Appendix Table A4) shows that Work Values Profiles are statistically not heavily related to income class and pre-existing partisan ideology, this does not rule out the possibility that unobserved psychological dispositions—such as dispositional anxiety, risk aversion, or general negativity bias—simultaneously drive both higher perceived AI threat and more intense political backlash. A second methodological consideration involves the exclusion of the N = 2 ,010 unclassified (“Other”) respondents (24% of the total sample, comprising N = 1 ,128 non-working adults and N = 882 working respondents with four-way straight-lined or ≥ 3.0 domain scores on the original 1–4 importance scale, i.e., < 3.0 reverse-coded) and the choice of the < 3.0 importance cutoff. Although excluding this sample ensures that all profile contrasts compare active workers with a clearly differentiated priority, it also restricts us to the N = 6 ,224 classified respondents. Relatedly, our Step 2 tie-breaking rule assigns the N = 2 ,000 respondents who tied at their maximum score (32% of classified workers) to the empirically rarer domain. This then frequently places workers who rated both pay and a non-monetary dimension at the ceiling into a non-monetary profile. While this makes our contrasts against the Transactional baseline conservative (since tied non-monetary workers also attach high importance to pay), continuous four-index PCA models—which remove the discrete categories and tie-breaking altogether—suggest both the positive Intrinsic effect and negative Status effect across the continuous workforce. Meanwhile, Appendix Table A11 rebuilds the Work Values Profiles across five alternative cutoffs ( < 2.0, 

< 2.5, < 3.0 baseline, < 3.5, and ≤ 4.0 [no minimum cutoff]) across 12 headline models. Across all five thresholds, the estimates remain virtually unchanged in both magnitude and significance, including the reservation income contrast and the AI threat slope contrast on perceived negative political impact. Third, the evidence presented in the paper on reservation wages, while striking in its consistency, also warrants epistemic modesty. The iterative auction-style model captures stated willingness to quit at hypothetical income replacement thresholds. As is common in economic surveys, we cannot capture revealed labor market behavior. No respondent actually left their job as a consequence of participating in the survey. Behavioral economics offers an abundance of evidence that preferences articulated in surveys can diverge significantly from real-world decisions, outside the bounds of a survey (Hausman, 2012). For instance, respondents may overstate their actual attachment to work when the choice is a costless survey, as opposed to a real-life decision with implications. So it can be difficult to truly substantively interpret the 20% of respondents who refused to quit at 120% of their current salary, and how they would actually behave in the real world. At the same time, excluding the 20% of right-censored respondents who refused all offers up to 120% of salary means that our baseline OLS estimates capture conservative lower-bound differences among uncensored compliers ( N = 4 ,860), whereas retaining right-censored observations via our two-part hurdle model, top-coded bounds (120% to 200%), and parametric right-censored Tobit maximum likelihood estimation increases the 38 Julian Jacobs

## 8 Conclusion 

This paper set out to better understand the ways in which people value their work, including the significance of employment’s non-monetary value. To look at this question, I created an original cross-national survey of working-age adults across the United Kingdom, the United States, Denmark, and India, fielded by More in Common and examining the pecuniary, intrinsic, communal, and status-driven motivations for work attachment. I then traced how non-monetary workplace attachments shaped the guaranteed replacement income workers would demand to quit their job, how people would spend their time if they could no longer work, and whether the non-pecuniary value of work shapes the way people respond to AI. I highlight four findings in particular. First, 81% of respondents across all occupations reported being satisfied with their jobs. Meanwhile, a majority of workers valued their roles for non-monetary reasons. Indeed, using a novel Work Values profile classification, I showed that one-third of workers say they are intrinsically motivated by work, finding their job valuable for its own sake. Meanwhile, one quarter of survey respondents value their job because of the community and social opportunities it brings them, and 17% of respondents say they value their job because of the recognition and prestige it offers. On the other side, just over one-quarter of respondents valued their job principally for its pay and benefits. Furthermore, non-monetary work attachment can be found in near-equal measure across income levels and within both blue and white collar occupations. Second, most people say they would choose to leave their job if offered a full guaranteed income replacement. Yet 20% of workers said they would stay in their job even if offered up to 120% as guaranteed income. This share is consistent across the income distribution and occupational categories, but it is significantly higher for intrinsically motivated workers, who attached significant non-monetary value to their work. Third, non-monetary work attachment is associated with divergences in how people would spend their time if they received a guaranteed income but could not work in their current role. Specifically, transactionally-motivated workers said they would functionally retire, while people attaching non-monetary value to their job said they would volunteer or pursue further work or business opportunities. Finally, I find potential evidence that non-pecuniary work attachment plays a moderating role in shaping the political and economic backlash to AI; however, I note more research is needed to understand these dynamics. The findings from this paper appear to carry significant implications for how governments ought to design social and economic policies to manage the AI transition. While income transfer provisions—such as basic income or negative income tax programs—may be necessary to preserve material livelihoods for workers who experience job displacement, they are unlikely to be sufficient .What is Work For? Labor, Politics, and Meaning in the Age of AI 39 Indeed, as this paper shows, workers appear to attach significant non-monetary value to their jobs, across the income distribution and occupational groups. Income transfers can fail entirely to compensate for this non-pecuniary value of work. And such a finding is entirely in line with an abundance of prior literature—cited in this paper—that points toward the socio-cultural and psychological salience of formal employment. Governments and researchers may need to adjust for these non-pecuniary aspects of work when conjuring ideas to support people during periods of economic change. Of course, if AI does indeed induce a major transformation of the labor market, people may find entirely new replacements for the non-monetary value they previously identified in their work. Such an outcome may require entirely new systems for economic redistribution and benefit sharing. Regardless of whichever scenario of AI-enabled economic change the world experiences, disruptions to work may force society to wrestle with pressing questions about how to protect living standards, purpose, community, and dignity for workers as the world changes. 40 Julian Jacobs
