## Abstract 

We study the problem of fair ranking in recommender systems. We provide a for-mal probabilistic framework in which ranking policies are distributions over permutations, and the utility of a user is the exposure given to the items they interact with, relative to that of an ideal ranking. We consider util-ity parity across user groups, as well as its category-aware refinement, conditional util-ity parity, which requires parity within each category of items. Following a recent view of fairness as distributional stability, we show that both criteria are equivalent to the stabil-ity of the utility under changes in the compo-sition of user groups. Optimizing over mix-tures of ranking policies leads to a second-order cone program, for which we provide generalization bounds. A numerical study validates our theoretical findings. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recommender Systems (RS), often trained as learning-to-rank models, play an increasing role in access to op-portunities. On job platforms, the offers displayed to a candidate are ordered according to their predicted relevance to the candidate’s profile. When trained on historical interaction data, these models can reproduce disparities related to sensitive attributes such as gen-der. For example, women may see fewer high-paying or high-responsibility positions than men (Imana et al., 2021). Since candidates primarily interact with the highest-ranked offers, what each user sees at the top of their ranking determines the quality of service they receive, and can ultimately affect their career path. A natural answer is to require comparable utility across user groups, where the utility of a ranking mea-sures how much exposure it gives to the offers the user is interested in. We refer to this criterion as utility parity. Existing works on user-side fairness in recom-mendation compare a single utility, aggregated over all offers (Li et al., 2021; Wang and Joachims, 2021; Yao and Huang, 2017). Such aggregate parity can be mis-leading: a ranking model may satisfy it while favour-ing one group on some categories of offers, such as ad-ministrative positions, and the other group on others, such as managerial positions, so that the gaps cancel out in aggregate. A similar phenomenon is known in classification, where parity on a population can hide disparities within its subgroups (Kearns et al., 2018). Recent work has pointed out this limitation and pro-posed item category-aware criteria (Kheya et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). Parity, whether aggregate or per category, also has a dynamic reading. On a job platform, the share of women among job seekers may change over time, for instance when the platform opens to new sectors or regions, and as long as a gap remains, the average quality of the recommendations rises or falls with this balance. We build on this reading, drawing inspira-tion from Taturyan et al. (2026), who view fairness in classification as the stability of a prediction rate under perturbations of group proportions, and learn fair clas-sifiers as mixtures of reweighted predictors. Extending this view to ranking is far from straightforward, due to the global nature of this learning task. For each observation, a classifier predicts a label, which can be modified without changing the other predicted labels. In contrast, a ranking policy organizes the entire cat-alogue: each item is assigned a specific position in the list, so that its exposure depends on the position of all other items. And changing the rank of one item can affect the rank of (possibly many) other items. We therefore represent ranking policies through the expo-sure they allocate. The utility of a user then depends linearly on this exposure, so that average utility plays the role of the prediction rate, and the utility of a mix-ture of policies is exactly the mixture of their utilities. The stability view thus carries over to ranking, and arXiv:2610.40182v1 [stat.ML] 29 Oct 2026 Hidden Disparities in Ranking: Category-Aware Fairness via Distributional Stability 

the resulting problem is solved exactly over mixtures of policies, whereas in classification the risk of a mix-ture is handled through a surrogate. The framework has several benefits. First, since the utility of a ranking is additive over the offers it con-tains, it decomposes according to their category, and category-aware constraints arise naturally in this for-mulation, leading to what we call conditional utility parity. Second, utility parity is equivalent to the sta-bility of utility under changes in the proportion of each user group, so that the guarantees hold uniformly over a range of group compositions. Third, the constraints can include a target of utility level per category, which prevents parity from being reached by lowering the utility that both groups derive from a category. 

Contributions First, we provide a formal probabilis-tic framework for recommendation setup (Section 2). Second, we formalize utility parity and conditional utility parity as stability properties, which yields a constraint controlling both the group gap and the util-ity level (Section 3). Third, considering mixtures of policies, we write the empirical problem as a second-order cone program (Section 4) and discuss its feasi-bility. Fourth, we provide finite-sample guarantees on fairness, deployment stability and utility, with explicit exposure rates for Plackett–Luce policies (Section 5). Finally, we conduct numerical experiments that con-firm our theory in practice (Section 6). 

Related Works There is a large amount of litera-ture on fair ranking, most of which focus on item-side fairness through allocated exposure. A semi-nal work of Singh and Joachims (2018) formulates fairness of exposure as a linear program over doubly stochastic matrices, from which stochastic rankings are sampled through a Birkhoff–von Neumann decompo-sition. Wang and Joachims (2021) build on this ap-proach to enforce user fairness, item fairness and di-versity together: they optimize both notions of fairness as a convex program, and derive the ranking policy through a new decomposition algorithm. Exposure-based fairness has also been integrated in the training of ranking policies, by backpropagating through con-strained optimization problems (Kotary et al., 2022; Dinh et al., 2024), or through efficient gradient esti-mators for Plackett–Luce policies (Oosterhuis, 2021). Closest to our work are the category-aware criteria of Kheya et al. (2025) and Wang et al. (2024). Kheya et al. (2025) compare the share of each category in the top-K lists of men and women and use the result-ing gap as a regularizer during training. Wang et al. (2024) require a recall-based metric of relevant items to be similar across all pairs of user and item groups, and reweight the training losses with a variant of group dis-tributionally robust optimization. Our criteria differ from both. Unlike the former, our utility only rewards exposure to relevant offers. Unlike the latter, we com-pare user groups within each category, without asking categories to be treated equally. Overall, despite the rich literature, most fair ranking methods are algorithmic and empirical, and do not rely on a probabilistic framework that would allow for sta-tistical guarantees. The existing guarantees are mostly of a different nature. For example, fair rank aggrega-tion algorithms come with approximation guarantees which bound how far their solution can be from the op-timum (Chakraborty et al., 2022, 2026, 2025). Statisti-cal guarantees have been provided in other setups, for instance in learning to rank, where a stochastic ranker built on a pretrained model is calibrated post hoc to guarantee a target level of utility, while item-exposure fairness is improved but not guaranteed (Guo et al., 2024), or in recommendation, where an online ban-dit algorithm decides with high probability whether some user would prefer another user’s recommenda-tions, without degrading the audited system’s perfor-mance by more than a fixed fraction (Do et al., 2022). In contrast, we provide finite-sample guarantees on both the user-side category-aware fairness and the util-ity of a learned ranking policy, with fairness gaps and utility levels controlled uniformly over a range of group compositions. As a result, we observe a clear trade-off curve between utility and fairness, which methods without guarantees cannot ensure.

2.3 Fairness as stability 

In a recent work, Taturyan et al. (2026) define fairness in classification as a property of distribu-tional stability. A classifier h : X → {0, 1} is 

ε–fair w.r.t. class of perturbation operators P, iff sup (D,P)∈M× P ∆( h; ( D, P)) ⩽ ε , where ∆( h; ( D, P)) is a statistical distance between the distributions of the prediction h(X) under D ∈ M and under P(D). The authors show, that the individual and group fair-ness can both be expressed as stability under specific choices of P and ∆. For group fairness, the perturba-tions change the composition of the sensitive groups.

Choosing the trade-off in practice. For a prac-titioner, the level of fairness is a choice that must be made after seeing its cost in utility, and possibly re-vised: an explicit front is therefore as important as the quality of a single operating point. Our guaran-tees are what make such a front reliable: the budgets and targets set the gaps and utility levels directly, and Theorem 1 ensures that these settings carry over to new users, whereas ITFR and CatFAIR control fair-ness only through training hyperparameters, with no guarantee on the resulting gaps. With CUP, the dictio-nary is trained once (about 2 min per split), and each point of the front, set by interpretable targets, is a second-order cone program solved in less than 10 ms. With ITFR and CatFAIR, each point requires a full training (about 38 and 2 min on the same CPU), and finding the trade-off means cross-validating hyperpa-rameters whose effects on fairness are not monotone. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We studied user-side category-aware fair ranking through the lens of distributional stability. Represent-ing ranking policies through exposure, we showed that utility parity and its category-aware refinement can be viewed as the stability of utility under changes in the composition of the population, and that targets fix the level of service that each category keeps, what-ever this composition. Considering mixtures of policies leads to a second-order cone program, for which we provided finite-sample guarantees on stability, fairness, deployment and utility. Since it only combines exist-ing ranking policies, the approach is model-agnostic: it can be applied on top of the most accurate ranker available, and the trade-off between utility and fair-ness can be set, and revised, after training at negligible cost. Our experiments showed that aggregate parity can hide opposite disparities across categories, which conditional utility parity removes, illustrated the de-ployment guarantee on new populations, and showed that the targets give an explicit control of the trade-off while limiting levelling down. Our guarantees cover changes in the share of each group; changes in the be-haviour of each group are left for future work. Gayane Taturyan Charlotte Laclau Stephan Cl´ emen¸ con

D.1 Implementation Details Utility. Policies rank the candidates of a user by decreasing score, and the uniform policy places every candidate at every position with equal probability. The position weights are vj ∝ 1/ log 2(j + 1) for j ≤ K = 20 and 

vj = 0 beyond, so that the utility U (P, z) is the NDCG@20 with binary relevance. The utility of category a is 

U a(P, z) = P 

> i∈I a

ρi(P ; x) yi/V (z), with the same normalisation V (z) as U , so that P 

> a

U a = U .

Rankers. The history of a user is encoded with PureSVD: Q holds the top d right singular vectors of the binary history matrix of the training users, and eu = huQ for any user with history hu. The features of a pair (u, i ) are ⟨eu, q i⟩, eu ⊙ qi, log(1 + ni), where ni is the number of training histories containing i, and the length of the history of u. The sensitive attribute is never a feature. Rankers are LambdaMART models trained with LightGBM (150 trees, learning rate 0 .1, 31 leaves, at least 40 samples per leaf, row and column subsampling 0.9 and 0 .8), on all relevant candidates of the training users and nneg non-relevant candidates per user drawn at random. Historical bias. The recorded interactions keep each true interest, except those of women in managerial offers and of men in administrative offers, which are kept with probability 1 − β only. The two biases have the same strength, so that the aggregate gap remains small while the gaps of the two categories have opposite signs. 

Histories, candidates and splits. For each user, the catalogue is split at random, independently of G, into a history pool (60% of the offers) and a candidate pool. The history is the set of recorded interactions in the history pool, and the candidates are the offers of the candidate pool. Users are split at random into training (50%), estimation (25%) and test (25%) users. The relevant candidates are the recorded interactions for training users, and the true interests for estimation and test users. ITFR and MF-BPR. We use the code of Wang et al. (2024), with minimal changes that leave the training algorithm untouched (device handling, and calls deprecated in recent versions of NumPy and PyTorch). The user groups are the two genders and the item groups the six genres. The training interactions are the histories of all users and 90% of the relevant items of the training users; the remaining 10% form the validation set. The relevant items of estimation and test users are never used. We keep the hyperparameters of the authors for MovieLens-1M: embeddings of dimension 64, Adam with learning rate 10 −3, no ℓ2 regularisation, batches of 1024, one negative per positive, η = 0 .05, γ = 0 .5, ρ = 0 .02 and τ = 0 .2. The model of the epoch with the best validation NDCG@20 within 100 epochs is kept. Candidates are scored by the cosine between user and item embeddings, as in ITFR, and ranked deterministically like the rankers of the dictionary. MF-BPR is trained with the same code and hyperparameters, without the sharpness-aware perturbation and with group weights equal to the share of each group in the batch, which gives the BPR loss; it keeps the normalisation of the scores. 

CatFAIR. We re-implement in PyTorch the MF model of the code of Kheya et al. (2025), on the same training interactions and validation set as ITFR. The model has 40 factors, user and item biases and a global bias, initialised as in their code, and is trained with the BPR loss, one negative per positive drawn uniformly, Adam with learning rate 10 −3, weight decay 0 .02 and batches of 1024. For the users of each batch, a differentiable top-50 over all items (hard in the forward pass, sigmoid(( s − s50 )/0.01) in the backward pass) gives the share of each genre in their list; the regularizer is the sum over genres of the absolute difference between the mean shares of women and men (eq. 10 of their paper), passed through sigmoid(0 .1 ( g − 0.5)) and weighted by α against the BPR loss, as in their code. Training stops when the validation NDCG@20 has not improved by 0 .0005 for 10 epochs, within 100 epochs. We use their grid α ∈ { 0, 0.1, . . . , 0.6}, where α = 0 .6, and α = 0 gives the backbone. Candidates are scored by the inner product plus biases. The variant of the released code, which divides the share of each genre by its share in the catalogue, gives comparable results.
