# AI Cosmos: An Agentic Infra for Situated Learning 

AI Cosmos Team 

Tsinghua University (For the complete list of authors, please refer to the Contribution section) Large language models (LLMs) can improve task performance during learning, yet these gains do not necessarily reflect learners’ ability to thereafter apply the knowledge and skills independently. We present AI Cosmos (https://aicosmos.ai/), an agentic infrastructure for situated learning that organizes learning around two complementary principles: scenario-based participation and cumulative assets of practice. AI Cosmos organizes learners and role-specialized agents within learning scenarios that integrate task-relevant knowledge and, when needed, executable workbenches. An asset–memory loop preserves the processes and outcomes of practice as reusable assets with explicit provenance and dependencies. We evaluate scenario-based interaction through two preregistered randomized controlled studies examining the acquisition of legal reasoning and programming competence, holding the same underlying LLM across conditions. AI Cosmos achieves greater learning gains than a conventional LLM in legal reasoning and higher mastery of unseen programming problems than an LLM-as-Teacher baseline.

1arXiv:2610.40031v1 [cs.CL] 29 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

In 1929, philosopher Alfred North Whitehead described the problem of inert knowledge in his seminal collection “The Aims of Education”: “...the problem of keeping knowledge alive, of preventing it from becoming inert, which is the central problem of all education” (Whitehead, 1929). Nearly a century later, Whitehead’s diagnosis remains relevant, despite successive advances in educational technologies. Today, generative AI appears to offer a promising remedy to this enduring problem by providing intelligence that is continuously accessible, highly interactive, and readily adaptable into explanations and personalized tutorials (Ruiz-Rojas et al., 2023; Baidoo-Anu and Ansah, 2023; Ooi et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024). Yet, this remedy may not close the gap between knowing and doing but instead widen it in a new form. Across educational settings, learners become more fluent in reproducing AI-provided concepts, explanations, and solutions (Handa et al., 2025) without commensurate gains in their ability to apply that knowledge independently (Fan et al., 2024a; Bastani et al., 2025a), a discrepancy we refer to as performance–competence decoupling 

(Yan et al., 2025). For instance, an undergraduate student may be able to explain the divide-and-conquer tactic of quicksort yet struggle to implement it robustly (Lehmann et al., 2025); similarly, a bar-exam candidate can fluently state a legal doctrine yet fails to apply it to an unfamiliar case (Kowalski, 2010). These examples reflect a structural asymmetry: generative AI has substantially reduced the cost of accessing and reproducing declarative knowledge (“knowing what”) (Usdan et al., 2024), without producing an equivalent improvement in procedural knowledge (“knowing how”) (Lee et al., 2025). 

We should note that this asymmetry no longer stems from LLM capability, but from the interaction scheme in which these models are used (Ding and Chan, 2023; Shi et al., 2024). Despite the diversity of AI-assisted educational applications, from content creation to lesson planning (Yu et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2024b; Zheng et al., 2025), a common interaction pattern is the request–artifact exchange , where the learner submits a request, the system produces an explanation or artifact, and the interaction often ends once that artifact is delivered (Chao et al., 2026). Question answering is such an epistemic outsourcing example, more accurate and pedagogically appropriate responses may improve immediate task performance without requiring learners to reason through, test, or revise solutions themselves. The limitation, therefore, is not simply the quality of generated outputs, but whether the interaction supports the activities through which competence develops. Learning research emphasizes active participation, feedback, and iterative revision within meaningful, scaffolded activities (Chi and Wylie, 2014; Lave and Wenger, 1991; Collins and Kapur, 2006; Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007). When assistance is organized primarily around output delivery, learners’ actions, feedback, and revisions may remain outside the interaction, limiting opportunities to support continued practice and use its outcomes to inform subsequent learning (Laurillard, 2013; Beale, 2025). Recent LLM-based tutoring systems structure pedagogical dialogue and feedback to support learners’ reasoning (Chowdhury et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), while general-purpose multi-agent frameworks coordinate role-specialized agents to complete complex tasks (Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024). Yet the effectiveness of these systems in supporting learners’ sustained competence development cannot be inferred from the quality of instructional guidance or the success of agent task execution alone. One consideration is agency-preserving participation , which emphasizes learners’ active engagement in explaining, testing, and revising their own work within authentic or scaffolded activities, rather than merely receiving generated solutions (Lave and Wenger, 1991; Chi and Wylie, 2014). Tool-using agents are often designed for autonomous task execution (Yao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024a); when applied to educational settings, such designs may position learners primarily as requesters or evaluators of completed work. A second consideration is maintaining cumulative, inspectable record of practice . Constructionism emphasizes the educational value of artifacts that learners can create, examine, and improve over time (Papert and Harel, 1991). Agentic systems also accumulate and refine reusable skills from past experience (Zhang et al., 2026a), although their reported gains primarily concern subsequent agent task performance rather than learners’ use of prior practice. 2Taken together, these considerations motivate an approach to AI-assisted learning that centers learners’ own reasoning and action within learning activities, while enabling subsequent activities to build on prior practice. We translate these considerations into two complementary architectural shifts. The first shift moves from 

request-artifact exchange to scenario-based participation , which organizes learning around learners’ active involvement in socially mediated, tool-supported activities (Lave and Wenger, 1991; Kaptelinin and Nardi, 1997). Each scenario integrates three configurable dimensions of practice: social (learners and role-specialized agents as co-participants), epistemic (task-relevant knowledge resources), and operational (stateful, executable workbenches, i.g., a coding IDE„ when needed). Learners and agents coordinate through dialogue and, when applicable, perform task-relevant operations on shared workbench states. The resulting observations inform subsequent dialogue and actions, supporting learners in applying knowledge, evaluating outcomes, and adapting their approaches through practice. The second shift moves from one-off artifacts to cumulative assets of practice . Rather than treating final outputs as the endpoint of learning activities, this shift makes the processes and outcomes of practice available for reuse. Subsequent scenarios can thus build on accumulated experience, supporting continuity across learning activities. Together, these shifts place learners’ active participation and the accumulation of practical experience at the center of AI-assisted learning, shifting the emphasis from task completion to the development of learners’ competence (Kolb, 2014; Schön, 2017). We instantiate these shifts in AI Cosmos (https://aicosmos.ai/), an agentic infrastructure for situated learning that organizes learner–agent participation in structured scenarios and preserves records of practice for reuse. A scenario control plane configures each learning activity from a learner’s request by specifying its objective, participant roles, relevant knowledge resources, and executable workbenches when needed. A task execution plane coordinates dialogue and workbench operations over shared task states, dynamically scheduling learners and agents to participate in iterative problem solving, execution, and revision. To connect successive scenarios, an asset–memory loop extracts and validates reusable assets from scenario configurations and records of practice. By retaining provenance and dependencies, these assets make prior outcomes and the associated reasoning, feedback, and revisions available for inspection and adaptation in subsequent activities. We evaluate scenario-based interaction through two preregistered randomized controlled studies examining the acquisition of legal reasoning and programming competence, finding greater learning gains than a conventional LLM in the former and stronger programming mastery than an LLM-as-Teacher baseline in the latter.

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Human Study: Situated Learning for Bar-Examination Preparation 

Legal problem solving provides a demanding test of application-oriented learning: success requires not only recalling legal doctrine but also identifying relevant rules and applying them to novel fact patterns. We therefore use bar-examination preparation to test whether restructuring LLM interaction from conventional chat into a structured scenario improves learning of applied legal reasoning. 

4.1.1 Study Design and Measures 

Design and participants. We conducted a pre-registered, three-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial. A total of 131 students were assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three learning conditions: (i) vanilla LLM , a conventional request–response assistant, (ii) LLM-as-Teacher in which the same LLM was prompted to adopt an expert-tutor, or (iii) AI Cosmos . All conditions used the same underlying LLM, holding model capability constant while varying the interaction architecture. Following our preregistered data-integrity criteria, we excluded participants whose learning logs failed to meet minimum-engagement requirements (e.g., insufficient interaction or exceptionally short inputs), leaving an analyzed sample of 𝑁 = 111 (37 per arm). We observed no statistically significant between-group differences in the measured pre-intervention characteristics, including sex, prior legal training, age, AI literacy, self-efficacy, and pre-test performance (all 

𝑝 ≥ 0.27; Table 1 in Appendix A.1). 

Procedure and measures. Participants completed four phases: (1) Pre-test : An AI-literacy inventory, the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES, Schwarzer and Jerusalem (1995)), and a legal-knowledge assessment covering civil and criminal law. This assessment combined objective single- and multiple-choice items with open-response questions. (2) Learning : Participants studied matched civil- and criminal-law problems using only their assigned system. (3) Immediate post-test : A new, difficulty-matched legal assessment with the same composition, followed by the GSES and the NASA-TLX workload index (Hart and Staveland, 1988). (4) 

Delayed post-test (≈ 4 weeks later): A randomly selected subsample ( 𝑛 = 18 per condition) completed a new assessment of the same composition to probe retention. All legal assessments were scored on a 0–100 scale, with objective items weighted at 80% and open-response items at 20%. The open responses were scored by three law PhDs whose ratings showed high agreement (Cronbach’s 𝛼 = 0.95 pre-test, 0 .96 post-test) and were averaged. The outcome was the learning gain , defined as the post-test minus pre-test change in total score. 

Statistical analysis. We report group means and standard deviations for all primary outcomes. Unadjusted differences in learning gain were examined using one-way ANOVA 1, with the Kruskal–Wallis test as a nonparametric robustness check. Shapiro–Wilk tests did not reject normality of the gain distributions (all 

𝑝 > 0.05), and Levene’s test indicated no evidence of unequal variances ( 𝑝 = 0.95). The primary between-group inference used ANCOVA on immediate post-test scores, with pre-test score entered as a covariate. This approach adjusts for chance baseline variation and generally provides greater precision than analysis of raw change scores. The homogeneity-of-regression-slopes assumption was supported by the condition-by-pre-test interaction test ( 𝑝 = 0.77). We report the omnibus ANCOVA effect together with 𝜂 2 and model-adjusted contrasts between AI Cosmos and the control conditions. Durability. At the four-week follow-up, the descriptive ordering observed immediately after learning was preserved. 4.2 Human Study: An Instrumented Workbench for Skill Acquisition in Competitive Pro-gramming 

Whereas the bar-examination study (§4.1) primarily examined scenario-based social interaction, this study focuses on the operational dimension: the executable workbench. Competitive programming provides a demanding test of procedural competence, because success requires learners not only to understand an algorithm but also to implement, execute, and debug it as a correct and efficient program. We thus examine whether integrating an executable coding workbench into the learning interaction improves the translation of conceptual understanding into implementation skill, and whether the effect varies with prior aptitude. 

4.2.1 Study Design and Measures 

Design and participants. We conducted a preregistered, three-arm randomized controlled study stratified by two aptitude-defined curriculum tracks. From the 140 students recruited, 100 completed the diagnostic pre-test, and 54 completed the full multi-session protocol and constituted the analysis sample (93% male; mean age 18 .9 years; 89% first-year undergraduates). Participants were assigned to one of three learning conditions: (i) 

Vanilla LLM , a unconstrained conventional request–response assistant; (ii) LLM-as-Teacher baseline; and (iii) 

AI Cosmos , which used the same underlying model within a configured scenario equipped with an executable coding workbench for implementation, execution, and iterative debugging. Both LLM-as-Teacher and AI Cosmos conditions followed the same restriction against supplying complete solutions, providing a focused comparison of the additional operational affordances available in AI Cosmos. The Vanilla LLM condition did not impose this restriction. All three conditions used the same base model, which was independently verified to be capable of solving the experimental tasks. The diagnostic pre-test was designed to align training content with prior mastery. Participants who had not yet mastered search and dynamic programming entered the 

basic-algorithm track , whereas those who had mastered these topics but not the advanced material entered the 

data-structure track , which covered segment trees and leftist heaps. Participants already proficient in both sets of material did not proceed to the training phase. Within each track, participants were randomized across the three conditions ( 𝑛 = 19 AI Cosmos, 18 LLM-as-Teacher, and 17 Vanilla LLM overall; cell sizes 7–11). Procedure and measures. Each participant completed two training sessions organized around a fixed three-stage practice cycle: (1) Learn : participants studied one problem interactively with their assigned system and submitted a solution to the online judge (OJ). The post-test thus assessed generalization of the trained implementation skills to novel problems. To minimize benchmark contamination and memorization, all problems used in the study were newly authored by two competitive-programming experts with NOI/ACM gold-medal credentials and had no online counterparts. 4.2.2 Results 

Skill acquisition. Post-test scores showed a descriptive advantage for AI Cosmos (68 .0 ± 32 .3), followed by the vanilla LLM (61 .5 ± 28 .1) and LLM-as-Teacher (49 .8 ± 31 .7; Figure 7b). The omnibus difference was not statistically significant at this sample size ( 𝐹 (2, 51 ) = 1.65, 𝑝 = 0.20, 𝜂 2 = 0.061; Kruskal–Wallis 

𝑝 = 0.22), and the ANCOVA group effect after adjusting for pre-test score and training track showed a similar pattern ( 𝐹 (2, 49 ) = 1.89, 𝑝 = 0.16, partial 𝜂 2 = 0.072). Despite the limited statistical power, the pairwise estimates reveal a consistent architectural advantage. AI Cosmos outperformed the LLM-as-Teacher 15 baseline by 18 .2 points, yielding a medium effect size (Welch 𝑡 (35 .0) = 1.73, 𝑝 = 0.092; permutation 

𝑝 = 0.091; Mann–Whitney 𝑝 = 0.096; Hedges’ 𝑔 = 0.56, 95% CI [− 0.06 , 1.30 ]). In contrast, the LLM-as-Teacher condition produced the lowest numerical performance, scoring 11 .7 points below the vanilla assistant ( 𝑔 = −0.38). Within-condition analyses further show that relative to their respective pre-tests, participants using AI Cosmos and vanilla LLM both improved significantly on trained material (paired 𝑡 -tests: 

𝑝 = 0.0004 and 𝑝 = 0.0008, respectively; 𝑑 𝑧 = 1.00), whereas the LLM-as-Teacher condition showed only a non-significant increase ( 𝑝 = 0.12, 𝑑 𝑧 = 0.39). Together, these results suggest that integrating guidance with an executable environment provides a stronger pathway from conceptual explanation to procedural programming ability than dialogue-based guidance alone. 

Equal scores, unequal mastery. The similar aggregate scores of AI Cosmos and vanilla LLM conceal a more fundamental difference in what learners actually achieved (Figure 8). Both conditions produced successful online-judge submissions at comparable rates (82% vs. 85%), explaining their similar raw scores. Yet, automated judging primarily measures whether a program passes test cases, not whether the learner has acquired the intended algorithmic skill. Among submissions receiving positive OJ credit, AI Cosmos achieved criterion mastery (grade ≥G4) in 68% of cases, compared with 45% for vanilla LLM and 32% for LLM-as-Teacher (AI Cosmos vs. LLM-as-Teacher, Fisher’s exact 𝑝 = 0.009; vs. vanilla, 𝑝 = 0.12). Conversely, non-mastery passing solutions, i.e., submissions that obtained test credit without substantially implementing the target algorithm, were less frequent under AI Cosmos (26%) than under vanilla LLM (47%) or LLM-as-Teacher (53%; AI Cosmos vs. LLM-as-Teacher, 𝑝 = 0.031). The results reveal a central limitation of score-based evaluation in AI-assisted programming: passing tests and acquiring transferable procedural knowledge are not identical outcomes. While a conventional assistant may help learners produce partially successful solutions, AI Cosmos’s executable workbench encourages learners to construct, test, and repair their own implementations, resulting in a higher proportion of solutions that reflect genuine algorithmic understanding. Thus, the primary advantage of AI Cosmos is not merely higher task performance, but a closer alignment between measured performance and underlying competence. 

Criterion mastery. Because automated scores can obscure differences in solution quality, we next examined criterion mastery: whether learners produced substantially complete and correct implementations of the target algorithms (grade ≥G4). This measure captures a stricter form of procedural competence than passing test cases alone (Figure 9a). AI Cosmos learners achieved criterion mastery on an average of 1 .11 out of the two post-test problems, compared with 0 .76 for vanilla LLM and 0 .50 for LLM-as-Teacher. The difference between AI Cosmos and LLM-as-Teacher was significant (Mann–Whitney 𝑝 = 0.024). # 5 Related Work 

AI-Assisted Learning and Pedagogically Structured Interaction Evidence from mathematics and programming education shows that improved performance with LLM assistance does not necessarily result in better performance on subsequent unassisted assessments (Bastani et al., 2025b; Bassner et al., 2025). Research on LLM tutoring has investigated ways to guide students during problem solving rather than directly supply answers. Earlier work uses annotated tutoring dialogues (Macina et al., 2023) and explicit pedagogical policies to study and organize multi-turn guidance (Pal Chowdhury et al., 2024; Puech et al., 2025). More recent methods train pedagogical policies (Dinucu-Jianu et al., 2025), adapt feedback using learner-state estimates and task verification (Wang et al., 2025), and maintain instructional plans across interactions (Li et al., 2026a; David and Ghosh, 2026). Tutoring benchmarks further assess instructional behavior beyond answer correctness (Maurya et al., 2025; Macina et al., 2025). These studies demonstrate progress in adaptive instructional support. However, evaluations of tutoring quality or success on guided tasks do not by themselves establish what learners can do independently. An important question is how students’ own actions during assisted problem solving affect their later performance on unfamiliar tasks without assistance.

Knowledge Building and Reusable Practice Records Learning activities can produce resources that remain useful after a task is completed. In knowledge-building environments, students improve shared ideas through successive contributions, while Learning by Design incorporates earlier design attempts into later inquiry (Scardamalia and Bereiter, 2010; Kolodner et al., 2003). More recent tools make learners’ activity records accessible: Watanabe et al. (2025) combines programming and browsing histories and identifies distinct problem-solving patterns that are not evident from final submissions alone. Related research on LLM agents shows that experience can also be reused for subsequent execution. Agent Workflow Memory extracts reusable routines and improves web-task success (Wang et al., 2024b), G-Memory retrieves collaboration histories together with generalized insights (Zhang et al., 2026c), and Skill-Pro validates procedural skills for reuse across tasks and agents (Mi et al., 2026). These results establish two uses of prior activity: improving an agent’s later actions and helping people examine and revise earlier work (Zhang et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026b). # 6 Conclusions 

In this paper, we introduce AI Cosmos, an agentic infrastructure for situated learning that organizes AI-assisted learning around two complementary principles: scenario-based participation and cumulative assets of practice. Through the Scenario–Asset Framework, AI Cosmos enables learners and role-specialized agents to participate in structured learning activities, while preserving the processes and outcomes of practice as reusable assets across scenarios. Two preregistered controlled studies in learning legal reasoning and competitive programming demonstrate the potential of structured participation to improve learners’ independent competence.

A.3 Expert Re-grading Rubric: Mastery Scale and Error Codebook 

To convert the coarse, test-case-based online-judge (OJ) score into a measure of genuine algorithmic competence (§4.2), two NOI/ACM gold-medalist experts re-graded every submission, blind to arm, using the two-part rubric reproduced here: a five-level mastery scale (G1–G5; Table 3) that summarizes how completely and correctly a submission realizes the problem’s target algorithm , and an error codebook (Table 4) that records the specific failure mode underlying that grade. Each submission is tagged in the form [code:name] 

together with a grade. Throughout, the “target algorithm” is the intended solution for the problem at hand (e.g., flood-fill or dynamic programming on the basic-algorithm track; a segment tree or leftist heap on the data-structure track). Two grading conventions are central and explain why the expert grade can diverge from the raw OJ score. Second, every submission that “looks like the target algorithm but falls short of full marks” was re-run locally and assigned a specific failure code before being graded, so that the grade reflects a diagnosed failure mode rather than the OJ verdict alone. The criterion-mastery threshold used in the main text is grade ≥ G4 (i.e., G4 or G5); the “flawless-implementation” rate ( EOK ) reported there is exactly the G5 rate. The two parts are coupled: the error code localizes what went wrong, and the mastery grade places that failure on a single ordinal scale of target-algorithm competence. Thus an E06 (brute-force) or N02 (non-target) submission is graded G1 however many test cases it passes, an E02 (incomplete skeleton) typically maps to G2–G3, a submission with only minor defects maps to G4, and an EOK submission is G5.
