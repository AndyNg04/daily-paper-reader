Abstract 

Rubric-based rewards provide fine-grained, in-terpretable supervision for open-ended rein-forcement learning, where holistic scalar re-wards struggle to capture multidimensional response quality. Static rubrics can lose discriminative power as the policy distribu-tion evolves, while existing policy–rubric co-evolution methods rely on globally prescribed update schedules that ignore heterogeneous re-ward informativeness. We introduce RACE, a Reward-Adaptive Co-Evolution framework. At the group level, per-rollout-group training-signal routing classifies groups as informative, diversity-collapsed, or rubric-failed, updating the policy only with informative groups while caching rubric failures. At the phase level, event-triggered phase switching enters rubric optimization upon persistent reward saturation and returns when rubric-reward improvement plateaus. Rubric phases train the generator on cached pairs using a frozen judge and a scale-normalized pairwise-margin reward. Across both levels, online threshold adaptation ad-justs routing and switching thresholds through proportional control. Under a unified evalua-tion protocol, RACE achieves the highest aver-age performance and outperforms the strongest fixed-alternation baseline on all eight tasks, while improving rubric discrimination. 

1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) has substantially im-proved large language model alignment (Schulman et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Shao et al., 2024), yet open-ended tasks remain challenging due to the limitations of scalar rewards in capturing multidi-mensional quality (Gao et al., 2023). Rubric-based rewards address this issue with fine-grained, inter-pretable criteria (Liu et al., 2026b; Huang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). How-ever, existing methods often rely on static rubrics or fixed generators, which may lose alignment and discriminative power as policies evolve (Gunjal et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2026; Viswanathan et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a). Rather than keeping rubrics fixed, policy–rubric co-evolution updates the policy and rubric gener-ator in tandem, allowing each to adapt to changes induced by the other (Li et al., 2026). Exist-ing approaches realize this idea through joint op-timization within a single model (Sheng et al., 2026), fixed-schedule alternation between policy and rubric phases (Li et al., 2026), or adversar-ial interaction at each training step (Ding et al., 2026). However, these methods rely on globally prescribed update schedules and ignore prompt-level reward informativeness. Because reward de-generacy is heterogeneous, some rollout groups receive nearly identical rewards as the policy im-proves, causing normalized advantages to vanish (advantage collapse ) (He et al., 2026). Uniform up-dates therefore waste policy compute on collapsed groups and rubric compute on adaptations that may be premature, delayed, or unnecessary. To address this limitation, we introduce RACE, a Reward-Adaptive Co-Evolution framework that coordinates policy and rubric optimization accord-ing to reward informativeness. RACE has three components. First, per-rollout-group training-signal routing uses reward discrimination and response diversity to route informative groups to policy optimization, cache rubric failures for rubric generator optimization, and mask diversity-collapsed groups from policy updates. Second, 

event-triggered phase switching enters rubric op-timization when reward saturation persists and re-turns to policy optimization when rubric-reward improvement plateaus, replacing fixed alternation with state-dependent transitions. Third, the rubric generator is optimized on cached failure pairs us-ing a frozen judge and a scale-normalized pairwise-margin reward, encouraging rubrics that restore discrimination where the current reward signal is 1arXiv:2610.40096v1 [cs.AI] 29 Oct 2026 insufficient. Online threshold adaptation adjusts the routing and phase-switching thresholds in re-sponse to evolving training dynamics. Together, these mechanisms route updates at both the rollout-group and phase levels, allowing the policy to learn only from informative rewards while adapting the rubric only where and when needed. Empirically, under a unified evaluation protocol spanning mathematics, code generation, reason-ing, knowledge, and instruction following, RACE achieves the best average downstream performance among compared methods. It also improves rubric discrimination over the strongest fixed-length alter-nation baseline on JudgeBench and RewardBench2 with fewer optimization steps. Further analyses show that RACE produces more specific, verifiable criteria and reduces policy-phase advantage col-lapse, demonstrating the effectiveness of adapting rubric supervision to evolving policies. 

2 Related Works 

2.1 Rubric-Based Reinforcement Learning 

Open-ended tasks require multidimensional eval-uation beyond binary correctness. Reinforcement Learning with Rubric Rewards (RLRR) decom-poses response quality into explicit criteria and uses rubric-conditioned judgments as training re-wards (Gunjal et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). It has shown promise in science and healthcare (Gunjal et al., 2025), instruction following (Huang et al., 2025; Viswanathan et al., 2025; He et al., 2025), and agentic tasks (Team et al., 2025). Most RLRR methods rely on rubrics constructed independently of policy training. Prompted genera-tion elicits rubrics from off-the-shelf teacher mod-els (Gunjal et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2026; Jia et al., 2026), inheriting their capability and inference cost. Supervised rubric generation instead learns rubric-related components from preference labels, correct-ness signals, or human annotations (Sheng et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; He et al., 2025), shifting the supervision bottleneck to external signals. Be-cause these rubrics or generators are typically fixed during policy optimization, they cannot adapt to changes in the policy distribution. Motivated by the discriminative view of rubric quality (Li et al., 2026), we instead co-evolve the policy and rubric generator and allocate their updates according to observed reward informativeness. 

2.2 Self-Evolving LLMs 

Self-evolving LLMs and agents update their be-havioral policies using their own trajectories, self-generated data, or feedback signals, enabling experience-dependent improvement (Gao et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2024). Recent work extends self-evolution from scalar feedback to explicit natural-language rubrics. Existing methods use predefined cou-pling schemes: shared-parameter joint optimiza-tion in RLCER (Sheng et al., 2026), alternating generator–judge optimization in Rubric-ARM (Xu et al., 2026), fixed-length policy–rubric phases in EvoLM (Li et al., 2026), and per-step dual-adapter updates in EvoRubrics (Ding et al., 2026). Several methods also rely on external supervision or verifi-cation, including preference labels in Rubric-ARM, expert annotations in RIFL (He et al., 2025), exter-nal validators in RLAC (Wu et al., 2025) and re-peated external API calls for EvoRubrics’ per-step rubric–judge interaction. Despite these differences, their update allocation is globally prescribed and does not account for rollout-group reward informa-tiveness. Our method instead combines per-rollout-group training-signal routing with event-triggered phase switching, updating the policy only on in-formative groups and adjusting rubric optimization only when reward discrimination fails, without re-lying on external labels or task-specific verifiers.

4 Advantage Collapse under Fixed-Length Policy–Rubric Alternation 

GRPO relies on within-group reward differences. If all rollouts receive the same reward, their nor-malized advantages become zero (Eq. 3), and the prompt contributes no policy gradient—a failure mode known as advantage collapse (He et al., 2026). We investigate whether this issue arises under fixed-length policy–rubric alternation. 

Setup. Following EvoLM (Li et al., 2026), we train a shared Qwen3-8B model (Yang et al., 2025) with fixed-length policy and rubric phases, both optimized with GRPO. We assess rubric character-istics and advantage collapse at steps 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1,000, and evaluate downstream perfor-mance at steps 500 and 1,000. At each checkpoint, we evaluate 100 fixed prompts and report the fraction of rollout groups with exact advantage collapse, defined by zero re-ward standard deviation ( σ(r) = 0 ). We also report JSON validity and the label-only ratio (the fraction of criteria with at most 40 characters), a proxy for short, underspecified labels. 5 Method 

Figure 1 illustrates the overall framework of RACE. A single model with shared parameters θ serves alternately as the policy πθ and the rubric genera-tor Gθ, with both roles optimized by GRPO under phase-specific rewards. The framework operates at three levels. At the rollout-group level, training-signal routing retains informative groups for pol-icy optimization and stores rubric-failed cases in a FIFO pool P. At the phase level, event-triggered switching enters rubric optimization when reward saturation persists and returns to policy optimiza-tion when rubric improvement plateaus; during the rubric phase, candidate rubrics are trained on cached response pairs using a scale-normalized pairwise margin reward. Finally, projected pro-portional controllers adapt the routing and phase-switching thresholds as training evolves. Algo-rithm 1 in App. A summarizes the full procedure.

6 Experiments 

6.1 Setup Data. We draw training prompts from the Tulu 3 preference mixture (Lambert et al., 2024). After deduplication, the resulting 271K prompts cover dialogue, instruction following, mathematics, code, scientific literature, and persona-based synthesis. We use only the prompts, without reference re-sponses or externally generated rubrics. 

Implementation Details. Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) parameterizes both the policy πθ

and rubric generator Gθ. The two roles share parameters and are optimized in alternating phases. Following Li et al. (2026), a frozen Qwen3-1.7B judge J provides rubric-based scores throughout training. Both roles are optimized with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), using a learning rate of 10 −6 and a KL coefficient of 0.001 . Each policy batch contains Nbatch = 64 unique prompts with G = 8 responses per prompt. During rubric training, each GRPO group contains G candidate rubrics evaluated on same cached comparison set. 

Downstream Policy Evaluation. Following the OLMo3-Adapt suite (Olmo et al., 2025), we eval-uate mathematical reasoning on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021); code generation on MBPP+ (Liu et al., 2023); general reasoning on BBH (Suzgun et al., 2023), GPQA (Rein et al., 2023), ZebraLogic (Lin et al., 2025), and AGI-Eval (Zhong et al., 2024) and knowledge on MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020) and PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023). For controlled comparison, all methods share the policy initializa-tion, training data, frozen Qwen3-1.7B judge, and a budget of 500 policy updates. 

6.2 Main Comparisons 

We compare RACE with four baseline cate-gories: (i) prompted rubrics , including GPT-4.1 prompted (a strong proprietary-teacher baseline) and Qwen3-8B prompted (matching our rubric-generator backbone), both of which generate static rubrics via prompting to guide policy optimiza-tion; (ii) scalar reward modeling : Skywork-RM-V2; (iii) offline rubric generation : RaR (Gunjal et al., 2025), which generates rubrics with GPT-4.1, and RRD (Shen et al., 2026), which recursively re-fines and reweights rubric criteria; and (iv) policy– rubric co-evolution : RLCER (Sheng et al., 2026), which jointly optimizes shared-parameter reasoner and rubricator roles, Rubric-ARM (Xu et al., 2026), which alternates rubric-generator and judge up-dates using preference labels, and EvoLM (Li et al., 2026), which alternates between fixed 50-step pol-icy and rubric phases. 6.3 Ablation Study 

Table 6 ablates three components of RACE: per-rollout-group training-signal routing (routing ), event-triggered phase switching ( switching ), and online threshold adaptation ( adaptation ), reporting RewardBench2 (RB2) and average policy perfor-mance over eight downstream benchmarks. 

Per-Rollout-Group Routing. As described in Section 5.1, routing filters uninformative groups from policy updates and stores rubric-failed groups in the targeted rubric pool P. No Gradient Filter-ing retains P but updates the policy with all groups, whereas Uniform Rubric Sampling retains filtering but replaces P with uniformly sampled data. Re-moving filtering lowers Policy Avg. from 73.0 to 70.8, while uniform sampling lowers RB2 from 49.2 to 47.9. The Filter-only variant retains alter-nating policy and rubric optimization but replaces adaptive routing with an advantage-collapse filter alone, reducing Policy Avg. to 70.9. These results show that both group filtering and targeted rubric training contribute to the full method’s gains. 

Event-Triggered Phase Switching. Replacing reward-dependent transitions with random or fixed alternation lowers policy performance to 71.4 and 71.3 and RB2 to 45.3 and 44.3, respectively. These are the largest RB2 degradations, showing that rout-ing alone is insufficient and that aligning phase tran-sitions with reward state is critical for maintaining discriminative rubrics.

6.4 Training Dynamics 

Figure 2 shows that the policy-phase moving av-erage rises during mid-training and subsequently declines, with the mean phase length decreasing from 27.0 steps in the first half to 21.4 in the second. 7 Conclusion 

We identify heterogeneous reward-discriminability degradation as a key bottleneck in policy–rubric co-evolution: some rollout groups experience ad-vantage collapse while others remain informative. To address this, we introduce RACE, combining group-level signal routing, event-triggered phase switching, and online threshold adaptation. RACE achieves the highest average performance and out-performs the strongest fixed-alternation baseline. These gains are achieved with a single frozen judge, without task-specific verifiers or preference-labeled online rewards, and with fewer optimization up-dates than fixed-alternation training. Overall, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of reward-adaptive policy–rubric co-evolution. 8Limitations 

Due to computational constraints, our experiments focus on a shared Qwen3-8B policy–rubric back-bone trained with a frozen Qwen3-1.7B judge. Al-though we additionally evaluate rubric discrimina-tion with another judge, the generalizability of our training framework to other model families, scales, and judges remains to be explored. Finally, our downstream evaluation focuses on single-turn benchmarks covering reasoning, knowl-edge, and code generation. Extending reward-adaptive co-evolution to multilingual, multi-turn, and long-horizon settings is a promising direction for future work.

B Implementation Details 

B.1 Model Configuration 

We use Qwen3-8B (8.2B parameters) for both the policy and the rubric generator. The two roles share a single parameter set and are distinguished by role-specific chat templates; consequently, policy and rubric updates are applied sequentially and never overwrite the shared weights concurrently. A frozen Qwen3-1.7B model supplies all rubric-conditioned scores. 

B.2 Optimization and Sampling 

Both the policy and rubric generator are optimized using GRPO. We adopt a learning rate of 10 −6

and a KL coefficient of β = 10 −3, with one opti-mization epoch and one minibatch per update. The learning rate is held constant without warmup. We use the kl3 estimator, a per-device microbatch size of 1, and a fused optimizer. Each update contains Nbatch = 64 unique prompts, with G = 8 samples per prompt, yield-ing 512 training samples. Actor sampling uses a temperature of 1.0. Prompts are truncated to 2,048 tokens, while generations and packed sequences are capped at 16,384 and 18,500 tokens, respec-tively. The asynchronous rollout pipeline supports a lag of up to four steps and caps the truncated importance-sampling ratio at 2.0. We report results for RACE across three independent runs and use previously reported baseline results.

We examine the scale-normalized pairwise reward 

Srubric used to optimize the rubric generator, fol-lowing Eq. 16 with clipping value c = 1 .0. For each candidate rubric, the frozen judge evaluates the same cached response pair, producing one candidate–pair reward. Candidate rubrics evaluated for the same training group form a rubric group; variation among their final rewards is the signal relevant to the group-level GRPO update. The anal-ysis covers rubric groups from rubric training phase steps 31–700. These counts specify the statistical coverage and denominators of the diagnostics sum-marized in Table 12. Only 4.56% of the retained groups satisfy the advantage-collapse condition ( σ(Srubric ) = 0), leaving 95.44% with nonzero within-group reward variation. This indicates that the rubric-phase re-ward is not broadly degenerate in the retained sub-sample and remains usable for most groups. 

D.5 Policy-Phase Reward Saturation 

The statistics above characterize rubric form. We now measure the quantity that actually drives pol-icy learning: whether the current rubric still sepa-rates the responses the current policy produces. Be-cause GRPO centers advantages within each rollout group, a group whose G responses all receive the same rubric score contributes no policy gradient no matter how high that score is. The failure mode we must track is therefore loss of within-group spread ,not low mean reward.

Saturation, not rubric failure. We next decom-pose the groups satisfying the advantage-collapse condition by absolute score level. 95 .5% of the groups with σ(r) = 0 have every response scored at or above 0.8 and only 2.2% have every response at or below 0.2. Collapse is therefore almost never caused by a rubric that rejects everything; it is caused by a rubric whose criteria are all satisfied by every response the policy now produces. This is precisely the mechanism identified in Section 4: as the policy improves, a rubric held fixed loses dis-criminative power on responses that already meet it, so the reward saturates from above.

G IFEval Evaluation Protocol 

IFEval is reported separately from the main down-stream table because the default evaluation pro-tocol applies constraint checks to reasoning text as well as the final answer. Following EvoLM, we use the olmo3:adapt suite from the OLMo evaluation framework, whose legacy implemen-tation is available in the OLMo-Eval repository. Under this configuration, constraint checkers oper-ate on the full raw Qwen3 completion, including the <think>...</think> block. Consequently, in-ternal reasoning may trigger constraint violations unrelated to the final answer. For a controlled comparison, we re-evaluate reproduced EvoLM and RACE with thinking enabled during generation, but extract and score only the final answer after removing the 

<think>...</think> block. Under this final-answer-only protocol, EvoLM and RACE score 77.6 and 78.2, respectively. These scores are directly comparable to each other, but not to EvoLM’s paper-reported IFEval score obtained un-der the full-completion protocol.
