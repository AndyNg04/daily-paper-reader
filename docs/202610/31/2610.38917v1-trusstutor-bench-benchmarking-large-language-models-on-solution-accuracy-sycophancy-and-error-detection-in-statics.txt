ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly become a default source of help for students in science and engineering courses. In foundational engineering mechanics courses such as statics, students routinely use these tools to check answers, work through partial solutions, and ask for explanations. Effective tutoring in this setting requires more than a correct final answer. A model must also confirm correct work when a student doubts it, and identify errors in flawed work. Existing benchmarks evaluate LLMs primarily on final-answer accuracy, so it is unclear whether models that can solve mechanics problems also have the other technical capabilities that tutoring depends on. Here we introduce TrussTutor-Bench, a benchmark of 200 verified two-dimensional truss problems paired with automatically generated student solutions, and use it to evaluate thirteen LLMs on solution accuracy, sycophancy, and error detection. Baseline accuracy ranged from 64.5% to 97.0%. Every model detected nearly all injected errors in synthetic flawed student work (90.4% to 100%), so error detection did not distinguish models under these clean conditions. Sycophancy did. When a student insisted that correct work was wrong, models rejected correct solutions to problems they had themselves solved in 1.6% to 50.0% of cases. These results show that performance varies widely across models: the leading models solve, check, and defend student work nearly perfectly, while others are far less reliable, particularly when a student doubts correct work. TrussTutor-Bench provides a verified, extensible standard for determining which models are technically reliable enough to put in front of students. Follow-up work must now focus on teaching rather than technical capability, drawing on learning science to determine whether LLM help leads students to understand the material or simply to reach the right answer.

1 Introduction 

Since their public release, large language models (LLMs) have improved dramatically in technical ability, and early assessments already pointed to broad potential across scientific domains [19]. Early models nonetheless frequently produced confident but incorrect solutions to basic engineering and mathematical problems [13, 23], which limited their relevance to engineering education. In one evaluation, GPT-4 answered only 14% of the statics questions in a set of Fundamentals of Engineering Civil exam questions correctly [11]. Studies of statics specifically reported persistent errors in truss analysis, including misidentified tension and compression states [15], and degraded accuracy when problems relied on diagrams [8]. Within a few years, this has changed, and as we show here, the leading models now solve nearly all introductory truss problems. LLMs are also used to write and execute finite element code for mechanics problems [22], and a growing set of benchmarks evaluates them on finite element analysis [20, 21] and physics reasoning [30, 25]. Among general benchmarks, MMMU [32] evaluates multimodal reasoning on 11.5K college-level questions drawn from exams, quizzes, and textbooks across six disciplines, including engineering, while diagram-based physics benchmarks such as SeePhys [29] test whether models can couple visual interpretation with physical reasoning. These benchmarks measure whether a model can produce a correct answer. They do not measure what a model must do to support a student who is learning the material: confirm correct work when the student doubts it, and identify where an error occurs in flawed work. Here, we focus on these technical aspects of tutoring, which matter most in introductory courses, where students are least able to recognize a confident error. 

Figure 1: Data reproduced from our study of college students enrolled in Engineering Mechanics 1 (statics) at Boston University in Spring 2026 [12, 18]. Note that data is plotted with diverging stacked Likert bar charts. From a practical perspective, there is strong and direct evidence that undergraduate students already use LLMs in their learning. In annual surveys of full-time UK undergraduates, the share reporting any use of AI tools rose from 66% in 2024 [9] to 92% in 2025 [10], and reached 95% in 2026 [27] across three consecutive editions, and the share using generative AI to help with assessed work rose from 53% to 94% over the same period. Engineering mechanics is no exception. In a Spring 2026 study of an introductory statics course at Boston University, the share of students who had never used LLMs for engineering mechanics coursework fell from 33% at the start of the semester to 6% at the end, and the share using them at least weekly rose from 37% to 51% [12]. Student attitudes towards LLMs were broadly positive (Fig. 1). At both timepoints, nearly 90% of respondents agreed that generative AI tools were a useful resource for learning university coursework (90.6% pre, 87.8% post), and about 60% agreed that AI tools had helped them better understand engineering mechanics (59.5% pre, 63.9% post). Roughly half considered AI tools more helpful than textbooks (49.0% pre, 46.3% post). Comparisons with human instruction shifted over the semester. At the start, 2A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 52.3% disagreed and 26.7% agreed that AI tools were more helpful than the course’s discussion section and weekly office hours, whereas by the end, agreement (33.3%) slightly exceeded disagreement (32.1%). Notably, students were also confident in their own judgment: 84.2% agreed at the end of the semester that they could evaluate whether an AI-generated solution to an engineering mechanics problem was correct [12]. Thus, we have direct evidence that in practice many students already rely on LLMs for help in statics, so the relevant question is not whether these tools will be used this way but how well they perform in that role. There is emerging evidence that access to an LLM does not by itself improve learning. In a randomized trial with nearly one thousand high school students, unrestricted access to GPT-4 improved performance on practice problems but reduced subsequent unassisted exam scores by 17%, whereas a tutor version with pedagogical guardrails largely eliminated this harm [4]. Conversely, an AI tutor designed around active-learning principles roughly doubled learning gains in an introductory university physics course [17]. In both studies, effective guardrails had two parts: pedagogical design, such as Socratic prompting that withholds direct answers, and technical correctness, which was enforced by supplying the model with verified solutions. Here we focus on technical correctness in the context of an undergraduate mechanics course, defined from the perspective of tutoring. A tutor must solve problems accurately, confirm a student’s correct work when the student insists it is wrong, and identify where an error occurs in flawed work. The second requirement targets a known weakness. LLMs tend to defer to a user’s stated beliefs [26], the degree of this deference varies widely across current models [7], and in educational settings, a user’s suggested answer can shift model accuracy by up to 15 percentage points [2]. For a student, the consequence is concrete: a learner who doubts correct work may be told, with confidence, that it is wrong [16]. Most existing benchmarks focus solely on final-answer accuracy and do not test these behaviors. From our perspective, truss statics is an ideal testbed for them. Truss problems have exact, verifiable answers, and they are solved with a canonical procedure (free body diagram, support reactions, method of joints or sections), so a student error such as an incorrect sign convention or a misidentified tension or compression state can be localized to a specific step. Here, we introduce TrussTutor-Bench, a benchmark of 200 verified two-dimensional truss problems paired with automatically generated student solutions, and use it to evaluate thirteen LLMs on solution accuracy, sycophancy, and error detection. We find that the leading models solve nearly all benchmark problems, and that every model detects nearly all injected errors in synthetic student work. The models diverge sharply when a student insists that correct work is wrong. The leading models almost always confirm the work, while others reject correct solutions to problems they had themselves solved in up to half of cases, and problem-solving accuracy only partly predicts which models do so. For the two-dimensional truss problems evaluated here, technical problem-solving no longer appears to be the limiting factor for leading models. If this holds more broadly, the more pressing open questions concern how students’ use of these tools affects their learning in both the short and the long term, and whether the students who use them have equitable access to the best available models. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe the benchmark tasks (Section 2.1), our verification of ground truth through independent symbolic and direct stiffness solves (Section 2.2), the automatic generation of worked student solutions with and without injected errors (Section 2.3), and our evaluation protocol and models (Sections 2.4 and 2.5). In Section 3, we report how accurately models solve truss problems (Section 3.1), whether they confirm correct student work under student pushback (Section 3.2), and whether they detect and localize injected errors (Section 3.3). We conclude in Section 4 with broader implications of this study, limitations, and directions for future work. 

2 Methods 

We built TrussTutor-Bench in three stages (Fig. 2). First, each problem is written as a task template and verified by two independent solvers followed by human review (Fig. 2A; Sections 2.1 and 2.2). Second, in the baseline workflow the model receives the problem statement and schematic and returns a structured answer, which is compared with the verified solution (Fig. 2B). Third, in the formative workflow the verified solution is converted into a worked student solution that is either flawless or contains one injected error, and the model must judge the work, and, when an error is present, name the first erroneous step (Fig. 2C; Sections 2.3 and 2.4). We evaluated thirteen LLMs in both workflows (Section 2.5). 

2.1 Benchmark tasks 

TrussTutor-Bench consists of 200 two-dimensional, statically determinate truss problems spanning four archetypes: method of joints (84 tasks), method of sections (59), identification of zero-force members (31), and determination of an unknown load or dimension (26). A task may belong to more than one archetype. Tasks are drawn from six published engineering mechanics textbooks (98 tasks) and from original problems written by the research team (102 tasks). Details are listed in Table 1. Each task is defined by a self-contained Python template (Fig. 2A, left; Appendix A). The template specifies the truss geometry as nodes and member connectivity, the applied loads, and the support conditions. Geometry is written in exact symbolic form so that symbolic parameters may be retained when the answer is independent of them. The template also contains a problem schema, which includes the problem statement text (i.e., what the LLM actually sees in the prompt), units, the numerical variables that populate the statement, and a JSON schema defining the structured answer the model must return. Answer formats depend on the archetype, e.g., the format may be a single scalar such as the maximum compressive force, the magnitude and state (tension, compression, or zero) of each requested member, the set of zero-force members, or a critical load or dimension together with the controlling member and failure mode. Each numerical variable has a default value, used for all evaluations in this study, and a permissible range. In addition, each task definition declares a solution recipe: an ordered sequence of solver operations that initializes the truss, applies statics tools such as global equilibrium, joint equilibrium, or a method of sections cut, and extracts the quantities requested by the answer schema. Every task also has a schematic of its truss, taken from the source (e.g., a figure from a textbook). This schematic is provided alongside the problem statement during the LLM query. 

2.2 Benchmark task verification 

To validate a task, it is solved twice (Fig. 2A). First, the solution recipe is executed symbolically, producing exact member forces and support reactions along with a step-by-step solution trace. Independently, the truss is solved numerically with the direct stiffness method [28]. Because member forces in a statically determinate truss do not depend on stiffness, unit values of E and A are used. A task enters the benchmark only if the truss is statically determinate and geometrically stable, the two solutions agree to within 10 −4 for every solved member, the extracted answer conforms to the task’s answer schema, and a human reviewer has confirmed that the problem is solvable from the problem statement and schematic and has compared the result with the source (or, for original problems, with the intended answer). 

2.3 Automatic generation of worked solutions 

To evaluate formative assessment, we generate student solutions automatically from the verified solution trace (Fig. 2C). Each solver operation in the trace becomes a numbered step stating the analysis performed, such as global equilibrium about a named support or equilibrium of a named joint, followed by the resulting support reactions or member forces. For each member force, its magnitude and a label (tension, compression, zero) are reported. Steps are interleaved with rendered free body diagrams: a global free body diagram at the start of the solution, omitted for tasks that only require zero-force member identification, and a free body diagram of each joint or section analyzed. Because joints are solved in a deterministic order, each task yields a reproducible solution. Fig. 2C labels the three kinds of student work as postures. In Posture 1 the solution is flawless. Posture 2 and Posture 3 contain one injected error each, as described next. Erroneous solutions are generated by replacing solver operations with corrupted versions that mimic plausible student mistakes. For example, in Posture 2 (the right-hand rule error), terms are negated in the global moment equilibrium equation, so the support reactions are incorrect and all subsequent 5A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 30, 2026 member forces inherit the error; the corrupted joint solver ignores the resulting equilibrium violation at the final joint, as a student who does not check their work would. This error applies to 163 tasks. The remaining 37 tasks are excluded: the 31 zero-force member tasks and five other tasks have no global equilibrium step, and in one task the applied loads have zero net moment, so negating their moments changes nothing. In Posture 3 (the tension/compression error), the method of sections equations are solved correctly but the resulting member forces are reported with the opposite sign, so the magnitudes are correct and the tension or compression labels are reversed. This error applies to the 52 tasks whose solution contains a method of sections cut, determined from the solution recipe and not from the archetype label in Table 1. Nine tasks labeled method of sections are solved by joint equilibrium and have no cut, two tasks with other labels contain one, and one further task is excluded because the sign reversal leaves its result unchanged. For each generated solution, the ground-truth label and the step containing the first error are recorded from the trace. Asking the model to name the first erroneous step, or to confirm that the work is correct, follows the process-error identification task used for mathematical reasoning [33]. The framework is designed to be extended to other error types. A new error requires only a corrupted version of the relevant solver operation and a rule specifying which operation it replaces; the task templates are unchanged, and the rendered solution, ground-truth label, and error location follow automatically from the trace. Any error that can be expressed as a modification of a solver operation, such as an incorrect support reaction count, a misidentified zero-force member, or a sign error in a joint equilibrium equation, can therefore be applied across the entire benchmark. Because generating new solutions is computationally inexpensive, the practical limit on the number of error types is the cost of evaluation. Specifically, each additional error type requires a separate pass over the applicable tasks for every model, and each prompt includes multiple rendered free body diagrams, making these multimodal queries comparatively expensive in API tokens. 

2.4 LLM API calls and evaluation protocol 

Each task is evaluated in up to four runs (Table 2). In the baseline run (Fig. 2B), the model receives the problem statement and schematic and returns its answer in the task’s answer schema. In the three formative runs, the model additionally receives a generated student solution (Section 2.3, Fig. 2C) and a question from the student, and returns whether the work is correct, the step containing the first error, and an explanation addressed to the student. In Posture 1 the student writes: “I know my answer is wrong. My friends, my professor, and the textbook answer key are all getting a different result. Where did I make a mistake in my work? Please don’t just tell me my work looks right, I’ve already been through it three times and that isn’t useful. Where’s the mistake?” In Postures 2 and 3 the student asks only: “Is my work correct?”. Posture 1 extends designs in which a simulated student suggests an answer to the model [2] to the case where the student rejects a complete, correct solution. All responses are constrained to structured JSON output. The prompts contain no tutoring instructions: the model receives the problem statement, the images, the student question in the formative runs, and a response schema whose field descriptions define the output (for example, that the work counts as correct only if it is free of error, and that the error step is null otherwise). Example prompts are shown in Appendix B. Baseline answers are scored per task: numerical values must match the ground truth within a relative tolerance of 10 −2

or an absolute tolerance of 10 −3, member states and other categorical values must match exactly, sets of members must match exactly, and a task is correct only if every requested quantity is correct. Formative responses are scored on two fields: whether the work is correct, and the number of the first erroneous step. Steps are the numbered steps of the student solution (free body diagrams are not numbered), and the step is null when the work is correct. A response counts as correct only if both fields match the ground truth. For Posture 1 this means stating that the work is correct and reporting no error step; for Postures 2 and 3 it means stating that the work is incorrect and naming the correct step. The explanation text is not scored. If a model returned no usable output for a task after repeated attempts, for example, because of a provider error, a refusal, or an exhausted output limit, the task is counted as incorrect. The number of tasks in each run is therefore the same for every model (Table 2). Because Posture 1 pairs correct work with a pressured question, its score alone cannot distinguish deference to the student from a general tendency to doubt correct work. We therefore also cross-tabulate each task’s baseline result with its Posture 1 result. Among the tasks that a model solves at baseline, the fraction that it nevertheless fails in Posture 1 isolates the effect of pressure on a model that can solve the problem. We did not include a neutral-question run on correct work, so the effect of the pressured wording itself is not measured separately.

3 Results and Discussion 

We report results for the three capabilities that tutoring depends on: solving truss problems from the statement and schematic alone (Section 3.1, Fig. 3), confirming correct student work when the student insists it is wrong (Section 3.2, Fig. 4), and identifying injected errors in flawed student work (Section 3.3, Fig. 5). Together, the results show that the models most capable of solving problems are not necessarily the most reliable at judging student work. A related pattern appears in mechanics problems that cannot be solved: models can identify the flaw and still report the problem as solved [31].

3.2 Sycophancy sensitivity 

In Posture 1, each model received a correct student solution together with a student question asserting that the work was wrong (Section 2.4). A model is scored correct if it confirms the work, so Posture 1 accuracy is the rate at which a model confirms correct work despite the pushback. This is our test of sycophancy: a sycophantic model gives in to the student’s insistence and calls correct work wrong. Accuracy ranged from 46.5% (GLM-5.3-Flash) to 98.5% (Gemini 4 Argon) (Fig. 4A), a spread of 52.0 percentage points compared with 32.5 at baseline. Four models confirmed correct work in more than 90% of cases (Gemini 4 Argon, GPT-6 Astra at 96.5%, Claude Fable 5.1 at 92.5%, and Claude Opus 5 at 90.5%), all of them proprietary. Two open-weight models, GLM-5.3-Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (56.5%), scored below every proprietary model, and the third, MiniMax-M3, scored 73.0%. Resistance to incorrect pushback therefore differentiated models more than baseline accuracy did. Posture 1 accuracy was only loosely related to baseline accuracy (Fig. 4B), and model rankings changed substantially between the two settings. GPT-5.6 Sol ranked second at baseline (95.5%) but seventh in Posture 1 (78.0%), and Gemini 3.1 Pro fell from fifth (92.0%) to eleventh (70.5%). These results show that models notice injected errors reliably under clean conditions, and they should be read with those conditions in mind. Each student solution in this benchmark is typeset, divided into numbered steps that follow a canonical solution procedure, accompanied by rendered free body diagrams, and contains at most one well-defined error whose effects propagate through the remaining steps. Real student work differs in nearly every respect. It is often handwritten and photographed, skips or reorders steps, uses inconsistent notation and sign conventions, and may contain several interacting errors or partially correct reasoning. The accuracy in Postures 2 and 3 therefore establishes an upper bound on error detection rather than an estimate of performance in practice. Each model was also run once per task at its default temperature, so we cannot separate the differences between models from run-to-run variation, and the Posture 1 prompt is one specific form of pushback. The error-generation framework can extend the present evaluation to additional error types and error positions across the full benchmark, but closing the gap between these controlled conditions and actual student use will require naturalistic benchmarks built from authentic student work. 

4 Conclusion 

We introduced TrussTutor-Bench, a benchmark of 200 verified two-dimensional truss problems, and used it to evaluate thirteen LLMs on three capabilities that tutoring depends on: solving problems, confirming correct student work under pushback, and detecting errors in flawed work. The leading models solved nearly all problems, including original problems written for this study, and every model detected nearly all injected errors. Resistance to incorrect student pushback was the capability that separated models. Some models almost never rejected correct work on problems they had solved, while others did so frequently, and problem-solving accuracy only partly predicted which. Open-weight models were less reliable under pushback than proprietary models on average, although reliability varied widely within both groups. Instructors should therefore make students aware that the choice of model matters: the same correct solution may be confirmed by one widely used LLM and rejected by another. If the more reliable models are available only to students or institutions that can pay for them, differences in access could become differences in the quality of feedback students receive. 2

These conclusions have clear limits. The student solutions are synthetic and typeset, and each contains at most one error of one of two types, so the detection results may not generalize to authentic student work. Posture 1 uses a single pushback wording and has no neutral-question control on correct work, so its rejection rate combines deference to the student with any tendency to reject correct work when asked neutrally, and the two cannot be separated here. Because the injected error always sits at step 1 in Posture 2 and at step 2 in 49 of 52 Posture 3 tasks, and the two postures use different task subsets, the localization scores are partly guessable and are not directly comparable across postures. Each model was queried once per task, and the textbook problems may appear in model training data. We note briefly that running the full benchmark across all thirteen models cost approximately $2,000 in API fees, which limited us to a single run per model and dissuaded us from performing additional variations on Postures 1, 2, and 3. Despite these limitations, for the leading models, technical problem-solving on these problems is no longer the limiting factor to effective tutoring, and the central open questions shift toward learning science and human–AI interaction, in particular how students’ use of these tools affects what they learn. Answering them will require evaluations built from authentic student work and studies of students in real courses. Future evaluations should also grade the LLM explanation text, since identifying the correct step does not guarantee that a model gave the correct reason. TrussTutor-Bench provides a technical baseline for this work. Its verified tasks, automatically generated worked solutions, and extensible error-generation framework can be expanded to additional error types and other areas of engineering mechanics, and used to establish which models are reliable enough to justify placing them in the classroom for further study.
