## Abstract 

Agentic embodied intelligence plays a foundational role in open-ended, dynamic physical environ-ments. Rather than treating policy learning as an end-to-end mapping from instructions to actions, recent studies reveal that embodied systems depend not only on pre-trained foundation models, but also on harness. Prior approaches largely treat rollouts as static logs for post-hoc reflection, producing harness updates without autonomously testing and refining them through subsequent embodied interaction. To address this challenge, we propose VISTA, a meta-agent framework that transforms post-hoc rollout reflection into active self-exploration for embodied harness improvement. Specifically, VISTA maintains a harness graph that records the lineage of proposed harnesses, which enables the meta-agent to retrieve prior hypotheses, build on effective mechanisms, and avoid re-peatedly exploring failed directions. Each iteration begins from a retained harness version and its execution evidence. Rather than directly committing an update from these historical trajectories, the meta-agent actively explores in the environment. It autonomously generates new rollouts, probes execution failures and recovery behaviors, and iteratively modifies the harness based on the result-ing evidence. The refined harness is then evaluated afresh and added to the harness graph. We evaluate VISTA on the LIBERO-Pro, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin benchmarks. Experimental results demonstrate that VISTA progressively improves task success and is more rollout-efficient than static reflection, achieving higher success rates with fewer simulator rollouts.

## 1 Introduction 

Embodied artificial intelligence has emerged as a cornerstone for enabling autonomous systems to perceive, reason, and interact seamlessly within dynamic physical environments, offering transformative potential across household assistance, healthcare logistics, and industrial automation [4, 40]. Catalyzed by multimodal foundation models and large-scale embodiment datasets, learning-based paradigms have demonstrated in-creasingly competitive capabilities in executing atomic manipulation tasks [3, 25, 28, 37, 39, 44, 51, 55, 60]. But scaling end-to-end policies to long-horizon, multi-stage tasks remains notoriously difficult. End-to-end models suffer severely from compounding execution errors over extended trajectories, out-of-distribution generalization under perturbations, and prohibitive sample complexity when attempting to fit multi-modal,

To overcome these fundamental limitations, the convergence of frontier large language model (LLM) agents and robotics has established embodied agentic systems as a pivotal research frontier [22, 32, 49, 50, 56]. By coupling high-level symbolic reasoning and stateful tool orchestration with low-level control policies, agen-tic embodied architectures offer decisive advantages in managing long-horizon complexity: they facilitate modular hierarchical task decomposition, support active perception to resolve visual ambiguities, and enable closed-loop error diagnosis and autonomous recovery. The performance, reliability, and long-horizon coher-ence of such systems depend not only on their pre-trained foundation model weights, but also fundamentally on their harness [48, 54]. Existing systems construct and improve these harnesses through code generation, reusable skills, and memory-guided execution [32, 49, 50, 56]. VISTA focuses on a complementary question: how can a coding agent improve an executable robot harness by acquiring additional evidence through direct interaction with the environment? Existing records do not always contain the observations or interventions needed to test a proposed explanation of a failure. To address this issue, we propose VISTA , a meta-agent framework that unifies active environmental exploration with closed-loop harness adaptation to achieve embodied self-improvement. The meta-agent controls the development process: it selects what to inspect, which public sensing and control capabilities to invoke, and which autonomous tests to request. Evidence from these interactions guides executable harness revisions. After development, the submitted harness is frozen and evaluated through fresh autonomous execution. Its code and evidence are retained in a harness DAG, allowing the meta-agent to retrieve prior hypotheses, reuse effective mechanisms, and explore from earlier candidates. We evaluate VISTA on LIBERO-Pro [59], RoboCasa365 [38], and RoboTwin [9]. VISTA improves task success by accumulating executable mechanisms across development rounds. These results support harness development as a practical way to convert embodied experience into persistent changes in agent behavior. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose VISTA, a framework that departs from conventional handcrafted heuristics and static mem-2ory augmentation by framing the robot’s operational harness as an evolvable, executable control plane, realizing autonomous embodied self-improvement. • We introduce an active exploration mechanism where the meta-agent empowers the embodied system to actively gather diagnostic physical evidence, linking exploratory evidence to executable harness revi-sions. Furthermore, we develop a Harness Graph to track the evolutionary lineage of candidate harnesses, facilitating hypothesis retrieval, component reuse, and principled branch exploration. • We conduct comprehensive evaluations on the challenging LIBERO-Pro, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin bench-marks. Experimental results demonstrate that VISTA progressively improves manipulation success rates through accumulated experience, improving over the corresponding initial harnesses. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Foundation Models for Robotic Manipulation 

Low-level action policies. Foundation models for robotic manipulation can be broadly categorized according to how their semantic knowledge is connected to physical control. One line of research extends pretrained vision–language models (VLMs) into low-level policies through vision–language–action (VLA) models, which generate continuous action chunks using diffusion or flow matching [3, 13], task-space keyframes or spatial-grid actions [45], and latent action codes learned from robot trajectories or videos [5, 10]. Recent video– action and world–action models further couple action prediction with future visual dynamics [31, 42]. Robot foundation models extend these capabilities across heterogeneous tasks and embodiments [15, 40], while subsequent studies improve action initialization, tokenization, and adaptation to new tasks and embodiments [53]. Despite their strong control performance, these approaches translate general pretrained knowledge into embodiment-specific motor signals and often require additional robot trajectories when the task family or platform changes [57]. 

Semantic decision making and physical grounding. A complementary line of research treats foundation mod-els as semantic decision makers rather than direct motor controllers. These models predict subgoals, keypoints, affordances, value maps, or spatial constraints, which are subsequently realized by downstream controllers [16, 22]. Although this preserves high-level semantic reasoning, it separates model intent from physical execu-tion and depends on system-specific grounding pipelines [18, 21], as well as long-horizon planning, closed-loop feedback, and failure recovery [20]. Together, these two directions reveal a trade-off between direct physical control and semantic generalization: low-level policies offer precise execution but are closely tied to specific embodiments, whereas intermediate representations preserve semantic structure but limit physical adaptabil-ity. This trade-off motivates interfaces that combine semantic interpretability with fine-grained executable actions. 

2.2 Embodied Agent Systems 

Agentic control architectures. Building on these foundation-model paradigms, agentic embodied systems place a pretrained VLM within an iterative harness that translates model decisions into robot actions and feeds execution outcomes back to the model, thereby closing the perception–decision–execution loop [33, 35]. Existing systems mainly differ in the primitives through which decisions are executed. Some models generate programs over perception and control APIs [6, 19], while others select skills from predefined libraries [29, 46], steer learned policies such as VLAs with language subgoals [8, 43], or delegate decisions to symbolic planners and numerical optimizers [11, 12]. To support long-horizon interaction, these systems typically maintain task context and persistent memory [2], perform feedback-based monitoring and reflection [23], and detect failures for replanning and recovery [17]. 

Interfaces for embodied execution. Within agentic robot systems, interface design strongly affects control performance. While high-level, human-designed abstractions can improve reliability, composing low-level perception and control APIs remains difficult [19]. ## 3 Method 

VISTA improves an embodied agent by evolving its harness, including the instructions, executable skills, and coordination logic through which the agent perceives, reasons, and acts. The online agent has access to a language-model planner, a VLA model, and public robot capabilities. VISTA keeps the underlying model parameters fixed and optimizes how these resources are composed into autonomous behavior. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting development loop. A meta-agent, implemented as a coding agent, retrieves a harness version and its execution evidence, identifies a behavior to improve, and develops a candidate. During development, it can actively investigate the environment, probe execution failures, and revise the harness in response to newly acquired evidence. It can also request autonomous tests of intermediate versions. The submitted candidate is then frozen and evaluated in fresh autonomous episodes. A host-controlled selection rule updates the incumbent, while the candidate and its evidence remain available for subsequent iterations. Improvement is thus carried forward through both executable harness versions and the experience accumulated while developing them. 43.1 Harness DAG

A frozen harness still induces closed-loop behavior: its code and persistent knowledge remain fixed during evaluation, while episode-local state and actions depend on incoming observations. Harness development produces a directed acyclic graph (DAG) G = ( V, E ) of immutable versions. We instantiate each harness as a Git-versioned package, with commits identifying snapshots and parent links defining lineage. Branches preserve alternative development directions, and explicit merges can combine histories. Execution records and development notes are linked to their corresponding versions. Each iteration begins from the incumbent by default, but the meta-agent may select another recorded base, including a previously rejected candidate, and inspect earlier implementations for reusable mechanisms. Retaining unsuccessful proposals makes their diagnostic findings available to later development. 

3.2 Evidence-Guided Harness Revision 

Each development record is tied to a frozen harness version and exact execution condition, including task initialization, repetition, randomness, and execution mode. A trajectory aligns planner decisions and tool calls with robot actions, images, and state, allowing the meta-agent to localize a failure from an aggregate outcome to a specific decision or physical execution interval. Given a base harness and these records, the meta-agent forms a concrete hypothesis and revises the impli-cated components, such as instructions, observation processing, skill termination, or coordination logic. The resulting version and its supporting evidence are retained for later development. When recorded trajecto-ries are insufficient to resolve the hypothesis, VISTA acquires additional embodied evidence through active exploration. 

3.3 Active Diagnostic Exploration 

Unlike static reflection, VISTA allows the meta-agent to collect new embodied evidence before submitting a candidate. It can launch a private simulator session from a frozen harness snapshot, inspect the current exe-cution state, and invoke public sensing or control capabilities to test a specific hypothesis. These interactions provide diagnostic evidence for subsequent harness revisions rather than modifying the formal evaluation process. After revision, the meta-agent can request fresh autonomous test rollouts to determine whether the candidate reproduces the desired behavior without intervention. Development therefore alternates among hypothesis formation, targeted interaction, harness revision, and autonomous testing until a candidate is submitted or the development budget is exhausted. Diagnostic traces and intermediate test results remain development evidence; promotion is determined only by the separate formal evaluation in 3.4.

4.3 Ablation 

Active vs. passive exploration. To assess the contribution of active exploration, we compare VISTA with a passive, rollout-only variant on four LIBERO-Goal tasks. The passive variant retains history inspection, har-ness editing, and autonomous candidate evaluation, but cannot open a live scene, probe a decision boundary, or execute diagnostic capabilities during development; it must diagnose failures from recorded rollouts alone. Meta tokens include cached and uncached input, output, and sub-agent usage. Table 4 shows larger gains with active exploration. After the first round, active exploration improves success by 50.0 percentage points using 42.2M Meta tokens per task, whereas the passive variant improves by 39.1 points while consuming 94.2M. After two rounds, the gains reach 85.9 and 56.3 percentage points, respectively, with mean cumulative usage of 91.6M and 137.7M tokens. Active exploration provides additional observations and interventions that help the Meta-agent test its hypotheses and develop effective harness changes. 

4.4 Analysis: Inside Harness Evolution 

Development Trajectories. During optimization, the Meta Agent maintains a Git-like lineage to explore and revise harnesses. We analyze the recorded lineages to characterize how harnesses evolve across successive revisions. Depending on the task, we observe different development patterns. In some cases, optimization proceeds linearly by refining the current harness. In others, the optimizer branches from an earlier revision when the current hypothesis appears ineffective and explores an alternative direction. We also observe cases where mechanisms developed in different branches are recombined into a new harness. Figure 4(b) shows a representative trajectory on LIBERO Goal-t08. Starting from an initial harness with 50% formal success, the first development round refines the placement behavior and promotes a candidate with 68.75% success. The next round explores an alternative stopping rule, but the submitted candidate does not improve over the incumbent and is therefore rejected. A later branch introduces a metric placement skill and reaches 100% formal success, after which the same frozen harness is re-evaluated and again obtains 100%. Acquired Harness Mechanisms. We examine the artifacts produced during harness development to under-stand what is accumulated through optimization. The revisions span perception interfaces, state verification, action composition, and the allocation of control between executable skills and the frozen VLA. These cate-gories are not mutually exclusive: a single revision may change both how an object is localized and how the outcome of the subsequent manipulation is verified. For example, across the developed harnesses, the opti-mizer introduces observation-dependent manipulation targets, explicit grasp and execution-state verification, bounded recovery procedures, and programmatic action sequences that complement VLA control. Figure 5 shows a representative example from Goal-t09. The initial harness succeeds in 0.0% of episodes. In the first development round, the meta-agent analyzes the failed trajectories and makes several concrete changes: it requires a verified lift before transport, re-segments the carton before a bounded grasp retry, and introduces an explicit transport-and-release procedure for placing the carton onto the rack. The resulting harness reaches 56.3%. The remaining failures expose a mismatch in the lift verification logic: lift was measured relative to an approach pose rather than the closed-contact state from which the carton was actually lifted. The second round therefore revises the detector to measure displacement over a continuous closed-contact segment and resets the accumulated evidence when contact is lost, together with further adjustments to the release behavior. After these revisions, the harness reaches 100.0% in autonomous evaluation. ## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced VISTA, a framework for improving embodied agents by evolving their operational harness while keeping the underlying model parameters fixed. Rather than treating completed rollouts as a static source of feedback, VISTA enables a meta-agent to actively acquire diagnostic evidence, revise executable harness components, and test intermediate solutions before submitting a frozen candidate for autonomous evaluation. Across LIBERO-Pro, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin, this process progressively improves task success over successive development rounds. These results demonstrate that active, execution-grounded harness de-velopment provides a practical mechanism for converting embodied interaction into persistent improvements in agent behavior. 

Limitations and Future Works. While VISTA demonstrates substantial gains across simulated benchmarks, our current evaluations are conducted strictly within high-fidelity simulation environments. Physical real-robot validation has not yet been performed. Real-world deployment inevitably introduces sensor noise, unpredictable actuation latency, and unmodeled contact dynamics. In future work, we plan to validate VISTA on physical robotic platforms (e.g., Franka Emika arms and mobile manipulators), and evaluate sensing, control constraints, and supervision needed for safe physical exploration.

## Appendix A Additional Experimental Details 

A.1 Infrastructure 

VISTA separates harness development from autonomous evaluation through three interfaces. The meta-agent can read previous execution records, edit a candidate package, run live diagnostics, and request autonomous tests. The host freezes package versions, starts simulator processes, records traces, and applies the final selection rule. Figure A1 shows the three execution paths. Historical inspection reads completed records and does not recreate a previous simulator state. Live development starts a private scene from a frozen harness snapshot. Autonomous evaluation starts fresh episodes with a frozen candidate and no meta-agent intervention. All paths write records with explicit episode, request, and harness identities. A live scene is not hot-swapped when the worktree changes. To test a later revision, VISTA starts a new session with the new frozen package. Live observations are stored as diagnostic evidence and are not silently added to the planner’s context. Historical depth is available only for trace modes that archive metric depth and calibration; full_rgb records contain RGB but no historical depth. 

A.2 Meta-Agent Skill and Tool Surface 

The meta-agent follows the evolve-harness skill. This skill describes how to inspect history, formulate a concrete hypothesis, revise the harness, use live sessions for diagnosis, and validate the resulting change. In the LIBERO reference package, this surface includes native file and termination tools, image reading, an observation skill, and a VLA-step skill. A package may additionally define a Policy with prepare_request , process_tool_result ,and optional decide hooks. These hooks operate inside the ordinary host planner loop: they can transform the visible request or choose among bound tools, but they do not replace the host loop or change the model weights and budgets. The distinction between the two surfaces is deliberate. Meta tools control development and inspection; harness tools determine what the online agent can use during an autonomous episode. A live call made by the meta-agent therefore does not imply that the corresponding operation was available to, or selected by, the online agent in the formal evaluation. 

A.3 Git-Backed Harness History 

Each frozen harness is stored as a Git commit. Ordinary revisions create parent edges, and an explicit merge creates a commit with multiple parents. The run store attaches evaluation requests, episode records, live diagnostics, and selection decisions to the corresponding commit. Rejected candidates remain reachable through retained references and may be selected as later development bases. The incumbent used for formal comparison is recorded separately from the parent selected for revision. Thus, Git records code lineage, while the run store records the evidence and decisions associated with each version. Figure A2 presents the recorded development trajectories for the four LIBERO-T suites. The heatmaps show the performance of the candidate evaluated in each round, whereas the curves below aggregate the best-so-far champion scores across tasks. The right-hand panels summarize the gain of each retained champion over H0. This distinction separates the quality of individual proposals from the performance accumulated through candidate selection. Across the four suites, the largest aggregate improvements occur during the early rounds, followed by smaller, task-dependent gains. Object reaches perfect development performance, while Spatial and Goal approach saturation. LIBERO-10 improves more gradually and retains a larger performance gap at the reporting endpoint. The per-task trajectories further show that progress is not synchronized across tasks: some reach high success rates within the first few rounds, whereas others continue to receive revisions throughout the displayed development period. For example, Goal T1 reaches 100 .0% only in R10. Individual candidate scores are often non-monotonic, even when the retained champion remains unchanged. For example, Spatial T7 reaches 87 .5% in R1, but several subsequent candidates obtain lower scores without replacing that champion. A plateau in the aggregate curve therefore does not necessarily indicate that development has stopped; it may instead reflect continued exploration without a successful replacement.
