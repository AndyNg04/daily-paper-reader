# ABSTRACT 

Language-model agents differ fundamentally from prior intelligent systems in their ability to learn from natural language in context. This capability enables agents to iteratively refine their answers in response to feedback, a prevalent mode of operation in modern agentic workflows. However, the community primarily ranks models by metrics such as Pass@1 or Pass@K, which cannot capture how they utilize feedback on prior attempts. We introduce Seq@K, a natural evaluation protocol that measures whether a model can solve a problem within K sequential attempts, provided feedback after each previous failure. Unlike multi-turn agentic benchmarks that examine long-horizon planning, Seq@K isolates and formalizes the core capability of refinement from feedback. We evaluate Seq@K on a suite of 7 benchmarks and 16 frontier models, and show that Seq@K reveals a ma-terially different model-selection landscape: under Seq@K, (1) model rankings often differ, (2) the Pareto frontiers admit more efficient configurations and (3) the gains from choosing the optimal model configuration can widen or shrink rel-ative to those of Pass@K. We further show that analyzing model trajectories under Seq@K helps characterize iterative refinement behavior, such as the rate at which models address issues raised in feedback, or how rigidly they hold onto their own answers. Finally, we study how the nature of feedback affects models’ iterative refinement, showing that practitioners must carefully design and benchmark the feedback loops of iterative agentic systems. Together, these results establish how Seq@K offers a more faithful metric for intelligent systems that typically operate in such feedback loop, and the necessity to consider Seq@K in evaluating models for these systems. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multi-turn interaction with feedback is a fundamental pattern of machine learning, driving classical paradigms including multi-armed bandits (Auer et al., 2002), active learning (Settles, 2009), and reinforcement learning (Sutton & Barto, 2018). Recently, this same principle has catalyzed much of the progress in large language models (LLMs), underpinning advances in test-time compute (Snell et al., 2024), automatic prompt optimization (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025), and agentic systems (Park et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2023). Yet a striking disconnect has emerged between how these systems operate and how they are evaluated. Even as modern methods (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023) and environments (Wang et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025) center on iterative refinement, the community still ranks models primarily by Pass@K (Chen et al., 2021), a strictly feedback-blind metric. We argue that relying solely on feedback-blind evaluation is insufficient for developing agentic systems—it may reward the wrong behaviors and obscure the right ones. For example, consider a coding agent: the user issues an initial request, the agent proposes an edit, the user responds with targeted critiques, and the agent revises. This loop of proposal, feedback, and revision is the 1arXiv:2610.37859v1 [cs.AI] 27 Oct 2026 dominant pattern of agent usage today, with applications extending far beyond coding. Yet Pass@K is blind to whether an agent can excel in this sequential decision-making loop. This blind spot hides failure modes and undervalues models that successfully improve through iteration. In this work, we quantify a model’s ability to improve under sequential feedback by measuring it along a new axis, which we name Seq@K. Seq@K measures whether a model solves a task within K attempts when it receives feedback after each failed attempt. Ranking models by Pass@K implicitly assumes that a model that is accurate in one turn also uses feedback well. We show that this assumption often breaks down. We evaluate Seq@K on coding (MBPP Pro, HumanEval Pro (Yu et al., 2025)), complex instruction following (AdvancedIF (He et al., 2025)), domain reasoning (CLBench DKR (Dou et al., 2026)), and medical benchmarks (HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025)). Our results reveal how model rankings, cost frontiers, and failure modes change when models are allowed to revise their work based on feedback. Our contributions and key empirical insights are structured around three primary questions: 1. Model Selection & Cost Dynamics: Model rankings can change between evaluations based on Pass@K and Seq@K. We demonstrate (a) the benefit of Seq@K by showing that it leads to better and more cost-efficient model as calibration metric, (b) Pass@K can un-derstate or overstate differences in model capability under sequential feedback. (c) Seq@K often exposes superior Pareto frontier at deployment than that of Pass@K and (d) how Seq@K tracks a different capability from Pass@K (Section 5.1). 2. Uncovering Revision Patterns: Beyond reporting Seq@K, we further analyze the way in which models revise their attempts: (a) models can differ in when they gain, with some gaining most on the first retry and others on later attempts, (b) revisions do not always address the feedback, and some models resubmit the same attempt unchanged, and (c) even revisions that address all of the feedback can fail by breaking requirements that passed before (Section 5.2). 3. Diagnosing the Feedback Loop: Seq@K also helps diagnose the feedback itself. We show (a) the usefulness of informative feedback depends on both the benchmark and the model capability, (b) critiques written with the grading rubric tend to help models repair far more failed requirements, and (c) summarizing the previous attempts can lower success rate, so how the history is kept should be reported along with Seq@K (Section 5.3). 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Refinement from feedback. A line of related work considers having a model reasoning and revis-ing in response to critique or execution feedback as methods to improve agent performance (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Gou et al., 2024). Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) is the closet to our setting by considering agent’s reasoning on top of binary pass/fail signal or results on self-generated test cases. In contrast, our work centers on Seq@K as an evaluation metric, and as a result, consid-ers situation where the feedback is provided under privileged information about the correct answer or grading criteria. This reflects the common setting where users of an agent provides information on how its attempts were inadequate. Other recent work evaluate this ability by introducing novel tasks. RefineBench (Lee et al., 2025) introduces a new set of tasks with checklist-based evaluation, and finds that self-refinement yields little improvement while targeted feedback drives frontier mod-els close to saturation. ICPC-Eval’s Refine@K (Xu et al., 2025) measures revision from execution feedback in competitive programming. Instead of constructing new tasks, we demonstrate that on existing benchmarks and settings the community already considers, the Seq@K evaluation protocol already reveals insights on model performance rankings and novel cost-performance Pareto fron-tiers. These insights demonstrate how Seq@K is an important metric to report alongside Pass@K on benchmarks or settings where iterative refinement is expected in practice. 

Multi-turn and agentic evaluation. Agent benchmarks such as SWE-bench, WebArena, and τ -bench (Jimenez et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025) and multi-turn evaluations such as MINT (Wang et al., 2024) score models over long interactions with tools and environments. In these settings, success depends jointly on planning, tool use, state tracking, and the use of feedback. Seq@K isolates the last of these, where each piece of feedback provides information on the entire 2attempt. Hence, differences in Seq@K reflect how models revise their attempts and not how they navigate an environment. 

# 3 SEQ @K E VALUATION PROTOCOL 

Both Pass@K and Seq@K evaluate whether a model succeeds at least once within an attempt budget of K, but they differ in whether candidate draws are independent or generated through iterative re-finement from feedback. Independent sampling generates each candidate from a static task prompt, whereas sequential evaluation conditions each revision on preceding candidate responses and feed-back. Feedback modes. We compare independent parallel sampling (Pass@K) against three distinct se-quential feedback modes, categorized by the signal provided to the actor following a failed attempt: 

• Binary Feedback: Provides only an outcome pass/fail verdict. This mode evaluates whether knowing that a candidate failed provides sufficient leverage to guide subsequent revision. 

• Raw Verifier Feedback: Returns direct environment or evaluator diagnostics—including exe-cution stack traces, failed test assertions, or rubric item breakdowns. This mode tests whether models can interpret and act upon evaluator diagnostics. 

• LLM Critic Feedback: Uses a language model to generate targeted critique from the candidate response and the information supplied to the critic. The critic role can be fulfilled by the actor model itself or another language model. We systematically vary the critic’s context, including whether it has explicit access to underlying grading rubrics. Feedback mode specifies the information boundary maintained during revision rounds. For example, raw test diagnostics can expose test failures and expected outputs, whereas rubric-aware critics use evaluation rubrics to generate guidance for the actor. Having established the benchmark coverage, feedback channels, and evaluation protocols, we next present our empirical findings on how Seq@K reshapes model selection, exposes revision dynamics, and quantifies feedback utility across these systems.

85.2.2 REVISIONS DO NOT ALWAYS ADDRESS THE FEEDBACK 

We next examine whether provided feedback are addressed in agents’ next attempts. Some unhelpful revisions are easy to identify since the next attempt entails the same answer as before with no changes. In coding, we find such examples with Qwen3 Coder Flash and DeepSeek V3.2: 48% and 

6% of revised attempts respectively are exactly the same attempt as before without modifications, whereas Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini 3 Flash never submit an unmodified revised attempt (Figure 5a). Other unhelpful revisions update the attempts but nonetheless fail to address the feedback. Unlike the aforementioned failure mode that can be programmatically identified, finding cases where revi-sions took place but are ineffective takes manual inspection. Below, we show 2 of the 14 examples we identify (Examples 5.1 and 5.2) and more in Appendix E.

Since manually going through all revisions is not feasible, we employ a similar approach to GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026): we use the 14 examples to develop a prompt, and use Claude Opus 5.5 to annotate all tasks in the four benchmarks (AdvancedIF, CLBench DKR, HumanEval Pro, and MBPP Pro) on attempts that contain revisions. For each feedback, the annotator judges whether the revised attempt satisfies it. A revision is unhelpful if it satisfies none of the remaining requests, 

addresses some of the feedback if it fully or partly satisfies at least one, and addresses all of the feedback if it fully satisfies every one. Across 842 revisions, we find that 186 (22% ) are unhelpful, 

289 (34% ) address some, and 367 (44% ) address all of the feedback (Appendix D.2). In Figure 5b, we further show how unhelpful revisions impact task successes in the sequential set-ting. First, as expected, unhelpful revisions almost never succeed in the next attempt. Success rises when some of the feedback are deemed partially addressed. However, addressing all of the feedback is not enough and many such revisions still fail; this is because the revisions can break a criterion that passed before as it addresses the current feedback. So far, we examined how agents perform under Seq@K and respond to feedback. In this section, we look at the feedback itself: what information it contains, whether the critic knows what is being graded, and how much of the previous attempts the agent sees. 5.3.1 EFFECTIVENESS OF BINARY FEEDBACK DEPENDS ON THE BENCHMARK 

Binary feedback only tells the agent that its attempt failed, without saying which requirements are not met. In DKR, binary feedback is largely unhelpful. To measure this, we take the requirements that the first attempt fails and measure the fraction of them fixed in the best later attempt (the attempt that satisfies the most requirements, taking the latest one if tied). We call this fraction criterion repair . In Table 5, we compare independent retries, binary feedback, and critiques written with and without access to the grading rubric. Binary feedback and critiques without the rubric repair about as many criteria as independent retries, whereas critiques with the rubric repair many more. Binary feedback is similarly unhelpful for DeepSeek V3.2 and Qwen3 Coder Flash in coding, where it changes Seq@5 by at most 3.3 points from Pass@5 (Appendix F.2). On the other hand, binary feedback is helpful for Sonnet 4.6 in coding, since Sonnet can reason on its own about why its code failed when it was run. Compared to Pass@5, Seq@5 with binary feedback is 17 to 26 points higher for Sonnet on HumanEval Pro and MBPP Pro (Appendix F.2), whereas in DKR it is only 1 point higher on average across six models. Overall, this shows that effective-ness of different feedback type is dependent on both benchmark and model-capability. Therefore, in designing feedback harnesses around models, practitioners may benefit more from highlighting criteria that agents would otherwise not come up on their own from reasoning. 5.3.2 SUMMARIZING THE HISTORY CAN LOWER SEQUENTIAL SUCCESS

# 6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

We introduced Seq@K, which measures whether a model solves a task within K attempts when it receives feedback after each failed attempt. Model rankings, gaps between actors, and cost-performance frontiers can all differ between Seq@K and Pass@K, so choosing a model by Pass@K can pick the wrong one for an agent that revises from feedback. Seq@K trajectories also show when models gain and how revisions fail, for example by ignoring the feedback or by breaking require-ments that passed before. Finally, Seq@K helps diagnose the feedback loop itself. We find how much feedback helps depends on what it tells the agent, whether the critic knows what is being graded, and how the history is kept. Practitioners building agentic systems around a feedback loop should choose models with Seq@K under the feedback the agent will receive, since Pass@K may favor a model that does not use that feedback well.

Annotation. For each revision the annotator sees the task prompt, the rubric text, earlier attempts and feedback, attempt t, the feedback on it, attempt t+1 , and a line diff. The actor, run, and all verdicts are hidden, and an annotation batch contains at most one revision per trajectory. The an-notator lists each change the feedback requests and labels it valid, interpretive, unclear, or invalid (for example, a test that contradicts the prompt). It then records whether attempt t+1 satisfies each request (yes, partly, or no), with a verbatim quote as evidence. A request also counts as satisfied 19 when attempt t already met it and attempt t+1 still does. Whether a revision is unhelpful, addresses some, or addresses all of the feedback is computed from these labels after dropping invalid requests. To develop the prompt, Claude Opus and Claude Sonnet independently labeled 68 revisions from the development tasks ( 16 random revisions per setting and the two example AdvancedIF trajecto-ries). The prompt was revised where they disagreed, then frozen before any held-out revision was annotated and before any verdict after attempt 1 was read. 

Reliability. Claude Sonnet independently annotated a stratified 15% of held-out revisions ( 165 ). The two annotators agree on this category for 80% of them ( κ = 0 .73 ); agreement is lowest in DKR (κ = 0 .53 ). A third blind Claude Opus pass resolved the 69 disagreements; using its labels leaves the conclusions unchanged. All 2,029 non-empty evidence quotes occur verbatim in the revised attempt. These are LLM labels, not human annotations, and three of the actors are Claude models. 

Broken criteria. For the counts of revisions that address all feedback yet fail, a criterion counts as broken if it passed at attempt t, fails at attempt t+1 , and no annotated request refers to it. Requests the annotator did not tie to a rubric item are treated as unmentioned, so these counts may slightly overstate breakage. Intervals in Figure 5b are 95% percentiles over 4,000 resamples of tasks within each setting.
