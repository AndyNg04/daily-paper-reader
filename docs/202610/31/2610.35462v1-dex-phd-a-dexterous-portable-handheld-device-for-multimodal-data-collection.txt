# DEX-PHD: A Dexterous Portable Handheld Device for Multimodal Data Collection 

Jooyoung Hong, Noboru Myers, Obin Kwon, Yixian Yang, and Joohyung Kim 

Abstract — Existing manipulation data collection methods typically offer either accurate robot-state acquisition through kinematic-chain-based teleoperation or convenient, portable operation through vision-based approaches. We present DEX-PHD, a portable handheld device that combines these advan-tages for dexterous manipulation by integrating a dexterous robot hand, continuous per-finger control, and multimodal logging. The system records proprioceptive information, finger-tip contact data, and wrist-camera video, while the operator directly controls the robot hand through joint-level mapping. Its untethered design enables data collection across diverse environments. We demonstrate DEX-PHD on contact-rich tasks requiring coordinated multi-finger manipulation and deploy the collected demonstrations through open-loop replay on a physi-cal humanoid robot. The results show that open-loop replay preserves the main temporal patterns of finger motion and task-relevant contact. Therefore, DEX-PHD provides a practical platform for portable, multimodal dexterous manipulation data collection. 

I. INTRODUCTION Robots have been increasingly deployed in industrial and household environments, where many tasks are repetitive, tedious, or physically demanding. As these applications expand, robust data collection becomes critical for advancing robotic manipulation capabilities. In particular, dexterous manipulation requires datasets that capture not only motion but also contact, force, and proprioceptive information [1]– [3]. However, collecting such data at scale remains chal-lenging because many existing systems rely on controlled environments, specialized hardware, or complex calibration procedures. Existing methods for demonstration data collection present a trade-off between data quality and user conve-nience. Kinematic-chain-based teleoperation systems, includ-ing leader-follower devices and exoskeletons, can provide accurate robot-action trajectories and high-quality joint-level data. However, these systems often require expensive hard-ware and bulky or fixed setups, limiting portability and scalable data collection. In contrast, vision-based methods, including approaches based on egocentric video, provide convenient and accessible data collection. Nevertheless, they depend on visual pose estimation and retargeting, may require substantial computation, and make it difficult to incorporate contact and proprioceptive information. Handheld devices offer a promising intermediate approach by combining simple setup with portable, in-the-wild data

A. Dexterous Data Collection Interfaces 

Dexterous manipulation demonstrations can be collected through teleoperation systems, wearable interfaces, vision-based methods, and handheld devices. Teleoperation systems provide a direct way to collect robot action trajectories for imitation learning [6], [7]. Direct joint-mapping and AR/VR-based systems further support precise bimanual teleoperation, large-scale robot data collection, and cross-platform trajec-tory transfer [8]–[12]. However, these systems often require specialized hardware, calibration procedures, or a fixed robot platform during demonstration collection. Wearable gloves, motion-capture systems, and hand ex-oskeletons capture dexterous human hand motion or provide haptic feedback outside conventional robot teleoperation setups [13]–[18]. Since these systems measure human hand motion, the recorded motion generally requires retargeting before it can be applied to a robotic hand. Handheld devices provide a more portable alternative for collecting demon-strations without the full robot, using wrist-view, gripper-centric, tactile, force, or tool-based interfaces for in-the-wild and contact-rich manipulation [5], [19]–[22]. Nevertheless, many existing handheld systems focus on parallel grippers or tool-level interfaces and provide limited continuous control of multiple robotic fingers. DEX-PHD integrates a dexterous robot hand into aportable handheld device, enabling direct robot-hand com-mand generation without human-to-robot finger retargeting. It also synchronously records wrist-camera video, hand states, and fingertip force signals without requiring a fixed robot platform or external computer. 

B. Multimodal Data Collection 

For contact-rich manipulation, visual trajectories alone are often insufficient because task success depends on contact timing, compliance, and interaction forces. Recent studies show that tactile, force, proprioceptive, and visuo-tactile feedback can improve policy learning for insertion, assem-bly, and compliant manipulation [23]–[25]. Demonstration-collection systems have also begun incorporating force or tactile sensing to capture interaction dynamics that are not directly observable from vision alone [19], [22]. DEX-PHD follows this direction by synchronously recording wrist-camera video, robot-hand commands, measured hand states, motor currents, and fingertip force signals directly from the dexterous hand during demonstration collection. A. Hardware System 

The DEX-PHD system, shown in Fig. 2, is a portable handheld data-collection device built around a 6-DOF robotic hand (Ability Hand, Psyonic [26]). The system is designed to allow a human operator to physically carry and manipulate the robotic hand while recording multimodal demonstra-tions from a hand-centered perspective. A custom handle is mounted alongside the dorsal side of the robotic hand, forming a compact handheld unit that can be operated without external teleoperation hardware or a fixed robot platform. The handle integrates independent flexion triggers for the index, middle, ring, and pinkie fingers, a thumb flexion trigger, and a dedicated thumb rotation knob. A potentiome-ter measures the thumb rotation knob, while each flexion trigger is instrumented with a Hall sensor-magnet pair. Low-stiffness return springs restore the triggers to their neutral positions after release without discretizing the continuous Hall-sensor input. These components provide the physical input channels used for the joint-level command mapping described in Section III-B. Beyond the input mechanisms, the handle geometry is intentionally refined to improve usability in contact-rich, real-world manipulation. The handle is mounted close to the dorsal side of the robotic hand to reduce the collision footprint of the device, thereby lowering the likelihood of unintended impacts with the environment during operation. In addition, the bottom surface of the handle is aligned with the robot palm. This co-planar alignment is important for planar, table-top manipulation because it preserves clearance comparable to a human hand and allows the robotic hand to approach and grasp table-level objects without the handle Fig. 2. B. Joint-Level Mapping 

Using the physical input channels described above, DEX-PHD converts the operator’s continuous trigger and knob inputs into joint-level commands for the robot hand. Before each data-collection session, the minimum and maximum values of each input channel are calibrated from the neutral and fully actuated configurations. Each measured input is then normalized between these calibrated bounds and linearly mapped to the corresponding motion range of the robotic hand. For the four fingers, the index, middle, ring, and pinkie triggers independently command the flexion of the corre-sponding digits. The thumb flexion trigger commands thumb closing, while the thumb knob commands thumb rotation. This one-to-one mapping provides an intuitive command space that allows the operator to independently coordinate the fingers for multi-finger reconfiguration, partial grasping, tool use, and sequential object release. During data collection, the generated joint-level com-mands are sent to the robotic hand, and the corresponding command inputs, motor positions, currents, and fingertip force values are logged. This logging procedure records both the commanded hand motion and the measured hand response, enabling the recorded robot-hand command trajec-tories to be used for later replay and analysis. We also characterized the responsiveness of the joint-level command pathway. The hand control and logging loop runs at 100 Hz, corresponding to a 10 ms update period. The end-to-end hand response delay was estimated by comparing the onset of the Hall-sensor trigger input with the onset of the corresponding measured joint-position response. Within the 10 ms temporal resolution of the onboard logger, typical finger responses were observed in the same control cycle as the corresponding input events, indicating that the input-to-response delay is below or close to the measurement resolution of the 100 Hz logging system. 

C. Trajectory Generation 

This paper adopted the visual SLAM pipeline similar to the UMI gripper to obtain portable, egocentric trajectories [5]. The wrist camera records RGB and IMU data, from which we estimate the device pose over time. Before each task, the user briefly scans a base fiducial and the scene to establish a local base frame {B} and keypoints for localization.

D. Data Synchronization 

As image data and robot hand data are saved in each local system, it is important to align the durations of both data for analysis and replay. To match the timestamps, users trigger the LEDs on the wrists by clicking the thumb flexion trigger button twice within 500 ms at the starting and ending points of each episode. Then the LEDs blink for 1 second. The synchronizing node clips the videos before and after the LEDs blink during post-processing to extract the wrist trajectory. During post-processing, the LED blink is detected at the video-frame level, and the hand-data sequence is aligned to the camera timeline. The hand data is logged at 100 Hz, corresponding to a 10 ms sampling interval, while the wrist-camera records at approximately 60 fps, corresponding to a 16.7 ms frame interval. After temporal alignment, each image frame is paired with the hand-data sample having the nearest timestamp. Therefore, the nominal hand–video synchronization resolution is on the order of tens of millisec-onds, determined by the 10 ms hand-data sampling interval and the 16.7 ms camera frame interval. For bimanual tasks, precise synchronization between the two devices is crucial as they produce asynchronous data streams with different durations. Following the methodology in [5], we utilized a rolling QR code, which is a sequence of time-encoded QR codes displayed on a screen and captured by both cameras, to map each frame’s timestamp to a unified system time. This allows for accurate offline alignment of multimodal data, ensuring temporal consistency across both hands. IV. D EMONSTRATIONS 

In this section, we evaluate three aspects of DEX-PHD. First, can DEX-PHD support untethered multimodal demon-stration collection across diverse physical environments? Second, does the continuous per-finger command interface enable demonstrations that require coordinated multi-finger manipulation? Finally, can the collected robot-hand com-mands and wrist trajectories be deployed on physical robot platforms while preserving task-relevant motion and contact patterns? The collected demonstrations were deployed using open-loop replay on two different robot embodiments in simula-tion: a humanoid robot with 7 DoFs in each arm (Unitree G1) [27] and a 7-DoF dual-arm mounted on a fixed table. In addition, the collected demonstrations were deployed on a physical humanoid robot. The same robotic hands were mounted at the wrist frame of each robot.

B. Inverse Kinematics 

Each embodiment possesses a unique configuration, char-acterized by its degrees of freedom (DoFs) and physical di-mensions. To replicate identical end effector motions across diverse robotic platforms, we solve the inverse kinematics (IK) problem for each specific embodiment. We employ a closed-loop IK solver based on the Pinocchio library [28] to compute joint trajectories while strictly adher-ing to joint limits as well as velocity and acceleration bounds. During the motion replay, joint commands are updated at each timestep and are rate-limited to ensure compliance with the mechanical velocity constraints. To deploy an identical motion sequence across heteroge-neous platforms, we apply embodiment-specific initial pose offsets to account for the structural differences in each robot’s workspace. Fig. 4(b) illustrates the resulting trajectories for various embodiments, demonstrating how these offsets are utilized to maintain the motion within the reachable workspace of each respective robot.

E. Results 

The quantitative results characterize one selected demon-stration–replay pair per task and therefore do not mea-sure variability across repeated executions or task success rates. Fig. 6 compares the joint position trajectories and fingertip forces recorded during DEX-PHD demonstration and physical humanoid replay, and Table II summarizes the corresponding quantitative results. The DEX-PHD hand trajectories are used as the reference, and the replayed G1 hand trajectories are treated as the measured response after resampling both signals onto a common time axis. Joint position error is reported as mean absolute error (MAE) to quantify absolute tracking deviation, while joint position correlation and contact correlation evaluate temporal trajec-tory and contact-pattern similarity, respectively. For both, values closer to 1 indicate better agreement between the DEX-PHD demonstration and physical replay. The fingertip force signals are analyzed as baseline-relative measurements. For each trial, the initial non-contact interval is used as the reference state, and the corresponding per-channel baseline is subtracted from subsequent measurements. Thus, the plotted values represent changes relative to the initial state rather than independently calibrated absolute force magnitudes. Contact correlation evaluates task-relevant contact-pattern similarity rather than absolute force reproduction. The six FSR values on each finger are aggregated into a single finger-level contact signal to reduce sensitivity to small differences in contact location.

2) Multi-object grasping: The multi-object grasping task requires sequential finger reconfiguration. The first cup is grasped with a tripod grasp, while the second cup is grasped using the remaining fingers and then released in reverse or-der. As shown in Fig. 6(b), the replayed trajectories preserve the overall timing and ordering of the demonstrated grasp and release events. The task achieved a mean joint MAE of 5.48 ◦ and a joint position correlation of 0.691. The primary grasping contacts were also reproduced, with a task-relevant contact correlation of 0.691 computed from the index finger and thumb. Differences in the secondary contact profiles are mainly attributed to small changes in cup placement and fingertip contact location during replay. Because the contact point can shift across the six FSRs on each finger, the measured baseline-relative force profiles can vary even when the grasp and release events occur in the correct order. Despite these variations, the main contact transitions associated with grasp-ing and releasing the cups remain clearly visible. 

3) Drilling: The drilling task requires coordinated tool grasping, thumb opposition, and independent index-finger actuation of the drill trigger. The complete task, including picking up the drill, fastening a screw, and replacing the tool, was demonstrated using DEX-PHD. During humanoid replay, however, the analysis focused on the in-air drilling motion because the drill weight and whole-body posture constraints made the full fastening sequence difficult to reproduce reliably on the humanoid platform. As shown in Fig. 6(c), the replayed hand motion preserves the main temporal structure of the demonstrated grasp and trigger-actuation sequence. Although drilling produced the largest joint MAE among the tasks at 7.54 ◦, it also showed the highest joint position correlation of 0.803, indicating that the overall motion pattern was largely retained despite pose shifts of the tool. The task-relevant contact correlation was 0.761, computed from the middle and ring fingers that maintained the primary power grasp on the drill handle. Variations in object positioning, tool weight, manipulator sagging, and torso orientation affected the drill pose during replay, so contact was not always applied to the same fingertip regions or with the same magnitude. As a result, the baseline-relative force profiles differ in timing and amplitude, while still preserving the main contact transitions associated with the power grasp and trigger-actuation sequence. These results indicate that DEX-PHD can replay coordinated per-finger command patterns for tool-use tasks while revealing how contact signals are affected by physical replay condi-tions. V. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented DEX-PHD, a portable handheld device for collecting multimodal demonstrations with dex-terous robotic hands. By physically integrating a dexterous robot hand into the handheld device, DEX-PHD enables continuous per-finger joint-level command generation while synchronously recording wrist-camera, hand states, and fin-gertip force signals. Its battery-powered and onboard logging architecture further enables untethered data collection across diverse indoor and outdoor environments without requiring a fixed robot platform or external PC. Across peg-in-hole insertion, multi-object grasping, and drilling tasks, DEX-PHD collected replayable demonstra-tions that preserved the main temporal structure of finger mo-tions during physical replay. The quantitative results showed task-dependent joint tracking errors, trajectory-shape similar-ity, and task-relevant contact-pattern agreement, suggesting that synchronized hand-state and contact signals can provide useful evaluation signals for contact-rich manipulation. Several limitations remain. The current replay experiments are primarily open-loop, so contact timing and fingertip force profiles can vary with object pose, alignment, and whole-body motion errors. Therefore, fingertip force signals are analyzed as baseline-relative contact patterns rather than independently calibrated absolute force measurements. In addition, the current interface is designed for the six actuated degrees of freedom of the robotic hand. Future work will extend the mounting structure, input mechanisms, and com-mand mapping to support other dexterous robotic hands, in-cluding hands with additional independently actuated joints.
