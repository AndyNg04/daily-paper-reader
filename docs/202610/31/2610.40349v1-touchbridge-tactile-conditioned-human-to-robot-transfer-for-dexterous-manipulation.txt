Abstract— Human demonstrations offer an efficient and scal-
able source of data for dexterous robot manipulation, but
differences in sensing and hand morphology complicate the
transfer of contact-rich interactions. We present TouchBridge,
a hardware–software co-designed framework for human-to-
robot data transformation. A wearable human demonstration
interface and a corresponding robot interface establish cross-
embodiment correspondence in visual and fingertip tactile
sensing. We introduce Tac-Retarget, a tactile-conditioned re-
targeting method that uses measured contact to align fingertip
contact patches and preserve their spatial relations within
and across hands. Unlike object-aware or simulation-based
transfer pipelines, Tac-Retarget requires no object mesh, object-
pose estimate, or physics simulation for retargeting, enabling
direct and scalable conversion of human demonstrations into
robot-domain training data. Combined with visual synthesis
and camera-anchored action conversion, TouchBridge pro-
duces robot-domain demonstrations for fine-tuning a pretrained
vision-language-action (VLA) model. Across four real-world
contact-rich tasks, policies fine-tuned solely on TouchBridge-
converted human demonstrations achieve a 72.5% average
success rate, compared with 77.5% using an equal number of
teleoperated robot demonstrations, while human demonstration
collection is 7.0× faster. Under the same policy and training
setup, Tac-Retarget outperforms DexPilot and UniDex by 25.0
and 42.5 percentage points, respectively.
I. INTRODUCTION
Building foundation models for contact-rich dexterous
manipulation requires large-scale demonstrations that capture
not only hand motion, but also physical interaction with
the environment. Collecting such data directly on dexterous
robots, however, is slow and demands substantial operator
effort. Human demonstrations provide a natural and poten-
tially scalable source of manipulation experience, and have
increasingly been used for large-scale pretraining [1], [2] and
direct human-to-robot transfer [3], [4]. Realizing this poten-
tial, however, requires transforming human experience into
robot-executable training data both faithfully and efficiently.
This transformation faces two key challenges. First, kine-
matic correspondence alone does not preserve physical in-
teraction. Conventional retargeting methods typically match
arXiv:2610.40349v1  [cs.RO]  29 Oct 2026


hand poses, fingertip positions, or hand-centric vectors [1],
[5], [6]. While effective for free-space motion, these objec-
tives do not explicitly capture contact. As a result, fixed
human-derived fingertip targets can displace a contacting
finger from its support region, distort relations among simul-
taneous contacts, or break contact altogether. For contact-rich
manipulation, kinematically plausible motion may therefore
still be physically ineffective.
Second, preserving physical interaction should not come
at the cost of data scalability. Object-aware approaches
incorporate hand–object geometry or physics-based con-
straints [7]–[10], while other human-to-robot pipelines refine
transferred trajectories or policies through simulation and
reinforcement learning [11], [12]. Such methods can improve
physical consistency, but require object meshes, object-pose
estimation, environment reconstruction, or simulation-based
rollout and refinement. These object- and task-specific re-
quirements introduce substantial modeling and engineering
overhead, making them difficult to scale with increasingly
large and diverse human datasets.
Our key observation is that physical contact itself provides
a natural correspondence across embodiments. Tactile sens-
ing directly reveals which fingertips are in contact, where
contact occurs, and how strongly different regions are loaded.
Because these signals are measured directly during human
demonstrations, they provide interaction constraints with-
out requiring explicit object reconstruction or simulation-
based refinement. Tactile sensing therefore addresses both
challenges simultaneously: it augments kinematic retargeting
with physical interaction information while preserving a
direct human-to-robot data conversion pathway.
Building on this idea, we present TouchBridge, a hard-
ware–software co-designed framework for scalable conver-
sion of human demonstrations of contact-rich dexterous
manipulation into robot-domain data. At the hardware level,
we develop TouchBridge-Cap, a wearable capture interface
that records RGB observations, hand motion, and finger-
tip tactile measurements. The corresponding robot interface
approximately matches the wrist-camera-to-hand geometry,
fingertip sensor placement, and tactile sensing layout of
TouchBridge-Cap, establishing cross-embodiment correspon-
dence in visual and tactile observations. At the software level,
TouchBridge transforms these human demonstrations into
robot-domain observations and actions for policy learning
without task-specific robot demonstrations or object-specific
environment modeling.
At the core of TouchBridge is Tac-Retarget, a tactile-
conditioned retargeting method that uses measured contact
to guide motion transfer across embodiments. Tac-Retarget
combines contact-patch alignment, which aligns tactile con-
tact regions between human and robot fingertips, with a
contact-activated graph, which preserves spatial relations
among simultaneous contacts within and across hands. To-
gether, these objectives transfer not only fingertip motion
but also the geometry and spatial structure of demonstrated
contacts. The optimization operates directly on measured
human kinematics and tactile responses, requiring no object
mesh, object-pose estimate, or physics simulation. Combined
with robot-domain visual synthesis and camera-anchored ac-
tion construction, the resulting pipeline converts raw human
demonstrations into robot-domain training sequences.
We evaluate TouchBridge on four contact-rich real-world
manipulation tasks: bulb screwing, sauce application, fragile-
object grasping, and page flipping. Under the same policy
and training setup, demonstrations converted using Tac-
Retarget yield an average real-robot policy success rate of
72.5%, outperforming DexPilot [5] and UniDex [1] by
25.0 and 42.5 percentage points, respectively, while also
achieving lower contact error. Policies fine-tuned solely
on TouchBridge-converted human demonstrations achieve a
72.5% average success rate, close to the 77.5% obtained
using an equal number of teleoperated robot demonstrations,
while human demonstrations are collected 7.0× faster. These
results show that TouchBridge retains much of the utility of
robot-collected demonstrations while substantially reducing
the cost of acquiring training data, providing a practical path
toward scaling robot learning from human experience.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We present TouchBridge, a hardware–software co-
designed framework that transforms contact-rich human
demonstrations into robot-domain training sequences.
Through aligned visual and fingertip tactile sensing
across human and robot interfaces, TouchBridge enables
policy learning for contact-rich dexterous manipulation
without task-specific teleoperated robot demonstrations.
• We introduce Tac-Retarget, a tactile-conditioned retar-
geting method that uses measured contact-patch align-
ment and a contact-activated graph to preserve physical
interaction across embodiments. Compared with kine-
matic retargeting, Tac-Retarget substantially improves
both contact preservation and downstream real-robot
policy success.
• TouchBridge converts human demonstrations without
object meshes, object-pose estimates, or simulation-
based rollout refinement. Together, the 7.0× faster
demonstration collection and policy performance close
to that obtained from robot-collected data make the
framework well suited for scaling robot learning from
human demonstrations.

collecting task data on physical robots. UniDex [1] and
EgoScale [2] use large-scale human-derived data to pre-
train transferable dexterous policies. These works primarily
emphasize scaling human-derived data, with less emphasis
on the fidelity of the converted demonstrations, particularly
the preservation of contact interactions across embodiments.
EgoMimic [13] and MotionTrans [14] co-train manipulation
policies on human and robot demonstrations. Many other
methods use simulation to ground human demonstrations
in the robot embodiment. ManipTrans [11] refines retar-

videos as deployment goals. Both involve additional effort
in environment construction and robot–object interaction
modeling. A further line of work develops interfaces and
transformation pipelines for learning directly from human

to match the target hand’s kinematics. Adapting to new
robot hands generally requires hardware redesign, which
is particularly challenging for high-DoF hands. Moreover,
the wearable mechanism is typically tailored to a limited

potentially compromising wearer comfort during demonstra-

without robot training data, but has only been demonstrated
with parallel-jaw grippers. AINA [4] extends robot-data-
free transfer to multi-fingered hands using visually estimated
hand–object geometry and fingertip motion, without explicit
tactile contact constraints. Our method instead uses measured
tactile signals to preserve contact patches and their spatial
relations during motion retargeting without simulation re-
finement, while using a glove-based capture interface that
requires no mechanical correspondence with the target hand’s
joint structure. This enables policies fine-tuned solely on
converted human demonstrations to be deployed on the real
robot without task-specific robot demonstrations.
B. Dexterous Retargeting

hands with different kinematic structures. DexPilot [5] and

vector correspondences. UniDex [1] combines fingertip in-
verse kinematics with manual adjustment of the hand-base
offset. These approaches are effective for pose transfer but
do not condition the mapping on tactile measurements.

uses simulation-trained control for execution. DexFlow [8]

sensors have corresponding placements and matched sensing
layouts. Differences in hand morphology and egocentric ap-
pearance are handled by the human-to-robot transformation
pipeline described in Sec. III-C.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
Overview: We evaluate TouchBridge through data analy-
ses and real-robot experiments to address four questions:
1) How closely do our human-derived robot demonstra-
tions resemble real-robot demonstrations in visual ob-
servations and actions?
2) Can policies fine-tuned solely on our human-derived
demonstrations achieve task performance comparable
to that of policies fine-tuned on an equal number of
teleoperated robot demonstrations?
3) How effective is Tac-Retarget compared with existing
simulation-free retargeting methods, and how do its
tactile components contribute to its performance?
4) How does policy pretraining affect learning from our
human-derived robot demonstrations?
A. Experimental Setup
1) Robot Platform and Policy Execution: All real-robot
evaluations are conducted on a Moz1 bimanual robot
equipped with two Wuji Hands. The visual and tactile sens-
ing configuration follows the deployment interface described


in Sec. III-A.2. All policies use the same observation acqui-
sition procedures, low-level controllers, and action-execution
rate.
2) Task Descriptions: We evaluate all methods on four
contact-rich tasks: Flip Book, Screw Bulb, Apply Sauce,
and Grasp Egg Roll. These tasks involve diverse contact
interactions, from precise fingertip manipulation to force-
sensitive object handling. Representative rollouts are shown
in Fig. 5.
Flip Book. The robot turns a single page of a book using
the right index finger. The motion requires sustained fingertip
contact throughout the page turn while avoiding displacement
of the book.
Screw Bulb. The robot screws a light bulb into a socket
until the bulb lights up. The task requires stable multi-finger
contact and accurate rotational motion.
Apply Sauce. The robot holds a baguette with its left
hand and squeezes ketchup onto it from left to right with its
right hand. The task requires precise fingertip force control
to produce the desired amount of ketchup.
Grasp Egg Roll. The robot picks up a fragile egg roll
with the right hand and places it onto a plate. The robot
must apply sufficient grasp force to prevent slippage without
causing visible deformation or breakage.
3) Data Collection: We construct separate human-derived
and teleoperated robot datasets for the four evaluation tasks.
The human demonstrations are converted into robot training
sequences using the pipeline in Sec. III-C. Tactile measure-
ments are used only for retargeting, not as policy inputs.
For each task, we collect 60 human demonstrations and 60
teleoperated robot demonstrations. The teleoperated demon-
strations serve as a reference for data-quality analysis and
are used to train separate policies for the data-source com-
parison. They are not mixed into the training data of our
method or the human-data baselines. Within each human
demonstration, the operator is instructed to minimize head
motion to reduce frame-to-frame viewpoint changes. Across
demonstrations, we vary the camera position and viewing an-
gle to increase the diversity of camera-object configurations.
4) Evaluation Protocol: Policy Success Rate. We evalu-
ate each policy over 20 real-world trials per task. Success rate
is the percentage of trials satisfying the task-specific success
criterion. Replay Success Rate. We replay 20 human-derived
converted demonstrations per task and report the percentage
of replays satisfying the same task-specific success criteria
used for policy evaluation. Data Collection Time. For each
task and collection method, we report the average collection
time per successful demonstration by dividing the total
elapsed collection time by the 60 successful demonstrations.

We assess our human-derived robot demonstrations from
two perspectives: visual similarity to real-robot observations
and the ability of the converted actions to reproduce success-
ful task execution.

observations exhibit greater separation from the real-robot

the visual gap. Fig. 7(a) provides a qualitative comparison

2) Action Replay: We replay the actions from our human-

Sec. IV-A.4, the converted actions achieve an average replay
success rate of 87.5% across the four tasks (Fig. 6(a)).
Together, these results suggest that our pipeline reduces
the visual gap with real-robot data while retaining effective
actions for task execution.
C. Policy Learning from Human-Derived vs. Robot Demon-
strations
We evaluate whether policies fine-tuned on our human-
derived demonstrations perform comparably to those fine-
tuned on teleoperated robot demonstrations. For each task,
we fine-tune GR00T N1.7 [21] on either 60 human-derived
demonstrations or 60 teleoperated robot demonstrations, us-
ing identical training settings.
As shown in Fig. 6(b), policies trained on human-derived
data achieve an average success rate of 72.5%, compared
with 77.5% for those trained on teleoperated robot data.
They achieve the same success rate on Flip Book and remain
within ten percentage points on each of the other three
tasks. These results show that policies fine-tuned solely on
our human-derived demonstrations achieve task success rates
close to those of policies fine-tuned on an equal number of
teleoperated robot demonstrations.
Human demonstration collection is also substantially faster
than robot teleoperation. As shown in Fig. 6(c), collecting a
successful demonstration takes 11.7 seconds on average with
TouchBridge-Cap, compared with 81.7 seconds through
teleoperation, corresponding to a 7.0× collection speedup.
Thus, faster demonstration collection and comparable policy
success rates suggest TouchBridge’s potential for scaling
robot learning from human demonstrations.

D. Evaluation of Tac-Retarget
We evaluate Tac-Retarget, its tactile ablations and base-
lines through contact preservation during trajectory replay in
simulation and downstream policy performance on the robot.
1) Contact Preservation in Simulation: We use Grasp Egg
Roll for this analysis because it provides a more tractable
setting for reliable contact simulation. We load the accurately
reconstructed object mesh into Isaac Sim [22] for evaluation.
We apply each retargeting method to the same 20 human
demonstrations, replay the resulting trajectories in the sim-
ulation environment and report the contact error of each
method in Tab. II. Contact error measures the Euclidean
distance between the response-weighted human tactile con-
tact center and the simulated contact center in corresponding
fingertip-local frames.
As shown in Tab. II, Tac-Retarget achieves the lowest
mean contact error of 4.667 mm, outperforming DexPilot [5]
and UniDex [1]. Removing either contact alignment or the
contact graph increases the error, with the kinematic-only
variant performing worst among the ablations. These results
show that both components improve contact-location preser-
vation. Fig. 8 illustrates common retargeting failure modes
across four tasks, including missing contacts, misplaced
contacts, and object penetration. Tac-Retarget preserves the
intended fingertip contacts without obvious object penetra-
tion in the examples shown.

V. CONCLUSION
We presented TouchBridge, a hardware–software co-
designed framework for learning contact-rich dexterous ma-
nipulation without task-specific robot demonstrations. It
combines TouchBridge-Cap for efficient human demonstra-
tion collection with Tac-Retarget for contact-preserving mo-
tion transfer, producing robot-domain data without object
meshes, object-pose estimates, or simulation-based refine-
ment. With faster demonstration collection and policy suc-
cess rates close to those obtained from robot-collected data,
we believe TouchBridge can serve as a practical framework
for the community to scale robot learning from human
demonstrations. Our current study uses a modest demon-
stration set; extending the framework to larger and more
diverse human datasets remains an important direction for
future work.

contacting fingertips, with an additional coefficient for cross-
hand pairs. These auxiliary terms preserve contact spacing
within and across hands.
