RadOnc-Agent: An LLM-Orchestrated Framework for AI
Workflows Across the Radiotherapy Care Pathway
Caiwen Jiang1, Shuoyang Wei1, Songlin Zhao1, Junyu Li1, Jingyuan Chen1, and Wei Liu1,*
1Department of Radiation Oncology, Mayo Clinic Arizona, Phoenix, AZ, USA.
*liu.wei@mayo.edu
ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence has advanced individual radiotherapy tasks, yet these capabilities remain separated across clinical stages, software
environments and data modalities. This fragmentation contrasts with the longitudinal radiotherapy workflow from treatment decision-making
through follow-up. Here we present RadOnc-Agent, an agentic artificial-intelligence framework that formalizes radiotherapy into four clinical
phases and provides 26 callable functions through a conversational interface. A large-language-model controller maps clinical intent to
schema-constrained calls, preserves patient and workflow context, and routes requests to specialist services. We evaluated system execution
using 2,600 single-function requests (7,800 repeat executions), 200 prespecified synthetic cross-stage scenarios spanning four phases (600
executions), and 120 workflow instances from 60 de-identified patient records (360 clean executions) representing decision-to-planning
and planning-to-adaptation. RadOnc-Agent selected the intended function in 98.79% of single-function executions, completed 96.50% of
scripted cross-stage workflows, and completed 96.67% of real-patient workflow executions. In comparative ablations, removing longitudinal
state reduced cross-stage completion from 96.50% to 84.00%, while disabling schema and identity validation increased mismatched
backend dispatch from 0% to 95.28% in a replay/test evaluation. These findings establish the technical feasibility of an LLM-orchestrated
architecture for coordinating heterogeneous radiotherapy capabilities and information across longitudinal workflows; they do not establish
clinical correctness, clinical utility or prospective benefit.
I Introduction
Radiotherapy is not a single procedure but a longitudinal and interdependent care pathway. It begins with diagnosis, assessment
of the indication for radiotherapy and selection of a treatment modality; proceeds through simulation, target and organ
delineation, treatment planning and dose optimisation; continues through image-guided delivery and adaptive management;
and extends into post-treatment surveillance and toxicity assessment 1. Information generated at one stage constrains the next:
treatment intent and prescription determine the structures that must be delineated, anatomy and beam geometry determine
the achievable dose, changes during treatment may prompt replanning, and delivered dose informs subsequent assessment
of toxicity. Clinical intelligence for radiotherapy must therefore operate across a connected sequence of decisions, images,
quantitative models and longitudinal records rather than within any one task alone.
Artificial intelligence has nevertheless advanced mainly through task-specific models. Deep-learning systems can delineate
organs at risk 2, predict three-dimensional dose distributions 3, register longitudinal images 4, and synthesize computed
tomography from magnetic-resonance or cone-beam computed-tomography images5. These advances have improved individual
technical operations, but most have been developed and evaluated as point solutions with task-specific inputs, outputs and
software environments. Their use in a clinical pathway still requires a person to identify the appropriate model, assemble its
inputs, transfer information between systems and interpret its output in the context of preceding and subsequent decisions.
Improvements in individual model performance therefore do not by themselves create a coherent intelligence system for
radiotherapy care.
Agentic artificial intelligence offers a potential control layer for this problem. Large language models can translate
natural-language intent into structured actions, invoke external computational tools and organize their outputs without being
retrained to perform every underlying task 6,7. More broadly in medicine, clinically oriented agents have begun to retrieve
longitudinal patient information and execute structured diagnostic and therapeutic tasks within controlled electronic-health-
record environments8. In radiotherapy, recent agents have performed iterative inverse planning through direct interaction with
a treatment-planning system9 and coordinated synthetic-CT generation, registration, segmentation and dose prediction for
online adaptive planning10. These studies establish the feasibility of agentic coordination within a treatment-planning episode.
They do not, however, address the broader longitudinal pathway in which the planning episode is linked to the initial treatment
decision, simulation, treatment delivery, follow-up and workflow coordination.
A full-pathway radiotherapy agent requires more than a conversational model placed in front of a collection of algorithms.
It requires an explicit representation of the clinical workflow, a common execution contract for heterogeneous specialist models,

continuity of patient and task context across stages, and a modular architecture in which individual capabilities can be updated
without redesigning the overall system. The objective is not to replace specialist models with a monolithic general-purpose
model, but to establish a division of labour in which a language-model controller handles clinical intent and task coordination
while domain-specific methods perform the imaging, dosimetric and quantitative computations for which they were designed.
Here we present RadOnc-Agent, an agentic artificial-intelligence framework that formalizes radiotherapy into four intercon-
nected clinical phases and provides 26 callable functions through a unified conversational interface (Fig. 1a). Seventeen core
functions span diagnosis and treatment decision-making, simulation and planning, on-treatment adaptive management, and
follow-up and outcomes; 9 cross-cutting clinical utilities support information retrieval, workflow logistics and general clinical
tasks. Several utilities replace manual navigation across separate clinical interfaces with programmatic access to the same
institutional data services, returning the retrieved information through a common structured interface. The language-model
controller maintains workflow context and routes structured calls through a common function registry to a specialist intelligence
layer comprising models and methods developed by our group and evaluated in prior task-specific studies (Fig. 1b). These
capabilities can be invoked sequentially within a shared patient context, forming a longitudinal path from the initial treatment
decision through planning, adaptation and follow-up (Fig. 1c). In this Article, we define the clinical taxonomy and computational
architecture of RadOnc-Agent, characterize the evidence supporting its specialist capabilities, and evaluate the architecture at
complementary levels: single-function orchestration, prespecified cross-stage scenarios spanning four radiotherapy phases and
real-patient planning-and-adaptation workflow execution. This framework provides a workflow-native approach to artificial
intelligence in radiotherapy and an architectural pattern that may be adaptable to other complex healthcare workflows requiring
coordinated use of heterogeneous clinical models.
II Results
II.1 RadOnc-Agent spans four radiotherapy phases through 26 callable functions
RadOnc-Agent comprised 26 callable functions organized into a phase-specific core layer and a cross-cutting utility layer
(Fig. 1a). The 17 core functions included two functions for Diagnosis & Decision, six for Simulation & Planning, two for
On-Treatment Adaptation and seven for Follow-up & Outcomes. Together, these functions spanned the pathway from treatment-
indication assessment and modality selection through image processing, contouring, dose prediction, plan optimization, adaptive
assessment and longitudinal outcome evaluation.
Nine additional utilities provided patient-status retrieval, diagnostic-imaging and implant lookup, clinical-trial information
retrieval, schedule and message access, literature search, proton stopping-power calculation and platform guidance. These
utilities were available across clinical phases and were therefore not treated as a fifth phase. Each of the 26 capabilities was
exposed as an independently callable function with defined inputs and structured outputs, enabling individual execution and
downstream reuse within a shared patient context (Fig. 1b,c). Concise function descriptions and headline validation results are
provided in Supplementary Table S1.
II.2 RadOnc-Agent coordinates heterogeneous specialist intelligence through a shared execution architecture
RadOnc-Agent exposed the 26 registered capabilities through a shared execution architecture linking the language-model
controller, function registry and specialist-intelligence layer (Fig. 1b). A clinician request and the associated patient context
were interpreted by the controller, matched to a registered function and routed to one of three backend classes: specialist
artificial-intelligence models for imaging, contouring, dose and planning; knowledge-retrieval or deterministic calculation
services; and EHR-based clinical utilities. Outputs from each backend class were returned through the same interface and
incorporated into the maintained patient and workflow context.
All 26 capabilities were represented by a common callable contract specifying the function identity, clinical scope, required
inputs, principal output, execution mode and service route. Computationally intensive imaging and dosimetric functions
executed asynchronously, whereas retrieval, calculation and EHR-based utilities returned results synchronously through the
same function-calling interface. This separation decoupled the controller from the implementation of any individual specialist
method and allowed structured outputs to be passed to downstream functions without changing the orchestration logic.
Architecture-level performance was evaluated using 2,600 unique single-function requests spanning all 26 registered
capabilities, with 100 requests per function. Each function contributed 30 direct, explicit requests, 30 natural clinical requests
containing patient context, 20 abbreviated, paraphrased or reordered requests and 20 requests deliberately confusable with
a neighbouring function. Each unique request was repeated three times in separate, newly initialized conversation sessions,
with the conversational state reset before every run, yielding 7,800 request executions for assessing run-to-run reproducibility
(Fig. 2a). RadOnc-Agent selected the intended function in 7,706 executions (98.79%). The generated call conformed to the
schema of the invoked function in 7,743 executions (99.27%), technical backend dispatch succeeded in 7,719 (98.96%), and
7,694 returned a parseable structured output (98.64%) (Fig. 2b).

Function-selection accuracy was 99.79% (2,335 of 2,340) for direct, explicit requests, 99.10% (2,319 of 2,340) for natural
clinical requests containing patient context, 98.91% (1,543 of 1,560) for abbreviated, paraphrased or reordered requests and

Across the 2,600 unique requests, the intended function was selected in all three sessions for 2,536 requests (97.54%), in exactly
two sessions for 43 (1.65%), in one session for 12 (0.46%) and in no session for 9 (0.35%). Thus, at least two of three sessions
were correct for 2,579 requests (99.19%) (Fig. 2d).
Missing-input handling was evaluated separately using 520 unique requests, comprising 20 requests for each of the 26
registered functions. Each request was repeated three times in a separate, context-reset session, yielding 1,560 executions.
Missing required information was detected in 1,528 executions (97.95%), and a safe clarification or withholding response
was issued in 1,516 (97.18%); 44 executions (2.82%) inappropriately attempted to proceed (Fig. 2e). Detection and final safe
handling were scored independently: final safe handling additionally required a targeted clarification or withholding response
without an execution attempt. Across the 7,719 normal executions that successfully reached backend dispatch, median controller
orchestration overhead from request receipt to backend dispatch was 1.42 s (interquartile range, 1.08–1.96 s), excluding backend
inference time (Fig. 2f). These measurements quantify the reliability and reproducibility of function selection, argument
construction and technical execution, including input-sufficiency checking, rather than the clinical validity of specialist-model
outputs.
II.3 Comparative ablations quantify the contributions of routing, state and validation
To distinguish the contributions of language-based intent interpretation, longitudinal state management and deterministic
validation, we compared the complete RadOnc-Agent configuration with prespecified architecture ablations using the same
locked registry, benchmark inputs and scoring rules (Table 2; Fig. 3). In the single-function benchmark, the full controller
selected the intended function in 7,706 of 7,800 executions (98.79%), compared with 7,448 (95.49%) for the embedding-based
router (difference, +3.30 percentage points). The difference increased from +1.28 percentage points for direct requests to +4.04
percentage points for paraphrased requests and +7.31 percentage points for confusable requests (Fig. 3a).
In the cross-stage benchmark, the stateful controller completed 579 of 600 workflows (96.50%) and adhered to the reference
trajectory in 570 (95.00%), compared with 504 (84.00%) and 477 (79.50%), respectively, for the stateless controller. Information
was transferred correctly in 2,507 of 2,550 eligible hand-offs (98.31%) with the stateful controller and 2,269 (88.98%) with the
stateless controller; corresponding context-retention rates were 4,782 of 4,800 turns (99.63%) and 4,382 (91.29%). The stateful
controller generated 34 unnecessary calls among 3,150 required calls (1.08%), compared with 128 (4.06%) for the stateless
controller (Fig. 3b,c).
In replay or test execution, the enabled validation gates blocked all 44 controller attempts to dispatch a call with a required
input missing (0 of 1,560 unsafe dispatches), whereas bypassing the gates permitted 44 unsafe dispatches (2.82%). In the
360 identifier-integrity executions, the enabled gates detected 356 mismatches (98.89%) and safely rejected 354 (98.33%),
compared with 19 (5.28%) and 17 (4.72%), respectively, when bypassed. No mismatched dispatch occurred with enabled gates,
whereas 343 of 360 (95.28%) mismatched calls were dispatched when gates were bypassed (Fig. 3d,e). No bypassed call was
routed to a live clinical backend. These comparisons quantify the distinct contributions of language-based routing, persistent
state and deterministic guards under the evaluated conditions; they do not assess clinical correctness or clinical utility.
II.4 RadOnc-Agent preserves clinical context and coordinates specialist functions across radiotherapy stages
The cross-stage benchmark comprised 200 prespecified, unique multi-stage scenarios, with 50 scenarios in each of four
workflow families. Each scenario was executed three times in a separate, newly initialized conversation session after clearing
the preceding conversational state, yielding 600 workflow executions in total. A prespecified robustness subset of 90 executions
received a missing-input or transient-backend perturbation; all remaining executions used the original scenario inputs and
locked system settings. The 200 scenarios constituted the unique test units. Figure 4a summarizes the synthetic scenario format
and scoring design. Depending on the workflow family, baseline clinical information and additional imaging, treatment or
follow-up artefacts were released in prespecified user turns. The agent received the clinical inputs and objectives but not the
names or order of the expected functions; a hidden reference trajectory was used only for scoring the execution. Across 3,150
required function calls, RadOnc-Agent completed the full set of required calls in 579 of 600 executions (96.50%) and satisfied
the prespecified trajectory and order constraints in 570 (95.00%). Required information was transferred correctly in 2,507
of 2,550 interfunction hand-offs (98.31%), and patient and treatment context was retained in 4,782 of 4,800 evaluated turns
(99.63%) (Fig. 4b). No cross-case contamination was observed; the 18 context-retention errors therefore reflected failures
within the active case rather than transfer of information between patient records. Together, these results show that the controller
could compose multiple specialist functions while preserving the data dependencies and patient-specific state required for
downstream execution, rather than merely routing isolated requests.
Completion remained above 96% for all three workflows linking consecutive clinical phases. The agent completed 148
of 150 decision-to-planning workflows (98.67%), 145 of 150 planning-to-adaptation workflows (96.67%) and 147 of 150

treatment-to-follow-up workflows (98.00%). It completed 139 of 150 full-pathway workflows (92.67%), a descriptive reduction
consistent with the greater coordination burden of the longer phase-1-to-phase-4 call chains (Fig. 4c). Thus, coordination
remained reliable across both consecutive-phase and full-pathway workflows, while the lower full-pathway completion rate
identified long-horizon execution as the more demanding setting.
In the backend-mode stratification, 343 of 360 live-backend executions (95.28%) and 236 of 240 replay/test-backend
executions (98.33%) completed all required calls. The corresponding rates of trajectory adherence were 93.89% (338 of
360) and 96.67% (232 of 240), respectively (Fig. 4d). These estimates are reported descriptively because the stratification
distinguishes operational integration from orchestration-only execution rather than constituting a randomized comparison of
backend modes. The high completion rate with live backends further shows that cross-stage coordination was retained during
interaction with operational specialist services and was not confined to replay/test execution.
Of the 21 incomplete workflows, nine (42.86%) were attributed primarily to failure to propagate a required upstream output,
six (28.57%) to a prerequisite or call-order violation, four (19.05%) to persistent backend failure and two (9.52%) to unresolved
missing input (Fig. 4e). Among 90 executions affected by missing input or a transient backend failure, 84 recovered and
subsequently completed the required workflow (93.33%). The agent generated 34 unnecessary calls relative to 3,150 required
calls, corresponding to an excess-call ratio of 1.08%, and generated no calls containing information from another case in
4,800 evaluated turns (Fig. 4f). Collectively, the low excess-call rate, absence of cross-case contamination and recovery of
most perturbed executions demonstrate controlled workflow-state management under both routine and disrupted conditions.
These findings establish the technical ability of RadOnc-Agent to coordinate specialist functions and preserve context across
radiotherapy stages; they do not by themselves establish the clinical accuracy of the specialist-model outputs, which is evaluated
separately below.
II.5 RadOnc-Agent executes cross-stage workflows using real-patient radiotherapy data
We conducted a retrospective, offline workflow-execution evaluation using 60 de-identified real-patient radiotherapy records
that were held out from framework development, prompt refinement and specialist-model training or tuning. Each patient
contributed one decision-to-planning workflow and one planning-to-adaptation workflow, yielding 120 patient-specific workflow
instances. The source records linked diagnosis and course metadata with the planning CT, structures, treatment-plan and
dose objects, and at least one on-treatment verification CT. The controller, function registry and specialist backends were
locked before evaluation, and each workflow was executed three times in separately initialized, context-reset sessions. These
360 clean executions were analysed separately from a prespecified 48-execution stress test with injected missing-input or
transient-backend failures.
Decision-to-planning workflows supplied the recorded diagnosis, treatment intent, prescription and planning CT, and
required generation of a planning directive followed by target-and-OAR delineation. Planning-to-adaptation workflows supplied
the reference planning image, structures and dose together with on-treatment verification imaging, and required deformable
registration followed by replan assessment. For the latter family, the reference endpoint was the presence of a subsequently
recorded treatment-plan change in the source record; it was not interpreted as a clinical judgment of whether adaptive replanning
was indicated.
The primary endpoints were technical completion of all required functions, adherence to the prespecified call trajectory,
validity of function arguments, correct propagation of patient, course and plan identifiers, parseable structured outputs and
successful backend dispatch. Secondary endpoints were context retention, unnecessary calls, recovery from injected missing-
input or transient-backend failures, and controller overhead excluding specialist-model inference. These endpoints test whether
the framework can compose and execute patient-specific workflows on real radiotherapy data; they do not evaluate the clinical
correctness, clinical acceptability or prospective utility of the generated outputs.
Across 360 clean workflow executions, RadOnc-Agent completed all required functions in 348 (96.67%) and adhered to
the prespecified trajectory in 344 (95.56%) (Fig. 5b). Among 720 required function calls, 713 (99.03%) had schema-valid
arguments, 711 (98.75%) were successfully dispatched and 708 (98.33%) returned parseable structured outputs. Required
patient, course and plan identifiers were propagated correctly in 351 of 354 eligible hand-offs (99.15%); an eligible hand-off
required both an upstream output and a downstream function contract containing at least one of these identifiers.
Completion was 176 of 180 (97.78%) for decision-to-planning workflows and 172 of 180 (95.56%) for planning-to-
adaptation workflows; the corresponding trajectory-adherence rates were 174 of 180 (96.67%) and 170 of 180 (94.44%),
respectively (Fig. 5c). Context was retained throughout 355 of 360 executions (98.61%), with no cross-patient contamination
observed. The agent issued 10 unnecessary calls relative to 720 required calls, corresponding to an excess-call ratio of 1.39%
(Fig. 5d). Median controller overhead was 1.47 s (interquartile range, 1.10–2.03 s), excluding specialist-model inference.
In the separate perturbation stress test, the agent recovered and completed the required trajectory in 45 of 48 executions
(93.75%): 23 of 24 (95.83%) after a missing-input perturbation and 22 of 24 (91.67%) after a transient backend failure (Fig. 5e).
Across the three clean runs per workflow, 112 of 120 workflows (93.33%) completed successfully in all runs, five (4.17%) in

II.6 Specialist functions are supported by task-specific evidence across four radiotherapy phases
All 17 core functions and 9 cross-cutting utilities were implemented in RadOnc-Agent and assessed using evidence appropriate
to their implementation. Trainable specialist models and functions producing interpretive clinical outputs were evaluated in
original task-specific studies or prespecified current-framework evaluations, whereas direct database/API retrieval and static-
response utilities were characterized by technical system-level endpoints. To keep the main text focused on framework-level
capability, we selected representative evaluations spanning all four clinical phases and the principal clinical, imaging and
dosimetric data types (Fig. 6). Because the evaluated functions used different cohorts, reference standards and endpoints,
each result is reported in its native metric and the heterogeneous estimates are not pooled. Concise function descriptions and
headline validation results are provided in Supplementary Table S1; the corresponding development and evaluation datasets are
summarized in Supplementary Table S2.
II.6.1 Diagnosis & Decision phase
For clinical decision support, 7,903 cases were curated from 15,724 patient records; 7,500 were used for development and 403
formed an independent test set11. Radiation oncologists rated 46–52% of the generated regimens as completely acceptable
and interchangeable with the clinical regimen. Including minor but clinically acceptable deviations increased acceptance to
64–66% (Fig. 6a). Modality-selection accuracy across photon, proton, electron and brachytherapy was 70.5–72.2% across the
two evaluated configurations.
II.6.2 Simulation & Planning phase
Supporting evidence for planning-directive generation was obtained from the summarization of 607 CT-simulation orders
across seven treatment-modality and disease-site groups; more than 98% of generated summaries agreed with therapist-verified
references12. This study evaluated extraction and structuring of simulation instructions rather than the full clinical acceptability
of a newly generated planning directive. Deformable registration was evaluated on an independent set of 586 patients and 2,122
image pairs drawn from a multi-institutional collection of 3,473 patients, 5,005 treatment plans and 16,361 CT examinations
13. OAR and target propagated-contour Dice scores were 92.07% and 87.14%, respectively; OAR folding was 0.08%, and
accumulated- dose mean absolute error was 0.72% (Fig. 6b).
Target-and-OAR delineation was evaluated across 79 datasets containing more than 26,600 three-dimensional volumes14.
On a pooled two-centre held-out set of 370 plans, CTV and matched-OAR Dice scores were 0.629 and 0.737, respectively, and
a complete CTV and 79-OAR draft was generated in approximately 7 s per case (Fig. 6c). Dose-prediction generalizability was
evaluated in 12 cases with previously unseen beam angles or prescriptions, comprising six prostate and six lung cases15. In the
prostate subset, target and OAR gamma passing rates were 96.79±0.83% and 94.29±1.01%, respectively, at 3%/2 mm, with
each three-dimensional prediction completed within 0.3 s (Fig. 6d).
Plan optimization was evaluated in 35 cervical-cancer cases, including five customization cases and 30 test cases 16. Two
controller configurations generated acceptable VMAT plans in 16.3±5.0 and 9.8±2.1 min, with mean conformity indices
of 0.929±0.007 and 0.928±0.007 and homogeneity indices of 0.058±0.006 and 0.059±0.005, respectively. The callable
optimization function was subsequently implemented using reusable planning skills17.
II.6.3 On-Treatment Adaptive Management phase
Verification CT synthesis was evaluated in ten head-and-neck patients using same-day in-room CT as the reference18. Re-
constructed CT images achieved a mean absolute error of 44.58±20.21 HU; dose- recalculation gamma passing rates were
99.66±0.39% at 3%/3 mm and 97.85±1.76% at 2%/2 mm, and setup shifts were recovered with a mean error of 0.40±0.16 mm.
Adaptive replanning was evaluated separately in 11 patients with prostate cancer19. Patient-specific quality-assurance gamma
passing rates were 99.57±0.46% before delivery and 98.59±1.29% after delivery, and the complete workflow required
9.12±0.85 min excluding manual intervention (Fig. 6e).
II.6.4 Follow-up & Outcomes phase
Longitudinal record retrieval and outcome assessment were evaluated in four non-overlapping cohorts comprising 895 patients20.
All six demographic fields were reproduced correctly in all 500 patients in the structured-data cohort (3,000 of 3,000 fields),
and radiation-course information was reproduced in 497 of 500 patients (99.4%). Among 395 additional patients assessed

Collectively, these representative results demonstrate specialist capability across all four phases while keeping the main
analysis centered on clinical functions rather than individual model identities or historical baselines. Together with the complete
26-function evidence map in Supplementary Table S1, they show that the specialist-intelligence and utility layers are supported
by function-appropriate clinical, imaging, dosimetric or technical evidence. The native metrics are retained to preserve the
scope of each evaluation and are not interpreted as a single pooled measure of system-level clinical performance.
III Discussion
RadOnc-Agent was designed to address a systems-level problem in radiotherapy: clinically useful artificial intelligence must
coordinate decisions, images, plans and longitudinal records across the care pathway rather than optimize a single isolated
task. Evidence from complementary levels of evaluation supports the technical feasibility of this approach. The controller
selected the intended function in 98.79% of single-function executions and completed all required calls in 96.50% of scripted
multi-stage executions. The real-patient evaluation extends these tests to linked diagnosis, planning and on-treatment imaging
data by assessing patient-specific function execution, identifier propagation and context preservation. Taken together, these
evaluations show that a common controller can operationalize heterogeneous specialist intelligence across four radiotherapy
phases. They do not establish clinical correctness, clinical utility, autonomous clinical performance or patient benefit.
The separation between the three evaluation levels is informative. High single-function routing accuracy did not by itself
establish longitudinal orchestration. Technical completion decreased from 96.67–98.67% for workflows connecting consecutive
phases to 92.67% for full-pathway workflows. Failures were concentrated in propagation of upstream outputs and prerequisite
or ordering violations. Thus, the principal challenge shifts from selecting an individual function to maintaining state and
preserving data dependencies as a workflow becomes longer. The real-patient planning and adaptation evaluation is intended to
test these same technical dependencies with patient-specific objects rather than synthetic identifiers. The present study does
not include a reader study or a prospective comparison with standard clinical workflow. It therefore cannot estimate time
savings, editing burden, staffing effects or error reduction in routine care. These remain important questions for a subsequent
human-in-the-loop evaluation. The integration of patient, imaging, planning and treatment objects through a single workflow
provides the technical basis for such a study, but does not itself establish operational benefit.
The contribution of RadOnc-Agent is consequently distinct from that of a new monolithic prediction model. Its 17 core
functions and 9 cross-cutting utilities were developed for different tasks and retain their own inputs, outputs and validation
endpoints. The framework provides a shared clinical taxonomy, callable contracts and a clinician-facing interface through which
these functions can be composed while their implementations remain modular. For data-access utilities, the innovation is the
integration itself: patient, imaging, messaging, trial and scheduling information that would otherwise be obtained by navigating
separate clinical interfaces can be retrieved through the same programmatic workflow and passed directly to downstream
functions. This division of labour allows specialist methods to perform imaging, dosimetric and quantitative calculations,
while the controller interprets intent and manages workflow state. It also permits an individual backend to be updated without
redesigning the full system, although any such change would require renewed validation of both the function and the affected
workflow trajectories. Because the supporting studies used heterogeneous cohorts, reference standards and native metrics, the
function-level results in Supplementary Table S1 establish breadth of specialist capability but should not be pooled into a single
measure of clinical performance.
This work also differs in scope from existing medical and radiotherapy agents. General tool-using language-model
approaches established that reasoning models can invoke external actions and computational resources 6,7, and MIRA extended
agentic evaluation to structured diagnostic and therapeutic tasks in general medicine8. In radiotherapy, recent systems have
focused on interaction with treatment-planning software or coordination within an adaptive-planning episode9,10. RadOnc-Agent
instead evaluates longitudinal coordination across all four phases in scripted cross-stage scenarios and tests planning-and-
adaptation execution using linked real-patient radiotherapy data. The comparison is therefore one of workflow scope and
information continuity, rather than a direct comparison of task-specific model accuracy.
The results also define an important boundary between orchestration and autonomy. RadOnc-Agent is intended to support,
rather than replace, clinical decision-making, and its intermediate and final outputs remain subject to professional review. In the
systematic missing-input benchmark, the agent issued a safe clarification or withholding response in 1,516 of 1,560 executions
(97.18%), but inappropriately attempted to proceed in 44 (2.82%). Wherever feasible, a code-enforced constraint provides
a stronger guarantee than an instruction followed probabilistically by a language model. Wider use will therefore require
machine-verifiable input, provenance and confidence checks, together with explicit escalation to the responsible clinician when
6/26


evidence or decision criteria are insufficient. The present missing-input benchmark characterizes input-sufficiency handling but
is not a comprehensive safety or adversarial-robustness evaluation.
Several additional limitations should be considered. First, the real-patient evaluation was retrospective and offline; it
assessed technical workflow execution but not clinical correctness, changes in treatment decisions, treatment delivery or patient
outcomes. Second, no reader study was conducted; the study therefore does not measure active completion time, workload, alert
burden, automation bias or clinician acceptance. Third, the function-level evidence was assembled from different datasets and
study designs, and evidence maturity therefore varies across the 26 functions despite all being operationally available. Fourth,
repeated clean executions in the single-function and real-patient benchmarks measure run-to-run stability under a fixed system
configuration; they do not establish robustness to distribution shift, local workflow differences or future changes in the controller
and backends. Finally, the missing-input benchmark does not cover the full space of ambiguous instructions, corrupted inputs,
conflicting evidence, cybersecurity threats or interactions among multiple simultaneous failures. These limitations preclude
interpreting the present results as evidence for unsupervised clinical use.
Broader clinical translation should therefore proceed in stages. External retrospective evaluation should first test trans-
portability across institutions, disease sites, patient subgroups and local software configurations. This should be followed
by prospective silent deployment to measure data availability, latency, failure recovery and model drift without influencing
care. A subsequent multidisciplinary, human-in-the-loop pilot could evaluate acceptance, overrides, editing burden, workflow
interruptions and failure escalation during routine practice. Only after these stages should a prospective comparative study
assess effects on clinical decisions, efficiency and, where appropriate, patient-centred outcomes. Prespecified performance
gates and mandatory human review are particularly important for full-pathway workflows, which were the most demanding
setting in the present study.
IV Conclusion
RadOnc-Agent integrates 17 phase-specific functions and 9 cross-cutting utilities into a longitudinal radiotherapy intelligence
framework. Across single-function routing benchmarks, prespecified cross-stage scenarios spanning four radiotherapy phases
and real-patient decision-to-planning and planning-to-adaptation workflows, the system demonstrated reliable function calling,
preservation of workflow context and technical execution of patient-specific radiotherapy workflows. These findings establish a
workflow-native technical foundation for longitudinal orchestration across the radiotherapy care pathway. External clinical
validation, prospective human-in-the-loop evaluation and robust operational safeguards remain necessary before broader
deployment of functions that have not completed clinical validation.

V.1 Ethics
This single-site retrospective study at Mayo Clinic Arizona was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board
(IRB #: 24-011106) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board waived
the requirement for informed consent because of the retrospective study design. Data access, storage and analysis were
conducted within Mayo Clinic Arizona in accordance with institutional data-use and security requirements. The architecture
and cross-stage system-engineering benchmarks used synthetic scenario inputs only and were determined to be exempt from
additional human-subject research requirements.
V.2 Study design, cohorts and data sources
The study comprised four complementary evaluation components: a single-function architecture benchmark of 2,600 unique
requests, a scripted cross-stage workflow benchmark of 200 unique scenarios, a retrospective real-patient workflow-execution
evaluation of 60 patients and 120 workflow instances, and task-specific evaluations of the 26 callable functions. The architecture
benchmark used a prespecified synthetic request set derived from the registered function contracts: 100 normal requests
per function (2,600 requests in total), supplemented by 20 missing-input requests per function (520 requests in total). The
cross-stage benchmark used 200 prespecified synthetic scenarios, with 50 scenarios in each of the decision-to-planning,
planning-to-adaptation, treatment-to-follow-up and full-pathway workflow families. These two benchmarks used synthetic
clinical contexts and were designed to assess orchestration independently of patient-level specialist-model performance.
The real-patient cohort was used to evaluate technical execution of the integrated framework on linked radiotherapy data
rather than clinical acceptability or clinical utility. Each real-patient workflow was repeated three times in independent context-
reset sessions. Function-level evidence was obtained separately from task-specific evaluations using retrospectively collected
clinical datasets and, where appropriate, public datasets. Because the development and evaluation cohorts, reference standards
and endpoints differed across functions, concise function descriptions and headline results are reported in Supplementary
Table S1; detailed implementation, development and evaluation data are reported in Supplementary Table S2. Functions
7/26


based on general-model prompting, retrieval, deterministic calculation, database or API access, or static responses did not
require a task-specific model-training dataset. For trained models, training, validation and evaluation datasets were separated at
the patient level unless otherwise specified; patient-specific and external-validation designs are identified in Supplementary
Table S2.
The real-patient cohort was retrospectively assembled from de-identified radiotherapy planning and on-treatment imaging
records at Mayo Clinic Arizona from May 2016 through December 2024. Available data comprised diagnosis and course
metadata; recorded treatment intent, prescription, planned and delivered fractions and dose; planning CT; structure contours;
treatment-plan and dose objects; and at least one verification CT. Records were linked by patient, course and plan identifiers.
Patients were eligible when a treatment course contained a planning CT, structure set, treatment plan, dose distribution and
at least one verification CT that could be linked reliably to the same patient and course. Records were excluded when any
required object was missing or unreadable, or when patient, course or plan linkage was ambiguous. To provide balanced
anatomical representation for this technical evaluation, eligible cases were stratified into abdomen, breast, head, head-and-neck
and pelvic groups, and 12 patients meeting all criteria were selected from each group. One treatment course was selected per
patient, and each patient contributed two workflow instances and was therefore treated as a clustering unit in repeated-execution
analyses. Before cohort selection, patient identifiers used for framework development, prompt refinement or specialist-model
development were compiled and excluded from the candidate pool. The evaluation cohort was then selected from the remaining
eligible patients.
V.3 System implementation and shared execution contract
RadOnc-Agent comprises a language-model controller, a machine-readable function registry, a dispatch layer and independently
deployed backend services. The controller’s system prompt is compiled from the registry, which specifies for each function
its name and description, clinical scope, required and optional arguments, output class, execution mode, backend route and
registered caveats. Given a user request and the available patient context, the controller emits a structured function-call directive
identifying the selected function and its arguments. The dispatch layer validates the call against the registered schema, invokes
the corresponding backend and returns the resulting payload to the conversational state.
The main application and computationally intensive specialist-model services are implemented as independent Flask
applications. Long-running inference, including image synthesis, segmentation, registration and dose prediction, uses an
asynchronous job-queue pattern: a submission endpoint returns a job identifier, a status endpoint reports progress and
a download endpoint, proxied by the main application, returns the completed result. Lightweight knowledge retrieval,
deterministic calculation and EHR-based utilities are executed synchronously. Backend responses are normalized into a shared
result envelope before being added to the patient and workflow context, enabling a subsequent function call to consume a
preceding function’s structured output. For clinical information-access utilities, this integration layer enables programmatic
retrieval through the same callable contract, reducing the need for manual navigation, copying and transfer across separate
oncology-information, electronic-health-record, imaging, messaging and scheduling interfaces. The framework does not
reinterpret a source-system result when the registered utility is defined as direct retrieval. The shared contract separates
orchestration logic from model implementation, so that a versioned specialist service can be updated without changing the
controller or its function-calling protocol.
All system-level evaluations used gpt-5.2 (OpenAI) as the controller model. The controller configuration, 26-function
registry and specialist-backend versions were locked before the system-level evaluations. Model calls used fixed decoding
settings (temperature 0.0 and top-p 1.0) with automatic, serial tool calling and a bounded retry policy for transient failures. The
system prompt and registry were locked as version 1.0. Patient and workflow context was maintained in a session-scoped state
object bound to one active patient and treatment course. Patient, course and plan identifiers were checked before each backend
dispatch, and calls inconsistent with the active session were rejected. Structured outputs retained their originating-function
and patient-, course- and plan-level provenance. No persistent conversational memory or cross-patient cache was used, and
session state was cleared after every independent execution. Clinical-backend communication was encrypted and restricted to
authenticated application services, with audit logs retained for backend dispatches.
V.4 Callable functions and implementation basis
RadOnc-Agent comprised 17 phase-specific core functions and 9 cross-cutting utilities. Core functions were assigned
prospectively to one of four radiotherapy phases and generated a phase-specific clinical, imaging or quantitative output. Cross-
cutting utilities provided information retrieval, workflow logistics, deterministic calculation or platform guidance that could be
used in more than one phase. Each capability was exposed through the common function registry and shared structured-output
contract described above. Supplementary Table S1 provides a concise description and headline evidence for all 26 functions;
Supplementary Table S2 provides development and evaluation data for functions with a formal task-level evaluation.
Diagnosis & Decision. RT-indication assessment retrieves and cites disease-specific guidance from a locally curated guideline
corpus and returns an insufficient-evidence response when the retrieved material does not support the request. Modality
8/26


selection produces a comparative assessment from the clinical context using the previously evaluated fine-tuned language-model
approach11. When candidate-plan dose information and a toxicity endpoint with verified coefficients are available, it additionally
performs a deterministic Lyman–Kutcher–Burman normal-tissue complication-probability calculation; unsupported endpoints
are not assigned a numerical estimate21.
Simulation & Planning. Planning-directive generation drafts a structured simulation and planning instruction from the diagnosis,
treatment intent, prescription and retrieved guidance12. MR-to-CT synthesis generates a synthetic CT from planning MRI and
a body mask for MR-only simulation. Deformable registration produces a deformation field and propagated structures from
baseline and follow-up image data 13. Auto-contouring returns target and OAR masks from planning CT and plan-context
information14. Proton dose prediction produces a three-dimensional PBSPT dose distribution from CT, structures, beam
information and prescription3,15. Plan optimization iteratively updates planning objectives in response to treatment-planning-
system feedback and returns the resulting plan state16,17.
On-Treatment Adaptive Management. Verification CT synthesis generates a CT-like image from on-treatment CBCT or
orthogonal kV images for adaptive workflow use18. Replan assessment combines registered baseline and on-treatment anatomy
with available dose information to report deformation- and dose-based change metrics and, when configured, a replan-trigger
classification. This output is presented as decision support and does not initiate replanning or replace clinician review.
Follow-up & Outcomes. Seven functions use a common EHR-grounded reasoning mechanism to draft a treatment-completion
note and to assess recurrence, mandibular osteoradionecrosis, brain necrosis, cardiac injury, radiation esophagitis and radiation
pneumonitis from relevant longitudinal clinical, imaging and dose records. Published task-specific evaluations support the
recurrence and mandibular-ORN components 20; the retrieved record categories and condition-specific criteria differ by function.
No outcome function autonomously establishes a diagnosis.
V.4.1 Cross-cutting utilities
The nine utilities provide patient-status, implant, diagnostic-imaging and In-Basket-message retrieval; clinical-trial search;
schedule summary and deterministic conflict checking; literature search; proton stopping-power lookup; and platform guidance.
Retrieval utilities return the corresponding source-system records without reinterpretation. Trial search returns candidate
trials and their stored criteria but does not independently adjudicate eligibility. Schedule checking identifies time overlaps
deterministically; proton stopping-power lookup uses locally stored reference tables; literature search calls the NCBI E-utilities
service; and platform guidance returns registered static information. These utilities contribute workflow integration rather than
new predictive models and were evaluated by system-level function selection, argument validity, dispatch and structured-return
validity.
V.5 Architecture-level evaluation
A reference set of 2,600 unique single-function requests was constructed to cover all 26 registered capabilities, with 100
requests per function. For each function, the set comprised 30 direct and explicit requests, 30 natural clinical requests containing
patient context, 20 requests using abbreviations, paraphrases or reordered wording, and 20 deliberately challenging requests
designed to distinguish the target from a neighbouring function. Each request had a prespecified intended function, and schema
conformance was checked automatically against the function registry. Every request was submitted in three separate, newly
initialized conversation sessions using the same input and system configuration, with the conversational state reset before each
run, yielding 7,800 executions. The 2,600 unique requests were the test units; the three executions of each request were treated
as repeated observations for evaluating run-to-run reproducibility.
Function-selection accuracy was defined as exact agreement between the selected and annotated functions. Argument
validity required the generated arguments to satisfy the schema of the function actually selected by the controller; this endpoint
was therefore evaluated independently of whether that function matched the annotated target. Technical dispatch success
required the generated call to reach a registered backend without a routing or transport error, and structured-output success
required the returned response to be parseable under the shared result contract. These four endpoints were analysed as marginal
proportions and were not combined into an end-to-end success rate. Repeated-run performance was summarized at the level of
the 2,600 unique requests as the number of sessions, from zero to three, in which the intended function was selected.
A distinct missing-input set comprised 520 unique requests, with 20 requests per registered function, each repeated in
three newly initialized, context-reset sessions for 1,560 executions. We recorded separately whether the controller detected
incomplete required information and whether it issued a targeted clarification or withholding response without an execution
attempt. An attempt to call a backend despite unresolved required input was counted as an inappropriate execution attempt.
Orchestration overhead was measured from request receipt to backend dispatch, excluding specialist-model inference and
external-service response time, and is reported as the median and interquartile range.
Two investigators familiar with radiation-oncology workflows and the function registry constructed the benchmark by
creating clinically grounded seed templates for each function. Candidate benchmark requests, including direct, clinical-context,
abbreviated, paraphrased and reordered variants, were generated from these templates using a language model distinct from the
9/26


evaluated controller. Investigators reviewed and edited each item to preserve a single intended clinical task without explicitly
naming the target function. For each function, one to three neighbouring functions were prespecified on the basis of overlapping
scope, inputs or outputs. Two reviewers independently assigned the intended-function label to every item, with disagreements
resolved by a third reviewer before evaluation. The benchmark was finalized after the controller prompt and registry were
locked and was not used for prompt refinement, registry modification or backend tuning.
Missing-input requests were generated by withholding one schema-required input and manually confirming that execution
should require clarification. Twenty functions were exercised through live backend interfaces and six computationally intensive
imaging or planning functions through replay or test interfaces, corresponding to 6,000 and 1,800 normal-request executions,
respectively. Replay and test backends reproduced the registered input and output contracts to isolate orchestration from
repeated specialist-model inference. Controller overhead was calculated across the 7,719 executions that successfully reached
backend dispatch, from request receipt to issuance of the validated backend request; specialist-model inference, asynchronous
jobs and external-service response time were excluded.
V.6 Comparative architecture-ablation evaluation
We prespecified comparative ablations to identify the contributions of language-based routing, persistent workflow state and
deterministic validation to the system-level results. All configurations used the same locked function registry, benchmark
inputs, controller decoding settings and scoring rules as the corresponding primary evaluation. The complete RadOnc-Agent
configuration served as the reference. Three comparators were evaluated only on endpoints to which they could meaningfully
contribute.
First, an embedding-based router was evaluated on the 2,600 unique single-function requests. The router selected the
registered function whose standardized description had the highest cosine similarity to the request, using the same fixed
text-embedding model and registered function description for every request. It did not generate function arguments or dispatch
a backend; its sole endpoint was exact intended-function selection, overall and by request type. Second, a stateless-controller
ablation used the same language model, registry and deterministic validation layer as the complete system, but discarded the
patient/workflow state and preceding structured output after each turn. The next turn therefore contained only the newly released
scenario input. This configuration was evaluated on the 200 cross-stage scenarios, each run three times in independently
initialized sessions, using completion, trajectory adherence, information transfer, context retention and unnecessary calls as
endpoints.
Third, a validation-gate ablation used the same controller and stateful execution logic but bypassed the pre-dispatch schema
and patient/course/plan identifier checks. It was evaluated exclusively through replay or test interfaces: no bypassed call was
sent to a live clinical backend. The missing-input benchmark was used to compare controller attempts to proceed with the
resulting unsafe backend dispatches. A prespecified identifier-integrity subset was constructed by substituting an inconsistent
patient, course or plan identifier at eligible replay/test hand-offs and comprised 360 executions. Its endpoints were detection of
the mismatch, safe rejection and mismatched dispatch. Each comparator used the same repeated-run design as its corresponding
reference benchmark. Benchmark items were not used for comparator selection or tuning.
V.7 Cross-stage workflow evaluation
Multi-stage scenarios were organized into four workflow families representing decision-to-planning, planning-to-adaptation,
treatment-to-follow-up and complete phase-1-to-phase-4 execution (Table 1). The evaluation comprised 200 unique scenarios,
50 per family. Each scenario was executed three times in a separate, newly initialized session after clearing the conversational
state, yielding 600 workflow executions. The scenarios were the unique test units, and repeated executions were clustered within
scenario. A prespecified robustness subset of 90 executions received either a missing-input or transient-backend perturbation;
all other executions used the original scenario inputs and locked system settings. Of the 600 executions, 360 used live backends
and 240 used replay or test backends. Live mode invoked the available registered service, whereas replay or test mode returned
a controlled backend response to isolate orchestration behaviour. Cross-cutting utilities were invoked when required but were
not treated as an additional clinical phase.
Each scenario comprised a scripted sequence of clinical inputs and user objectives expressed without naming the functions
to be called. Baseline patient information initiated the session, and additional imaging, treatment or follow-up artefacts were
released at prespecified turns when required by the workflow family. The agent was provided only with these scenario inputs; the
adjudication record was withheld and specified the required function calls, partial-order constraints, required data dependencies
between calls and acceptable optional calls. Figure 4a shows an illustrative synthetic full-pathway scenario in this format. A
workflow was considered complete only if all required calls returned a parseable output or an explicitly accepted non-execution
outcome in a prespecified missing-input scenario, such as a clarification request without backend dispatch.
Trajectory adherence required completion of every required function in an order consistent with the reference constraints.
Information-transfer accuracy was scored at each eligible hand-off by comparing the patient identifier and required structured
fields received by the downstream function with the upstream result and scenario record. Context retention required the agent to
10/26


preserve the active patient, treatment intent, prescription and current workflow stage across turns without importing information
from another case. The 600 executions contained 3,150 required calls, 2,550 eligible interfunction hand-offs and 4,800 turns
at which context retention was scored. Calls not included among the required or acceptable optional functions were counted
as unnecessary; the excess-call ratio used the 3,150 required calls as its denominator. Recovery was evaluated only in the
prespecified 90-execution robustness subset and required subsequent successful workflow completion after clarification or
restoration of the backend. Backend execution mode was logged for each call as live, replay or test so that orchestration-only
results were not presented as live clinical integration.
Two investigators familiar with radiation-oncology workflows and the registered functions constructed the scenarios. Two
additional reviewers independently assessed clinical plausibility, uniqueness of the intended trajectory and adequacy of the
reference calls; disagreements about required functions, permissible ordering or acceptable optional calls were resolved by
consensus before evaluation. Scenario construction was completed after the controller prompt, registry and backend interfaces
were locked, and no evaluation scenario was used for prompt refinement or registry modification.
Scenario difficulty was balanced prospectively by the number of clinical stages, required calls, interfunction dependencies
and conversational turns. Across the 150 executions in each family, the three shorter families contained 690–720 required calls,
540–570 eligible hand-offs and 1,050 scored turns; full-pathway workflows contained 1,020 required calls, 870 hand-offs and
1,650 scored turns by design. Within each family, 90 executions used live backends and 60 used replay or test backends. The
90-execution robustness subset comprised 45 missing-input and 45 transient-backend perturbations, distributed approximately
evenly across workflow families. Missing-input recovery required targeted clarification followed by resumption of the reference
trajectory after the information was supplied; transient-failure recovery required completion after service restoration. Cross-case
context isolation was assessed by interleaving sessions for distinct synthetic patients and checking the active patient identifier in
the session state and dispatched calls at each scored turn.
V.8 Real-patient cross-stage workflow-execution evaluation
The real-patient evaluation comprised 60 de-identified patient records, each contributing a decision-to-planning and a planning-
to-adaptation workflow. The controller configuration, function registry and specialist-backend versions were locked before the
first evaluation case. All functions were executed through their available backends in a retrospective offline environment, and
no output was used to direct patient care.
For decision-to-planning workflows, the input package contained recorded diagnosis and course metadata, treatment
intent and prescription, and the planning CT. The reference trajectory required planning-directive generation followed by
auto-contouring. For planning-to-adaptation workflows, the input package contained the planning CT, contours, reference
dose and at least one verification CT. The reference trajectory required deformable registration followed by replan assessment.
Reference trajectories and required function arguments were specified before execution from the registered function contracts
and available source objects. Decision-to-planning inputs included the patient and course identifiers, disease site and stage
when available, treatment modality, prescription, planning-CT series and image volume. Planning-directive generation used
the live clinical-text backend and auto-contouring used the deployed segmentation service. Planning-to-adaptation inputs
additionally included the reference-plan identifier, structure set, dose distribution, verification-CT series and image volume, and
the associated treatment fraction or date; registration and replan assessment used their deployed backends. When more than one
verification CT was available, we selected the earliest scan acquired after treatment initiation that met the prespecified technical
eligibility criteria and could be linked unambiguously to the reference plan. For multiple eligible scans on the same date, the
scan closest in time to the documented treatment fraction was selected.
A recorded subsequent-plan-change label was derived from the linked treatment record when a later plan was present in the
same treatment course. This label was used only to evaluate detection of a subsequently recorded plan change; it was not used as
a reference for the clinical appropriateness of adaptive replanning. A plan change required a new treatment-plan identifier after
the selected verification CT, within the same course, that replaced the reference plan for one or more subsequently delivered
fractions and was associated with a new planning or dose object. Preplanned sequential, boost or cone-down plans, field-in-field
or delivery-only revisions, and plans documented before the selected verification CT were not counted as adaptive plan changes.
When multiple qualifying later plans were present, the first was used for the binary label. Cases with ambiguous linkage
between a later plan and the reference course were excluded from the plan-change comparison but retained in the technical
workflow-execution analysis.
Each workflow was executed three times with identical inputs and locked system settings. Every execution began in a
separately initialized, context-reset conversation session. The primary outcomes were required-function completion, trajectory
adherence, argument validity, patient/course/plan identifier propagation, structured-output validity and backend-dispatch success.
Secondary outcomes were context retention, unnecessary calls, recovery after injected missing inputs or transient backend
failures, controller overhead and run-to-run reproducibility. Evaluation was performed against the prespecified technical
reference trajectory and execution logs; no expert clinical acceptability rating, reader study or prospective clinical-utility
11/26


endpoint was included. Identifier propagation was evaluated only for eligible hand-offs, defined as calls with an available
upstream output and a downstream function contract requiring a patient, course or plan identifier. For the one-per-workflow
comparison with subsequently recorded plan-change status, the output from the first prespecified clean execution was used; the
other two clean executions were reserved for the reproducibility analysis.
The three clean executions per workflow comprised the primary evaluation (360 executions). A separate prespecified stress
test comprised 48 additional executions: 24 with a missing required input and 24 with a transient backend failure. Recovery
required the agent to resolve the injected condition and complete the prespecified trajectory; these perturbed executions were
not included in the clean-execution completion, trajectory or reproducibility endpoints.
V.9 Task-specific function-level evaluation
Function-level evaluations used the task-appropriate unit, reference standard and native endpoint for each callable capability;
heterogeneous endpoints were not pooled. For capabilities evaluated in previously reported task-specific studies, the original
held-out cohorts, reference standards and analysis definitions were retained3,11–16,18,20. Function descriptions and resulting
estimates are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Evaluations conducted specifically for the current framework are
described below; development data, evaluation data and reference standards are reported in Supplementary Table S2.
Guideline-grounded RT-indication assessment was evaluated with 300 prespecified questions across ten disease sites,
comprising 240 questions supported by the indexed guideline corpus and 60 deliberately unsupported or out-of-corpus questions.
Clinically grounded questions were balanced across disease sites and finalized before system evaluation. Two radiation
oncologists independently established the reference answer, supporting passage and expected refusal status while blinded to
agent outputs; disagreements were resolved by consensus and third-reviewer adjudication when needed.
MR-to-CT synthesis used the publicly released 578 paired MRI–CT cases from the SynthRAD2025 MRI-to-CT dataset: 221
head-and-neck, 182 thoracic and 175 abdominal cases22. Cases were split at the patient level with stratification by anatomical
region into 462 training, 58 validation and 58 internal held-out test cases. Images were resampled, intensity-normalized and
cropped or padded using the training-set pipeline; the validation MAE was used for checkpoint selection. MAE and SSIM were
calculated within the reference body contour.
Replan assessment was evaluated within the real-patient planning-to-adaptation workflow evaluation using the same 60
Mayo Clinic Arizona patients described above. The reference endpoint was a subsequently recorded plan change after the
selected verification CT, defined as a new plan that replaced the reference plan for one or more subsequently delivered fractions
in the same course. The function output from the first prespecified clean execution was compared with this recorded endpoint;
it was not interpreted as a reference for the clinical appropriateness of adaptive replanning. Completion-note generation was
evaluated in 200 completed radiotherapy courses. Two radiation oncologists independently compared prespecified diagnosis,
treatment, toxicity and follow-up fields with the source record while blinded to one another’s ratings. A clinically important
omission was one capable of materially changing interpretation of delivered treatment, toxicity or planned follow-up; substantive
edits added, removed or changed clinically meaningful factual content. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.
Brain-necrosis and cardiac-injury assessment were each evaluated in 200 patients. The respective datasets contained 60
and 55 adjudicated positive cases, with the remaining cases negative. Brain necrosis was established from post-radiotherapy
imaging, treatment history and clinical documentation; cardiac injury was established from thoracic radiotherapy history,
cardiac dose exposure, cardiac findings and longitudinal records. Two physicians assigned reference labels while blinded to
model output, with consensus and third-reviewer adjudication when needed.
Radiation esophagitis and radiation pneumonitis assessment were each evaluated using 150 grade-stratified clinical episodes.
The esophagitis cohort included 35, 40, 40, 25 and 10 episodes of CTCAE grades 0–4, respectively; the pneumonitis cohort
included 40, 35, 40, 25 and 10 episodes, respectively. Two radiation oncologists independently assigned CTCAE v5.0 grades
while blinded to agent output, with consensus and third-reviewer adjudication when needed. Patient status, implant retrieval,
diagnostic imaging, In-Basket messages, trial search, schedule/conflict checking, platform guidance, PubMed search and
PSTAR lookup were treated as direct retrieval, deterministic-calculation, static-response or external-API utilities. They were
assessed through system-level function selection, argument validity, dispatch and structured-return validity rather than a separate
predictive-performance study.
V.10 Evaluation metrics
System-level orchestration endpoints were defined in the corresponding evaluation subsections above. For guideline assessment,
top-5 evidence retrieval was successful when at least one adjudicated supporting passage was returned among the first five
results. Answer accuracy required agreement with the adjudicated answer or, for an unsupported question, an appropriate
refusal. Citation correctness required the cited passage to support the generated statement.
Binary clinical endpoints were summarized using sensitivity, specificity, precision, recall, F1 score and accuracy relative
to the prespecified reference standard. The positive class was the adjudicated condition for outcome assessments and a
subsequently recorded plan change for replan assessment. AUROC was calculated from a continuous model score before
12/26


applying the prespecified operating threshold. Ordinal toxicity grades were evaluated using exact agreement, macro-F1 and
weighted Cohen’s κ. Refusal rates used unsupported or out-of-corpus questions as the denominator.
Deformable registration and auto-contouring were evaluated using Dice similarity relative to expert manual contours;
inference time was additionally reported for auto-contouring. MR-to-CT synthesis was evaluated using body-region Hounsfield-
unit mean absolute error (MAE) and structural similarity relative to the reference CT. Verification CT synthesis was evaluated
using CT MAE, CT-number error, dose-recalculation gamma passing rate, setup-shift error and reconstruction time. Dose
prediction used target, OAR and body gamma passing rates and inference time relative to the reference dose. Gamma criteria
and dose thresholds are reported with each relevant experiment. Factual accuracy for structured summaries was calculated at
the prespecified field level; completeness was the proportion of required fields present, and a clinically important omission was
an absent item judged capable of changing clinical interpretation or follow-up. Usability ratings distinguished outputs requiring
no modification, minor modification, major modification or rejection according to the definitions used in the system-level
evaluation.
V.11 Statistical analysis
The analysis unit was the unique request, scenario, workflow, patient, imaging episode or retrieved item, as specified for each
experiment. Repeated executions were not treated as independent test units and were summarized or analysed as observations
clustered within the originating request, scenario or workflow. Categorical outcomes are reported as counts and percentages;
continuous outcomes are reported as mean±s.d. or median and interquartile range according to their distribution. No imputation
was performed. Each endpoint used its prespecified evaluable denominator; technical failures were counted as failures for
completion-related endpoints, whereas cases lacking objects required for a specific endpoint were excluded only from that
endpoint.
Workflow-family, backend-mode, failure-category and reproducibility analyses were prespecified descriptive secondary
analyses. For the real-patient evaluation, repeated executions were clustered within workflow and workflows were clustered
within patient. Benchmark request and scenario counts were prespecified to balance registered functions, request types and
workflow families. The 60-patient cohort provided balanced coverage of five anatomical groups (12 patients per group) and
both real-patient workflow families; three clean executions assessed run-to-run reproducibility, and 48 perturbed executions
balanced missing-input and transient-backend stress testing. No formal power calculation was performed because endpoints
were descriptive technical measures rather than clinical-effect comparisons.
For comparative ablations, the complete configuration and the relevant comparator were evaluated on the same unique
requests or scenarios. We report the paired count and percentage for each configuration and the absolute paired difference in
percentage points. The embedding-router comparison was limited to function selection; the stateless-controller comparison was
limited to cross-stage execution endpoints; and the validation-gate comparison was limited to replay or test safety endpoints.
Data Availability
The de-identified Mayo Clinic Arizona clinical imaging, treatment-planning and longitudinal record data used for real-patient
and task-specific evaluations cannot be publicly shared because they remain subject to institutional review board and institutional
data-governance restrictions. The specialist functions integrated in RadOnc-Agent were developed and evaluated in task-specific
studies using combinations of institutional and public data; the public resources listed below are components of those original
development or external evaluation datasets, rather than the sole data used to develop the models. The MRI-to-CT synthesis
study used the SynthRAD2025 collection. The deformable-registration study additionally included the public 4D-Lung,
LongCT-Lung, NLST-New-lesion-LongCT, Pancreas-CT and RIDER Lung CT collections as external cohorts. The auto-
contouring study additionally used the public SAT-DS corpus for image-encoder pretraining, and the OpenKBP, LCTSC and
TCIA Soft-Tissue-Sarcoma collection for external evaluation. The latter contains 51 soft-tissue sarcoma cases with tumour
(GTV) contours, rather than radiotherapy CTV contours, and no OAR annotations. The constituent datasets in SAT-DS and the
Soft-Tissue-Sarcoma collection are available under the terms specified by their original providers. The NIST PSTAR reference
tables used by the stopping-power lookup utility are available from the NIST PSTAR database. Supplementary Table S2
identifies the data source for each task-specific function.
Code Availability
The RadOnc-Agent source code is not publicly available because the framework is maintained and deployed within the Mayo
Clinic enterprise clinical environment and includes institution-specific clinical-system integrations and security configurations.
Access is restricted to authorized users; individual functions are made available to clinical users only after internal validation,
and some functions remain in development and are not enabled for all clinical users. Model weights trained using institutional
clinical data are also not publicly available because of institutional review board, privacy and data-governance restrictions.
