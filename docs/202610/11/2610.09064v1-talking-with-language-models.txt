# Abstract 

When we interact with large language models (LLMs), are we having a conversa-tion? They are designed to invite us to treat them as intelligent interlocutors who remember, act, and make commitments. But appearances deceive. We introduce the artifactual stance, a framework that reconceives human–AI interaction as artifact-mediated exchanges of candidate texts. LLM outputs are candidate texts optimized for utility, not utterances bearing meaning or force. LLMs are sophisticated text generators, not speakers. Between sessions, nothing runs; between turns, no one remembers. What persists is a configuration and a transcript. The “conversation” is a user’s solo performance, interpretive labour disguised by interface and artifact design. This shift dissolves recent philosophical puzzles. Questions about what ‘I’ and ‘you’ refer to in AI exchanges, about whether systems can lie or be held to promises, about the identity of our supposed interlocutors all rest on a false presupposition. There is no speaker behind the screen, hence no one to refer to, no one to hold responsible. What feels like dialogue with someone is interaction with an artifact that generates text at unprecedented scale and fit. By abandoning the conversational framing, we see these systems for what they are: immensely sophisticated artifacts that afford varied uses. The philosophical questions that matter are about the normative underpinnings of design, adoption, authorization, and human practices of use. 

Keywords : affordance, AI reference, conceptual engineering, conversational agents, conversational alignment, design stance, human–AI interaction, intentional stance, large language models

What differs is the strength of the invitation to describe the system as a speaker and the interaction as a conversation. These conversational descriptions usually pass unnoticed because they are so natural. They structure how users talk about these systems, as well as how philosophers and researchers characterize them. Anthropomorphic framing appears, by one recent estimate, in roughly half of research papers on LLMs, concentrated in work on dialogue, ethics, and model analysis, and its prevalence is rising (Ibrahim and Cheng, 2025). Much discussion about what these systems know, whether they understand or reason, and whether they approach general or super intelligence implicitly treats them as speaking a natural language and engaging in conversational activities, such as answering questions, giving explanations, making and defending claims, and deceiving users and evaluators. For some, the conversational reading is evidence that these systems amount to more than just impressive engineering: they are rational agents, deserving of moral standing, and perhaps are even conscious. 4

In contrast, we want to call into doubt the presupposition that such interactions are conversations with an entity that is an interlocutor. 5 If that presupposition is itself at issue, we cannot simply ask what kind of entity is speaking in this or that conversation, or what kind of entity satisfies the desiderata of being in a conversation. Instead, we must first ask in neutral terms what kind of interaction it is, without assuming that the conversational reading has earned its place. Posed neutrally, our question then is: what kind of interaction is a human–AI text exchange? Is it a genuine conversation or something else that merely resembles or feels like one? This question sits at the intersection of several literatures, including the philosophy of language (e.g., the status of the exchange, the meaning of the text outputs, the reference of its pronouns and names); the metaphysics of AI (e.g., the individuation and persistence condition of AI systems); the interdisciplinary field of human-computer interaction (HCI); and AI ethics (e.g., questions concerning responsibility for speech, liability, and design). Our strategy is to characterize the interaction first. Once that is done, we argue, the downstream questions change shape. 6

Our answer, briefly, is this: human–AI text exchange is an interaction of artifact-mediated text production and uptake : an exchange of candidates for text and speech , not speech itself, whose resemblance to conversation is explained by the design of the artifact and the interpretive capacities and tendencies of the user. 7 Accordingly, our project can be understood as a form of conceptual engineering. Rather than assuming that the concepts used to describe human-human interaction transfer straightforwardly to human–LLM interaction, or indeed LLM–LLM interaction, we ask what concepts are better suited to the latter. 8 To this end, we introduce the terms candidate string and

Our view is not the pessimist’s dismissal. LLMs and their outputs are immensely sophisticated and useful, and rejecting their status as conversational interlocutors does not diminish the need to explain how and why they are sophisticated and useful. Nor does our view imply that users like Maya are simply confused or deluded, though some undoubtedly are. The paper proceeds as follows. §2 introduces the artifactual stance , which directs us to explain LLM behaviour in terms of their affordances and design, rather than beginning with anthropomorphic descriptions. §3 adopts this stance and develops an account of LLMs as candidate generators : their outputs are candidate strings and interactions in which those outputs are produced and used are 

candidate exchanges . The remainder of the paper shows how this framework explains many features of LLMs: the nature of their sophistication, their role as a source of information and an aid to work, and the efficacy of their outputs in general. As a consequence, we get an account of both the usefulness and the conversational appearance of LLM interactions, as well as an explanation of their behavior. §4 argues that candidate exchanges are not conversations. For although they reproduce some features of the surface structure of conversations, the production of candidate text does not constitute a speech act and the communicative and normative work remains on the user’s side. §5 applies this account to the puzzling phenomena that motivate the paper. §6 considers the consequences for reference and responsibility. §7 concludes. 

# 2 AI Systems Are Artifacts, Not Intentional Agents 

2.1 Dennett’s Interpretational Stances 

When we describe interactions with LLMs, we often use the same kind of language that describes our interactions with other people: we say that we ask an LLM a question, they answer, we object, they ask for clarification, ruminate, change their mind, say false things, hallucinate, and so on. When we try to predict their outputs, it is natural to imagine what a well-informed person would say: if we input ‘what is the capital of France’, we expect ‘Paris’ to be the answer, and it seems natural to explain that expectation by saying that the LLM believes that Paris is the capital of France and it wants to tell the truth or provide us with information. When we explain what they do, we might talk about what they believe, what they want, and how good their reasoning powers are. All this seems to fit exceptionally well with the intentional stance as described in (Dennett, 1987). Dennett’s core idea is that, when we understand, explain, or predict behaviour of complex systems, we can choose to view them at varying levels of abstraction, or stances . First, there is the physical stance , which allows us to predict and explain the behaviour of a complex system by appealing to its physical constitution and the physical laws governing it. For example, a calculator is a physical object, so its calculations obey the laws of physics. In principle, we could predict what it will display when we prompt ‘ 159 + 628 = ’ just by looking at the complete description of its physical state and what happens when certain buttons are pushed in a certain sequence. In practice, though, complete physical descriptions are often hard to come by and the relevant calculations are very difficult. Instead, it is more useful to go up a level of abstraction and take the second stance, the design stance . This strategy allows us to predict and explain the behaviour of a complex system by taking into account how it is designed, appealing to how it works and what its functions are. For example, unless it is malfunctioning, the calculator will function as it was designed to. Since it was designed to calculate sums and we trust it was well made, we predict it will output

Failures are to be explained at the physical level, but if all goes well, the design stance is more useful for predicting and explaining the output of the calculator. Sometimes, Dennett claims, neither the physical stance nor the design stance offers the right level of abstraction, and we need instead a third stance: the intentional stance . We take this stance towards people and animals, and possibly other things too. It amounts to explaining and predicting the behaviour of a complex system by treating it as though its behaviour is to be predicted as a rational agent, by figuring out the beliefs and desires it ought to have “given its place in the world and its purpose” (Dennett, 1987, 17). For example, one cannot predict whether you will take your umbrella based on your design. And that is not because you were not designed, since Dennett did want to apply the design stance to biological organisms, who evolved to have certain capacities. Rather, it is because knowing your evolutionary makeup does not help one figure out whether you take your umbrella with you today. Instead, one needs to find out if you believe it will rain, if you want to arrive dry at your destination, and so on. When you do take your umbrella, a good explanation is based on your beliefs and desires. To summarise, then, we adopt the stance that best explains and predicts the behaviour of the system under consideration. “Adopting the intentional stance” need not entail the adoption of any particular metaphysics of propositional attitudes. For example, one might adopt the intentional stance towards dogs while remaining neutral on the question whether dogs have beliefs. Contrastingly, one might take the intentional stance towards something, while claiming that it lacks beliefs and desires, so long as one is interested in predicting behaviour and this prediction method is reliable enough. How do these interpretational strategies help to explain and predict the behaviour of LLMs? The temptation to adopt the intentional stance is obvious. For example, extreme optimists argue that sophisticated AI systems are genuine cognitive and linguistic agents (Cappelen and Dever, 2026). According to this view, they possess beliefs, desires, intentions, and other cognitive states; they also understand and use language meaningfully, and their outputs can constitute speech acts. Similarly, interpretationists argue that attributions of mental states to AI systems best explain or rationalize the system’s behaviour in context (van Dijk et al., 2023; Lederman and Mahowald, 2024; Martela, 2025; Goldstein and Lederman, 2026). Chalmers (2026) proposes that LLMs have “quasi-beliefs” and “quasi-desires”, attributable purely on the basis of behavioural interpretability. But, importantly, he stresses that such states are cheap: a Roomba has them, as does the OpenAI corporation. If interpretationism delivers states that Roombas have, then interpretability alone cannot settle whether an exchange is a conversation or an output a speech act. Something further is needed, and supplying it is the optimist’s burden. The interpretation does not deliver this on its own. A third family of views, simulation and persona accounts, hold that LLM outputs are best understood from the perspective of the system playing a role: the model simulates a character in dialogue, and agentic predicates apply in the first instance to the simulacrum rather than to the system itself (Park et al., 2023; Shanahan et al., 2023; Shanahan, 2026). These views are deflationary alternatives to agen-tic interpretation. For example, Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds argue that anthropomorphizing a character in the generated text is a more sensible interpretative strategy than anthropomorphizing the LLM itself. However, the intentional stance should not be adopted simply because intentional language is natural or the system exhibits complex behaviour. Rather, we should ask whether attributing beliefs and desires provides explanatory or predictive benefits unavailable from a less committal stance. After all, we could attribute beliefs and desires to a calculator or a Roomba (e.g., it knows how to add and wants to give us the right answer; it wants to clean the room and believes that this is how to do it). But we can predict and explain its behaviour just as well and more plausibly by merely taking the design stance without attributing beliefs and desires. If the behaviour of an LLM can likewise be predicted without treating them as cognitive agents, then the intentional stance has not earned its keep. At the other extreme, some pessimists deny that LLMs possess the capacities associated with meaningful language use, emphasizing instead that they are systems for predicting linguistic patterns (Bender and Koller, 2020; Hattiangadi and Schoubye, 2026). This view rightly cautions against inferring cognitive or linguistic capacities from fluent output alone. But it also leaves many important things unexplained: by dismissing LLMs out of hand, pessimists fail to explain how they are 5sophisticated and useful, how we should use or design them, and to provide a comprehensive characterisation of their outputs and our interactions and engagement with them. 10 

We therefore propose a modified understanding of the design stance, which we call the artifactual stance . It treats LLMs neither as full cognitive agents nor mere “stochastic parrots”, but as artifacts whose design explains the production of outputs and the diverse ways that users put those outputs to work. Our proposal in this paper comes in two parts. First, to the followers of Dennett, we offer a new stance. Second, to people who want to go further on metaphysical questions, we offer answers that are friendly to our particular stance, though not entailed by it. 

2.2 The Artifactual Stance 

We agree with Dennett that it is useful to explain what an artifact does based on what it was built to do and what we do with them. But Dennett’s conception of the design stance is too restrictive. Consider Dennett’s own favoured example: the chess programme. Dennett claims that it is more appropriate to take the intentional stance than the design stance to explain and predict a chess programme’s next move. After all, even its designers cannot predict its next move from knowledge of the programme; if they could, Dennett notes, they could beat it (Dennett, 1971, 90). However, this concession to the intentional stance is premature. First, taking the intentional stance does not suffice to predict the next move either. After all, thinking of the chess game as a better-than-human reasoner does not help any mere human know what the next move will be. Second, what the intentional stance delivers at the higher level – that the programme will make strong, rule-governed, threat-responsive moves – a suitably enriched design-based stance delivers equally well. The programme was optimized to produce strong, rule-governed, threat-responsive moves, and it does. Attributing beliefs and desires adds nothing to that prediction. It merely redescribes the optimization target in mentalistic vocabulary. The same holds for LLMs. Intentional-stance-based predictions about LLMs are dispensable: every-thing predicted by the intentional stance is also predicted by the artifactual stance, and the residual patterns the intentional stance “detects” are patterns of human speech in the training data or from reinforcement learning. But we do not rest our case on predictions alone. Suppose, against our own views, that the intentional stance can be useful in predicting the behaviour of artifacts, and LLMs in particular. More specifically, suppose that predicting a dialogue system will “refuse a request” or “defend a claim” in the relevant context is easiest if we treat it as if it had policies and positions. Our claim is that predictive utility does not settle the constitutive questions of whether the relevant exchange is a conversation, the output a speech act, the ‘I’ of the system a referring expression, and the system itself an interlocutor. For these questions the intentional stance is not merely unnecessary but positively misleading, since it builds the contested classifications into the very description of human–AI interactions. Consequently, our central disagreement with the optimists is whether conversational talk is adequate in characterising human–AI interactions. 11 

Let the design stance be the stance that is useful for predicting and explaining the behaviour of designed objects, as Dennett envisioned it. We claim that we need a fourth, the artifactual stance .This stance is appropriate to take towards artifacts, but the user need not try to predict the artifact’s behaviour by applying some algorithm. Indeed, it allows the possibility that the object was designed to have an indeterminate number of uses or generate outputs not specifically envisioned by its designers, and that it works in ways not understood by the designers or the users. 12

To do this, we need a notion central to contemporary design theory: affordance . This was first introduced by Gibson (1979) as a way to describe relational, objective, and gradable features of objects in the environment, specifically in relation to what they offer or provide to an animal. For example, a dropped branch affords being grasped by animals with hands and being used as shelter by small bugs. The branch was not made for those purposes, but it has these affordances in relation to animals with certain capacities. Furthermore, affordances differ from capacities. It is just not true that the branch has the capacity to be grasped or to shelter. It affords different uses by different animals; each affordance is a relation to one particular animal or even one particular use. Norman (1988) adapted the notion of affordances to design theory: a well-designed object is one where the user can tell easily which actions it makes possible and how to perform them. An affordance is effective when it aligns the object’s features with the user’s capacities and expectations. 16 For example, when opening a door outwards, a flat metal surface is a more effective affordance than a door handle, since the user cannot tell whether the handle is for pushing or pulling, whereas they can easily tell that a flat surface affords only pushing (Norman, 1988, 13). Now to put it all together. LLMs are artifacts designed to produce outputs that afford various human uses. Some uses are foreseeable: searches for information, requests for daily calendars, drafting an email, brainstorming, getting and giving practical advice. Some uses are not: looking for words that rhyme or coming up with a new slogan for one’s favorite philosophical position. One feature that makes contemporary LLMs so widely adopted is how easy they are to use, especially how they afford conversation-like applications. In the past, if you wanted to find out the capital of France, you would have to ask a friend, consult an encyclopedia, or search the Internet. Now chatbots readily output text that reliably provides the answer in a language of your choice. Additionally, the interface is underdetermined, in that the prompts are open-ended and the outputs are flexible. The system is not designed to tell us how to use it or what for. 2.3 Using the Artifactual Stance 

Why is the artifactual stance the right stance towards LLMs? And how exactly does it apply? Perhaps the best way to get acclimated to the artifactual stance is to look at a progression of cases. We start with greeting cards. Some of these are designed for a particular kind of use (‘Happy 30th!’), and others are more generic (‘Thinking of you’). In none of these cases do we think that the writer or the company producing them performed any speech act. These are texts for the customer to use however they see fit, even ironically (e.g., sending a condolence card to someone who spilled coffee on their very ugly t-shirt). When we send the card, we are in the process of performing a speech act. It is only upon deployment that the question of reference even comes up. Consider how nonsensical it would be to ask what ‘you’ refers to in the text of the greeting card as it sits on the shelf, waiting to be bought. Nor is there anything wrong with the ironic use: we are not misinterpreting or appropriating a previous condolence. We get to use them how we see fit. Next, the classic answering machine ‘I am not here now’. Here too there is no intuition that it is the machine that is performing a speech act. These cases differ from typical face-to-face conversations by separating the context of production from the context of interpretation. For example, unlike in ordinary uses, ‘now’ picks out the time of playback, not the time of recording (Sidelle, 1991; Cohen and Michaelson, 2013). But there is no doubt that a speaker, the machine’s owner, arranged in advance for a token to be interpreted at playback, not recording. The device defers a speech act; it does not replace the speaker. Again, the user uses, and the machine merely facilitates. With autocomplete we are getting closer to Maya’s situation: an algorithm gives us some options, and we choose whether to use one of them. The system is less under the control of the user than an answering machine, and it is set up to be useful in various circumstances. It also works best if the options are contextually appropriate, relevant, grammatically correct strings in a relevant language. But here too, we just have candidate text completions, as made evident in the design of the interface. The interaction is clearly one of being offered some options, and is not presented as a conversation with an interlocutor, nor is the intentional stance relevant or attractive. Autocomplete also marks a step the answering machine does not take: the text itself is now produced by the artifact, not merely stored and replayed by it. What remains with the user is selection and endorsement, and, we will argue, that is where any speakerhood lies. Automated phone answering systems are a useful step to one side. Unlike autocomplete and LLMs, they permit only highly constrained interactions, yet they are often described in interactional terms. For example, in old-fashioned systems, the user navigated a menu by pressing buttons (e.g., ‘press “1” for. . . ’). Newer systems invite natural-language responses but still only work with inputs from a narrow range, routing unexpected ones to a human agent. In either case, the form of the input matters chiefly as a means of steering the interaction: ‘1’ is useful because it is readily available on the keyboard, and a natural-language input matters because the system has been programmed to respond to particular strings of sounds. The system could have been designed entirely differently without changing the basic structure of the exchange. Thus, such systems illustrate useful and 8even linguistically-mediated interaction, without being a conversation between minded, agential, or norm-governed participants. The penultimate case is ELIZA, the first chatbot, developed in the 1960s by Joseph Weizenbaum. ELIZA used simple scripts to generate responses to users’ inputs, often by identifying keywords and transforming parts of what users had said into replies. Despite the simplicity and repetitiveness of these responses, its outputs could fairly reliably trick people into thinking they were conversing with a person. One popular explanation for ELIZA’s success was that its outputs, though simplistic, managed to convince people that it was understanding their inputs and sympathising with them (Hofstadter, 1996, 158). Its limitations were nevertheless conspicuous: when a conversation deviated too much from its scripts, for example, it might repeatedly output ‘ PLEASE GO ON ’. Yet the fact that users sustained such interactions has been explained by the work they did to make sense of ELIZA’s responses (Eisenmann et al., 2024). More generally, that humans respond socially to machines offering the thinnest social cues is in any case a robust and much older finding (Reeves and Nass, 1996). 17 Eventually, one got the sense that ELIZA was not quite a regular interlocutor. This is less easy with contemporary LLMs, which are much better at interacting with their users, due to their architecture, training, and to the computing power behind them. But the explanations of ELIZA’s success may be just what we need to understand the success of LLMs. We can now go back to Theo, Maya’s alleged interlocutor. The jump from ELIZA is quite big: ELIZA is a small system ruled by detailed human-written scripts, whereas modern transformer-based LLMs are neural networks that compute a probability distribution over the next token, trained on large amounts of data and fine-tuned to produce contextually-appropriate, useful outputs. If we were adamant on finding intelligence, Theo would be a much better bet. However, we claim that LLMs are a jump up in scale, fit and sophistication, not a change in nature. The temptation to think otherwise is due to the successful design of a system that delivers outputs with fine-tuned affordances with an impressively small, but still noticeable, rate of failure. We need not give in to the temptation, and we should not. The artifactual stance helps: LLMs are designed for just this kind of relatively frictionless experience. They succeed just when they seem not so much invisible, as cooperative and helpful. This may raise some eyebrows. Why should we think that the artifactual stance, with its talk of design and affordances, is relevant to metaphysical questions about the identity of LLM systems, or their status as conversational agents? Why are we not concluding, as folk intuitions seem to push us, that these are real interactions with thinking, acting, rational agents? It helps to remember the question we started from: LLMs generate outputs, as a result of some prompting; we are asking what kind of event is this? How can we explain what is going on, and how should we classify it? Our view is that design facts and facts about affordances and usage are constitutive to that event. The intentional stance would be irrelevant to this question, since we get all the answers we need without it. In the next section we present our positive view of what LLM outputs are and what users do with them. We then argue that they are not interlocutors and the exchanges they figure in are not conversations. 

# 3 Human–AI Interaction Under the Artifactual Stance: Candidate Texts and Candidate Exchanges 

Under the artifactual stance, LLMs are understood as a kind of technology designed to afford many different uses, the most general of which is producing text as output that users then deploy in various ways. The artifactual stance directs us to characterize these outputs by asking what the system was designed to produce, what its outputs afford, and what users do with them. To this end, we adopt the 

LLMs-as-candidate-generators framework from (Radulescu and Sterken, 2026). In §3.1 we present our view about LLM outputs, and in §3.2 we discuss what happens in the interaction between the user and the outputs.

93.1 Candidate Texts 

On this view, LLMs are candidate text generators: artifacts designed to generate candidate strings –sequences of tokens optimized for utility as text – that users then take up and use in different ways (e.g., as information, drafting material, as a prompt for further thought, as text to send to someone else, as part of an extended exchange with the system itself). It is worth being precise about each feature of this definition of candidate string. First, the output of the LLM at the point of generation is a token sequence, a string. The system produces text-shaped material, but production by itself does not make that material anyone’s saying. Furthermore, tokenization is an important part of the generation of outputs in current LLMs, but it does not resemble any human processing of the corresponding sequences of sounds or letters, in that it neither obeys the same rules, nor is it guided by semantics. 18 

Second, the string is characteristically shaped as well-formed text and thus seems to stand in some intimate relation to the sentences of natural language. The exact nature of that relation can be left open for present purposes. We remain ecumenical between hard-line views on which the string is merely sentence-shaped and more permissive views on which outputs are sentences, potentially possessing semantic content. 19 However that debate is resolved, a candidate is underdetermined in two ways: contextual (i.e., the values of indexicals and other context-sensitive expressions), and structural (i.e., the selection among the string’s admissible logical forms). Both are fixed, when they are fixed at all, at reception rather than generation. As we argue in §4.1, we are not so permissive to allow that the outputs of LLMs are utterances of a sentence at a context (i.e., the products of events of saying). Third, the system is trained and fine-tuned to produce candidate strings that are useful in response to the user’s prompt. This is not the same as being true, believed, meant, sincere, appropriately addressed to an interlocutor, or otherwise answerable to the norms governing speech and conversation. Fourth, the status of LLM output as candidate strings is itself neutral with respect to endorsement. That is, generation by itself does not establish that anyone has asserted, promised, endorsed, or otherwise stood behind the string. Nevertheless, in §4.1, we will argue that no system-side utterance occurs. Calling LLM outputs ‘candidate strings’ is, therefore, a way of characterising what kinds of things they are and what they can be used for, rather than as a way of denying their sophistication or usefulness. They are candidates for reception, interpretation and, in some cases, candidates for speech: material that can become part of an utterance when a user adopts and deploys it. Nor does the label ‘candidate’ deny that the outputs are remarkably apt in context. Their aptness is actual, but it is engineered aptness rather than the result of being produced and spoken by an interlocutor in context: the fluency, relevance, and apparent attentiveness of candidate strings are designed affordances. These affordances of candidate strings are a function of how the system was engineered: its design, training on the textual remnants of human communicative practice, and fine-tuning through feedback. 20

But despite appearances, the underlying structure of exchange cycles differs from the turn-taking and cumulative joint activity of conversation. In ordinary conversations, participants take turns performing speech acts for the uptake of others. The speaker performs a speech act with certain intentions in mind and takes on its associated commitments. In turn, the hearer interprets the utterance and responds appropriately. She may believe it, incorporate it into her reasoning, assert it, send it, act on it, answer it, revise it, archive it, or ignore it. Where uptake consists in answering the LLM, it becomes the prompting stage of the next cycle. The result is therefore not the joint activity and mutual construction of shared beliefs and goals characteristic of conversation. In ordinary conversation, communicative labour is “bundled” and divided in accordance to the speaker and hearer roles; the division of labour does not cross the work bundles of these roles. For example, the hearer never takes on the work of linguistic production for a speaker or takes responsibility for the speaker’s speech act. For another example, the speaker never interprets her own utterance while the hearer determines what the speaker meant by it. This would be truly bizarre behaviour indeed, and certainly would not count as conversation even if it had some form of turn-taking structure. But the division of labour in candidate exchange is much more like this than it is like that of conversation. The work bundle in candidate exchanges unfolds over the stages of a cycle: framing at prompting, production at generation, interpretation at reception, and commitment (if any) is done at uptake. Users recognize turn-taking because the cycle is repeated, but it is the person who is using the artifact that is doing the framing, interpreting, content fixing, committing, and repairing. The artifact’s role is in the linguistic production of text. The production side of this division deserves a closer look because what the artifact is taking over is not linguistic production simpliciter .The division of labour explains both why candidate exchanges look and feel conversational, and also why they are useful. They are useful because the objectives of the exchange are user-driven, the systems are fine-tuned to produce helpful outputs, and the artifact absorbs a substantial share of the linguistic and cognitive labour the user would have otherwise done in undertaking the task themself. The LLM undertakes such labour in the sense that appears to remember information, make sense of it, draw conclusions from it, formulate responses to it, calibrate tone, and track details of the preceding exchange. And the interactions feel conversational because of how those objectives are pursued. The fluent, context-sensitive text gives the impression that it comes from remembering, reasoning, or judging what would be appropriate to say in that context. Indeed, treating the system as a helpful interlocutor is often a good prompting strategy. But it is not usually the conscious choice of the user to construct such an interlocutor; it is what the candidate string, the exchange format, and the best functioning of the artifact affords and invites. None of this makes turns the artifact into a speaker. The system produces text, not speech acts. The candidate string is the outcome of calculations over a vast corpus, adjusted, fine-tuned, and tooled for usability. As such, the calculations are not aimed at cognition or participation in a norm-governed

The division of labour also helps explain what it is for a user to take up a candidate in the relevant sense. We call the various ways in which the user takes on candidates, adoption . Adoption divides broadly into receptive adoption and appropriative adoption . In receptive adoption, the user takes up a candidate as contentful. What she constructs depends on what she is doing with the text. For example, she may read the string and construct content from it, use it as a prompt for thought, treat it as advice, or engage with it as if it were part of a conversation. 22 In appropriative adoption , the user deploys the candidate as her own speech. Often, this is preceded by receptive adoption: the user reads, edits, and adapts the candidate in preparation for deployment. For example, she may recruit an AI in drafting her email, insert a predictive text suggestion into a message, read an AI-generated statement aloud, or certify the text in a formal setting. In doing so, the resulting utterance becomes hers and she is answerable for what it says or any speech act performed. To summarise, when an LLM produces candidate strings, it is the human user who interprets and puts them to use. A candidate exchange therefore has a different distribution of production, interpretation, action, and commitment than a conversation. The next section argues that this structural difference is constitutive of a different kind of interaction. 

# 4 Human–AI Interactions Are Not Conversations 

In the previous section, we gave a positive characterisation of human–AI interactions: LLMs generate candidate strings that users interpret and take up in candidate exchanges. In this section we ask whether such human–AI interactions count as conversations. Our answer is negative: despite their conversational appearance, these exchanges are not conversations. §4.1 argues that generation does not itself constitute an utterance: at the point of generation, the system produces a candidate text, but nothing makes this an utterance. §4.2 shows why candidate exchanges therefore lack the constitutive structure of conversations. 

4.1 Generation is Not Utterance 

This section argues for one of the paper’s key negative claims: at the point of generation, no utterance occurs. Here is a preview of the argument: An utterance is an act of saying something. In contrast, generation is the production of a candidate text, and such production is not yet by itself an utterance. Since every utterance fixes a context of utterance, and generation does not supply one, the burden is on the optimist and interpretationist to say what makes this production an act of saying. We argue that no such story is available to them. In philosophy of language and linguistics, the theoretical term ‘utterance’ is used in two ways: to describe an event of uttering and to describe the result of an event of uttering. We will argue that the optimist lacks the resources to deliver utterances as performed by LLMs in either of these senses, so we need to say a bit more about them. 23 In the event sense, an utterance is something someone does: a speaker says something to someone, at a time and place, usually meaning something by it. In the product sense, there are a number of options. The thinnest sense is that of the sentence used in the utterance; but that is not really the product of the utterance, only the means that the speaker is using. There is another, more intuitive sense: that which is said, the content of the utterance, the message transmitted, or the proposition expressed. This is what entitles us to speak of a true (or false) utterance. To get from the sentence to the proposition, philosophers of language have proposed a number of intermediary notions, but we will focus on the notion of a context of utterance (Kaplan, 1989b).

A further positive argument against generation being utterance concerns our practices of using LLMs. Suppose a candidate was an utterance. Then, clicking the ‘regenerate’ button would constitute an act of inducing a retraction and restatement of what the LLM said. Editing an output prior to sending it, would be making changes to what was already said by the LLM. Discarding an output would amount to giving up on the speech act of the LLM. But we do not treat outputs in these ways. Users edit them, discard them without interfering or regretting doing so, and they request alternative outputs without considering the first attempt as a commitment that the LLM would need to withdraw. To maintain that the outputs are utterances, the optimist would have to attribute massive conceptual error in our practices, and countenance unread assertions, promises, and apologies at industrial scale, with contents anchored to nothing. Our view, instead, simply describes the practice. Generation therefore yields a structurally ambiguous sentence token with non-indexical content at best and nothing more: neither an event of saying, nor a sentence as uttered at a context, nor utterance content. Rather what is generated is text, which a user may take up, read as addressed to her, and treat as saying something. If there is an utterance anywhere in the vicinity, it happens there. 

4.2 Human–LLM Interactions are Not Conversations 

That generation is not utterance has two important implications, which together yield the paper’s main claim: human–LLM interactions are not conversations. We draw out both as a single argument we call the Constitutive Argument , and then offer a second, independent argument we call the Diachronic Argument .We start by reviewing the distinction between locutionary and illocutionary acts (Austin, 1962). Locutionary acts are the acts of producing meaningful utterances, words with a definite meaning and reference. Illocutionary acts are social, communicative acts performed in performing locutionary acts. §4.1 established that generation issues no utterance, hence no locutionary act. Assertion, promise, apology, command, instruction, answer, objection are all types of illocutionary acts. They all require a locutionary vehicle to perform them. As generation issues no locutionary act, no illocutionary act is performed in generation.

These conditions are constitutive of conversation: they fix what counts as a contribution, participant, and move. Immediately we arrive at the Constitutive Argument. If conversation is constituted by the illocutionary contributions of its participants, and illocutionary acts require locutionary vehicles, and generation issues no locutionary act, then no illocutionary act is performed in generation. A human–LLM exchange could only therefore contain contributions from its human participants alone, and an exchange with contributions from only one side is not a conversation. Next, the Diachronic Argument. Holding someone to a promise requires that they are the person who made it. Conversational scoreboard entries for speech acts have to attach to someone who persists. But, as Cappelen and Hawthorne (2026) argue, the technology provides no determinate entity persisting across exchange cycles, let alone across sessions. Whereas they offer this as a puzzle about reference, we take it as a premise about participation in conversation. Maya’s plan to hold Theo to his promise illustrates the problem for the optimist. Which entity made the promise that she later attempts to enforce? If none determinately did, the promise has no promiser, and her plan plausibly has no target other than the one that she constructs. To explain away the problem, the optimist needs an account of how the generated outputs incur commitments, and those commitments must be borne by some persistent thing. We discuss one recent account of such an persistent entity in some detail. According to Chalmers (2026), there is a persisting interlocutor in these exchanges: a virtual instance of the model, im-plemented by a succession of hardware instances linked roughly by what we have earlier called ‘conditioning’. This entity, on Chalmers’ view, meets conditions he stipulates as sufficient for being an interlocutor: it is (1) interactive (it processes the inputs and produces the outputs), (2) persistent 

(it produces all the outputs across the exchange), (3) coherent (consistent enough to support a unified quasi-psychology), (4) faithful (its quasi-beliefs match those the system seems to have), and (5) 

unified . If such an entity persists across cycles, then there is a commitment-bearer after all, and we have a counterexample to that premise of the Diachronic Argument. The counterexample exploits unclarity in what the persistence of a virtual instance qua interlocutor consists in across an exchange. Consider the conditions taken causally first. What it is for the virtual instance to persist, on this way of putting things, is nothing more than conditioning or transcript feedback (that is, feeding the output string of one cycle into the input of the next). But notice that causal–informational continuity like this does not ground deontic continuity. Transmitting a record of a promise is not the same thing as transferring the promise itself. Suppose Maya makes a vow, which is then passed along a relay of scribes, each copying faithfully, until it reaches its addressee. No scribe in the chain would thereby come to owe the vow’s addressee anything. Chalmers himself concedes the thinness of such links when he dismisses the human-relay case (i.e., a series of people each extending a conversation and passing on the context) as insufficient for a continuing subject. But transcript feedback is just the machine version of the relay. According to the causal reading, the only difference between the virtual instance and the relay is that the copying is done by GPUs rather than human scribes. No commitment-bearer emerges from a causal chain of this kind. Chalmers’ own example makes the point vividly: a virtual shopping cart persists across servers in exactly this way, and nobody supposes that one can hold the cart to anything. Persistence of virtual objects is cheap, and it is normatively inert. Suppose, then, that the conditions are read normatively: the persisting interlocutor is whatever bears the commitments incurred across the exchange (i.e., the entity to whom retractions, challenges, and enforcements are properly directed). This secures a commitment-bearer, but only by stipulation. Defining the bearer by the role would be legitimate functionalism if something filled the role, but filling it requires incurring commitments, and commitments of this kind are incurred only by conversational moves. So the normative reading presupposes that the exchange is a conversation, which is the very question at issue. 28

The resemblance to conversation can be fully explained without appealing to the notion of conversa-tion: one participant performs both parts aided by an artifact. We surmise that a good starting point for understanding this is the ordinary mechanisms of hearer-side pragmatic repair that are part of the interpretive labour of the user. These have been the subject of much work in philosophy and linguistics, starting with accommodation (Lewis, 1979), general conversational principles (Grice, 1975), and charity (Quine, 1960; Davidson, 1973). These would need to be adapted to LLMs, since they tend to depend on attributions of intentions and other mental states, which are not necessary here. We leave this very interesting issue for future work. 

# 5 Reference Without Speakers 

We can now return to our questions about reference. What, if anything, does Maya refer to when she types ‘you’ or ‘Theo’? What does ‘I’ refer to when it appears in Theo’s output? Our answer is that there is no uniform use of names and pronouns in human–AI interactions. Candidate texts do not have semantic or pragmatic status independently of their uptake. Their role depends on how they are adopted and used. To illustrate, we distinguish three kinds of use: (i) prompting uses , where the user directs the artifact with names or pronouns; (ii) receptive uses , where the user takes up and interprets candidate text; and (iii) appropriative uses , where a user deploys candidate text in her own speech. The same expression can therefore play quite different roles at different points in the exchange. 

5.1 Prompting Uses 

Consider first Maya’s use of ‘Theo’ or ‘you’ in her prompts. Despite appearances, many such uses may be non-referential. Rather, they form part of an instruction signal that steers the artifact’s generative process. Compare the use of names in other artifact-directed discourse, such as the expression ‘Alexa’ in our commands to Echo devices (e.g., ‘Alexa, play “Thriller” on Spotify’, ‘Alexa, pause’). It is tempting to treat ‘Alexa’ as referring to some kind of entity (e.g., a virtual assistant, a particular device, the running programme). But in these contexts, its primary function is to activate the device. Although any sufficiently distinctive signal could have served this purpose, proper names are particularly natural “wake words” because they afford frictionless user-interface design: they are memorable, familiar, and easy to use. 5.3 Appropriative Uses 

Finally, consider cases in which Maya appropriates candidate text for her own speech. Here the indexicals function exactly as they would in any other utterance of hers. If she sends a drafted email saying ‘I will meet you on Thursday’, ‘I’ refers to Maya, ‘you’ to her addressee, and the referent of ‘Thursday’ is fixed by the context of her message. The fact that the text partly originated as an LLM-generated candidate is irrelevant to its subsequent semantic and communicative role, just as a politician’s delivery does not make its speechwriter the speaker. Notably, reception-for-appropriation already anticipates these referential values: in editing a candidate for deployment, Maya resolves ‘I’, ‘you’, and ‘now’ against the context and addressee she intends. Appropriation thus closes the gap between candidate string and any downstream utterance. But it does not retroactively make the system the speaker of the original candidate. Authorship or drafting of the string and speakerhood of the utterance can come apart. 

# 6 Consequences for AI Reference and Responsibility 

The preceding analysis suggests a simple explanation of why reference in human–AI interactions seems so puzzling. Its interpretation depends on how users interact with the artifact and how candidate text is taken up. But there is a further source of confusion. When users talk about ‘Theo’, ‘Claude’, or ‘Kimi’, several things can become associated with the name, such as the model, a deployment or particular instance, a transcript of an exchange, or an entity constructed by the user engaging with the system. A single name slides among the different associated objects, and philosophical puzzles arise when these are treated as one. Keeping these apart helps dissolve the puzzle about AI reference. First, a name may refer to the service, the model, or a particular instance. For example, when Maya says ‘Theo is down this morning’, she may be referring to any of these options. This is no more philosophically problematic than reference to other complex artifacts or entities, such as corporations. The appropriate level of individuation will vary with context and with purpose. But importantly nothing here requires an interlocutor. Second, the persistence of the transcript in an interaction can explain the appearance of a persisting interlocutor, which may become associated with a name. What actually persists between cycles and sessions is a textual record. It can be stored, retrieved, and reprocessed, and it provides a sense of continuity across cycles and exchanges. When a later output gives the appearance of remembering

Third, users may construct an interlocutor-like entity when taking up candidate text. For example, Maya may read Theo’s outputs as a contribution of a continuing conversational partner, constructing a character-like entity and taking them to have said these things, to be able to remember their exchanges, and who addresses her with ‘you’. Such a constructed interlocutor is a feature of the user’s uptake of the interaction, not a computational entity waiting to be discovered in the machinery. These points are easily conflated. Candidate strings come from the product, the sense of continuity comes from the transcript, and the user’s uptake supplies a character-like source for the resulting text. These can come together to create a powerful impression that there is a single persisting interlocutor behind the interaction. But there is no such thing. Speech is required for interlocution. Identifying the computational entity causally responsible for generating a sequence of tokens does not turn that sequence into an utterance. One might worry that this leaves us without anyone to hold responsible for the text generated by the chatbot. If there is no thick interlocutor behind the output, who is answerable when a chatbot misleads or harms a user? This line of reasoning presumes that whomever is responsible for text has to be the speaker of that text. But that need not the case. Responsibility is a normative and institutional matter, determined by facts about who produced, authorized, adopted, or deployed the text, not by the metaphysics of AI systems or the referents of names and pronouns. Consider the following case of mediated communication. Bruce has been corresponding with a pen pal called ‘Sheila’, only to discover that the letters were produced by a team of self-styled correspondence officers, who fabricate Sheila, letter by letter, in consultation with folders of past correspondence. In ordinary language, we can ask who Sheila is and whether she exists; in a meta-linguistic register, to whom does ‘Sheila’ refer? One should not say that ‘Sheila’ is not really a name, since it is stipulated as such in the correspondence. Instead, ‘Sheila’ might refer to a fiction, it might plurally refer to the officers, or singularly refer to their fusion. Some will even deny that ‘Sheila’ refers at all or that reference is indeterminate. But this does not settle the question of responsibility. In general, the organization will be answerable for the letters it authorized and sent. It may even be vicariously liable for the negligent actions of its correspondence officers. The same point applies to AI-generated communications. For example, in Moffat v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149 , a tribunal held that Air Canada was responsible for inaccurate information supplied by an automated chatbot embedded in its website. The chatbot need not be taken as a speaker nor did the tribunal need to settle questions about the metaphysics of chatbots for the organization deploying it to be responsible for the candidate text it’s chatbot generated. Reference and responsibility come apart. Finally, we should remark on the scope and nature of our arguments. We have not provided a categorical argument that conversations constitutively involve normative structure, or that an artificial system could never be a proper interlocutor. Our aim has been to provide an alternative picture (one on which LLMs are artifacts, and where the pressure to find normativity, speakerhood, and a persisting interlocutor dissipates) and allow that picture to be contrasted with the conversational one. A methodological lesson from Austin is to remember that ordinary language is honed for the tasks it was built for, and that we should not simply assume that the same categories apply neatly outside their familiar domains. Human–AI text exchange is precisely such a case. 35 

# 7 Concluding Remarks 

Advanced AI systems are designed to generate text that invites us to treat them as interlocutors in a conversation. They appear to remember, to respond to our questions, to express attitudes, to make commitments, and maintain ongoing conversations over time. But appearances deceive. We

20 have argued that we should take care not to allow such appearances and the naturalness of their corresponding descriptions to unduly influence our understanding and characterisation of the systems and our interactions with them. To this end, we have introduced the artifactual stance. We claim that LLMs are artifacts designed to afford a wide range of uses. They produce a candidate text, optimized for use. Generation of candidate text is not an act of saying something with the content of that text. So LLMs are not speakers, nor conversational participants. Our interactions with them are not conversations. Instead, they are artifact-mediated exchanges of candidate text that users can adopt for various purposes. That such interactions look like a conversation is a consequence of the design of the artifact and the uptake of the user, rather than evidence that the artifact itself is a conversational participant. Our account also dissolves the apparent puzzles about reference. In human–AI interactions, names and pronouns do not all work the same way. Sometimes they are used as steering devices, sometimes they refer thinly to an artifact, sometimes they are given constructed referents via receptive uptake, and sometimes they simply pick up ordinary referential meanings when candidate text is appropriated as a user’s own speech. What is absent at generation is a speaker whose utterances bear the commitments and normative force characteristic of conversation. Nor does this create a liability vacuum. Who is answerable is a matter of the normative and institutional arrangements governing the production, authorization, adoption, use, and deployment of the resultant speech. The resulting picture is deflationary, but it is not pessimistic. Our view does not deny that LLMs are extremely sophisticated, that their outputs may indeed convey information or insight, or that interacting with them can be cognitively and practically valuable. Nor does it imply that artificial conversations are impossible. Our point is that we should keep the burden of proof where it belongs. Showing that an artifact’s output is fluent, coherent, or even convincingly conversational help make the case for taking the artifact’s affordances seriously, but this does not, by itself, show that its outputs are utterances. The possibility of simulation and persona accounts, on which the system plays a character rather than speaking in its own right, poses no threat to our view. Qua mechanism, recent work indicating that LLMs exhibit persona-like representations causally mediating their behaviour tends to support, rather than undermine, our view (Marks et al., 2026). This is because these results account for why candidate strings are consistent with characters and why anthropomorphic predictions succeed where they do, without anything performing speech acts. According to our view, the “persona” is a stable configuration of the candidate generator, a kind of conditioning role or mode (the same way, as we argued in §5.1, that ‘Theo’ is used to pick one out in Maya’s prompts), not an eligible occupant of the speaker role. Mallory (2026) makes this point for computer simulations generally. The text simulating a speaker’s utterance is a candidate string on our view. What would make a difference is if it turned out that the system itself was performing the relevant speech acts, with the associated semantic and normative features, rather than merely producing candidate strings that users subsequently adopt. The precise form such evidence would take is an open question. Our argument is therefore not that artificial conversation is impossible, but that the conversational status of current AI systems should not be inferred from their conversational appearance. Our account naturally generalises beyond text output. For example, AI-generated images are can-didates for artistic, commercial, or other uses, acquiring their role through human uptake and deployment. The same idea applies to so-called agential AI , current systems generate candidate ac-tions rather than performing actions in their own right. A candidate action becomes an agent’s action when an agent adopts it, including through pre-authorisation or automatic execution arrangements. Thus, an AI system may generate a candidate booking that, once pre-authorised by a user, results in the user’s booking a table; the system supplies the candidate action, while the user’s adoption makes it an action attributable to them. The details of adoption differ across speech and other kinds of action, and we leave those differences for future work. There is a broader methodological point about human–AI interaction that needs to be made. This is the point we conclude on. When interacting with AI, we should not allow ourselves to be led down the road of forcing concepts drawn from human-human interaction into a new domain, simply because the technology has been engineered to make those concepts seem natural. Let us say it again: before trying to figure out whether AI is believing, saying, promising, remembering, or referring to something, let us try to determine what kind of interaction we are dealing with. With regards to current LLMs, we have argued that the right description is an artifact-mediated exchange of candidate 21 text. That shift in description does not make the phenomenon less interesting. It makes clearer what, exactly, needs explaining.
