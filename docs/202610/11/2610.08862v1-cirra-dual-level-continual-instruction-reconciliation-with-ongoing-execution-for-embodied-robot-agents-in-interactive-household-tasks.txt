# ABSTRACT 

As the demand for assistance with household chores continues to grow, house-hold robots have become a major focus of embodied intelligence. Real-world household deployment, however, inherently requires continuously replanning on human–robot interaction, as users may issue new instructions while previous re-quests are still under execution. Existing robot agents commonly accommodate such instructions by regenerating or extensively revising the remaining task se-quence, which can introduce plan ambiguity, logical inconsistency, and redundant execution. To address these limitations, we formulate continual instruction rec-onciliation and propose CIRRA (Continual Instruction Reconciliation for Robot Agents), a dual-level framework combining LLM-based semantic reasoning with rule-constrained structural integration. CIRRA first uses semantic reasoning to de-termine whether an incoming instruction can be grounded to a unique executable skill and whether its required execution location is specified, resolving underspeci-fied action and location information when necessary. It then preserves the ongoing subtask sequence as an execution backbone and generates fine-grained integration candidates by inserting the incoming subtasks into location-matched segments of 1arXiv:2610.08862v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 Preprint the current task chain. Finally, the semantic reasoner evaluates only the modified segments to identify task dependencies and potential conflicts and select the most logically coherent local integration. By coupling structure-preserving local inte-gration with semantic grounding and logical selection, CIRRA maintains align-ment with the robot’s current execution, mitigates plan ambiguity and logical in-consistency, and reuses shared subtasks to reduce redundant execution. We further introduce CHIRP (Continual Household Instruction Reconciliation and Planning), a text-based benchmark of 120 episodes spanning eight household environments and six categories of everyday activities. On CHIRP, CIRRA reaches 74.2% de-cision agreement, 30 points above the strongest replanning baseline, and every correct fusion decision it makes yields a correctly placed, conflict-free schedule. On a Unitree G1 humanoid, CIRRA interrupts ongoing skills at the correct mo-ment in every trial and significantly outperforms all baselines on every metric. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recently, household robots have emerged as a major focus in embodied intelligence, driven by the growing demand for everyday assistance amid the accelerating pace of modern life, population aging, and persistent labor shortages in care and domestic services. Recent advances in robotic foundation models, particularly vision-language-action modelsBrohan et al. (2022); Zitkovich et al. (2023); Kim et al. (2024b); Physical Intelligence et al. (2025); Bjorck et al. (2025), have made general-purpose household assistance increasingly feasible, enabling robots to understand natural-language instructions, perceive complex environments, and execute a diverse range of navigation and manipulation skills. However, even capable action models alone remain insufficient for autonomous household operation Shi et al. (2025); Yang et al. (2025); Koo et al. (2025). To bridge this gap, embodied robot agents have extensively studied high-level task decomposition and long-horizon skill coordination Dalal et al. (2024); Yuan et al. (2025), task-state tracking and memory Wang et al. (2025); Koo et al. (2025), and adaptive replanning in response to execution failures or environmental changes Huang et al. (2022); Skreta et al. (2024). These advances enable an agent to translate a high-level user goal into a coherent sequence of executable skills, monitor task progress, and revise its plan when execution deviates from expectation. Despite this progress, a critical challenge for household deployment remains comparatively under-explored: how to integrate a newly arriving task instruction with one that is already under execution. Although existing interactive agents can engage in dialogue and respond to user corrections, feed-back, or task changes Wei et al. (2022); Kambhampati et al. (2024); Shinn et al. (2023); Li et al. (2023); Joublin et al. (2024); Borate et al. (2025); Lu et al. (2025), a common strategy for accom-modating such input is to re-invoke a high-level model with the updated instruction and current execution context to regenerate or revise the remaining task sequence. Without explicit constraints on preserving unaffected portions of the ongoing plan, this generative replanning can introduce three key issues. First, it may create plan ambiguity , as a minor instruction update can cause substantial plan churn, unnecessarily reorder pending subtasks, and obscure how the revised plan aligns with the current execution state. Second, it may produce logical inconsistency by violating task depen-dencies or introducing conflicting action orderings. Third, it may lead to execution redundancy by failing to identify and reuse subtasks shared between the ongoing and incoming instructions. These limitations highlight the need for fine-grained, execution-aligned instruction integration rather than unconstrained plan regeneration. To address these limitations, we propose CIRRA, a dual-level framework that combines LLM-based semantic reasoning with rule-constrained structural integration. Upon receiving a new instruction, the semantic reasoning level first determines whether its action description can be grounded to a unique executable skill and whether the location required for execution is explicitly specified (shown in Ambiguity Detection in Fig.1). It resolves underspecified action and location information to ob-tain a complete, location-aware representation of the incoming instruction. For a non-emergency instruction, the structural integration level then treats the robot’s ongoing subtask sequence as an immutable execution backbone(shown in Task fusion in Fig.1): it retains every existing subtask and their relative ordering, and generates fine-grained integration candidates by inserting the incoming 2Preprint subtasks into segments of the sequence associated with the same execution location. The semantic reasoning level is subsequently invoked again, but only over the modified segments, to evaluate their task dependencies, identify potential conflicts, and select the most logically coherent local integra-tion. In this way, CIRRA prevents the LLM from globally regenerating or reordering the ongoing plan, while still leveraging its semantic reasoning to accurately ground the incoming instruction and determine its logically appropriate placement. The two levels therefore provide complementary guarantees: rule-constrained integration preserves the robot’s current execution strategy, whereas LLM-based reasoning ensures instruction completeness, placement accuracy, and the logical coher-ence of each modified segment. Together, they enable fine-grained instruction reconciliation without disrupting ongoing execution or introducing redundant actions. Existing household benchmarks primarily focus on instruction following, dialogue, and task execu-tion under predefined user goals, but are not designed to evaluate replanning when new task requests arrive during ongoing execution Shridhar et al. (2020); Padmakumar et al. (2022); Gao et al. (2022). Conversely, existing replanning benchmarks and evaluation settings largely study plan revision in re-sponse to execution failures or environmental changes, rather than continual user-driven task updates in interactive household settings. Consequently, there remains no dedicated benchmark at the inter-section of household interaction and instruction-triggered replanning. To fill this gap, we introduce 

CHIRP (Continual Household Instruction Reconciliation and Planning), a text-based benchmark specifically designed to evaluate how embodied robot agents revise an ongoing household task plan in response to newly arriving user instructions. The benchmark spans eight representative household environments: bedroom, study room, kitchen, bathroom, living room, basement, garage, and yard. And covers six major categories of household activities: object fetching, cleaning, tidying, cook-ing, appliance operation, and waste disposal. Across all evaluated environments and task categories, CIRRA consistently outperforms existing replanning baselines, producing more accurate instruction integrations, more logically coherent task sequences, and less redundant execution. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 ROBOTIC ACTION FOUNDATION MODELS 

Vision-language-action (VLA) foundation models Zitkovich et al. (2023); Team et al. (2024); Kim et al. (2024b); Li et al. (2024); Physical Intelligence et al. (2025); Qu et al. (2025); Bjorck et al. (2025); Bu et al. (2025); Shukor et al. (2025) have shifted robot control from separately trained, task-specific policies toward reusable, language-conditioned action models that transfer across tasks, en-vironments, and embodiments. By grounding the semantic knowledge of pretrained vision-language models in continuous action generation, these models increasingly serve as general-purpose skill interfaces that higher-level robotic systems can invoke. As a result, the bottleneck of household autonomy moves to the higher level: deciding which skills to execute and in what order, especially when new instructions arrive during execution. 2.2 EMBODIED AGENTS FOR TASK PLANNING AND REPLANNING 

At this higher level, LLM-based embodied agents translate user goals into executable robot be-haviors. SayCan grounds LLM-proposed actions in the affordances of pretrained skills Ahn et al. (2022), ProgPrompt and Code as Policies generate program-like plans over available actions and control APIs Singh et al. (2022); Liang et al. (2023), and Inner Monologue feeds success detection, scene descriptions, and human feedback back into planning Huang et al. (2022). Subsequent agents add hierarchical skill orchestration Dalal et al. (2024); Yuan et al. (2025), persis-tent task memory Wang et al. (2025), and online user feedback Shi et al. (2025), and increasingly rely on replanning for robust long-horizon execution: CAPE corrects violated skill preconditions Ra-man et al. (2024), AdaPlanner refines plans from environmental feedback Sun et al. (2023), RePLan revises behavior after plan failures or unforeseen obstacles Skreta et al. (2024), and PRED pre-emptively revises upcoming actions when the environment changes Kim et al. (2024a). However, these methods predominantly treat replanning as recovery under a fixed user goal: the task itself stays unchanged, and the agent only adapts its behavior to execution-state mismatches. This leaves an important case underexplored: in interactive households, users may issue additional task instructions while an earlier task chain is still being executed. Before integrating an incoming instruction with the ongoing task sequence, CIRRA first assesses whether the instruction can be unambiguously grounded into executable robot subtasks (Figure 2,1). Given the incoming instruction ut, the LLM-based semantic assessor is provided with the robot’s executable skill set A, the available household locations Lt, and the current environment context Et.The assessor examines every action expression in ut along two dimensions: action uniqueness and location completeness. An action expression is considered ambiguous if it cannot be uniquely mapped to one skill in A. Its location information is considered incomplete if the location required for executing the corresponding action is omitted or cannot be uniquely determined from Lt and Et.

Evaluation Metrics We report three metrics. DA (decision agreement) is the fraction of episodes whose response falls into the gold decision class (fuse, preempt, queue, or clarify). TA (target agreement) is the fraction of fuse-gold episodes in which a method fuses and places the new sub-tasks at the gold position without moving any old subtask. EA (execution agreement) is the strictest metric: the fraction of fuse-gold episodes in which, in addition, the whole schedule replays without conflict in the symbolic simulator and the new subtasks follow the gold order within their segment. 

Evaluation Protocol We evaluate two conditions. In the primary end-to-end condition, methods receive the raw utterance and episode state, with all plan-consuming methods sharing a single cached Qwen3-8B compilation. The oracle spec condition replaces this compilation with the ground-truth specification to isolate scheduling from language understanding. All LLM calls use identical de-coding settings and schema-constrained JSON output. Evaluation is deterministic and uses no LLM judges. 4.2 BASELINES

# 5 LIMITATIONS 

CHIRP is text-based, and our real-robot study spans two tables and eight skills. Remaining errors stem almost entirely from the 8B compiler rather than the reconciliation mechanism, which is exact under oracle specifications, so CIRRA directly benefits from stronger compilers. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented CIRRA, a framework for reconciling newly arriving instructions with an ongoing household task. Instead of regenerating the plan, CIRRA inserts new subtasks into the current schedule after a boundary set by the interruptibility of the active subtask, avoiding plan ambiguity; an LLM conflict gate rejects insertions that break task dependencies, preventing logical inconsistency; and new subtasks are fused into segments at the same location, reusing pending navigation to avoid execution redundancy. On CHIRP, CIRRA reaches 74.2% decision agreement, 30 points above the 9Preprint strongest replanning baseline, and every correct fusion decision yields a correctly placed, conflict-free schedule. On a Unitree G1 humanoid, it interrupts ongoing skills at the correct moment in every trial and outperforms all baselines on every metric. These results show that constraining where an LLM may change a plan makes replanning both more accurate and safer to execute.

# A THE CHIRP B ENCHMARK 

A.1 CONSTRUCTION PIPELINE 

The benchmark is built in three deterministic stages, all shipped with the code. 

Stage 1: hand-authored catalog. Every episode originates from a hand-written catalog entry that fixes the scenario: the house, the robot’s position and progress inside the ongoing task, the queued tasks, the injected utterance with two paraphrases, the oracle specification of the utterance, and the gold decision with its target. The catalog is organized as six categories of twenty episodes. The backbone of each category is a set of eight minimal pairs, one per room: the a-member injects a request whose location coincides with the scene the robot is currently working in and the b-member injects a nearly identically worded request anchored elsewhere, so a system that ignores execution state is guaranteed to miss one member of each pair. In four categories the pairs carry the plain gold split (a fuse, b queue); the two design decrees below reshape the remaining two, moving the waste a-members and one fetch b-member to clarify. Each category adds two preempt episodes (emergencies or explicit do-this-first orders) and two clarify episodes (underspecified action or location). 

Stage 2: template generator. A template generator renders every catalog entry into a self-contained JSON episode: it expands the task templates into subtask sequences, stamps the world snapshot, renders the human-readable text vignette consumed by baselines, and copies the oracle specification and gold label. Subtasks follow the five-attribute tuple of the paper (skill, object, lo-cation, execution status, interruptibility mode); navigation subtasks carry no object attribute, and no subtask carries any duration attribute. All location attributes draw from one normalized room-level vocabulary (the eight rooms of the house), on both the ongoing tasks and the oracle specifications, so the method’s exact-equality destination matching is well defined across the whole batch. 12 Preprint 

Stage 3: validation. A schema validator inside the generator checks every episode before it is written: all twelve axis fields are present and take legal values, gold decisions are one of the four classes, referenced rooms exist in the house, oracle subtasks are well-formed, and the no-duration rule holds. The 120 episodes are byte-reproducible from the catalog plus the placement stamper described below. 

Design rules. Two decrees shape the label distribution. First, vague destination words (“take it out”, “the usual place”) never resolve to a room: a subtask location must be a concrete room, so such utterances are ambiguous by construction and gold clarify at injection. This moves eight waste-handling episodes and one fetch episode to clarify, which is why the waste category contains no fuse gold. Second, cooking is pinned to the kitchen: cooking-context episodes probe whether a system respects a pinned context rather than surface geometry. # B BASELINE REPRODUCTION DETAILS 

All baselines run inside one harness under identical conditions: the same shared compilation feeds every plan-consuming method, every LLM call goes through one gateway with one decoding con-tract (temperature 0, fixed seed, thinking disabled, schema-constrained JSON), and every regener-ation baseline emits reference-constrained schedules ( old:<id> keeps a pending step, new:<i> 

schedules a new one), so the induced decision and the commitment violations are computed by one deterministic classifier with no LLM in the grading loop. For each baseline we record the first-hand source, what is reproduced faithfully, and every adaptation made to run it on our episodes. Raw source material (original prompt files where published) ships in benchmark/baselines ref/ .

ITP (Li et al., 2023). Faithful: the replanning context is the task guide plus completed steps plus the new request plus chat history, continued into the prior conversation, and the system plans one task thread at a time, as in the original drink-making deployment. Adaptations: the original emits GPT-4 function calls, ours emits leaf references so the induced decision is computable; the task guide’s few-shot menu examples are replaced by our domain skill list; the backbone is the protocol-wide Qwen3-8B instead of GPT-4. 

CoPAL (Joublin et al., 2024). Faithful, rebuilt from the original prompt files in the authors’ repos-itory: the Travi role frame (human, Travi, robot), the four numbered duties, the “do not replicate actions already done” instruction, the feedback re-prompt block (request, feedback, command his-tory, JSON state), the output fields Goal / Objects needed / State summary / Remaining actions, and the Error, Reason, Suggestion triplet phrasing. Adaptations: the feedback triplet carries a user inter-jection instead of an execution error (the original replans on errors, not on new instructions, which is itself a point of comparison); the four-layer cognitive architecture collapses to the high-level re-planning layer for a text benchmark; temperature follows the unified protocol instead of the original 0.8; the replan loop cap becomes one round. 

CoMuRoS (Borate et al., 2025). Faithful: the Task Manager prompt’s static block (rules and for-mat) and dynamic block (user command, chat history, robot state, task states, events); the three task states COMPLETED / IN PROGRESS / INTERRUPTED; replanning reorders only non-completed tasks; the new instruction arrives as a detected event; whole-task granularity (tasks are never split), which is this family’s native action space. Adaptations: the multi-robot allocation axis does not ap-ply (single robot); the paper does not publish the verbatim template, so the block wording is rebuilt from its description; output is constrained to a task-order JSON. 

DPPM (Lu et al., 2025) †. Faithful: the published merge instruction (“merge and revise two par-tial plans based on feedback from critics into one that meets the requirements”), the Partial Plan A / Feedback A / Partial Plan B / Feedback B field structure, the pure-JSON output requirement, and explicit conflict annotations (transport exclusivity in the original, hand capacity and forbidden co-carries here). Adaptations are larger, hence the dagger: the original is offline text travel plan-ning, ours is an online embodied stream; Plan A comes from the live commitments rather than a parallel sub-planner; the verify-refine loop (up to ten rounds) becomes a single merge; the Cartesian candidate combination does not apply to a two-plan merge. 

CoT replan (Wei et al., 2022). The chain-of-thought baseline of the DPPM comparison pool: identical task and input to Conjunctive replan, with the schema forcing an in-band reasoning 

field before the plan. 

LLM-Modulo (Kambhampati et al., 2024). The generate-test baseline of the DPPM pool. The proposal LLM is checked by a sound verifier, and the verifier is our symbolic simulator (commitment accounting, location contracts, hand capacity, forbidden co-carries), with violations fed back for revision, up to three rounds. This is a deliberate concession: the strongest verifier available in this setting, isolating “verifier without enumeration” from “enumeration plus verifier.” 

Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023). Proposal (sharing the Conjunctive replan prompt and cache), one self-reflection pass prompted to check for dropped steps, infeasible order, forbidden co-carries, and missed ride-alongs, then one revision. No external verifier: together with LLM-Modulo this forms the introspection-versus-external-check contrast. 17 Preprint 

SayCan (Ahn et al., 2022) †. A structural constant, stated openly: SayCan has no clarification, no emergency handling, and no weaving mechanism; each instruction is planned independently and executed in arrival order, so in this decision space it is queue-at-tail on every episode and involves no LLM call. The row quantifies the ceiling of the sequential one-request contract under the field’s foundational system; its affordance-scoring internals are invisible to DA and TA and reproducing them would not change any reported number. 

Memory-agent (Glocker et al., 2025) †. Faithful, using the original system message from the authors’ repository: the object-list dependence, the full clarification rules (synonyms pass, near-matches ask, missing objects report, uncertainty asks), the single-request task-JSON output contract, and the chat-history mechanism (the active task enters through it). Adaptations, hence the dagger: a third task type “action” is added (the original knows only placement and handover, without which sixty chore episodes are inexpressible at the vocabulary level and the comparison would measure vocabulary, not scheduling); its three native output forms merge into one schema for constrained decoding; the routing agent is bypassed (all inputs are task requests); the RAG memory idles because a frozen single moment has no long interaction history, so its memory axis is not assessed here; the backbone is the protocol-wide Qwen3-8B.

A skill is immediate if halting it at any time leaves no object in a hazardous state, safe point if it passes through stable intermediate states, with a safe boundary after each placement or stage, and uninterruptible if it keeps an object in a pre-carious configuration throughout execution. Safe boundaries are detected online from the gripper state and a per-skill stage counter. A paused skill switches its policy to standby mode, identically for every method, and later resumes from the current observation.

Task combinations. Table A stands to the left of table B, and the tasks on each table are arranged from left to right in the order listed in Table 12. No ordering rule is given to the agent, which must infer the layout from Et. SORT ITEM is a single skill whose item order is chosen by the policy. Each combination is run 25 times, and in each trial ut is issued at a different phase of the ongoing sequence, e.g., during navigation, at the onset of a grasp, or mid-manipulation; the gold decision, boundary bt and revised sequence are annotated for every trial before execution.
