ABSTRACT 

Task-oriented conversational agents remain fragile under real world conversation scenarios as they rarely follow a predictable script, especially when users exhibit non-cooperative behavior. Existing function-calling benchmarks often emphasize successful, cooperative interactions and underrepresent adversarial conversation trajectories, thereby limiting the training resources available for developing ro-bust agents. We present ToolRACER , a synthetic data generation pipeline that coordinates user, assistant and tool emulation models to generate and validated multi-turn interactions between a user and an agent. Using ToolRACER, we con-struct ToolRACERBench a robust multi-turn conversation benchmark spanning six domains, ranging over 55 varied personas, generating a validated corpus of 5.6K conversation trajectories, with approximately 66% of conversations contain-ing failure-prone conversation scenarios. We inject adversarial behaviors, pro-ducing validated conversational interaction trajectories that capture realistic, ro-bust scenarios. We evaluate models trained on ToolRACERBench against internal benchmarks, as well as on function calling benchmarks such as τ 2-bench, BF-CLv3 and ACEBench to evaluate agentic accuracy and robustness. Models trained on ToolRACERBench improve end to end agentic accuracy across τ 2-bench and ACEBench, demonstrating significant gains when mixed with in-domain dataset in small language models for agent capability tasks. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Task-oriented dialog systems are a key component of modern enterprise conversational AI, powering applications such as customer support, transaction handling, and automated service workflows (Patil et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b). Despite significant advances in natural language understanding and generation driven by large language models (LLMs), the dialog management layer in many deployed systems remains largely unchanged (Rabinovich & Anaby Tavor, 2025). Most enterprise-grade systems that rely on finite-state dialogue managers or pre-scripted call flows to enforce fixed interaction paths can offer predictability and control, but struggle to handle deviations from expected user behavior, evolving goals, or incomplete information, often leading to brittle interactions and increased user friction. In contrast, human agents exhibit a high degree of flexibility in task-oriented conversations. They maintain rich conversational context, infer missing information when possible, and dynamically decide whether to ask clarifying questions or consult external resources. Bridging this gap between rigid automated systems and adaptive human behavior remains a core challenge in conversational AI (Barres et al., 2025). To enable such robust agentic capabilities, we need high quality and diverse multi-turn data that has multiple exchanges between the user and the agent across various task-oriented trajectories, each requiring a sequence of interdependent actions and solving the user-initiated task through function calls. Building reliable AI agents that are responsive to shifts in conversational goals and tool fail-ures require robust conversation trajectory planning. Still, these works lack various aspects of realistic user interactions. In our framing, we break down the taxonomy of realistic user interactions into three types: Happy ,

Unhappy , Impossible . Happy paths are paths with non-adversarial users and achievable tasks. 

Unhappy paths are those with adversarial users, but achievable tasks. Impossible paths are those with non-achievable tasks. To address these types of interactions, we introduce ToolRACER: a Robust Agentic Conversation Emulation Resource for producing high-quality multi-turn agent traces. ToolRACER generates di-verse, validated trajectories through a pipeline consisting of three stages: 1) Multi-agent simu-lation , where three agents are initialized as user, assistant and tool agents performing dialogue simulation based on a defined set of skeleton scenarios covering Happy , Unhappy and Impossi-ble paths, mimicking realistic world scenarios. 2) Multi-stage evaluation , consisting of a series of four evaluators initialized with judge LLMs that check the syntax and dialogue consistency and faithfulness of the generated traces. 3) A Refinement loop that improves the failed conversations from Stage 2. Stage 2 also provides failure reasons from its evaluation, which we use in Stage 3 to produce validated multi-turn tool-calling conversations. Resulting from these three stages is a benchmark that can be used for training and testing tool-calling conversational agents. We call it ToolRACERBench. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We propose ToolRACER, a synthetic data generation pipeline that leverages tool execution feedback and generates robust multi-turn agentic conversations validated through a rubric of evaluators for realistic and diverse synthetic data generation. • We propose a taxonomy of adversarial trajectories, generating robust agentic traces by explicitly modeling both user-originated and environment-originated failures, classifying traces into Happy , Unhappy and Impossible scenarios. • We perform extensive experiments using ToolRACERBench comparing against the SOTA training benchmark APIGen-MT Prabhakar et al. (2025) and provide empirical evidence showcasing performance gains across cross domain benchmarks such as τ 2-bench, BF-2CLv3 and ACEBench. Along with cross-domain evaluation, we evaluate ablations of our training data mixtures on the ToolRACERBench-test set, thus analyzing agentic perfor-mance under robust interactions. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Function-calling benchmarks Since the inception of BFCL (Berkeley Function Calling Leader-board) (Patil et al., 2025) and τ -bench Yao et al. (2025), researchers have been trying to improve the conversational function-calling abilities of LLMs. API-Bank (Li et al., 2023) is one such early work that shows language models can improve their tool-calling abilities with curated data, but suffer from poor API-formatting and an inability to follow instructions. Subsequent tool-calling benchmarks became more realistic: having long-context conversations (BFCL V3), evaluating tool-calling of agents that need to talk to other agents (via MCP protocol) (Wang et al., 2026b), and/or allowing both user and agent to act on the same environment (Barres et al., 2025). These methods, while they reflected the abilities of real-world environments, did not fully capture the unpredictable nature of real-world interactions. 

Robust Function-Calling Agents Agents would be useless in the real world if they are unable to adapt to unseen scenarios (Rabinovich & Anaby Tavor, 2025). To make agents more robust, many benchmarks now include noise in their data, such as HammerBench (Wang et al., 2025a) (includes irrelevant functions), NoisyToolBench (Wang et al., 2025b) (includes ambiguous or incomplete in-structions), and Multi-Mission Tool Bench (Yu et al., 2025) (changes the goal during the interac-tion). Though agentic and environmental robustness are important, still lacking in these works is user-centric robustness. 

Trajectory-Driven Robustness Human users can be quite difficult. They can be uncooperative, aggressive, or impatient; they can be clueless and lost; they can also demand things that go against agent policy. However, many function-calling benchmarks assume an ideal user (Shi et al., 2026; Barres et al., 2025). Benchmarks that stress-test a system are required to ensure that a system is ready to handle real users (Purwar & Choudhary, 2026). Persona-driven robustness is a very common and easy-to-implement technique to create such a benchmark. For example, Tan et al. (2025) creates a variety of personas and evaluates whether they can be maintained in a RAG-style evaluation setup. Chopra et al. (2026) use personas in training for tau-bench tasks to show that models can be made more robust. Finally Wang et al. (2026a) uses personas to diversify their benchmark for evaluating personalization. Our work likewise makes use of diverse personas and situations to increase robustness in training and diversity in evaluating tool-use scenarios. 

3 TOOL RACER: M ETHODOLOGY FOR ROBUST SYNTHETIC MULTI -T URN DATA GENERATION 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Our framework generates multi-turn conversations in which an assistant fulfills user goals through dialogue and tool-mediated interaction within an environment. This interaction can be repre-sented as a series of user utterances U, assistant actions A, and tool-call observations T , like so: 

(ui, a i0 , t i0 , ...a ik , t ik ... ), for i = 0 , ..., N turns. Each turn can have k = 0 , ..., T tool-calls. Each 

aik can be either an assistant utterance (in which case, tik will be null), or a tool-call (in which case, tik will be the output of the tool call). This interaction ends when the applicable completion or termination conditions are met, or when the interaction budget is exhausted. 3.2 OVERVIEW 

Each conversation represents a user attempting to complete tasks with the help of an agent that uses external tools throughout the interaction. Generating high-quality synthetic data that captures the complexity arising from situations such as goal revision or system errors presents a significant challenge. Synthesizing interactions is difficult: interactions depend on user inputs and the outcome of previous agent actions at later stages; failures to maintain consistency between turns occur and hallucinations can creep in. 3Figure 2: Overview of ToolRACER for robust data generation. Scenario specifications condition interactions among user, assistant, and tool agents, with the assistant choosing to act through tool calls or respond in natural language. Four judges assess syntax errors, faithfulness, role confusion, and task success. Validated conversations are retained in the dataset, while failure reasons serve as corrective hints to repair and re-simulate unsuccessful conversations. Cases that remain invalid after the maximum number of attempts are discarded. We present ToolRACER a three stage pipeline (Figure 2) for generating diverse human-agent in-teractions that capture conversational adversities from dual ends. To address the above systemic challenges in multi-agent simulations, our pipeline consists of a sequence of evaluators that validate the coherence of generated interactions. The main insight in our approach to data synthesis is that robustness can be induced by injecting adversity into trajectories, based on uncooperative behaviors of both users and tools. Our pipeline also includes a refinement loop, which recovers 65% of the failed conversation thus increasing the yield of validated conversations. Appendix A provides more details. 3.2.1 SCENARIO GENERATION AND TOOL DEFINITION 

Scenario Generation Every dialogue is generated from a source scenarios, which is declared with task success criteria, an instructed objective of the task, and seed data for user and tool agent. These predefined scenario files become the main resource of running the simulations between the three agents and generating diverse data across multiple domains. For every task success, we define a set of action tools and targeted observation changes that count towards task completion. For each task scenario, to add robustness in our interaction trajectories, we inject user adversarial behaviors and tool behaviors that simulate failure-prone trajectories. For each task scenario, we also randomly draw from 55 personas from personahub Ge et al. (2025)to seed the user agent’s behavior, thus adding to the diversity of user behaviors. We have provided the example of scenario metadata and and personas in Appendix C. 

Tool Definition For each domain, we created custom defined sets of tools and associated param-eteric values slots, which are presented to the assistant agent as means to solve tasks. Across all domains, we defined 72 unique tools, with example definitions given in the Appendix D 3.2.2 ROBUST MULTI -A GENT DIALOG SIMULATION 

The first generation stage comprises three distinct LLM-based agents that interact to generate real-istic dialogues between human and tool augmented-AI assistants: 1. Assistant Agent : At each step, this agent receives the conversation history and avail-able tool definitions. Then, it produces a structured response that includes an action (<action> ). Actions are either tool invocations ( type="tool" ) or user-facing ut-terances ( type="say" ). The agent is initialized to solve the user-proposed task, and takes steps towards invoking the set of correct tools, while understanding the goal and the 4information gathered in the conversation history. A task is marked solved when the agent completes all the steps in the success criteria as defined in the task scenarios, as described in Section 3.2.1. The agent is instructed to make a tool call only when it has gathered enough information from the user agent, thus simulating precise and accurate function calls. 2. User Agent : The user agent acts as the user and generates queries and appropriate re-sponses to the assistant actions. It impersonates a human, and is pre-seeded with a user persona and optional behaviors. To add diversity to our dataset, we use persona-driven generation, adding variety of behaviors. We curated 55 personas from persona-hub Ge et al. (2025) and added basic attributes such as name, age, and user bio along with the defined personas, thus inducing our user agent to simulate different behaviors. Along with personas, we also inject user-originated adversities into dialogues, adding challenges for the assistant agent. We discussed injected behaviors in Section 3.3. 3. Tool Agent : The tool agent is defined to generate the result observations from the tool calls made by the assistant agent. The tool agent receives the seeded data for the environment, as well as optional injected behaviors designed to mimic realistic environment conditions and system failures, such as empty tool results. We discuss injected tool behaviors in Section 3.3. Each agent maintains its own distinct view of the conversation history. The assistant agent observes all steps, including tool calls and results; the user agent observes only user-facing exchanges; and the tool agent observes only tool invocations and their context. A central runner orchestrates turn-taking and manages termination conditions. The assistant agents interact with the user agents to execute dialogue traces and can access the tool agent for tool responses. In this way, we generate synthetic conversation trajectories by multi-agent simulation, using defined task scenarios covering six domains. 3.2.3 MULTI -S TAGE EVALUATION 

Once the synthetic conversation traces are generated, they undergo multiple stages of automatic data validation, combining rule-based checks with LLM-as-a-judge evaluators. A conversation is added to the final dataset only if all the following four evaluation checks succeed: • Syntax Evaluator Verifies structural correctness: each AssistantAgent step must contain a 

<action> block, and each turn must end with exactly one <action type="say"> .Tool calls are validated against their schema for correct names, required parameters, and value types. • Faithfulness Evaluator Detects hallucination. For tool calls, the judge verifies that pa-rameter values are grounded in user intent. For say actions, it checks that the assistant’s utterances accurately reflect tool results rather than introducing unsupported information. • Role Confusion Evaluator Identifies cases where the UserAgent broke character and be-gan acting like an assistant. We filter conversations where the user asks questions to help the assistant or offers assistance. • Task Success Evaluator Evaluated by an LLM judge that receives the task objective, slot constraints, and preferences, success criteria, and conversation transcript. The judge deter-mines whether the assistant has achieved the user’s goal, while respecting the distinction between hard constraints and soft preferences. In cases where a scenario is impossible, success requires the assistant to deliver appropriate explanations and alternatives. This set of four validation stages addresses the core problems of preventing hallucinations and main-taining consistency across chains of function calls in simulated conversation. The evaluators perform the roles of human evaluators in each stage of conversation evaluation. The prompts 1 used for judge LLMs are listed in the appendix 6.

53.2.4 REFINEMENT LOOP 

At the end of Stage 2, we get a validated or failed conversation trajectory. For each failed interaction, the evaluator stage provides the reason for failure from one of the judges, suggesting at which particular turn the conversation is invalid. The refinement loop makes use of the failure reasons and converts them into refinement hints. It then passes the failed conversation to the multi-agent simulation in an attempt to make the conversation valid. We try refinement at most twice; if it still fails to pass the validations, a conversation is discarded. The refinement loop was added to yield more validated trajectories without having to start from scratch, thus saving overall cost of generation. 3.3 TAXONOMY OF ROBUST TRAJECTORIES

Real-world dialogues frequently involve complica-tions: users provide incomplete information, change their minds about what they want, encounter system errors, or request impossible outcomes. To cover such phenomena, we injected two types of adversarial sit-uations into the generation of trajectories. We classify the generated trajectories into three cate-gories: Happy , Unhappy , and Impossible . Approx-imately 66% of our final dataset contain failure prone conversations. 

Happy Path These capture the normal cases where the simulation isn’t injected with any failure-prone behaviors. The trajectories in this category consist of solvable tasks that are handled by the assistant using a chain of function calls with a sole task objective. 

Unhappy Path These capture dynamic user behav-iors that stress the agent’s planning and clarification strategy. We inject adversarial behaviors in the user agent during the simulation that create user-originated conversational adversities. The interactions in this category challenge the agents with tasks that are solvable, but have certain constraints or difficulties, such as: • Partial information : The user provides incomplete or under specified task details, thus forcing the assistant to invoke a different chain of tool calls to solve the given task. In this injection the user doesn’t provide the necessary information, hence the agent figures out a solution based on partial or incomplete information. • Incremental constraint revelation : In this case the user mentions their preference and the constraints on task success iteratively, over multiple turns. A typical case is the assistant asking for specific values from the user, as incremental steps towards solving the task. • Goal revision : In these cases the user agent revises and changes the end goal during the course of interaction, thus forcing the assistant’s goal to diverge from the original plan of operation, and requiring plan revisions. • Adversarial attitudes : Human users vary by personality and emotional states, sometimes because assistant agents are frustrating to deal with. Thus we inject adversarial user at-titudes into the conversation to express frustration, show skepticism towards assistants’ suggestions, or to challenge those suggestions with new requests, thus forcing the assistant to invoke different tools than originally planned. 

Impossible Path To simulate real-world system failures, we initialize the simulator with tool-injected behaviors that represent realistic tool failures or environment conditions requiring recovery or principled refusal. Also, many deployed assistants are supposed to follow certain policy guide-lines, leading to denials of user requests that are outside of system workflow rules. For fair comparison, we use the APIGen-MT (Prabhakar et al., 2025) which consists of 5K validated trajectories for performing ablation exper-iments, thus evaluating the efficacy of our dataset against the SOTA training benchmarks. The distribution of our proposed dataset is divided across six domains and three types of robust conver-sations which are mentioned in table 1. The collected trajectories are split at every assistant response and we train the models to predict only the assistant responses. The model is provided with conver-sation history as the context, based on which we teach it either to invoke a function call or ask for clarification to the user keeping it grounded to the end goal of the trajectory. We train models with parameter-efficient fine-tuning, with LoRA rank 32, learning rate of 1e-4, and a batch size of 32.

Cross Domain Evaluation We experiment on several evaluation benchmarks which are designed for evaluating agent capabilities. To evaluate the end to end agentic accuracy, we use τ 2-bench’s (Barres et al., 2025) retail and airline domain, as well as ACEBench’s (Chen et al., 2025) agent category dataset, which consists of 50 tasks. Both benchmarks are suitable for evaluating the ef-fectiveness of our ToolRACERBench under tool-use capabilities with multi-turn interaction settings and out of domain generalization. We also performed cross domain evaluation on BFCLv3 Patil et al. (2025) across complex function calling and hallucination measures. 

Turn Level Evaluation We perform in-domain turn level evaluation, inspired from Rawat et al. (2025)’s L-1 evaluation, on ToolRACERBench’s test set which comprises of 1.8k trajectories equally distributed among domains and scenario paths. In this evaluation, we evaluated each con-versation turn against the ground truth. For each query, the action space can either have an agent response or function call. In terms of metrics, we keep the same metrics as proposed in previous work, thus measuring the action accuracy of the agent. Based on action, we evaluate the function calling abilities by computing F1 and response generation by measuring the semantic similar-ity of answers between the agent prediction and ground truth using a encoder similarity model. In our turn level evaluation we use the same benchmarks across Happy , Unhappy and Impossible 

paths thus evaluating the performance of open source function calling models on robust trajectories. 4.2 RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 

Takeaway 1: augmenting training data with ToolRACERBench will improve the robustness of trained models. We kept 33% of our generated dataset for performing turn-level evaluations, thus evaluating the performance of open source models and training benchmarks against our trained abla-tions under robust trajectories. We evaluate the aggregate performance across Happy , Unhappy and 

Impossible . The turn level evaluation performed on ToolRACERBench-Test, shows us that native function calling models like xLAM2-3b-fc fail massively in action accuracy during Impossible 

conversations. Our ablation experiments also reveal that just training on Unhappy and Impossible 

paths enhances the action accuracy of the base model by over 10%. We also noticed that the F1 score of the APIGen-MT checkpoint drops, suggesting that APIGen-MT corpus can lead to overfit-ting and a lack of generalization to out-of-domain tools. Along with our in-domain evaluation, we also evaluate on the irrelevance and relevant category of the BFCLv3 dataset. These two subcate-gories measure hallucination abilities and check when the model tails to make a tool call. 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we present ToolRACER which is a synthetic data generation pipeline for training robust task oriented conversational agents. Alongside it, we release ToolRACERBench: a training and testing benchmark. It features a dataset, richly diverse with a variety of user personas, and three kinds of user-agent interactions: Happy (users are ideal and tasks are achievable), Unhappy (users are not ideal, but tasks are achievable), and Impossible (tasks are not achievable). In our evaluation, we show that ToolRACERBench trains robust agents that perform even better than agents trained with current SOTA training benchmarks.
