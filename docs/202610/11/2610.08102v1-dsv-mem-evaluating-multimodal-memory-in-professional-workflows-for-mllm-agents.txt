# DSV-M EM : EVALUATING MULTIMODAL MEMORY IN PROFESSIONAL WORKFLOWS FOR MLLM AGENTS 

Jike Zhong 1,2∗† Ritwick Chaudhry 1∗ Xuanbai Chen 1 Tianchen Zhao 1

Linghan Xu 1 Yifan Xing 1 Nishant Sankaran 11Amazon AGI 2University of Southern California 

ABSTRACT 

Conversational MLLM agents are increasingly expected to assist in professional workflows: from AI research and engineering design to product management and business operations. Despite this growing need, this capability has not been well explored: existing benchmarks largely focus on informal, everyday interactions and personal-life scenarios that typically feature photographic natural images, iso-lated, static artifacts, and recall-oriented questions. In contrast, professional sce-narios often involve structured, information-heavy artifacts that undergo frequent revisions and authority updates, as well as compositional queries that require rec-onciling many artifact versions in memory while tracking state precisely. To ad-dress these challenges, we introduce DSV-Mem , a carefully curated benchmark for evaluating Dense Stateful Visual Mem ory. DSV-Mem comprises a diverse suite of expert-reviewed scenarios and 1,000 questions across five user-oriented categories (Current State, Past State, Derived State, Change History, and Con-flict/Refusal). Moreover, a Hartley-inspired criterion is introduced to favor ques-tions with broader visual-evidence inspection demands. To support further study, we introduce a generation harness that produces high-quality, domain-specific evaluation suites by decoupling state-transition synthesis from conversation fill-ing. Rigorous evaluation over 27 configurations spanning frontier, open-weight models and popular memory management methods reveals that even the strongest baseline scores below 45% on DSV-Mem. Comprehensive analysis surfaces sev-eral important findings: 1) multimodality and information density both contribute to difficulty, but state evolution , particularly the number of governing updates, is the dominant tested factor. Raw conversation/haystack length, OCR and arith-metic are not the primary bottlenecks; 2) models frequently fail to verify user premises against prior state updates before answering; 3) popular interventions such as increased reasoning effort and memory management methods yield lim-ited gains, whereas state-aware designs prove more effective. The benchmark & code will be publicly released. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Conversational MLLM agents are increasingly deployed in extended professional workflows beyond isolated everyday tasks (Hu et al., 2025; Xi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). For instance, designers generate and iteratively refine complex campaign graphics, while ML researchers repeatedly an-alyze evolving tables and plots, and revise paper drafts as experiments progress. Unlike everyday conversations, these scenarios often exhibit distinctive features: 1) information-heavy visuals: they center on structured, data-heavy artifacts such as dense worksheets, dashboards, or intricate graphic designs rather than ordinary photographic scenes; 2) statefulness: uploaded artifacts frequently un-dergo revisions, scope changes, and authority updates; and 3) analytical queries: users go beyond single-fact questions to compositional ones that compare, aggregate, or derive quantities across vi-suals and revisions. Despite this growing need, existing efforts to evaluate multimodal memory largely focus on in-formal, everyday interactions and personal-life scenarios, featuring photographic visuals (Maharana et al., 2024; Bei et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026), static artifacts (Maharana et al., 2024; Bei et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026), or recall-oriented questions (Wu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a), rather than the dense visuals, stateful artifacts, and analytical queries that professional workflows demand. Moreover, our careful analysis shows many of them average fewer than three evidence images per question, often one or none (Bei et al., 2026), and may be largely saturated for frontier systems: Gemini-3.7-Flash already exceeds 90% on LoCoMo (Ma-harana et al., 2024). Most importantly, none of these settings jointly capture the properties and complexity of real-world workflows encountered in practice (Figure 1). To bridge this gap, we introduce DSV-Mem ( Dense Stateful Visual Mem ory), a comprehensive benchmark designed around the core properties above to assess MLLM agent memory in profes-sional workflows. DSV-Mem features realistic scenarios with expert-reviewed dense artifacts, ex-tended and topic-centric conversations with diverse stateful evolutions, and user-oriented categories (Current State, Past State, Derived State, Change History, and Conflict/Refusal) covering diverse real-world requests. However, curating such a benchmark is highly nontrivial: Without explicit state propagation control, directly prompting an LLM/agent often yields repetitive dialogues with homogeneous or corrupted state transitions. These degraded trajectories in turn produce shallow QAs with incorrect, unverifi-able labels. Conversely, manual creation limits controlled modifications, making systematic study of the underlying factors difficult. To this end, we develop a generation harness that produces high-quality conversations requiring only modest curation effort. Our key design principles are 1) the decoupling of state-trajectory construction from surface conversation realization, and 2) a Hartley-inspired criterion for cross-visual QA construction, with 7.78 candidate visual artifacts per QA in DSV-Mem (geometric mean), substantially higher than similar benchmarks (Table 1). This paves the way for scalable future study. • Evaluations and findings: We evaluate 27 configurations, conduct in-depth analysis, and share important insights that could inform better design of MLLM conversational agents in the future. 

2 RELATED WORK 

MLLM memory benchmarks. LoCoMo, LongMemEval, and EverMemBench evaluate conver-sational recall, temporal reasoning, and knowledge updates, but do not center on reconstructing fine-grained state across revisions of dense visual artifacts (Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026). Mem-Gallery, MemLens, and M 3Exam evaluate multimodal histories and hetero-geneous evidence, while H2HMem studies human–human interactions; these settings do not focus on cumulative artifact revisions and authority changes within sustained professional workflows (Bei et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026). LongMemEval-V2 and World-MemArena include dynamic state tracking, but emphasize reusable environment knowledge and 3Preprint action–world memory, respectively, rather than compositional analysis of dense, repeatedly revised records (Wu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a). MemEye evaluates fine-grained visual evidence and evolutionary synthesis, but emphasizes visual granularity and reasoning depth across life scenarios (Guo et al., 2026). DSV-Mem instead centers on the interaction between visual density and state evolution : compositional queries require reconciling field-level updates and source authority across revisions of dense professional artifacts, rather than recalling individual state changes in isolation. 

MLLM memory-management methods. Generative Agents and MemoryBank emphasize re-flection and personalized recall, while MemGPT and MemoryOS manage hierarchical memory, prioritizing conversational continuity rather than reconstruction of evolving visual records (Park et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2023; Packer et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2025). LongMem retrieves cached text, HippoRAG links knowledge for multi-hop retrieval, and A-Mem evolves interconnected notes, emphasizing contextual access and semantic organization rather than explicit field-level authority across artifact versions (Wang et al., 2023; Jim´ enez Guti´ errez et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025). Mem0 extracts and updates salient facts, while SimpleMem and LightMem compress and consolidate in-teractions; such compression poses challenges for professional workflows, where minor details may govern later state-dependent queries (Chhikara et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026b; Fang et al., 2026). MIRIX and MemVerse support multimodal memory, but retaining evidence leaves open how to rec-oncile successive revisions and source authority (Wang & Chen, 2025; Liu et al., 2026c). DSV-Mem tests these demands: all four evaluated integrations underperform their baselines, highlighting the challenge of preserving and reconciling dense, state-bearing details .

3.1 BENCHMARK OVERVIEW 

Figure 3: DSV-Mem category breakdown. DSV-Mem comprises 1,000 carefully curated compositional user queries across 10 professional scenarios (subsection C.2), each grounded in a long, coherent conversation with structured, information-heavy artifacts that undergo successive revisions and diverse authority updates. It extensively evaluates whether MLLM agents can 1) track authoritative multimodal state, 2) re-tain and reason over update history and evolution trajectory, and 

3) detect conflicts with maintained state and refuse unsupported requests. On average, each scenario features 212 conversation turns with 24 dense visual artifacts and up to 50k tokens. 

Question categories. Questions span five capability axes, grouped into three families (illustrated in Figure 3) that mirror the capabilities mentioned above; counts in parentheses. Authority-aware state resolution covers Current State (100), identifying the latest authoritative value; Past State 

(100), recovering the state at a specified point in the conversation; and Derived State (200), filter-ing, comparing, or computing over reconstructed state. Stateful change tracking consists of Change History (350), recovering the sequence or frequency of updates. State-based conflict/refusal com-prises Conflict/Refusal (250), detecting false premises, unresolved conflicts, or insufficient evi-dence. Stateful conversation generation. Rigorous evaluation of dense stateful visual memory requires conversation trajectories that jointly satisfy three constraints : (i) domain-faithful dense visual arti-facts, (ii) diverse and explicit state transitions with verifiable ground truth, and (iii) coherent long-form interactions. However, directly prompting an LLM to generate complete trajectories often produces repetitive dialogue, poorly motivated shifts in the workflow, and limited transition di-versity. More fundamentally, unlike static settings, evolving scenarios require accepted state to be explicitly propagated and validated across all updates to prevent inconsistent evidence and QA labels. To overcome these challenges, we decouple latent state-trajectory construction from di-alogue realization through a generation harness (Figure 2). Quality assurance and expert validation. To ensure a high quality bar, we conduct three layers of validation: automated checks, independent human auditing, and domain-expert review. Automated checks cover release integrity, accidental answer leakage, duplicate questions, and computational consistency. Three PhD students independently audit a 100-question sample set, with reviewer-averaged gold-answer agreement of 98.3% , question clarity of 97.7% , visual-evidence consistency of 98.7% , and confirmation of cross-visual necessity of 99.0% . On this sample, Gemini-3.7-Flash achieves only 2% accuracy without images and 1% with a single image, versus 93% when supplied with the supporting evidence, confirming cross-visual dependence and answerability . To address this, we design controlled interventions by varying each factor individually: 1) multi-modality (oracle visual-to-text ), replacing each visual artifact with a faithful textual rendering; 2) information density (density reduced ), retaining only information required for the target question; and 3) state evolution (statefulness removed ), removing stateful changes to the targeted field. Across the three categories applicable to all interventions, oracle visual-to-text raises mean accuracy from 52.7% to 60.9% (+8.2 pp), while density reduction raises it to 65.2% (+12.6 pp). 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced DSV-Mem, a benchmark and generation harness for evaluating dense stateful vi-sual memory in conversational MLLM agents. Across realistic professional workflows, DSV-Mem shows that strong static visual understanding does not necessarily translate into reliable state main-tenance: models often read individual artifacts correctly yet fail to reconcile textual and visual up-dates, distinguish near-identical revisions, preserve transition history, or reject stale premises. By providing controlled multimodal transitions, validated state graphs, and executable QA, DSV-Mem offers a rigorous testbed for diagnosing these failures and advancing reliable MLLM agents for long-running, evolving professional workflows. 10 Preprint

A LIMITATIONS 

Although we document the generation harness, transition library, and validation procedures, regen-erating DSV-Mem may produce different conversations and artifacts due to differences in models, agent implementations, and stochastic generation. The harness constrains state consistency and ev-idence requirements but does not guarantee identical outputs or difficulty across generation runs. This variability concerns dataset regeneration rather than evaluation on a fixed benchmark release. DSV-Mem is designed around properties motivated by professional workflows, including dense artifacts, repeated revisions, source-authority changes, and analytical queries. However, it is a researcher-constructed benchmark rather than a direct sample of deployed professional workflows. Its 1,000 questions also provide more limited breadth than very large-scale QA benchmarks; how-ever, each question is grounded in a long, stateful multimodal workflow with many artifact versions and validated state transitions, making individual examples substantially more costly to construct and verify than isolated QA pairs. The current scale supports the controlled analyses reported here, while expanding to more workflows and questions would further strengthen coverage. We therefore do not claim that its scenarios reproduce the full distribution or complexity of real-world use, or that 15 Preprint quantitative findings will transfer unchanged to deployment settings. Rather, DSV-Mem isolates and stress-tests a set of memory demands motivated by such workflows; evaluating transfer to naturally occurring professional interactions remains future work. The self-ledger explored in section 5 is intended as a lightweight diagnostic prototype motivated by our analysis, rather than a new general-purpose memory architecture. Its role is to test whether explicitly preserving updates, provenance, and accepted state can address part of the observed failure mode. We do not claim that this design is optimal or broadly general, and leave the development of more complete state-aware memory architectures to future work.

C ADDITIONAL BENCHMARK DETAILS 

C.1 QUESTION CATEGORY DESIGN 

Design motivation. We organize questions around how users consult evolving professional records in a sustained workflow. Users may ask what is currently authoritative, recover an ear-lier decision, compare or compute over accepted records, review how a value changed, or make a request whose premise conflicts with the available evidence. These needs motivate our three fami-lies: authority-aware state resolution , stateful change tracking , and state-based conflict/refusal . The five categories distinguish the primary operation needed to satisfy the request, rather than treating all 21 Preprint questions as retrieval of previously mentioned facts. The examples below illustrate these question forms; they are not verbatim benchmark items. 

Authority-aware state resolution: what is true at the relevant point? Professional records are repeatedly revised, so answering a factual question requires identifying the state that governs the user’s request. We distinguish three forms of this need: • Current State (100 questions). Users need the accepted information on which to act now. For example, “What is the approved budget for Project A after the latest corrections?” This motivates evaluating authoritative-state resolution, rather than merely reading the newest artifact. • Past State (100 questions). Users also revisit earlier decisions or reconstruct what was ac-cepted at a particular stage. For example, “What budget was approved immediately before the scope revision?” This motivates retaining historical snapshots rather than overwriting memory with only the current state. • Derived State (200 questions). Practical requests often require analysis rather than repe-tition of a stored value. For example, “Which projects remain within their revised budgets, and what is their combined allocation?” This motivates compositional queries that com-bine filtering, comparison, or computation with reconstruction of the relevant authoritative state. Together, these categories test whether memory supports current decisions, retrospective inspection, and analysis over evolving records. 

Stateful change tracking: how did we get here? A final value does not explain the sequence of decisions that produced it. Users may need to audit revisions, review changes, or establish how often a field was updated. Change History (350 questions) captures this need. For example, “How many accepted budget changes occurred after the initial approval, and what were the successive amounts?” Two conversations can end with the same budget while having different update histo-ries; endpoint retrieval alone therefore cannot answer this request. This category evaluates whether models preserve the relevant trajectory, including the effects of disregard and reinstatement, rather than collapsing it into a final summary. 

State-based conflict/refusal: is the request supported by the record? Users may refer to an outdated value, assume that an unresolved decision has been finalized, or request a conclusion that the records do not support. Conflict/Refusal (250 questions) evaluates whether models recognize these conditions instead of accepting the premise and producing an unsupported answer. For ex-ample, “Using the finalized budget, calculate the remaining allocation” is not answerable as stated if conflicting revisions remain unresolved. The appropriate response is to identify that issue rather than invent a finalized value. The design targets evidence-grounded correction or refusal, not refusal as a default behavior. 

Compositional questions, primary-operation labels. These categories organize user needs with-out assuming that the underlying operations are independent. A comparison may require recovering past states, and a conflict check may require tracing which update superseded another. We therefore assign each question to its primary requested operation while allowing multiple supporting opera-tions. Across all categories, questions must satisfy the shared cross-visual evidence requirement. This design connects recognizable professional requests to the benchmark’s central challenge: pre-serving and reasoning over authoritative state as dense visual records evolve .C.2 SCENARIOS OVERVIEW AND BREAKDOWN

D.3 ADDITIONAL ERROR ANALYSIS 

We expand the error analysis in subsection 4.2 to clarify where failures arise and which state oper-ations they involve. The analysis distinguishes failures to acquire evidence from failures to recon-struct its authoritative state, compute over that state, or produce an appropriate response. 

Error classification. We classify all incorrect responses (Gemini-3.7-flash, Kimi-K3, MiniMax-M3, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B) by the earliest failure stage into five categories: 1) evidence ac-quisition , retrieving the wrong artifact or misreading visual content; 2) state reconstruction , in-correctly tracking, retaining, or reconciling updates across the conversation; 3) downstream com-putation , performing incorrect arithmetic over correctly reconstructed state; 4) decision/response ,missed refusals, hallucinations, or incorrect answers despite correct preceding reasoning; and 5) other , failures not covered by these categories. Labels are assigned by majority vote among GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.6, and Gemini 3.1 Pro, with unanimous agreement on 87% of responses. Three PhD students independently classify 200 sampled incorrect responses, achieving 87% unanimous agreement. Human-majority labels agree with automated classifications in 96% of cases and at-tribute 85% of failures to state reconstruction, corroborating its dominance. The earliest-stage criterion distinguishes the source of an error from its downstream consequences. For example, computing a total from a superseded value is a state-reconstruction failure when the arithmetic itself is correct. Similarly, an incorrect response caused by a missed authoritative correc-tion is attributed to state reconstruction rather than counted again as a decision/response error. 

State reconstruction is the primary bottleneck. As shown in Figure 5a, state reconstruction ac-counts for 89.7% of errors , substantially exceeding decision/response (5.5%), evidence acquisition (3.5%), and downstream computation (1.0%). The dominant challenge is therefore not extracting an isolated value or computing with it, but determining which values and updates remain authoritative in the evolving conversation. A model may correctly read an artifact yet answer incorrectly because it overlooks a later correction, retains a withdrawn update, or combines values from incompatible states. Evidence acquisition and downstream computation together account for only 4.5% of errors, indicat-ing that visual reading and arithmetic are not the primary bottlenecks in this evaluation. The bottleneck persists across question categories. State reconstruction dominates across the category groups in Figure 5b. Although their outputs differ, these tasks share a prerequisite: identi-fying the operative state and its supporting updates. Current and Past State questions require select-ing the correct state at the relevant point; Derived State questions require reconstructing that state before computation; Change History questions require retaining the accepted update sequence; and Conflict/Refusal questions require checking a request against the maintained state. Consequently, a reconstruction failure can affect all five categories rather than appearing only in questions explicitly asking about update history. 

Lost updates and stale values are the largest reconstruction failure modes. We further classify state-reconstruction errors by their dominant erroneous state operation (Figure 5c). Lost/collapsed updates account for 30% of all errors , while stale values account for 27.1% . The former occurs when a governing update is omitted or collapsed during reconstruction, so its effect is absent from the maintained history or state. The latter occurs when a superseded value remains in use despite an operative replacement. These subtypes distinguish failure to preserve an update from failure to resolve which value it makes authoritative. Their percentages use all incorrect responses as the denominator, not only state-reconstruction errors. 

Reliable reconstruction requires multiple state operations. The remaining subtypes span com-putation on the wrong state, entity tracking, withdrawn values, authority resolution, and update integration. Each accounts for approximately 10% or less of all errors, but together they demon-strate that reconstruction is not reducible to a single operation. Retaining an update is insufficient if it is associated with the wrong entity, treated as authoritative outside its scope, or combined in-correctly with other changes. Likewise, selecting the latest displayed value is insufficient when a textual correction or withdrawal determines the accepted state. These findings complement the update-count analysis in Figure 7, where accuracy generally de-clines as more governing updates must be integrated. Together, the update-count trend and diverse error subtypes suggest that reliable memory requires a state-aware approach: preserving and rec-onciling accumulating updates across multiple state operations . This explains why DSV-Mem tests more than isolated visual recognition or retrieval: successful answers depend on maintaining the authoritative evolution of dense professional artifacts. By design, these methods typically distill interaction histories into compact memories and answer using retrieved memories at inference time. Subsequent answers therefore depend on what survives memory construction and retrieval. We supply historical visual information during construction: SimpleMem and LightMem receive detailed, question-independent transcriptions through their text-based interfaces, while Mem0 and MIRIX receive images directly through their multimodal inges-tion interfaces. To reduce transcription loss, the configured model is explicitly instructed to preserve labels, values, statuses, spatial groupings, and formatting cues. Each method then applies its own memory-construction and retrieval procedures to the chronological conversation. At answer time, the backbone receives retrieved textual memories and any question-attached images. Historical visual information is therefore mediated by constructed memories, rather than withheld during ingestion. Their underperformance on DSV-Mem highlights the challenge of preserving dense, state-bearing details through this process. This also partially motivates our self-ledger design: preserving update-level records and source links allows fine-grained details to be revisited rather than relying solely on compressed representations. Memory construction is scenario-specific and question-independent, using only visible evidence without gold answers or hidden state graphs. 28 Preprint D.7 VISIBLE -EVIDENCE SELF -LEDGER 

We explore a training-free self-ledger that explicitly preserves updates and their provenance. The model processes the conversation in chronological windows, using visible dialogue and images, image-derived OCR, and visual-difference hints. It appends entity–field events recording observed values, changes, source turns, and authority cues, while maintaining a compact index of entity aliases, standing instructions, and unresolved conflicts. Displayed values are distinguished from accepted working values, allowing textual corrections or rejected visual revisions to remain explicit without overwriting history. The ledger is frozen before evaluation questions are introduced. For each question, a retrieval step selects relevant events, temporal scope, source dialogue, and original visual evidence. The model uses this evidence to check an initial answer and produce a ledger-assisted draft; a final arbitration step reconciles the candidate answers against their visible sources. Ledger construction receives no evaluation questions, gold answers, or benchmark state graphs. This exploratory procedure tests whether explicit update preservation and reconciliation can improve performance without model training. 29
