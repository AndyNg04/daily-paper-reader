# Abstract 

In Continual Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (CMARL), agents learn co-operative policies across sequences of tasks, aiming to adapt effectively to new tasks while preserving the ability to solve previously encountered ones. In many applications, tasks differ in their underlying structure , which can represent, for example, distinct operational conditions or target configurations (e.g., different network topologies in power grids or arrangements in formation control). Existing CMARL methods lack dedicated mechanisms to leverage this structural informa-tion when learning new tasks, failing to promote transfer and mitigate forgetting. To fill this gap, we propose Continual Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (CGMARL), a novel framework for CMARL problems in which task sequences are mapped into a series of attributed graphs, each modeling a task-specific structure. In CGMARL, each graph determines the environment dynamics (next states and/or rewards) and the number of agents for the corresponding task. Then, we present Graph-based Formation (GRAFO), the first CGMARL benchmark, and show how forgetting arises in this setting. Finally, to address this limitation, we propose Frozen Graph Encoder (FROG), a method that relies on a frozen graph backbone to preserve past structural information in graph-based CMARL policies. Experi-ments on GRAFO show that pairing FROG with existing CL methods substantially improves performance on multiple CGMARL scenarios. 

# 1 Introduction 

In cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL; [41]), multiple RL agents learn to coordinate their behaviors to accomplish a shared task. This paradigm has proven effective across a variety of domains, such as autonomous driving [12], traffic signal control [37], or video games [76]. However, its applicability remains limited: most existing methods train RL agents on a single problem, whereas real-world scenarios typically involve sequences of related tasks that are often not known in advance. Continual RL (CRL; [35, 2]) formalizes this challenge by considering RL environments in which the agent never stops learning from an unknown sequence of tasks and cannot access previously observed ones. In particular, the agent aims to adapt to new tasks while preserving performance on previously learned ones [47, 60], i.e., to transfer knowledge across tasks (plasticity ) while avoiding catastrophic forgetting ( stability ). The multi-agent extension of the CRL paradigm, namely, Continual MARL (CMARL; [58, 75]), poses additional challenges. Specifically,

learning new tasks while mitigating the forgetting of past ones is complicated by multiple interacting learners [83]. This introduces a tightly coupled adaptation problem: agents must adapt to external changes in the environment, while accounting for changes in other agents’ behaviors due to policy updates. In many real-world domains, such as autonomous mobility-on-demand [24] or power grid con-trol [81, 26], the multi-agent problem is characterized by an underlying structure [42], which encodes dependencies across agents (e.g., physical or relational). Importantly, this structure determines the environment’s dynamics, i.e., it shapes state transitions and rewards. For example, in a traffic light network where each agent controls a specific intersection, local rewards and observations depend on incoming/outgoing vehicles from/to connected agents. Crucially, agents often face sequences of MARL tasks that differ in their underlying structures [16], requiring them to adapt their strategy to distinct structure-dependent dynamics. These structures may be entirely unrelated, such as flying formation problems with distinct target configurations, or may share similarities, e.g., adding or removing intersections and roads in a traffic light network. However, the current CMARL framework is not suited to address such sequential structure-based MARL problems: task shifts are typically attributed to variations in environment dynamics or team size [58], whereas the impact of task-specific problem structures is largely overlooked. Moreover, standard CL methods lack dedicated mechanisms to model structural information [88], making them ineffective at preserving past structural knowledge when adapting to new tasks. For example, regularization-based methods [36] mitigate forgetting by preserving important features or parameters, but fundamentally ignore the task’s structure. Given these limitations, we argue that graphs provide a natural and effective approach for capturing task-specific structures by representing agents as nodes and encoding pairwise (agent-agent) dependencies through edges. In this work, we introduce Continual Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (CGMARL), a novel framework for continual structure-based MARL problems. In CGMARL, task sequences are characterized by a series of attributed graphs, each modeling a task-specific structure and determining the environment dynamics and the number of agents. We first show how distinct graph distributions induce CGMARL problems relevant to real-world scenarios. Then, we present the first CGMARL benchmark, named Graph-based Formation (GRAFO), and show the failure modes of graph-based policies in CGMARL. Finally, we propose Frozen Graph Encoder (FROG), a simple approach tailored for CGMARL problems that relies on decoupling graph representation learning from action prediction. Specifically, FROG leverages node representations generated with a frozen graph backbone to provably eliminate forgetting in the graph representation learning module caused by structural changes. At the same time, the action prediction module can be paired with standard CL approaches to further mitigate forgetting. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that formalizes continual graph-based MARL problems and can pave the way for novel methodologies tailored to CGMARL. 

Main Contributions: 1) We introduce the Continual Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (CGMARL) framework, in which continual structure-based MARL problems are modeled as a series of attributed graphs. 2) We present Graph-based Formation (GRAFO), a novel graph-based navigation benchmark that allows us to design arbitrary CGMARL task sequences. 3) We provide empirical evidence that, in CGMARL, standard graph-based methods fail to generalize across different graphs and strongly suffer from forgetting. 4) We propose Frozen Graph Encoder (FROG), a simple technique that relies on a frozen graph backbone, and showcase its effectiveness across multiple CGMARL sequences. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

Cooperative MARL: Cooperative MARL problems can be modeled as a Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP; [56]), i.e., as a tuple: 

M = ⟨N , S, O = {O i}i∈N , A = {A i}i∈N , P, R, γ ⟩ (1) where N is the agent set, S is the state space, Oi and Ai are the i-th agent’s observation and action spaces, P : S × A → S and R : S × A → R are the transition and reward functions depending on the joint state and action, and γ ∈ [0 , 1) is a discount factor. Local observations {oit}i∈N are given by an unknown function of the global state st. The agents’ objective is to learn a joint policy π composed of agent-specific policies {πi}i∈N that maximizes the expected discounted cumulative reward, denoted 2as J(π). We assume agents to be homogeneous [4], and the joint policy is a collection of identical single-agent policies, i.e., πi = π ∀ i ∈ N .

Continual MARL: In Continual MARL (CMARL; [58, 75]), the multi-agent system undergoes a (possibly infinite) sequence of tasks T = M1, . . . , MK , where each Mk is a Dec-POMDP (Eq. 1) occurring for a fixed number of iterations. Tasks are drawn from a fixed, but unknown, task distribution [1]. The subscript k denotes a Dec-POMDP with potentially different components, i.e., different Nk, Pk, Rk.1 In the remainder, we denote tasks using the index k to distinguish them from the time step t within an RL episode. 

# 3 Continual Graph MARL 

In this section, we formalize the Continual Graph MARL (CGMARL) framework. First, we introduce the graph-based MARL problem, which corresponds to the single-task formulation. Then, after defining the CGMARL problem, we illustrate relevant task sequences. 

3.1 Graph-based MARL Task 

In a graph-based MARL problem, the Dec-POMDP in Eq. 1 is further equipped with a graph 

G = ⟨N , E, X, E⟩, with N and E representing the set of nodes (agents) and edges, respectively, each equipped with feature matrices X ∈ R|N |× dn (nodes) and E ∈ R|E|× de (edges). Importantly, the graph G represents the task structure and determines the environment dynamics, i.e., the transition function PG and/or reward function RG depend on G, providing local observations and rewards [69]. Thus, the Dec-POMDP tuple in Eq. 1 becomes: 

M = ⟨G , S, O, A, PG , RG , γ ⟩ (2) For example, in a traffic light network, rewards and next observations depend on incoming and outgoing vehicles at each intersection, i.e., the 1-hop neighbors of each node in G. Furthermore, agents can propagate messages through the network to coordinate effectively. We note that X and 

E may contain both static and dynamic features. In graph-based MARL, dynamic features are given by the agents’ observations, which change during the episode. Therefore, we use X and E

to denote the static graph features only, representing fixed node (e.g., agent identifier or type) and edge (e.g., sensor distances) features, respectively. We represent the edge set E with the adjacency matrix A ∈ { 0, 1}|N |×|N | , where aij = 1 if an edge connects node j to i. In the general formulation, 

A can vary within a task, i.e., the edge set E can change across steps (e.g., in navigation problems with distance-based connections). Specifically, edges are given by a state-dependent connectivity function φ : N × S → { 0, 1}|N |×|N | , which, at each step t, generates the adjacency matrix of the graph according to the current state st. In this case, the graph is characterized by the tuple 

G = ⟨N , φ, X, E⟩, where we remind that the non-static graph features consist of the observations. 

Comparison with Communication MARL: Graph-based MARL is fundamentally different from communication MARL [89]. In our formulation, the graph represents the problem structure and determines the dynamics. This is more similar to other graph-based MDPs, where, e.g., each node’s rewards and observations depend on its neighbors in the graph [30, 67]. Conversely, communication MARL focuses on developing effective communication methods across agents rather than using graphs to represent environment configurations with different dynamics. Specifically, the transition 

P and reward R functions do not depend on the graph structure G, which is used only for message passing. While communication methods can exchange messages on different graph structures, they assume the underlying task to be fixed and, as a result, fail to adapt to unseen problems. We empirically analyse these failure modes in Sec. 5.1.

Definition 3.1 (CGMARL Problem) . Let S, O, A, γ be the fixed components of the graph-based Dec-POMDP (Eq. 2) representing state, observation, and action spaces, and discount factor, respectively. Let G = ⟨N , φ, X, E⟩ be a graph, and let PG and RG be the graph-dependent transition and reward functions. Given a fixed but unknown distribution Z over graphs, the Continual Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (CGMARL) problem is defined as a sequence of tasks T = M1, . . . , MK ,where each task Mk is defined as in Eq. 2 and characterized by the graph Gk sampled from the distribution Z.This is the most general formulation of Continual Graph MARL. This class of problems can be motivated by considering a navigation task with distance-based connections. In this setting, each pair of agents is connected if their distance is within a specified threshold, such as the vision range [52]. Across tasks, the team composition Nk may vary depending on, e.g., the number of target positions to be covered or map size. Additionally, different scenarios may exhibit varying thresholds, thereby influencing the connectivity function φk that generates the communication structure (edge set) at each time step. Depending on the graph distribution Z, we can derive different classes of CGMARL problems, each with its relevance and potential applicability. In the following, we present formal definitions alongside real-world examples fitting the CGMARL framework in decreasing order of generality. Please refer to Fig. 1 for a visual summary. 

3.2.1 Topology Switch 

In the Topology Switch (TS) problem, connections (edge set Ek) remain fixed within a task, but may vary across different tasks. Formally, for each task Mk, the graph Gk sampled by the distribution Z

has a connectivity function φk that is state-independent, thus generating a fixed edge set Ek = φk(Nk).In other words, the adjacency matrix Ak remains fixed within the task Mk.

Definition 3.2 (TS Problem) . We call the Topology Switch (TS) problem a CGMARL problem where the task sequence T is generated under state-independent connectivity functions φ : N → {0, 1}|N |×|N | ; it follows that each task Mk of T is characterized by a graph Gk = ⟨N k, Ek, Xk, Ek⟩

with a static edge set Ek = φk(Nk).A graph-based control policy for efficient traffic light control [54, 19] is an example of a TS problem. In this setting, the same model could be deployed and updated on completely different network topologies, e.g., different cities or districts within a city. Importantly, the connections do not change within the same task. 43.2.2 Connectivity Switch 

The Connectivity Switch (CS) problem is a sub-case of the TS setting in which the node set remains fixed across tasks. In other words, in this CGMARL setting, we consider a static team of agents that cooperate to solve different tasks, each with its own communication structure and graph features. Therefore, the sampling process from Z corresponds to sampling a sequence of different adjacency matrices 2 and graph features given a fixed node set. 

Definition 3.3 (CS Problem) . We call the Connectivity Switch (CS) problem a CGMARL problem in which the task sequence TN is generated under a specific choice for the node set N ; it follows that each task Mk of TN is characterized by a graph Gk = ⟨N , Ek, Xk, Ek⟩, with Ek = φk(N ).The CS problem can be relevant, for example, in efficient power distribution management [81]. Here, the physical network representing the power grid consists of a fixed set of nodes (e.g., substations), while the connections may vary according to the current operational regime. Specifically, the network can transition between predefined edge configurations in response to external operational conditions (e.g., fault isolation or maintenance). 

3.2.3 Graph Features Switch 

When the topology (i.e., node and edge sets) remains fixed throughout the task sequence, static graph features can discriminate between different graphs (tasks). In the Graph Features Switch (GFS) problem, the graph distribution Z is a distribution over static graph features ( X and E). 

Definition 3.4 (GFS Problem) . We call the Graph Features Switch (GFS) problem a CGMARL problem in which the task sequence TN ,E is generated under a specific choice for the topology ( N and 

E = φ(N )); it follows that each task Mk of TN ,E is characterized by a graph Gk = ⟨N , E, Xk, Ek⟩.In this class of CGMARL problems, graph features play a critical role in tackling the task. For instance, consider a robotic agent where each joint (actuator) is represented as a node, and the limbs connecting them are the edges [77, 45, 79]. In scenarios where limb lengths depend on the task, it is essential to learn a policy that adapts to different configurations. For example, limbs may be lengthened or shortened in response to different obstacles or terrain. In this case, Ek can encode task-dependent distances between node pairs, allowing robots to operate effectively with longer or shorter limbs. Similarly, node features Xk can encode information on task-specific conditions, such as node-level body masses. Note that, unlike TS and CS sequences where the topology implicitly serves as the communication structure, in the GFS problem the features X and E characterizing the graph are explicitly used in decision-making.

Note that freezing the graph encoder before training provides node-level representations as random graph features [84, 51], which have been proven effective in CL problems on graphs [21]. Nonetheless, we argue that this option may hinder learning in complex graph-based MARL settings, especially if the GNN lacks sufficient expressive power. We compare FROG with this untrained variant in Sec. 6.2. 

# 6 Empirical Evaluation 

In this section, we present a series of experiments to empirically support our claims. Specifically, we have two main objectives: evaluate how standard CL methods perform in CGMARL problems (Obj. 1 ), and investigate whether FROG, i.e., a simple method designed to account for structural changes, can provide benefit in this setting ( Obj. 2 ), paving the way for novel CGMARL approaches. 

CGMARL Sequences: We consider multiple CGMARL sequences from our proposed GRAFO benchmark (please refer to App. C and App. E.2 for further details). Specifically, for the Topology Switch (TS) scenario (Sec. 3.2.1), we consider a sequence of 6 tasks, with 3 to 7 agents; the Connectivity Switch (CS) scenario (Sec. 3.2.2) is a sequence of 5 tasks, each with 6 agents; for the Graph Feature Switch (GFS) scenario (Sec. 3.2.3), we consider a sequence of 4 tasks, each with 4

agents. Furthermore, we analyze the impact of task order for the TS sequence in Sec. 6.2. Following related work [75], we consider the task-incremental learning paradigm with separate task heads and known task identities. We assess the impact of conditioning on task identities in App. D.4. 

Models: We employ InforMARL [52] as the single-task GNN-based policy, as it is a well-established model for graph-based navigation domains (more details in App. E.1). To address forgetting in the CGMARL sequence, we pair InforMARL with common CL approaches [75]. Specifically, we consider L2, where we apply an L2 penalty to the weights, Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC; [36]), and Memory Aware Synapses (MAS; [5]). For EWC and MAS, we consider two variants depending on how parameter importance is computed: the "multi" (M) versions aggregate the importance matrix from all tasks via summation, while the "online" (O) versions employ an online exponential-decay running average of the importance matrix from past tasks. We pair FROG with all the CL methods, and consider an additional Fine-Tuning (FT) baseline without any regularization. Hyperparameters for InforMARL and the CL methods are reported in App. E.4. 

Metrics: We evaluate the models using metrics from standard CL literature [78, 11, 73], i.e., Average Final Performance (AFP), Average Forgetting (AF), and Average Plasticity (AP). To do so, for each task, we measure the percentage of solved episodes during evaluation, where an episode is considered solved if the agents reach the target configuration within the time limit. 6.1 Main Results 

We report the main results on three different CGMARL scenarios in Fig. 3. Standard CL methods exhibit mixed performance across the various sequences, with none consistently outperforming the others. On average, EWC achieves better AFP than MAS and L2, but at the cost of plasticity loss (lower AP). Combined with the high variance, this suggests that standard CL methods may require additional mechanisms tailored for CGMARL to be more effective ( Obj. 1 ). Overall, compared with their counterparts that do not incorporate it, FROG consistently improves performance when combined with standard CL methods ( Obj. 2 ), and is less prone to forgetting (higher AF). The only exception is EWC on the TS sequence, where the standard method has a higher AF value. However, this can be explained by plasticity loss: as the lower AP metric highlights, the standard version fails to learn new tasks. This reduced learning capacity necessarily leads to less forgetting, since the more difficult tasks are not successfully learned in the first place and therefore cannot subsequently be forgotten. We hypothesize that this may be caused by using a single regularization coefficient for both the graph encoder and action module, and we investigate this behavior in App. D.2. Finally, empirical evidence shows that, on average, using frozen graph representations stabilizes learning.

This implies that the quality of the node representations generated throughout the CGMARL sequence depends on the first task encountered. To assess this behavior, we consider two variants of the TS sequence used in Sec. 6.1 with an incremental/decremental number of agents, and dub them TS (inc) and TS (dec), respectively. The results reported in Fig. 4 show that, on average, FROG achieves competitive performance against the standard counterpart. In particular, in TS (inc), the first task requires the lowest coordination degree ( 3 agents). This causes plasticity loss (lower AP) in FROG when paired with MAS and L2, while it still guarantees good performance when combined with EWC. Conversely, in TS (dec), results are similar to the standard TS sequence, favoring FROG and suggesting that the learned graph encoder is rich enough to perform well on the full sequence. This shows that the first task can have an impact on FROG, but despite that, it can still achieve competitive performance. Finally, this analysis reveals that task order in GRAFO can also impact learning in standard CL methods, which achieve, on average, lower AFP and AP in the TS (inc) sequence. 

# 7 Related Work 

Graph representations have been used in MARL to characterize the task structure in several settings. For example, many approaches [30, 67] consider problems where local (agent-specific) dynamics are conditioned on actions of each agent’s neighbors in the graph. Practical applications of graph-based MARL range from networked systems [42] to power grid control [26]. Communication MARL [89] emerged as an effective paradigm for addressing those tasks by improving agents’ coordination through communication. This is commonly achieved using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs; [25]) in decision-making to learn message-passing policies over the graph structure [34, 55, 52]. In Continual RL (CRL; [35, 2]), the agent aims to sequentially adapt to new tasks without deteriorating performance on previously learned ones. To prevent forgetting, CL methods such as replay-based mechanisms [13] or parameter regularizations [36] have been employed. The CRL problem has been extended to multi-agent systems [58, 75], but to the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that considers continual graph-based MARL settings. Related to our method, frozen modules or representations have been effectively used to mitigate forgetting in CRL [68, 61, 53, 29]. Please refer to App. B for an extended discussion. 

# 8 Conclusion and Future Work 

In this work, we introduced the Continual Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (CG-MARL) framework, which formalizes sequences of MARL tasks characterized by different struc-tures. Through this framework, we identified several real-world applications induced by different 9task (graph) distributions. Motivated by the failure modes of standard approaches in CGMARL, we proposed Frozen Graph Encoder (FROG), a simple approach that leverages frozen graph represen-tations to mitigate forgetting. Experiments on a novel CGMARL benchmark, named Graph-based Formation (GRAFO), validated FROG’s effectiveness when combined with standard CL methods. We foresee several possible directions for future theoretical and empirical research. First, a theoretical investigation of the failure modes of standard CL approaches in this setting could help design more effective CGMARL methods. Furthermore, considering off-policy MARL algorithms would pave the way for testing experience-based CL methods. Lastly, it would be interesting to develop other CGMARL evaluation benchmarks, possibly with different practical applications (e.g., power distribution management or traffic light control).

# B Extended Related Work 

Graph-based MARL and Communication Graph-based MARL formalizes settings where the environment dynamics depend on the underlying graph. This has been adopted across several settings, e.g., where the dynamics of each agent depend on its neighbors in the graph [30, 67]. The same graph possibly serves as the agent communication structure. Such problems have been extensively studied in networked systems [85]. For example, NeurComm [17] employs an effective communication protocol that prevents information loss during message aggregation. Conversely, BayesG [22] learns a context-aware interaction graph that reduces unnecessary message propagation. Other works address networked problems by either leveraging consensus-based mechanisms [64, 80] or incorporating relational information [40, 65]. Communication MARL [89] focuses on developing effective methods for improving agents’ coordination through communication. Pioneering methods [23, 74] focused on learning communication protocols end-to-end. Subsequent works [33, 72, 18, 31] employed message-broadcasting schemes that adaptively select communication channels. Other approaches employ GNNs in the decision-making process to learn message-passing policies [34, 55, 8, 46], possibly focusing on the zero-shot capabilities of the learned behaviors [3, 52]. Here, the communication topology is either given by the environment or learned end-to-end, and does not necessarily determine the environment dynamics. 

Continual RL In Continual RL (CRL; [35, 2]), the agent learns from a sequence of RL problems, aiming to effectively adapt to new tasks while retaining acquired knowledge from previously observed ones. CRL has been applied to different domains, including robotics [62] and medical imaging [87]. A common approach to address CRL problems is to leverage standard CL methods [58], such as replay-based mechanisms (e.g., AGEM; [13]), parameter regularizations such as EWC [36] or MAS [5], or parameter isolation (e.g., PackNet; [44]). The Continual MARL (CMARL; [75]) framework extends the CRL problem to systems of multiple cooperative agents, requiring novel methodologies tailored for this paradigm [83]. 

Frozen Representations Frozen representations have proved their effectiveness as both purely random features [59, 32, 66] and as pre-trained features [28, 38, 15]. In Reinforcement Learning (RL), random features have been used to improve exploration [9, 10], maintain accurate uncertainty estimates [57], or generate intrinsic learning signals [14]. In contrast, pre-trained representations have been successfully applied to different domains, such as visual RL [50, 43] or graph-based MARL [6]. Frozen representations have also been explored in Continual Learning problems [27, 17 39, 48, 63]. Notably, Donghi et al. [21] leverage randomized representations generated by different GNN backbones [84, 51] in CL problems on graphs [20]. In Continual RL, several works rely on frozen representations or modules [68, 61, 53, 29] to mitigate forgetting and facilitate learning by reducing the number of learnable parameters [86]. Nonetheless, to the best of our knowledge, no existing approaches have investigated frozen GNN-based encoders for graph-based CMARL settings. 

# C Graph-based Formation (GRAFO) Benchmark 

In this section, we present our novel graph-based MARL benchmark, named Graph-based Formation (GRAFO). Building on other formation environments [3], GRAFO is a collection of graph-based MARL tasks in which the agents’ objective is to navigate to a target configuration that resembles the input graph. Notably, such configurations can be generated arbitrarily, making GRAFO a suitable benchmark for CGMARL problems. 

C.1 Single-Task Description 

In GRAFO, the goal of the agents is to navigate to a partially unknown target configuration, which depends on the input graph. A single task configuration is specified by: • the adjacency matrix A of the graph, which influences the environment dynamics and possibly serves as the communication structure (Sec. 2). • a tuple of target distances g = ( g1, . . . , gN ), indicating the relative position shifts with respect to the known landmark in the 2-dimensional plane; a target distance gi = (0 , 0) 

corresponds to the reference node, i.e., the node assigned to the known landmark. • possibly, a set of node indices K ⊂ N that are structurally equivalent in the graph. • optionally, static graph (node and edge) features X and E (please refer to Sec. 3.1). When two agents are structurally equivalent (i.e., symmetric under a graph automorphism), their target positions are assigned at each step to candidate landmarks using a linear sum assignment problem that minimizes the total distance. In the example, agents 3 and 4 can be swapped without altering the graph topology, and therefore, they belong to the set K of structurally equivalent nodes. Lastly, the position of the known landmark is randomly initialized at the beginning of each episode, and other target positions are assigned using g accordingly.

D.2 Impact of Regularization Coefficients 

To investigate the results achieved by EWC (M) in Fig. 3, we perform an additional experiment on the TS sequence to assess the impact of using two separate regularization coefficients. Specifically, we set the coefficient for the graph encoder λψ = 10 10 (as in Fig. 3), which is the best value achieved during parameter tuning (refer to App. E.4.2). In contrast, we vary the coefficient λμ for the action prediction module from 10 4 to 10 12 , and evaluate each configuration (λψ , λ μ) on the TS sequence. We note that, when λμ = 10 10 = λψ (pink box), the results are those reported in Fig. 3. Our hypothesis to explain the low plasticity of EWC (M) in Fig. 3 is that the graph encoder requires a much stronger regularization coefficient than the action prediction module, since EWC is not designed to preserve structural knowledge. Therefore, despite being a single value in practice, these coefficients lie on two different scales: large coefficients for the action module (large λμ) can deteriorate learning new tasks, whereas small coefficients for the GNN (small λψ ) cannot preserve past structural information. The results in Fig. 6 support this hypothesis: the best tradeoff is achieved with λμ = 10 6, which is much lower than the value associated with the graph encoder ( λψ = 10 10 ). Specifically, smaller regularization coefficients for the action module increase plasticity, but may suffer from forgetting for very low values (in this case, λμ = 10 4). Conversely, strong regularization (large λμ) causes plasticity loss, i.e., the model struggles to learn new tasks. D.3 Comparison with Untrained FROG 

As stated in Sec. 5.2, FROG relies on a frozen graph backbone to mitigate forgetting arising from structural changes. To achieve this, a possible design choice consists of freezing the graph encoder 

before learning the first task, i.e., after initializing the network. This is more general, as it does not adapt the GNN to a specific graph, and has proven to be effective in continual graph learning problems [21]. Nonetheless, relying on random graph features may not provide enough expressiveness to solve MARL tasks, as agents must explore possibly large state spaces using random signals only. This may hinder learning, and for this reason, the experiments reported in Sec. 6 are done by freezing the graph encoder after learning the first task. This enables learning an encoder that generates features that may also be beneficial for solving future tasks. In this section, we compare FROG against the untrained variant (FROG (RND)), where the graph encoder is frozen at initialization, and the generated graph features are random. Results in Fig. 7 show that, for all CL models, FROG (RND) struggles to learn most tasks (low AP values), thus consistently achieving worse performance (AFP) compared to FROG.
