Organizations have long sought to make workers’ expertise reusable by others. Agentic AI changes the nature of such reuse by enabling AI systems to act on workers’ knowledge with limited human involvement. This raises the question of how this shift shapes workers’ willingness to share their expertise. We conducted an experiment with knowledge workers who created materials incorporating domain knowledge and decided whether to authorize human or AI reuse. We find that AI and human reuse differ primarily in whether 

workers choose to share their knowledge, rather than in what they choose to share. Participants’ reasoning shifts from prosocial considerations when sharing with humans toward concerns about loss of control, replacement, and downstream governance when sharing with AI. These findings suggest that AI reuse may intensify tensions between organizational knowledge reuse and contributors’ interests. We discuss implications for workplace AI and knowledge management policies that preserve workers’ rights and agency. Organizations have therefore relied on documentation, training, mentoring, and knowledge-management systems to preserve workers’ expertise and enable its reuse [4, 14]. These practices help make individual expertise available to colleagues and future employees, supporting the continuity and development of organizational knowledge. Generative and agentic AI introduce new possibilities and challenges for how organizations capture and reuse this expertise [6, 65]. Workers’ task outputs, reasoning, and workplace interactions can serve as resources for AI systems to perform subsequent tasks. For example, emerging systems package workers’ practices and decision heuristics into reusable “agent skills” that AI systems can draw on when performing similar work [37, 66]. Consider a worker whose explanation of a difficult workplace decision is incorporated into such a skill and applied without their continued involvement. The worker might welcome its potential to help others handle similar situations more efficiently, yet hesitate to include sensitive conversations or allow the resulting skill to be applied in unfamiliar contexts. This tension raises a question about how knowledge workers perceive and approach sharing their expertise for AI reuse. 

Understanding such sharing requires attention to both the contexts in which knowledge is reused and the social relationships through which it is contributed. Knowledge-reuse research shows that knowledge can be difficult to interpret or apply when producers and reusers do not share the same context [41]. For contributors, this raises questions about whether others will use their knowledge appropriately and what they are willing to make available for reuse. Social-exchange perspectives further explain how contributions are shaped by anticipated reciprocity, recognition, and personal costs [7, 26]. Together, these perspectives suggest that willingness to share depends on both the anticipated uses of knowledge and the balance between collective benefits and individual costs [10]. AI reuse may change how workers weigh these considerations when deciding whether to share their knowledge. Even workers who value AI and wish to improve its performance [17] may question whether it can apply their knowledge with appropriate sensitivity to context [11, 39]. Contributing expertise may also create tension between improving workplace efficiency [17] and protecting one’s professional role [13]. These questions are becoming increasingly consequential as organizations use workers’ activities and expertise to develop AI capabilities. For example, employee backlash against Meta’s initiative to collect work activity for AI training illustrates how such efforts can become contested. 1 Understanding workers’ perspectives is therefore relevant both to supporting knowledge contribution and to establishing boundaries that workers consider acceptable. Research on AI in organizational knowledge management has largely focused on how AI can support the capture and reuse of workers’ expertise [6, 65, 66]. A smaller body of emerging work has begun to examine workers’ perspectives on contributing knowledge to AI, identifying motivations such as learning opportunities [50] and modeling how giving workers collective ownership of their data can encourage knowledge sharing [15]. However, less is understood about how workers translate these considerations into explicit authorization decisions over concrete materials derived from their own work: whether they permit reuse, which materials they release, and what conditions they expect to govern subsequent use. This leads to our central question: How do knowledge workers decide whether and what to share with AI systems, and how do they understand the consequences and conditions of that sharing? 

To investigate the question, we conducted a mixed-methods study in which 61 full-time professionals completed simulated workplace tasks. Participants were randomly assigned to share their knowledge with either AI systems or human professionals as the intended recipient, while the task structure and knowledge-extraction process remained consistent across conditions. They decided whether to authorize reuse and, if so, which categories of materials to release. Our findings show that participants were less likely to authorize organizational knowledge reuse by AI systems than by human professionals. At the category level, differences in release rates between conditions were not statistically significant. Among participants who authorized AI reuse, conversations were less often shared than task outputs or written reasoning. Qualitative responses linked these sharing boundaries to concerns about sensitive information and contextual interpretation. Continued control and credit were also expected to increase willingness to share more with AI than with humans, while open-ended responses revealed varied preferences for acknowledgment and anonymity. This work aims to make three contributions to HCI: 

• We show that knowledge workers are less likely to authorize reuse of their expertise by AI systems than by human professionals. This extends knowledge-sharing research to AI reuse by providing empirical evidence of how workers authorize the use of materials derived from their own work. 

• We provide suggestions for organizations seeking to encourage knowledge sharing with AI. By identifying work-ers’ expectations for recognition and continued control, we highlight ways to support voluntary participation while accommodating different preferences for credit and anonymity. 

• We identify implications for system design and organizational governance that protect workers’ interests as their expertise becomes a resource for AI capabilities. Drawing on concerns about expert influence, interpretive control, and professional replacement, we highlight the need for ongoing contributor agency and safeguards against adverse downstream uses. 

2 Related Work 2.1 Organizational Knowledge Management 

Organizations have long sought to preserve and transfer workers’ knowledge across individuals, teams, and time, as organizational knowledge is a critical driver of performance and competitive advantage in the knowledge economy [61]. When experienced employees change roles or leave, organizations risk losing not only documents and formal procedures, but also the judgment required to recognize exceptions, navigate relationships, and adapt routines to particular situations. Knowledge management addresses this problem by examining how organizational knowledge is created, retained, transferred, and reused [4, 16]. A central strategy is knowledge codification: representing workers’ experience and expertise in forms that can be stored and made available beyond the situations in which they were originally developed [54]. Yet describing work is not the same as knowing how to perform it [45, 48]. Ryle’s distinction between “knowing-that” 

and “knowing-how” suggests that workers may know how to perform a task without being able to state everything their performance involves [51]. Research on situated learning further shows that such knowledge is embedded in 4 Tianqi Song, Zicheng Zhu, Hancheng Cao, Yi-Chieh Lee practices, tools, relationships, and organizational settings [8, 33]. HCI and CSCW research further brings a human-centered perspective to knowledge-sharing systems, by examining how people work, communicate, and develop shared understanding in practice, and how repositories and communication tools support these activities [1, 53]. Taken together, codification can make knowledge more portable, but systems must also support access to the context and expertise needed to use it. Moreover, whether codified knowledge can be successfully reused also depends on who produced it, who draws on it, and the relationship between their work contexts. Markus’s knowledge-reuse framework distinguishes situations in which people reuse knowledge from their own shared work, draw on others’ similar experiences, seek expertise they lack, or repurpose knowledge for new uses [41, 42]. These situations create different needs for explanation and contextual support, suggesting that making knowledge available does not by itself ensure that others can apply it appropriately. 

2.2 Knowledge Contribution to AI in Organizations 

Generative AI is increasingly used to support workplace tasks and improve productivity [9, 46], changing how knowledge is shared and used at work. Researchers have examined how its presence shapes knowledge sharing and hiding among employees [36, 38, 64], as well as how workers learn from AI systems and apply what they learn [55, 56]. Knowledge also flows in the other direction: workers’ own expertise can become a resource for AI. Socially interactive agents and AI expert twins, for example, elicit and preserve knowledge grounded in professional experience [6, 65]. Once captured, this knowledge can guide AI systems in carrying out subsequent tasks. Agent skills provide one way to put this knowledge into practice by bringing together instructions, examples, scripts, and resources for recurring workflows [35, 37]. AI companies such as OpenAI [47], Anthropic [3], and Google [20] offer tools and guidance for creating such skills, helping users turn established ways of working into reusable instructions for AI. These instructions can describe general procedures, but they can also reflect how a particular worker approaches a task. COLLEAGUE.SKILL, for example, draws on an individual’s workplace materials to distill their practices and decision heuristics into an executable skill [66]. A worker’s expertise may therefore continue to guide tasks without their continued involvement, raising the question of whether they are willing to share what they know for this kind of reuse. While much of the work on knowledge contribution to AI focuses on building systems that capture and apply expertise, less attention has been paid to how workers decide whether to make their knowledge available for such use. A few studies examine this process from workers’ perspectives. For example, Renggli and Wäfler identify motivations and barriers to providing knowledge to AI through exploratory interviews [50]. Cullen et al. combine worker surveys with a field experiment to document gaps between workers’ expertise and what organizations have captured, and examine how career incentives affect knowledge provision for AI training [15]. While these studies show how workers’ interests and circumstances shape their contributions, they leave open how workers translate their willingness to contribute into authorization decisions over specific materials from their own work. A worker may be willing to share a task output while withholding the reasoning or conversations behind it. Thus, the question is both whether workers permit reuse and what they agree to release, as well as how these decisions differ when the intended reuser is an AI system rather than another professional. 

2.3 Knowledge Sharing and Worker Agency 

2.3.1 Knowledge Contribution as a Social Dilemma. Although organizations benefit when workers codify and share what they know, doing so requires individual time and effort, while the resulting benefits may accrue mainly to colleagues or How Knowledge Workers Share Their Expertise with AI Systems 5the organization. Knowledge sharing has therefore been described as a social dilemma : collective performance depends on individual contributions that may not serve each worker’s immediate interests [10]. Prior research explains this decision through both social and individual considerations [26, 62]. For example, social-exchange theory suggests that people contribute when they expect benefits such as reciprocity, reputation, stronger relationships, or the satisfaction of helping others [26]. Studies of electronic knowledge repositories similarly identify enjoyment in helping, knowledge self-efficacy, organizational support, and perceived personal costs as important predictors of contribution [7, 24, 26]. From this perspective, knowledge sharing is not simply a matter of possessing useful information, but also on how workers weigh their contributions against benefits to others and expected returns. AI reuse brings this social dilemma into focus by potentially changing who benefits from workers’ knowledge and what contributors receive in return. 

2.3.2 Worker Agency in Knowledge Contribution to AI. HCI research has increasingly examined how workplace AI reshapes workers’ participation in and relationship to work, including workers’ sense of inclusion, control, and ownership in AI-mediated communication; meaningfulness and accountability in human–AI collaboration; and the interpersonal and organizational implications of workplace AI agents [23, 25, 52]. Much of this work takes AI capabilities as an existing feature of the workplace. A related but less examined question concerns how those capabilities are created, particularly when they are built from workers’ knowledge. This shifts attention to workers’ perceived rights and control over whether and how their expertise is used. 

2.4 Research Questions 

This study seeks to understand how knowledge workers perceive and approach sharing their expertise for AI reuse. Prior research shows how AI systems can capture workers’ expertise and turn it into reusable capabilities [6, 65, 66]. Yet making expertise reusable also requires understanding workers’ willingness to contribute, including what they expect to gain or lose through sharing [15, 26, 50]. When their knowledge can be used beyond their direct involvement, questions of control and ownership discussed in workplace AI research also become relevant [25]. Together, these perspectives guide our examination of what workers agree to share, how they understand the potential consequences, and what conditions would make them more willing to contribute. We address these questions through three research questions: 

RQ1 Do knowledge workers authorize AI systems to reuse their professional knowledge, and what do they agree to share? This question examines both whether workers permit reuse and the boundaries they place on their contributions. By asking workers to review materials derived from their own work, we examine their choices about what to release rather than willingness to contribute in general. 

RQ2 What benefits and risks do knowledge workers anticipate when sharing their professional knowledge with AI systems? This question examines how workers understand what may happen once their knowledge is shared. Building on research on knowledge contribution and reuse [26, 41], we consider both the benefits of making expertise available to others and concerns about its subsequent use and consequences for the contributor.

3 Methods 

We conducted a mixed-methods study combining a between-subjects experiment, quantitative survey measures, and qualitative open-ended questions. The study received approval from our university’s ethics review committee before data collection began. 

3.1 Task Setup 

Work simulations have been used in organizational behavior research [2, 59, 63] and in assessment contexts, such as e-tray exercises 2, to elicit job-relevant behavior and make workers’ application of domain knowledge observable under controlled conditions. We adapted this approach to provide participants with a concrete, simulated work experience through which they produced task-grounded material that could subsequently be transformed into an AI agent skill. This design allowed us to examine sharing decisions grounded in participants’ own work rather than in an abstract or hypothetical skill. 

3.1.1 Occupational domains selection. Following prior experimental research on workplace AI use [46, 49], we chose four white-collar occupational domains, including marketing , sales , human resources , and management , because workers in these roles are likely to encounter AI systems that can reuse or act on professional knowledge. 3 Moreover, these domains involve judgment-oriented tasks that could be realistically simulated within the study’s time constraints. 

3.1.2 Task selection and scenario development. To extract the skill with domain-knowledge, we designed the specific task and scenario differed by occupation. 3.2 Study Procedure 

The study procedure is illustrated in Figure 3. Participants first completed an initial survey assessing their background, work experience, baseline attitudes toward AI, and general attitudes toward knowledge sharing. Participants were then randomly assigned to either the Human or AI recipient condition and completed an occupational simulation. After completing the simulation, participants reviewed and corrected a preview of the knowledge extracted from their responses. Next, participants made authorization decisions about whether to share the knowledge and, if so, how much and which types to share with their assigned recipient—either human professionals or AI systems . Finally, participants completed a post-survey assessing their perceptions of the task, the extracted knowledge, and the assigned recipient, followed by a debriefing. 

3.3 System Design 

The study was administered through a custom-built web platform that participants accessed using a desktop or laptop computer. After completing onboarding and reading the task instructions, participants proceeded through three main stages, as illustrated in Figure 2. First, in the workspace simulation (Figure 2a), participants reviewed occupation-specific source materials and completed the assigned workplace task. Second, they produced three forms of task artifacts (Figure 2b): an actionable task output, an explanation of their underlying reasoning, and conversations with simulated coworkers. Third, the platform extracted reusable knowledge points from these artifacts, which participants reviewed How Knowledge Workers Share Their Expertise with AI Systems 9and corrected (Figure 2c). After completing this workflow, participants made their knowledge-sharing decisions and received a confirmation of their selections. 

3.3.1 Frontend, Backend, and Database. The platform’s frontend was implemented using React and built with Vite. Participant progress was automatically synchronized with a Supabase database 6, while local browser storage enabled recovery from accidental interruptions. For each session, the system recorded participants’ written outputs, conversation transcripts, extracted knowledge and subsequent revisions, sharing decisions, and relevant interaction timestamps. Server-side APIs handled database operations and all LLM requests. 

3.3.2 LLM Integration. The system used gpt-5.4-mini via the OpenAI Responses API for two primary functions: (1) generating responses from simulated workplace coworkers (as shown in Figure 2, b) and (2) extracting and synthesizing professional knowledge from participants’ work products, reasoning, and conversations (as shown in Figure 2, c). Prompts for the simulated coworkers specified each counterpart’s role, interaction goals, and behavioral constraints. Prompts for knowledge extraction instructed the model to identify discrete knowledge points and classify each as 

factual , procedural , or conditional knowledge .We iteratively tested and refined both prompt sets during system development. As a check on the quality and comparability of the knowledge produced by the extraction prompts, the system presented the extracted points in an editable skill preview and allowed participants to revise or remove individual points. Participants generally made few revisions to the extracted knowledge points. Content-review behavior by condition is reported in Appendix Table 8. Illustrative examples of the prompts are provided in Supplementary Materials, Section 3. 

3.4 Participants 

Participants were recruited through CloudResearch 7. Our target population comprised college-educated, full-time professionals working in one of four target white-collar occupational groups: human resources, people management, sales, or marketing. The study was expected to take approximately one hour, and participants who completed the main study received a base payment of US$10. When recruiting participants, we used a two-stage screening process, because CloudResearch’s platform filters did not capture all of our study-specific eligibility criteria. First, we used CloudResearch’s platform filters to restrict recruitment to participants who met these broad education and employment criteria. Second, eligible participants completed a prescreening survey assessing three study-specific criteria: (1) current employment in one of four target occupational groups; (2) prior experience performing workplace tasks similar to those used in our study; and (3) prior awareness of AI. Because participants were asked to evaluate an explicitly AI-enabled system, we required that they had heard of AI before the study. This minimal familiarity criterion follows prior HCI research [27]. Data collection took place from June to July 2026. Of the 113 participants who completed the prescreening survey, 65 met all eligibility criteria. Of these, 64 entered the main study and were assigned evenly to the Human and AI recipient conditions ( 𝑛 = 32 per condition). Three participants were subsequently excluded for failing attention checks that instructed them to select a specific response option: one from the Human condition and two from the AI condition. The final analytic sample therefore comprised 61 participants (Human: 𝑛 = 31; AI: 𝑛 = 30). Recruitment remained open throughout the planned data-collection period but was constrained by the occupation- and task-experience-specific eligibility criteria, which limited the pool of qualified participants.

3.5.2 Perceptions of Knowledge Sharing (RQ2). We measured participants’ anticipated consequences of knowledge sharing using six closed-ended items informed by research on knowledge reuse [41] and social exchange [26]. All items followed the stem, “Sharing my knowledge with the repository would . . . ” The six anticipated consequences were (1) loss of control over how one’s knowledge is interpreted, (2) reuse without the necessary context, (3) reduced influence derived from one’s expertise, (4) reduced uniqueness of one’s expertise, (5) helping others handle similar tasks, and (6) preserving professional knowledge. These consequences represented three broader categories: knowledge-use risks [41], loss of knowledge power [26], and collective benefits [26]. The corresponding open-ended question asked participants which anticipated benefits, risks, or other consequences most influenced their authorization decisions and why. 

3.5.3 Governance Preferences for Knowledge Sharing (RQ3). We measured the conditions under which participants would be more willing to contribute their knowledge using seven items following the stem, “I would be more willing to contribute if . . . ” The items captured two complementary sets of conditions. The first four items were adapted from the three-C framework for governing data contributions involving intellectual property [32, 40]. As Kyi et al. emphasize, however, the three Cs do not encompass all governance arrangements workers may value; consent, in particular, 12 Tianqi Song, Zicheng Zhu, Hancheng Cao, Yi-Chieh Lee should be an ongoing process rather than a one-time decision. We therefore added control as a fourth principle to capture contributors’ continuing authority over their knowledge after sharing it. Specifically, participants rated whether they would be more willing to contribute if (1) they could give consent for specific people or groups to use their knowledge for specific purposes (consent); (2) review or correct the contributed knowledge, restrict its use, or withdraw their authorization (control); (3) receive credit when their knowledge was reused (credit); or (4) receive payment for contributing (compensation). The remaining three items addressed workplace-specific concerns associated with AI use in employment and worker management [19]. These items assessed (5) protections against using contributed knowledge for performance evaluation, (6) requirements for qualified professional oversight of high-stakes uses, and (7) protections against using contributed knowledge as grounds for replacing workers. The open-ended question asked participants to identify the governance rule or safeguard that mattered most to their willingness to contribute and explain why. 

3.5.4 Additional measures and data-quality checks. Following previous understanding of potentail confouding variables of knowledge shairng [12, 26, 57], we collected several potential measures to check whether the participants two conditions differed on these measures that might affect the dependent variables. These included knowledge (1) self-efficacy, (2) enjoyment in helping others, (3) generalized trust, (4) perceived organizational norms supporting knowledge sharing, and (5) organizational identification. All measures were collected in the pre-study survey and were used to characterize the sample and assess baseline balance. To support data quality, we administered a comprehension check before the task. Participants selected what they would do next from four response options. To proceed, they had to correctly identify that they would complete a workplace task in a simulated setting. We also included attention checks that instructed participants to select a specific response option. To assess potential demand characteristics [43], we administered a suspicion probe after the task, asking participants to describe what they believed the study was intended to investigate. Participants generally described the study as examining how workplace professionals handle job-relevant tasks, suggesting that its specific focus on knowledge sharing with AI was not apparent to most participants during the simulation. Full measures are provided in Supplementary Materials, Section 2. 

3.6 Analysis 

Our measures produced four response formats: 1–7 rating-scale responses, binary authorization decisions, checkbox selections, and open-ended responses. We analyzed each format using the procedures described below and integrated the quantitative and qualitative findings when interpreting each research question. All inferential tests were two-sided, with 𝛼 = .05. We report effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals alongside 𝑝 -values where applicable.

Because nearly all outcomes departed from normality, we compared the two independent recipient conditions using two-sided Mann–Whitney 𝑈 tests. 3.6.2 Binary Authorization Decisions. Each overall authorization decision was coded as 0 ( did not authorize ) or 1 (authorized ). We compared authorization rates between the two recipient conditions using two-sided Fisher’s exact tests because some expected cell counts were small. 

3.6.3 Checkbox Selections. We analyzed category selections at two levels: across the full sample and among participants who authorized organizational reuse. First, in the full sample, each category was coded as 1 if selected and 0 otherwise, with participants who declined overall authorization coded as 0 for all categories. We compared authorization rates for each category between the AI condition and the human baseline using two-sided Fisher’s exact tests. We also counted the categories released separately for written materials and extracted knowledge, yielding two counts ranging from 0 to 3 for each participant. We compared each count distribution between the AI condition and the human baseline using Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact tests. Second, among participants who authorized organizational reuse, we examined whether release rates varied across categories within each recipient condition. We conducted separate Cochran’s 𝑄 tests for the three participant-authored categories and the three extracted-knowledge categories. Significant omnibus tests were followed by pairwise exact McNemar tests, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction applied across the three comparisons within each tested category group. 

3.6.4 Open-Ended Responses. We analyzed responses to the three open-ended questions using reflexive thematic analysis. Two authors independently familiarized themselves with the responses and generated preliminary codes. They then met regularly to compare interpretations and collaboratively develop and refine the coding structure and candidate themes. Differences in coding were treated as opportunities for reflexive discussion rather than as errors requiring inter-rater agreement [58]. The authors iteratively reviewed the candidate themes against both the coded extracts and the full dataset before defining and naming the final themes.

4.2 Perceived Consequences of Skill-Sharing (RQ2) 

To address RQ2, we examined participants’ ratings of the consequences of sharing knowledge with AI systems or human professionals, summarized in Figure 8, alongside their open-ended responses about perceived benefits, risks, and concerns. We organize the quantitative and qualitative findings around three themes below. Full item-level statistics are reported in Appendix A.2.2. 

4.2.1 Greater Concern About Loss of Expert Influence When Sharing with AI. The largest difference in mean ratings concerned the perceived loss of expert influence when sharing with AI, while qualitative responses highlighted related concerns about professional replacement. In the survey, participants expected sharing with AI systems to weaken the influence associated with their expertise more than sharing with human professionals. In the open-ended responses, concerns about professional replacement were concentrated in the AI condition: five participants raised the issue (16.7%), compared with only one participant in the Human condition (3.2%). These participants worried that contributing their expertise could help build a system that would subsequently replace or evaluate them. For example, P41 (AI condition) stated, “I would be more concerned about it (sharing with AI)... if it ends up replacing you because you have trained AI to replace your functions.” These concerns complemented the quantitative finding about reduced expert influence by highlighting possible employment consequences of contributing expertise. 

4.2.2 Shared Decontextualization Concerns but Different Interpretive Challenges. Participants expressed concerns about how their shared knowledge would be interpreted and used outside its original context. Quantitatively, participants expected less control over how their knowledge would be interpreted when sharing with AI systems (𝑀 = 4.70,

In the open-ended responses, decontextualization was the most common concern in both conditions, raised by 16 participants (AI: 𝑛 = 7, 23.3%; Human: 𝑛 = 9, 29.0%). Participants’ explanations nevertheless highlighted a distinction How Knowledge Workers Share Their Expertise with AI Systems 17 between having sufficient contextual information and being able to interpret it appropriately. In the AI condition, many participants questioned AI systems’ capacity for contextual judgment itself . For example, P27 described the extracted skills as suitable for “simple, straight forward conversations, but not anything that requires interpreting nuance” .

P37 emphasized that workplace situations could involve feelings and emotions, stating that “AI systems do not know empathy or compassion” and that more complex cases should be handled by humans. In the Human condition, the responses instead emphasized gaps in the information available to recipients and identified additional context as a way to support appropriate interpretation. For example, P29 noted that the context needed to understand an employee rating “may not be fully expressed in the knowledge that is shared” , while P57 requested “full guidelines and context regarding usage” . These responses suggest that, although decontextualization concerned participants in both conditions, 

concerns about AI reuse extended beyond the availability of context to whether the system could make appropriate judgments using it . This distinction helps contextualize the higher ratings of lost interpretive control in the AI condition. 

4.2.3 Prosocial Motivations for AI Sharing Are Often Conditional. Participants in both conditions generally endorsed the potential of sharing to help others handle similar tasks and preserve professional knowledge. Survey ratings of these two benefits did not differ significantly between conditions. However, the open-ended responses revealed differences in how participants connected these benefits to their willingness to contribute. Benefits to others were mentioned more frequently in the human condition ( 𝑛 = 16, 51.6%) than in the AI condition (𝑛 = 8, 26.7%). Human-condition participants often described helping other professionals as a direct reason to share. 

P10 explained, “if my method of negotiation helps someone else, I’m happy to provide pointers [...] there’s no reason to gatekeep my sales tactics” . In the AI condition, recognizing the benefits of knowledge sharing did not necessarily translate into willingness to share through AI. For example, P27 stated, “I like being able to share my knowledge, experience, and advice with coworkers or trainees, but I’m hesitant to share with AI tools.” Among the eight AI-condition participants who mentioned prosocial benefits, five explicitly endorsed the value of sharing through AI. Their accounts reflected 

a reciprocal view in which benefiting from others’ knowledge through AI motivated them to contribute their own . For example, P36 linked their contribution to the value they received from AI, explaining, “I find AI to be extremely useful when I deploy it at work and it is built on repository information gathered from various sources in the responses it provides to me.” They added, “I felt that everything I provided would be useful so I had no issue with setting boundaries” . Together, these responses suggest that prosocial motivations extended to AI-mediated sharing, but were less frequently expressed and often accompanied by reservations or conditions .

Together, these findings suggest that sharing expertise for AI reuse involves more than making knowledge available: workers also consider what its reuse may mean for them and what say they retain over its subsequent use. We discuss the implications for understanding knowledge contribution and designing workplace AI systems that support workers’ interests and control. 

5.1 Boundary Condition of Knowledge-Reuse Authorization for AI 

Organizations are increasingly seeking to convert workers’ situated expertise into reusable AI capabilities through agent-based knowledge-capture systems [65, 66]. These developments raise a question not fully addressed by conventional accounts of organizational knowledge sharing: does the intended recipient of workers’ knowledge matter? Our findings suggest that it does. Participants were less willing to authorize AI systems than human professionals to reuse their materials in both organizational and public settings (RQ1). However, among participants who authorized reuse, recipient type did not reliably predict which categories of material they released. The clearest recipient difference therefore emerged in whether reuse was permitted, rather than in fine-grained category selection after authorization. Participants’ accounts help interpret this pattern, revealing persistent concerns about decontextualization alongside heightened concerns about the personal consequences of contributing knowledge to AI. 

5.1.1 Decontextualization as a Persistent Knowledge-Reuse Problem. Some perceived challenges of knowledge reuse were shared across human and AI recipients, with decontextualization emerging as the most common concern in open-ended responses in both conditions (RQ2). Participants worried that knowledge could become detached from the circumstances that made it valid and subsequently be misunderstood or misapplied. This concern is consistent with 

knowledge-reuse theory , which emphasizes the difficulty of transferring knowledge when producers and reusers do not share the same context [41]. Our findings suggest that decontextualization is not unique to AI reuse; rather, this problem remains fundamental whenever situated professional judgment is made reusable beyond its original setting. 20 Tianqi Song, Zicheng Zhu, Hancheng Cao, Yi-Chieh Lee

5.1.2 Extending Social Exchange Theory to Knowledge Contributions to AI. Beyond the shared concern about decon-textualization, participants invoked different considerations when explaining their decisions. Participants in the AI condition more frequently expressed professional-replacement concerns, whereas those in the Human condition more frequently invoked prosocial motivations. AI-condition accounts also expressed prosocial motivations, often alongside reservations or a reciprocal understanding of contribution (RQ2). This contrast extends a social-exchange perspective 

on knowledge contribution to AI reuse [26]. Our findings suggest that AI reuse does not displace the considerations underlying human knowledge sharing, but may change their relative importance. Workers may recognize the collective benefits of contributing while placing greater weight on potential losses of professional value, influence, or control. AI reuse may therefore intensify an existing tension in organizational knowledge sharing, whereby contributions that benefit others and the organization may also impose costs on the individuals who provide them. Our findings also contribute empirical evidence to emerging accounts of workers’ “knowledge supply” to AI, which conceptualize employee expertise as an adjustable organizational input shaped by career incentives and individual or collective ownership arrangements [15]. Whereas this work explains variation in how much workers contribute within AI knowledge-capture programs, our study compares otherwise matched human and AI recipients. This comparison shows that knowledge contribution cannot be understood only as variation in the quantity workers supply to AI. The intended recipient itself shaped authorization, while recipient type did not reliably shape category composition among How Knowledge Workers Share Their Expertise with AI Systems 21 those who granted permission. Taken together, our findings suggest that knowledge sharing with AI carries forward the contextual and social-exchange concerns of sharing among humans, while potentially intensifying tensions between the collective benefits of reuse and contributors’ own interests. 

5.2 Design Implication: Designing Provenance-Aware and Controllable Knowledge Reuse 

Based on our findings, we identify two implications for designing AI-mediated workplace knowledge reuse. Both concern how organizations can preserve the value of reusable knowledge without treating workers’ situated expertise as an unrestricted organizational input. 

5.2.1 Preserving Context While Enabling Knowledge Reuse. Knowledge management and CSCW research has long shown that expertise cannot simply be detached from its producer and deposited in a repository: successful reuse depends on the reuser, purpose, and context in which knowledge is applied [1, 53]. Our findings extend this problem to AI. Recipient type primarily shaped whether participants authorized reuse (RQ1), but did not reliably predict which categories authorizers released. Nevertheless, organizational authorizers released workplace conversations less often than task outputs or written reasoning, and decontextualization was the most common unprompted concern in both conditions (RQ2, RQ3). AI knowledge repositories should therefore make the boundaries of reuse explicit, including the source material, whether it is retained as a raw record or transformed into an executable skill, its intended recipient and destination, and its authorized purpose. This is especially important for agentic systems, whose value comes partly from generalizing and recombining knowledge across tasks [37, 66]. The design goal should not be to prohibit generalization, but to distinguish adaptation within an approved domain from expansion to new purposes, populations, or consequential decisions. 

5.2.2 Making Knowledge Reuse Traceable and Contestable. Participants expressed greater concern about losing control over how their knowledge would be interpreted or applied in the AI condition (RQ2), and their qualitative accounts called for notification, revocable permission, limits on reuse, and responsibility for downstream decisions (RQ3). Consistent with prior HCI work emphasizing control and ownership in AI-mediated workplace communication [25], these findings suggest that transparency should support continuing influence rather than merely disclose that reuse has occurred. Systems should allow contributors to inspect where their knowledge is used, correct or restrict its representation, and prospectively withdraw it as deployment contexts change. Attribution can form part of this infrastructure. Prior HCI work has proposed richer attribution statements that distinguish contribution type, amount, initiative, and human review in human–AI co-creation [22]. In our setting, visible attribution was expected to increase willingness more strongly in the AI condition (RQ3), but participants also noted that identity disclosure could create social exposure and sometimes preferred anonymity or pseudonymity. Systems should therefore separate internal provenance for auditing and accountability, identity disclosure to downstream users, and credit or rewards. 

5.3 Policy Implication: Institutional Governance for AI Knowledge Management 

Beyond interface design, organizations need governance arrangements that specify who has the authority to define, modify, and enforce the conditions of knowledge reuse. Our study allowed participants to choose whether and what to release, but workplace contributions may instead be required or automatically captured by employers, making formal consent an incomplete safeguard. Prior research on datafied workplaces similarly argues that individual data rights become meaningful only when supported by institutions that enable worker power and workplace democracy [18]. AI knowledge reuse is therefore also a matter of organizational governance. Organizations should establish binding 22 Tianqi Song, Zicheng Zhu, Hancheng Cao, Yi-Chieh Lee rules that limit reuse to stated purposes, involve workers or their representatives before expanding reuse to materially different purposes, assign responsibility for high-impact uses, protect contributors from undisclosed performance evaluation or adverse employment consequences, and specify how benefits generated from pooled expertise are shared. Participatory approaches to algorithmic management offer one model for incorporating workers’ preferences into workplace systems and decisions [34]. Such arrangements may complement contextual permissions and traceability by making reuse boundaries enforceable and giving workers a voice in defining and revising them. 

5.4 Limitation and Future Work 

First, how workers understand and approach knowledge sharing may depend on the nature of their expertise. Although our sample covered four white-collar roles, sharing practices and concerns may differ in professions with distinct expertise requirements, regulatory constraints, or decision stakes, such as healthcare, law, and education. Our modest sample size also limited statistical precision, particularly in the analyses restricted to participants who authorized reuse (Section 4.1.2). The absence of statistically significant differences in category-level sharing should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that no differences exist. Larger and more diverse samples are needed to estimate smaller effects and examine variation across occupations and organizational contexts. Second, we described the AI recipient broadly to examine workers’ responses to AI reuse without tying their decisions to a particular system or implementation. This choice, however, may have bundled several properties that participants associated with AI, including scalability, autonomy, and generalization. The study therefore cannot determine which of these properties contributed to the observed authorization gap. It also did not distinguish among model training, retrieval-augmented generation, and agentic task execution. Future research should vary these technical uses and governance conditions independently to identify which characteristics most strongly shape workers’ decisions. Lastly, although the controlled simulation enabled systematic comparisons, it could not fully reproduce the conditions of knowledge contribution in actual workplaces. Participants may have viewed sharing as part of a paid study and therefore been more willing to contribute. They also had explicit choices about whether and what to release, whereas organizational expectations, mandatory participation, or automatic data collection may limit such choices at work. Contribution in practice may therefore involve greater reluctance or more complex constraints than our study captures. Longitudinal field studies are needed to examine how organizational conditions and experiences of reuse shape workers’ participation over time, alongside the quality of their contributions and the downstream uses they support. 

6 Conclusion 

This study examined how workers approach sharing their expertise for reuse by human professionals and AI systems. In a mixed-methods study with 61 professionals, participants were less likely to authorize AI reuse than human reuse, while evidence of category-level differences was inconclusive ( RQ1 ). The clearest difference therefore concerned whether workers permitted reuse at all. Participants in both conditions raised concerns about knowledge being taken out of context, while those in the AI condition expressed greater concerns about loss of interpretive control and possible professional replacement ( RQ2 ). They also emphasized continued control over subsequent use, with individual preferences varying between receiving credit and remaining anonymous ( RQ3 ). These findings suggest that making expertise reusable through AI requires attention to the conditions under which workers are willing to contribute. Knowledge-management systems should preserve the context and provenance of contributions, support review, correction, restrictions, and prospective withdrawal, and accommodate different attribution preferences. Treating How Knowledge Workers Share Their Expertise with AI Systems 23 workers as continuing participants in decisions about how their knowledge is used can help protect their interests and support sustainable knowledge sharing with organizational AI systems.

A.2.1 Public-Repository Sharing as an Ecological Validity Check. As an ecological validity check, participants also made an authorization decision for an actual public repository. We examined whether the effects observed for the primary organizational-sharing measure extended to this decision with real consequences. 

Sharing permission. Participants assigned to the AI condition were less likely to authorize sharing through the public repository. Eighteen of 30 participants in the AI condition granted permission (60 .0%, 95% CI [42% , 75% ]), compared with 28 of 31 participants in the Human condition (90 .3%, 95% CI [75% , 97% ]). A two-sided Fisher’s exact test indicated a significant difference between conditions, 𝑂𝑅 = 0.16, 95% CI [0.05 , 0.68 ], 𝑝 = .008. This result was consistent with the primary organizational-sharing measure: in both settings, participants were less likely to authorize AI systems than human professionals to use their knowledge. Figure 10 presents authorization full-sample category-level authorization rates. Participants who declined overall public sharing were coded as not releasing any of the six categories. Authorization rates were lower in the AI condition for all six categories, consistent with the direction of the organizational-sharing results.
