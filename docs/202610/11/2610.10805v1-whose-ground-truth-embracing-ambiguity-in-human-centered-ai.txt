# Abstract 

As AI systems increasingly interact with people and make decisions about them, understanding human interpretations becomes an important part of developing human-centered AI. Conventional machine learning and AI systems are largely developed under the assumption that a single definitive ground truth exists, with variability in human annotations often resolved through aggregation or treated as noise. However, for many human-centered tasks, human interpretation is inherently ambiguous, and multiple interpretations of the same input may be simultaneously reasonable and valid. Reducing such ambiguity to a single target risks overlooking meaningful information about the diversity of human perception, judgment, and experience. In this position paper, we call for a shift towards modeling the inter-pretation space of plausible human judgments, while distinguishing meaningful ambiguity from annotation noise. We argue that this perspective should guide how AI systems are represented, learned, evaluated, deployed, and governed, supporting more human-centered AI that better reflects the diversity of human interpretation. 

# 1 Introduction 

Figure 1: The Duck–Rabbit illu-sion illustrates ambiguity in hu-man interpretation [1]. Current AI systems are advancing rapidly, with the next gen-eration aiming to understand and interact with humans in in-creasingly natural ways. As artificial intelligence becomes in-creasingly embedded in everyday social environments, from healthcare and educational platforms to conversational agents and decision support systems, machines are no longer simply processing information but participating in human interaction. In this emerging human-machine symbiotic society, AI systems must appropriately understand human expressions, behaviours, and interpretations to develop technologies that are trustworthy, socially compatible, and capable of meaningful interaction with people. However, understanding humans often involves ambiguity . In many human-centered tasks, different individuals may interpret

the same text, speech, or interaction differently depending on contextual understanding, personal experience, cultural background, or emotional perception [2]. Figure 1 illustrates the classic Duck Rabbit illusion, where the same visual stimulus may be interpreted as either a duck or a rabbit. In this paper, ambiguity refers to variation among people’s interpretations of the same input, where multiple interpretations may be reasonable and valid. We distinguish ‘ambiguity’ from ‘model uncertainty’ used in the broad machine learning literature, which reflects limitations in a model’s knowledge or confidence and may be reduced or calibrated, whereas ambiguity represents meaningful variation in human interpretation that should be preserved and learned [3]. Such ambiguity is not necessarily caused by missing information or annotation error. Rather, it may arise from the inherent subjectivity of human perception and interpretation. For example, a smile may signal happiness, politeness, irony, or discomfort depending on the circumstances [4]. This inherent ambiguity distinguishes many human centered tasks from traditional pattern recognition problems such as speech recognition or object identification, where ground truth is relatively well defined [5]. For many human centered tasks, a single definitive label may not exist because several interpretations of the same input may all be reasonable [5, 6]. As a result, computational systems should not be expected to recover a precise label when such a label may not even exist. This raises a central challenge for artificial intelligence: how should AI systems understand and model subjective phenomena when no single, definitive ground truth exists? 

Generally, AI systems assume that each input should ultimately correspond to a single correct label. When multiple annotations are available, they are often aggregated through majority voting or averaging, with variation treated as noise or uncertainty to be reduced [7]. However, in tasks involving human interpretation, such variation may arise from both meaningful ambiguity [8, 9] and annotation noise [10]. While noise may result from errors, inattentiveness, or poor task design, ambiguity can reflect genuine differences in how the same input is interpreted across people, contexts, and domains. Collapsing these interpretations into a single target can therefore remove meaningful information. Although recent work has begun to recognize ambiguity in AI and machine learning, existing approaches remain fragmented, often address only specific tasks or stages of the AI pipeline, and rarely distinguish meaningful ambiguity from annotation noise [11, 12, 4]. We therefore argue that human-centered AI should embrace meaningful ambiguity rather than collapse diverse human interpretations into a single consensus, while explicitly distinguishing ambiguity from annotation noise. 

In this position paper, we present a unified perspective for considering ambiguity across the AI life cycle, including representation, learning, evaluation, deployment, maintenance, and governance. Through this perspective, we aim to establish shared principles for handling ambiguity in human-centered AI and support the development of systems that are more responsible, inclusive, and better suited to diverse human and societal contexts. 

# 2 Current AI Approaches to Ambiguity 

Annotations for human-centered tasks may vary across annotators, and this variation can contain important information rather than merely reflect annotation error [13–16]. This phenomenon has been studied across multiple domains, including semantic reasoning tasks such as natural language inference [17, 18], and subjective perception tasks such as emotion recognition and affective comput-ing [19–21, 12]. A range of approaches have been proposed to move beyond single-label supervision, including multi-annotator modeling, distributional labels, uncertainty estimation, and annotator-aware learning. In categorical tasks, e.g., natural language inference and discrete emotion recognition, prior work has explored soft-label supervision [22, 23], multi-label formulations [24, 25], and ambiguity-aware learning approaches [26]. In continuous affect prediction, ambiguity has been represented using distributions (Gaussian [27], Beta [21, 28, 29], and non-parametric label distributions [30]), mostly focusing on variance as a measure of uncertainty, where overlap measures ambiguity. More recently, language and multimodal models have increasingly explored ways to account for variability in human judgment, including through ambiguity-aware reinforcement learning [8] and inference-time decoding strategies [31]. Others have problematized treating ground truth labels as objective if the underlying knowledge is uncertain, they furthermore describe issues occurring from evaluating AI systems based on know-what measures when domain experts work is evaluated according to 2know-how [32]. Collectively, these developments represent important progress towards recognizing that human annotations are not always reducible to a single ground truth. Despite this progress, current approaches remain largely method-driven and task-specific. Different representations, learning strategies, and evaluation protocols are often developed independently, resulting in inconsistent assumptions about how ambiguity should be modeled and utilized. More fundamentally, most existing methods focus on modeling the observed annotations rather than the underlying space of plausible human interpretations from which those annotations arise. Conse-quently, ambiguity is often treated as a property of individual datasets or algorithms, rather than as a fundamental characteristic of subjective AI tasks that should be considered consistently throughout AI development and deployment. 

# 3 Why Ambiguity Matters for Human-centered AI 

Ambiguity in human interpretation has implications beyond the annotations themselves. Whether variation reflects meaningful human interpretation or annotation noise determines what information an AI system should learn, while the interpretations it preserves can directly shape its subsequent decisions and actions. Understanding both aspects is therefore central to developing AI systems that appropriately operate under ambiguity. 

3.1 Ambiguity in Human Annotation 

Human perceptions and annotations naturally vary across different individuals, and importantly, not all variation among annotations has the same origin. Some variation reflects meaningful ambiguity, whereas other variation arises from annotation noise. Distinguishing between the two is essential for determining which signals AI systems should learn from and which should be mitigated. 

Sources of Ambiguity. Ambiguity in human annotation can arise from different sources, including individual differences, contextual dependence, variation across domains and geographic regions, changes over time, and ambiguity in the input itself. Sources of Noise. Annotation noise arises from imperfections in the annotation process rather than meaningful variation in human interpretation. It may result from annotation errors, low-quality or corrupted inputs, reduced attention or fatigue, and poorly designed instructions or annotation inter-faces [38, 7]. Unlike ambiguity, which reflects multiple reasonable interpretations, noise represents unintended variation that should generally be reduced. Ambiguity vs. Noise. Distinguishing ambiguity from annotation noise is essential. While noise should generally be minimized, ambiguity carries meaningful information about variation in human perception and interpretation. Treating all annotation variability as noise risks discarding valuable signals about how different individuals understand the same input. Recognizing ambiguity as an inherent characteristic of human annotation has important implications for AI systems. For subjective tasks, the goal should not be to eliminate variation in pursuit of a single ground truth, but to preserve and learn from meaningful ambiguity while mitigating annotation noise [39]. This distinction motivates a closer examination of how current AI approaches represent, learn from, and evaluate ambiguity, which we discuss in Section 4 . 

3.2 Implications in Decision Making Under Ambiguity 

Such an AI system should include, where appropriate: the most likely interpretation, alternative plausible interpretations, the degree of ambiguity or uncertainty, the context in which the output is valid, and guidance on what kinds of decisions the output can and cannot support. This section explicitly discusses the ideas in different applications including affective computing, natural language understanding, robotics, and medicine. 

Affective Computing. Consider a conversational AI system that detects a user’s emotional state and adapts its response accordingly. Such systems learn from human perceptions of emotion, for which a single consensus interpretation may not always exist. For example, one person may perceive a user’s expression as angry, while another may interpret the same expression as sarcastic. An AI that sees only “angry” may respond defensively or escalate unnecessarily. On the contrary, an AI system that sees “could be angry, but also substantially sarcastic” may instead recognize that the person’s state is ambiguous and select a response that acknowledges both possibilities, such as de-escalating the tone while also asking clarifying questions. 

Natural Language Understanding. Consider a content moderation system that determines whether an online post is harmless, offensive, harassing, hateful, satirical, or politically critical. These categories often depend on context, target group, local norms, and the identity or experience of the people interpreting the content. For example, most annotators may mark a post as non-offensive, while annotators from the targeted group may consistently perceive it as harmful. Such patterned disagreement is not annotation noise: it reflects a coherent perspective associated with how the 4content affects a particular group. In that case, the minority interpretation may be the most relevant signal for moderation. 

Robotics and planning. Robotics provides another example where preserving multiple possible interpretations can directly affect safety [45]. A robot working near a human may need to infer whether the person intends to hand over an object, move away, ask for help, or stop the interaction. For example, a person reaching toward an object may be intending to hand it to the robot, reposition it, or take it back. The decision value of the output is not only the most likely intention, but whether the ambiguity is low enough to justify physical action. Such ambiguity should be distinguished from transient sensor errors or corrupted observations, which should be filtered out. 

Healthcare. In healthcare, the same patient information may be interpreted differently by different clinicians. For example, clinicians reviewing the same patient-reported symptoms may reasonably differ in their assessment of symptom severity or clinical significance based on their expertise, experience, and contextual understanding. Collapsing these assessments into a single label may obscure meaningful differences in clinical interpretation. Ambiguity-aware AI could instead preserve such variation and communicate multiple plausible interpretations, supporting more transparent and responsible clinical decision-making. Across these examples, the central point is that AI outputs should be designed for the decisions they are meant to support. In some cases, a single label or point estimate may be sufficient. In others, it may hide information that is important for human judgment, such as disagreement among annotators, multiple plausible risk outcomes, uncertain human intention, or context-dependent interpretations of harmful content. Decisions should be made with an understanding of what the model output represents and what it leaves unresolved. 

# 4 A Conceptual Framework for AI Systems to Learn Under Ambiguity 

Building on the observations in Section 3, we argue that subjective tasks should be understood through an interpretation space rather than a single ground-truth label. Instead of treating ambiguity as noise to be removed, we view it as an informative signal that reflects the diversity of human judgments, perceptions, and experiences. This perspective shifts the objective of AI systems from predicting a single correct answer to modeling the space of plausible human interpretations while distinguishing meaningful ambiguity from annotation noise. Let A(x) = y1, y 2, . . . , y K denote the set of annotations collected for an input stimulus (x). We conceptually distinguish the observed annotations into two components: 

A(x) = S(x) ⊕ N (x), (1) where S(x) denotes the interpretation space containing meaningful human ambiguity and disagree-ment. N (x) denotes annotation noise arising from factors such as annotation errors, low-quality stimuli, or inattentive labeling, and ⊕ conceptually denotes that the observed annotations contain both components. We emphasize that this decomposition is conceptual rather than algorithmic; the objective is not to prescribe how S(x) and N (x) should be separated, but to recognize that they should be treated differently throughout the AI pipeline. Under this framework, the development of AI systems for subjective tasks can be viewed through three interconnected components: representation, learning, and evaluation. The following sections discuss how ambiguity and annotation noise should be considered across each stage of the AI pipeline. Fig. 2 depicts an overview of the proposed conceptual framework.

We envision representation paradigms that preserve meaningful ambiguity while explicitly distin-guishing them from annotation noise, enabling AI systems to operate on the full interpretation space of human judgments rather than a single consensus outcome.

Preserve interpretive diversity. The interpretation space may be represented in different forms, including distributions of annotations, sets of valid labels, preference structures, annotator-specific representations, or latent representations that capture interpretive variability. While no single repre-sentation is universally appropriate, the key principle is that representations should retain information regarding the diversity of human judgments rather than reduce them to a single consensus outcome. 

Distinguish ambiguity from annotation noise. A fundamental challenge of human-centered AI is not only preserving inherent label ambiguity, but also distinguishing meaningful ambiguity from annotation noise. Annotation variability arising from differences in perception, context, experience, or cultural background should be retained as part of the interpretation space, whereas variability introduced by annotation errors, inattentive labeling, or poor stimulus quality should be explicitly identified and treated separately. Although we do not prescribe a specific mechanism for this distinction, future representation paradigms should explicitly account for both components, providing a principled foundation for downstream learning, evaluation, and governance.

Evaluate beyond correctness. No single metric is universally appropriate for subjective tasks. Depending on how the interpretation space is represented, evaluation may consider alignment with distributions of judgments, consistency with multiple valid labels, preservation of interpretive variability, or agreement with preference structures. Evaluate consistency with subjectivity. Evaluation should remain aligned with the assumptions adopted during representation and learning. Such consistency ensures that ambiguity is treated as meaning-ful information throughout the entire AI pipeline rather than being inadvertently discarded during evaluation. 

# 5 Governing Human-Centered AI Systems 

We call for action: AI systems should not simply determine what is true. Instead, they should account for what interpretations are plausible, how strongly they are supported, where ambiguity exists, and what actions are appropriate under that ambiguity. 

Conventional AI governance largely assumes that an AI system approximates an objective truth and that system quality can therefore be assessed by correctness, accuracy, or agreement with a reference label or a performance benchmark. While these assumptions are appropriate for many prediction tasks, they become insufficient for inherently subjective problems where multiple interpretations may simultaneously be legitimate. Consequently, the objective of governance is no longer to ensure that an AI system consistently produces the correct answer. Rather, governance should ensure that the system faithfully represents legitimate interpretive diversity, distinguishes meaningful disagreement from annotation noise, com-municates ambiguity appropriately, and supports human decision-making within established ethical and regulatory boundaries. 

This perspective fundamentally changes how human-centered AI systems should be validated, de-ployed, monitored, and maintained throughout their life cycle. 

5.1 Validation Beyond Deterministic Ground Truth 

For objective tasks, validation evaluates agreement between model predictions and a reference truth (benchmarking). For subjective tasks, validation should instead evaluate whether the model appropriately represents the interpretation space associated with the intended deployment context. Rather than asking whether a prediction is correct, governance should ask whether the model preserves legitimate diversity of interpretation, distinguishes disagreement from annotation noise, remains calibrated under ambiguity, behaves consistently across relevant populations and contexts of use, and communicates uncertainty in ways that support appropriate human decisions. 7Importantly, acceptable ambiguity depends on application risk. Low-risk recommendation systems may tolerate substantial interpretive variability, whereas high-risk applications, including healthcare, pharmaceutical development, finance, and legal decision support, require considerably narrower operational boundaries, stronger evidence, and increased human oversight. Governance should therefore adopt a risk-based perspective in which acceptable ambiguity is determined by the potential consequences of incorrect interpretation rather than by technical model performance alone. 

5.2 Human Oversight Under Ambiguity 

Traditional human-in-the-loop systems frequently assume that humans resolve residual uncertainty by selecting the correct answer or by course-correcting the AI system in question. In human-centered AI, however, disagreement may itself represent the appropriate outcome. Human oversight therefore shifts from identifying a single correct prediction to interpreting multiple plausible explanations within their operational context. Indeed, ambiguity may even arise between the outcome of the AI system and the human-in-the-loop, in a way where it becomes unclear to the human if course-correction is needed or not, due to the subjectivity of the task at hand. Consequently, human-centered AI systems should avoid presenting outputs as definitive whenever multiple interpretations remain plausible. Instead, systems should communicate the most likely interpretation, the confidence in the given interpretation, alternative plausible interpretations, the degree of disagreement, contextual assumptions underlying each interpretation; and situations in which additional evidence or human review is required. Such communication enables decision-makers to reason about ambiguity explicitly rather than implicitly assuming certainty. This may reduce inappropriate automation, improve transparency, and support more calibrated trust in AI-assisted decision-making. 

5.3 Life Cycle Monitoring and Interpretation Spaces 

Deployment does not eliminate subjectivity. Human interpretation evolves over time as cultures, organizations, regulations, and professional practices change. Traditional machine learning operations therefore requires extension beyond concept drift and data drift to include subjectivity drift: changes in legitimate human interpretations despite relatively stable input data. Governance should monitor interpretation spaces throughout the system life cycle through mech-anisms such as re-annotation, stakeholder feedback, expert review, and calibration exercises. Im-portantly, governance must distinguish evolving ambiguity from annotation noise, as these require fundamentally different responses. Changes in human interpretation may justify updating models, whereas noise primarily motivates improvements in data quality, annotation procedures, and quality assurance. Accordingly, governance should evaluate whether AI systems maintain an appropriate representation of the interpretation space over time rather than whether they collapse ambiguity into consensus. 

5.4 Ambiguity Does Not Imply Ethical Relativism 

It is important to clarify that accounting for subjectivity and ambiguity in AI does not imply ethical relativism. Throughout this paper we argue that many human-centered AI tasks lack a single definitive ground truth because human interpretation inherently varies across individuals owing to different contexts, expertise, cultures and other factors. This observation concerns how AI systems should model empirical human judgments. It does not imply that ethical principles themselves are subjective or that all interpretations should be treated as equally acceptable. Instead, we distinguish three fundamentally different concepts: • Interpretation space , representing legitimate diversity in human judgment; • Annotation noise , representing unintended errors and artifacts that should be reduced; and • Normative constraints , representing ethical principles, legal obligations, organizational policies, and domain-specific standards that govern how AI systems ought to behave. Ethics therefore exists outside the interpretation space. Rater ambiguity describes how people interpret the world while ethical governance determines how AI systems should act upon those interpretations. 8Indeed, preserving ambiguity may strengthen ethical AI rather than weaken it. Minority perspectives frequently reveal systematic harms, cultural blind spots, demographic disparities, or emerging risks that majority-vote aggregation may obscure. Maintaining these perspectives enables organizations to identify potentially vulnerable populations, recognize fairness concerns earlier, and support more inclusive decision-making while remaining fully constrained by established ethical principles and regulatory requirements. 

5.5 Design Principles for Governing Human-centered AI 

The preceding discussion suggests seven principles for governing AI systems operating under inherent human ambiguity. 

Principle 1. Validate interpretation spaces rather than deterministic labels. Evaluation should assess whether model behavior faithfully represents legitimate human interpretation rather than maximizing agreement with a single aggregated label. 

Principle 2. Distinguish disagreement from annotation noise. Governance should preserve mean-ingful interpretive diversity while continuously reducing errors arising from poor-quality data or annotation artifacts. 

Principle 3. Govern ambiguity according to application risk. Acceptable ambiguity should depend on the potential consequences of downstream decisions, with progressively stronger controls required as potential harm increases. 

Principle 4. Monitor subjectivity throughout the system life cycle. Governance should monitor evolving human interpretations alongside conventional indicators such as concept drift and model degradation. 

Principle 5. Communicate ambiguity rather than conceal it. AI systems should transparently communicate multiple plausible interpretations and their associated levels of disagreement whenever this information is relevant for decision-making. 

Principle 6. Separate empirical subjectivity from normative governance. AI systems should faithfully represent legitimate human ambiguity while remaining constrained by ethical principles, regulatory requirements, and organizational values. 

Principle 7. Do not consider human-centered AI systems as isolated entities during monitoring, evaluation, and retraining but as sociotechnical systems interacting with users, IT infrastructure, and other AI systems or AI agents through both input and output potentially with multiple purposes. These principles extend AI governance beyond deterministic notions of correctness toward governance mechanisms capable of supporting AI systems operating under inherent human subjectivity. Rather than seeking to eliminate disagreement, subjective AI should enable organizations to reason about disagreement responsibly, transparently, and ethically throughout the entire AI life cycle. 

# 6 Conclusion 

In this position paper, we argue that ambiguity is an inherent part of human interpretation and should be explicitly considered in the development of human-centered AI. We propose a general conceptual framework that distinguishes meaningful ambiguity from annotation noise and models multiple plausible human interpretations through an interpretation space. Building on this framework, we call for action to account for ambiguity throughout AI representation, learning, evaluation, decision making, deployment, and governance. We hope this perspective provides a foundation for rethinking how AI systems learn from human interpretations and paves the way towards more human-centered and responsible AI. 9Acknowledgment
