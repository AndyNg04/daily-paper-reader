# Juicy Interactive Visualization: Evaluating How Excessive Feedback Design Shapes Visualization Engagement 

Shano Liang , Max Chen , and Lane Harrison 

Figure 1: Overview of our work. We operationalize game-studies juiciness as a visualization design space. Grounding a palette of juicy feedback techniques (Tab. 2), we instantiate three dimensions (interaction type, feedback timing, and intensity) across 26 controlled prototypes and evaluate them in three exploratory online studies. 

Abstract —Visualization research has long examined embellishment and animation, while the design of rich interaction-contingent feedback remains under-articulated as a systematic design space. We introduce JuicyVIS , a theory-informed operationalization of juicy feedback for interactive visualization that translates ideas from game studies into a visualization-centered framework. JuicyVIS introduces three dimensions, interaction type, feedback timing, and feedback intensity, which we explore through 26 controlled prototypes, grounded in established visualization interaction categories and prior work on juicy feedback in games. We evaluate the juicy design space through three exploratory mixed-methods online studies. Results show that juicy feedback was consistently associated with higher engagement and aesthetic experience, while the clearest gains came from post-interaction feedback, not, for example, by simply increasing intensity. We discuss how juicy feedback can be thought of as a design material in visualizations, shaping how visualization interactions feel, how action consequences are perceived, and how users remain engaged with data representations. 

Index Terms —Interactive visualization, feedback design, juiciness, user engagement 

1 INTRODUCTION 

“Juice ,” or “ juiciness ,” a concept widely adopted in game and interactive media studies, refers to the quality of excessive amounts of feedback in relation to user input [30], while design and feedback that exhibit this quality are considered “ juicy .” Juiciness changes how actions feel rather than what an interface can do, amplifying the perceived responsiveness, salience, and “liveness” of its reactions to input. It has been used to explain why seemingly small micro-feedback can motivate continued interaction [24, 25, 29, 30]. Visualization research, meanwhile, has long examined forms of “extra” presentation beyond minimalism, including static embellish-ment [3] and animation techniques [2, 23, 38]. Many works treat such effects primarily as presentation-level styling; far fewer studies frame them as interaction-contingent feedback tightly coupled to moment-to-moment user input, that is, what a visualization richly “says back” as users act on it. In parallel, visualization interaction researchers have argued that feedback is central to effective interaction [15], yet few stud-

As a result, the quality of interaction-time visualization feedback remains under-articulated as a systematic design space: many systems are functionally correct yet respond so minimally that interaction feels experientially “thin.” Recent work by Campos has begun to bring juiciness into the visu-alization field, through a content-analysis survey of effect categories in existing infographics [8], a designer ethnography [10], and a com-parative juicy-versus-dry study [9]. This work establishes that juicy techniques already circulate in visualization practice, while operating largely at a descriptive, representation-level register. What remains open is a theory-grounded design space that ties juicy feedback to specific interaction events and treats it as a primary design factor. Games have long treated interaction quality as a core design material. We turn to game studies for a vocabulary suited to this gap, taking juicy feedback design as a lens for articulating the experiential “ thickness ”of visualization feedback. We therefore ask: how can juiciness be translated from game studies into a visualization-centered design space for interaction feedback, and how do its dimensions relate to users’ experiences and task-related outcomes in interactive visualizations? Building on this prior groundwork, we present JuicyVIS (Fig. 1), a theory-informed operationalization of juicy interaction feedback for data visualization. We map juiciness onto established visualization interaction types [55] and instantiate three design dimensions: inter-action type , feedback (timing) type , and feedback intensity , across 

26 controlled prototypes. We then conduct three exploratory mixed-1arXiv:2610.08681v1 [cs.HC] 6 Oct 2026 methods online studies that pair comparative quantitative measures with qualitative accounts, examining how these dimensions relate to en-gagement, aesthetic experience, preference, and task-related outcomes. Across these studies, juicy feedback was consistently associated with higher engagement and aesthetic experience, with the clearest gains from post-interaction feedback and no simple “ more is better ” effect for intensity. Our aim is to make this under-articulated design space prac-tically and empirically inspectable, and to surface promising patterns, trade-offs, and boundary conditions for future visualization design. 

2 BACKGROUND 

Both juiciness in game studies and interaction in visualization have been discussed in prior literature and widely used as umbrella terms, yet their concepts are usually ambiguous and inconsistently defined across studies. Therefore, we first review literature and articulate how these concepts are contextualized and operationalized in our study to clarify the scope of using and constructing “ juiciness ” and “ interaction .” 

2.1 Operationalizing Interaction in Visualization 

Because juiciness concerns input-contingent feedback, studying juicy feedback in visualization first requires specifying which user actions constitute interaction events and thus can be “juicified.” For this work’s purposes, we seek an interaction taxonomy that is sufficiently actionable to serve as the framework for constructing controlled juicy design. Two frameworks are mostly relevant: Yi et al. [55] propose a grounded, intent-oriented taxonomy of seven interaction archetypes. In parallel, Dimara and Perin [13] synthesize an intentionally inclusive view of interaction for data visualization, framing interaction as a broad space of actions across the visualization pipeline. Other adjacent works of visualization research conceptualize interaction at different analytic resolutions. Steed [43] approaches interactive visualization from a pipeline and workflow perspective, emphasizing that user intervention may occur at multiple stages. Amar et al. [1], by contrast, focus on the analytic work users seek to accomplish, proposing ten low-level analytic tasks as a common vocabulary. Brehmer and Munzner [7] similarly introduce a multi-level typology that distinguishes why a task is undertaken and how it is carried out. These perspectives are valuable for situating interaction within broader analytic activity. Yet, because they are not suitable for serving as an input-event-level operational backbone for operationalizing interaction feedback, we treat them here as contextual lenses. As Tab. 1 shows, Yi et al.’s seven intent categories [55] fall largely within the portion of Dimara and Perin’s framework [13] concerned with perceptualization: actions on how data are represented, arranged, and presented, with Filter also bordering processing-oriented actions. This pattern follows from the different organizing logics of the two frameworks. Yi et al. classify interaction by user intent, offering seven categories for characterizing what users seek to accomplish through interaction. Dimara and Perin, by contrast, broaden the scope of visual-ization interaction and organize it by where actions occur semantically in the visualization pipeline: on raw data (i.e., the “ Data Actions ”, including Input Data Actions and Processing Data Actions ), on its per-ceptualization (including Mapping Data Actions and Presentation Data Actions ), or on non-data entities (i.e., “ Non-Data Actions ”, including 

Meta Actions , Social Actions , and Interface Actions ). This comparison clarifies the scope of juicy interaction in the present work. We exclude the non-data actions because they concern meta-level activity, social coordination, and general interface management rather than direct in-teraction with representations. More importantly, Dimara and Perin distinguish between actions on raw data itself and actions on its percep-tualization. For our purposes, we focus on the latter: input-contingent feedback tied to representation-level interactions experienced by users within an already instantiated visualization. Accordingly, Yi et al.’s tax-onomy provides a more suitable operational framework for constructing controlled stimuli.

This description defines juiciness from a person-internal experiential intuition, while subsequent work retains this general intuition and expands the definition (but diverges in emphasis). Longanecker and Brown [33], for example, frame juiciness from the perspective of experiential results, claiming the juicy feedback is an interaction quality to elicit visceral and highly satisfying responses. Hicks et al. describe juiciness as abundant or redundant audiovisual feedback triggered by player action [25], and frame it as a phenomenon emerging from the coherent integration of mechanics, visuals, and confirmatory feedback [24]. Kao et al. similarly note that the term was used in different ways, ranging from person-external feedback features to person-internal experiential qualities, while recurring themes from literature include moment-to-moment action-contingent response and an amplified (or excessive) amount of feedback relative to input [30]. Moreover, there are more works that have also extended the concept beyond heavy visual embellishments or effects to audio [29], hap-tic [42], and even text-based feedback [19]. These variations make juiciness a productive but ambiguous term. Following recent work [30] in human-computer interaction (HCI), we acknowledge that it is es-sential for any study engaging with this term to clarify the specific definition or context it adopts, and, thus, here in this study, we use their definition of juiciness as “ the design feature of immediate ex-cessive amounts of feedback in relation to user input at the level of moment-to-moment interaction ” [30]. The use of this definition does not attempt to cover every quality sometimes associated with juicy design (e.g., haptic or other multisensory aspects), but is based on this operational (and we consider tractable) definition to translate the juiciness concept into visualization interaction. 

2.3 Feedback Design in Visualization Interaction 

Having scoped this study as abovementioned, in this section, we situate our work within prior visualization studies on feedback design. In the context of interactive visualization, feedback has been discussed less as an isolated aesthetic layer than as a condition for effective and engaging 2© 2026 IEEE. interaction. Elmqvist et al. [15] argue that interaction is central to users’ dialogue with data and propose Fluid Interaction as a unifying design concept for information visualization. Within this framework, design guidelines such as “ provide immediate visual feedback on interaction ”, and “ reward interaction ” are treated as key properties of the fluidity. Their account links feedback not only to responsiveness, but also to sense of control, and the minimization of Norman’s Gulfs of Execution 

(the gap between a user’s intentions and the system’s allowable actions), and Gulf of Evaluation (the gap between the system’s actual state and the user’s perception) [35]. Notably, Elmqvist et al. also suggest that well-designed visualization interaction may share similarities with games [15], an insight our work recalls: we draw on video games’ design strategies of the juicy concept to further enhance users’ active interaction with data. Subsequent work has made the role of feedback more explicit. Li and Zhu examine visual feedback for interaction in 3D visualization and argue that feedback helps users understand system function, state, and outcome [32]. They further distinguish feedforward , real-time feedback , and delay-time feedback , showing that feedback can support interaction by confirming actions, anticipating possible actions, and clarifying consequences. This temporal framing is closely aligned with our interest in exploring and specifying juicy feedback design spaces in interactive visualization. Moreover, other visualization works broaden what feedback can mean and why it matters. Chundury et al., for example, show that interaction support need not be purely visual – sound and touch can function together as feedback channels for understanding chart semantics [11]. Willett et al., from a different angle, suggest that exaggerated feedback may shape not only task success but also users’ felt sense of agency and capability [49]. Recent works by Campos [8–10] come closest to the present topic, but operate at a different level of analysis. In one study, Campos sur-veys 195 publicly accessible interactive visualizations and interprets juiciness through five observable effect categories (animation, particles, audio feedback, screen shake, and persistence), giving an initial de-scriptive inventory of how such features appear in existing practice [8]. Importantly, this does not suggest that juiciness has been systemati-cally theorized in visualization; rather, it indicates that techniques now legible through a juicy lens already appear in practice, but without a shared conceptual or taxonomy-grounded account. A separate ethnog-raphy, drawing on interviews with designers, suggests that juicy design may enhance engagement, understanding, and memorability, while also risking distraction and overstimulation [10]. Most directly related is Campos’s comparative juicy-versus-dry 

study [9], to our knowledge, one of the few user studies of how juicy design affects engagement and short-term retention in visualization settings; it reports no significant difference between the dry and juicy versions, with mixed effects on retention. We extend this opening from a representation-level has/has-not, juicy-versus-dry 1 [9] contrast to a theory-informed design space: we map juicy feedback onto established interaction categories [55], build controlled stimuli around interaction events, and examine how juiciness varies across interaction type, tempo-ral placement, and intensity. Our controlled exemplars and exploratory evaluation do not claim a definitive universal effect, but to characterize how different juiciness dimensions may shape experience across inter-action types. Campos’s work helps open the problem space; our study seeks to systematize it through a more explicit taxonomy-grounded operationalization of juicy visualization feedback. 

3 STUDY DESIGN : U NPACKING DESIGN SPACE DIMENSIONS OF JUICY INTERACTIVE VISUALIZATION 

Grounded in Yi et al.’s taxonomy of visualization interaction [55] and Kao et al.’s operational definition of juicy [30], we operationalize juicy feedback as a theory-informed design space for interactive visualization. We decompose juiciness, often treated as a single stylistic toggle [9], into a set of analytically distinct and experimentally controllable dimen-sions. This operationalization is an exploratory research instrument that

We first lay out a palette of juicy feedback techniques used to build and document our stimuli (Sec. 3.1), then unpack three design dimensions against a non-juicy baseline: juiciness interaction types , juiciness feedback types , and juiciness intensity-parameter (Secs. 3.2 to 3.4). 3.1 A Palette of Juicy Feedback Techniques 

Building juicy stimuli systematically first requires a vocabulary of the feedback techniques available to a visualization designer. We ground this vocabulary in Campos’s content analysis of interactive infograph-ics [8], which identifies five recurring, observable effect categories: animation, particles, audio feedback, screen shake, and persistence. Campos’s work codes whether such effects occur, but not how they are realized or tied to an interaction. We therefore extend the five categories into a finer set of implementation-grounded sub-categories, the Juice Elements Palette (Tab. 2). Each technique in our prototypes is assigned to one sub-category by its dominant mechanism: sound-producing cues to audio feedback, whole-view displacement to screen shake, small spawned units to particles, and visual traces that remain during or after an event (e.g., guides, previews, sustained highlights) to persistence; the remaining motion and transformation effects (e.g., pops, pulses, flashes, sweeps) fall under animation. This palette is an operational vocabulary, not an exhaustive taxonomy: every sub-category is derived from a technique we actually instantiated. We use it both to construct the stimuli and to document them, with the colored tags in Tab. 3 and Tab. 4 tracing each described effect back to its sub-category, making the feedback descriptions inspectable against shared reference. To instantiate this design space, we developed JuicyVIS ↗ (GitHub 

repo ↗), a web-based prototype ( React , TypeScript , D3.js ) in which condition-specific infographic variants (hereafter, “ infographic ”) are served from a shared codebase and parameterized through URL query strings; this implementation underlies the interactive stimuli linked in the following subsections. The stimuli were constructed from publicly accessible datasets obtained through Our World in Data (OWID ) [16– 18, 20, 21, 27, 45–47, 50–54]. We followed the source attribution and citation guidance provided on the corresponding OWID pages when reconstructing custom study stimuli from the data. 

3.2 Dimension 1: Juiciness Interaction-Types 

The first dimension concerns where juiciness is attached in the in-teraction types. We operationalize it at the level of Yi et al.’s seven interaction categories [55], choosing these seven because together they cover that taxonomy’s full intent space rather than an ad hoc subset. JuicyVIS includes an infographic for each category (Select, Explore, Reconfigure, Encode, Abstract/Elaborate 2, Filter, and Connect); for each chart, we built a matched pair, a non-juicy baseline and a juicy variant, yielding 14 conditions (for Dimension 1). The juicy techniques attached to each interaction are drawn from the palette (Sec. 3.1) and chosen to fit that interaction’s affordances, for instance a commit-and-confirm pattern for Reconfigure’s sorting action and matched-range cues for Filter’s thresholding; Tab. 3 documents the full mapping and links to the prototypes. We do not frame our implementations as a universal recipe. The effects and appropriateness of juicy feedback are contingent on in-teraction intent, representational context, and timing, and a feedback family that helps one interaction may play a different role, or even be-come inappropriate in another. Our aim is controlled, theory-grounded variation: a principled set of exemplars that makes cross-interaction comparison possible. These exemplars were designed by the authors from this theoretical grounding and refined through pilot tests; they were not validated by external design professionals, a step we leave to future work (Sec. 6).

3.3 Dimension 2: Juiciness Feedback-Types 

Beyond which interaction type is juicified (Dimension 1), a second dimension concerns when juicy feedback is presented relative to the unfolding interaction. Prior visualization work has shown that feedback can be temporally differentiated [32]; we build on this insight for a different purpose, parameterizing temporal placement as a design space dimension of juicy visualization feedback. We operationalize three compact feedback types, Pre-interaction , In-interaction , and Post-interaction , named for when juicy cues are attached relative to a focal interaction event in our stimuli. We further motivate these categories through Hicks et al.’s account of juicy direct feedback [24]. In our reading, pre-interaction juicy feed-back echoes the “ Confirmatory ” feedback, “a direct response to physical input of a button,” while in-interaction echoes their “ Unambiguous ”feedback, “information [that can] be connected to actions and only interpreted in one way” [24]. These levels, however, have typically been set through designer judgment and iteration rather than a reported, reproducible parameterization [30]; we make ours explicit. 4.1 Procedure of Each Study 

Each study consists of four sections: (i) instructions and a digital consent form; (ii) an attention check, an audio speaker test (juicy feed-back includes sound), and a color blindness test (juicy elements rely 5on color); (iii) a series of JuicyVIS trials with tasks and measures (Sec. 4.2); and (iv) a follow-up questionnaire on overall experience and demographics. To prevent learning or carryover effects, each study used a separate participant sample. Both Study 1 and Study 2 took about 20-30 minutes, while Study 3 took about 15-20 minutes. Sev-eral conventions were shared across studies: Each trial embedded one visualization with three task questions, included both to ensure active interaction and to assess readability and task performance. Different trials used different dataset topics to limit repeated exposure to iden-tical content, and trial order was counterbalanced with a Latin-square scheme. Within each study, the first encountered trial(s) served as tutorial familiarization and were excluded from analysis. 

4.1.1 Study 1: Juiciness in Interaction Types 

Study 1 (Dimension 1, Sec. 3.2) examines how juicy vs. non-juicy feedback is experienced across the seven interaction types. Each partic-ipant completed seven trials, one per type. Following the matched-pair sequencing of prior work [3], a participant saw only one variant of each interaction type (either the juicy or the non-juicy version; Tab. 3), keep-ing the comparison between-participants at the level of each type while allowing an individual’s sequence to mix juicy and non-juicy trials; which variant each participant saw was counterbalanced across partici-pants. The two versions of a type used identical task questions. The first two trials were tutorials, leaving five analyzed trials per participant. 

4.1.2 Study 2: Juiciness in Feedback Types 

Study 2 (Dimension 2, Sec. 3.3) examines how different temporal placements of juicy feedback shape experience. Presenting all eight timing conditions in one session would risk fatigue (and therefore have potential fatigue effects), so we adopted an incomplete-block within-subjects design: each participant saw five trials, the non-juicy baseline and the full pre +in +post condition, plus three of the six intermediate combinations (Tab. 4). The intermediate conditions were counterbalanced so that all six appeared evenly across the sample. After the one tutorial trial, four trials per participant were analyzed. 

4.1.3 Study 3: Juiciness with Different Intensity 

Study 3 (Dimension 3, Sec. 3.4) isolates how intensity relates to experi-ence. Holding the interaction set and feedback timing fixed, we varied only the intensity preset, giving four conditions. In this within-subjects design, each participant completed four trials, one per intensity. After the one tutorial trial, three trials per participant were analyzed, yielding an incomplete repeated-measures set in which the omitted intensity varied across participants by counterbalanced order. 

4.2 Tasks and Measures 

Each trial included three task questions for assessing readability and task performance across conditions. After the task, participants rated the trial on two scales: (i) the BeauVis 5-item scale to assess participants’ aesthetic experience of the visualization [22]; and (ii), to align with prior work on juicy visualization infographics, we used Campos’s adapted 

User Engagement Scale Short Form ( UES-SF ) [9], which was adapted from O’Brien et al.’s work [36], to measure engagement in the context of juicy visualization experiences. After completing all trials within a study, participants answered a set of overall preference questions: a forced-choice default-style preference, an open-ended prompt on which infographic they preferred overall and why, a 10-point slider on their overall preference for adding versus not adding juicy feedback, and a final open-ended prompt explaining their choices. So that participants could refer to conditions without our seeing the numbering logic, we labeled each condition by its infographic’s title-takeaway (e.g., “ Energy ”from the “ Per Capita Energy Consumption by Source ” infographics). In addition, Study 2 asked about preferred feedback timing and Study 3 asked about preferred intensity.

5.3 Evaluating Juiciness with Different Intensity (Study 3) 

In Study 3, results (Tab. 8) provided no reliable evidence that increas-ing juicy-feedback intensity changed engagement, aesthetic experience, or task performance. In the ordered-intensity mixed-effects models, intensity was not a significant predictor of UES-SF total, BeauVis total, or task performance. Secondary categorical mixed-effects follow-up models showed the same null pattern: intensity category did not sig-nificantly predict UES-SF total, BeauVis total, or task performance, and no Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparison was significant. Al-though the estimated marginal means suggested modest descriptive variation across intensity levels, particularly somewhat higher UES-SF and BeauVis means at the medium condition, these differences were not statistically significant. The RTA result was broadly consistent with the null quantitative findings. While the medium-intensity condition was most often pre-ferred ( n = 10), low intensity was the next most frequently preferred option ( n = 6), and participants who preferred none ( n = 2) or low intensity often raised concerns about distraction (i.e., the S TAYING 

FOCUS theme). Rather than indicating a clear monotonic preference for stronger feedback, these accounts suggest a trade-off between re-inforcement and distraction, helping explain why no reliable overall intensity effect emerged in the quantitative models.

Three study results anchor this discussion: First, broadening the tem-poral coverage of juicy feedback raised engagement and aesthetic ex-periences, and that gain came almost entirely from post-interaction feedback (Study 2). Second, scaling intensity from none to high pro-duced no reliable change in any outcome (Study 3). Together, these recast juiciness from “ more-is-better ”embellishment into a placement- and context-sensitive design material. We develop this through the game/work boundary (Sec. 6.1), content and context (Sec. 6.2), and a set of design considerations (Sec. 6.3), before stating limitations (Sec. 6.4). 

6.1 The Boundary Between “ Game ” & “ Work ”

When juiciness moves from games to data visualization, what transfers and what shifts? Game design centers on engaging player experience through mechanics, visuals, and systems [39], whereas interactive visualization is often framed as helping people to make sense of data [28, 37]. That sensemaking stance surfaced in our preference data: in Studies 1 and 3, participants judged conditions largely by which one helped them stay focused and extract information, and some ( n = 21, across three studies) who preferred the non- or less-juicy versions often described the effects as interference when they wanted to “ get the work done .” This echoes prior literature [42] describing juicy feedback as “polarizing ,” and, when excessive, as a burden. Even so, our results suggest that experience design still matters in this work-like setting when it lands at the right moment. In Study 2, the engagement and aesthetic gains from broader feedback coverage came from post-interaction feedback rather than from pre- or in-interaction cues. We read this through Norman’s action cycle [35] and Hicks et al.’s notion of supplementary feedback [24]: post-interaction juice closes the evaluation phase, confirming the consequence of a completed action. It also fits evidence that juicy feedback can foster intrinsic motivation; in a non-game VR setting, juiciness on its own satisfied the competence, autonomy, and relatedness needs that Self-Determination Theory ties to intrinsic motivation [26]. Feedback placed earlier carries the opposite risk; some participants ( n = 7, Study 2) found pre-interaction cues annoying when they fired without an intended interaction, a false-positive signal that undercuts rather than rewards. The valence of juiciness thus appears to hinge on where it sits in the interaction loop and on whether a user treats the activity as work or as play. The experiential “ thickness ” we set out to characterize seems welcome when it confirms an action and intrusive when it pre-empts one. 

6.2 Content & Context Considerations 

Does every dataset and interaction benefit from juiciness, or do some demand restraint? Our accounts suggest its value is contingent rather than uniform, shaped by two factors. The first is how much the visual-ization demands of attention and interaction. Some participants ( n = 15, across studies) said juicy elements helped them stay locked in, pointing 8© 2026 IEEE. This is the author’s version of the article that has been published in IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics. The final version of this record is available at: xx.xxxx/TVCG.202x.xxxxxxx/ 

to value where sustaining attention is hard; others ( n = 22) said the cues helped confirm actions and signal where they were, pointing to value in complex, multi-step interactions. Both describe settings that must coax users into an active exploratory role. This is a long-standing concern in data-driven storytelling, where news media increasingly use interactive visualization to attract and retain reader attention [34], and where the balance between author- and reader-driven narratives is cen-tral [34, 40]; it is also the aim of work that frames visualization through empowerment and agency [49]. Reward-oriented juice offers one lever on this balance, nudging readers along an intended path without tak-ing away their control. Such contexts already recur in practice, from gameful and feedback-rich designs in visual analytics workflows [41] to student-facing learning-analytics dashboards [4]. The second factor is the seriousness of the content. Some participants (n = 3, across three studies) preferred an infographic partly because its topic, obesity by country, was “ interesting ” (P13), which prompted us to ask whether certain subjects should stay sober and avoid juicy treatment. We did not manipulate content seriousness and so cannot settle this, but Campos’s ethnography [10] reports the same tension, noting that subject matter conditions whether juicy elements are appropriate and cautioning against overwhelming the core message; work on gamifying a sensitive health simulation likewise finds that playful layers can sit awkwardly with reflective, weighty tasks [44]. Here, a workable reading is that juiciness is most promising where attention and interaction complexity are high and the subject is light, and should be applied more sparingly as content grows heavier. 

6.3 Design Considerations for Juicy Interaction 

From building JuicyVIS and running the studies, we distilled design considerations. We offer them as empirically- and literature-informed considerations rather than validated guidelines: each draws on prior work and our findings (Sec. 6.4). 

Ensuring granularity in intensity, type, and modality : Prior work in games treats juicy intensity as a design variable and warns that both too little and too much can hurt [30, 42]. Study 3 adds that, within the range we tested, raising juicy intensity from none to high in visualiza-tion produced no reliable benefit, while accounts described a trade-off between reinforcement and distraction. A moderate default is there-fore a sensible starting point, and intensity, type, and modality should stay adjustable so users can tune feedback to their preferences. Expos-ing modality also serves accessibility, echoing multimodal-feedback practice [24] and sensory-substitution work in visualization [11]. 

Match feedback to the interaction it serves : A juicy family that suits one interaction can misfit another, so we chose effects to fit each interaction’s affordances, for instance a commit-and-confirm pattern for sorting and matched-range cues for filtering (Tab. 3). Our Study 1 rein-forces this: the same feedback was experienced differently across the seven interaction types [55], so cues should follow what an interaction means rather than a single juicy template applied everywhere [10]. 

Calibrating juiciness to content and context : As Sec. 6.2 argued that, the same feedback does not fit every setting equally. It is most valuable where attention or interaction complexity is high, and should be restrained for serious or weighty subjects, where playful layers can undercut the message [10, 44]. Treating content seriousness and task demand as first-class design inputs helps decide how much juice a visualization should carry. 

Providing confirmation of successful interaction : Confirming a completed action is a familiar goal, tied to Norman’s evaluation phase [35] and to Elmqvist et al.’s call for immediate feedback that rewards interaction [15]. Our contribution is to pin down where it pays off: in Study 2, post-interaction feedback drove the engagement and aesthetic gains, whereas pre- and in-interaction cues did not, and pre-interaction cues that fired without a committed action read as annoying false positives. Designers should prioritize feedback that closes the loop after an action and avoid exceeding cues that anticipate one. 

Reinforcing designer-intended interaction : Juicy feedback can reward particular actions and steer attention, letting designers express intent through when and where feedback is enabled. Set against the balance of author- and reader-driven narrative [34,40] and visualization framed through empowerment [49], the aim is to guide users along an intended analytic path while leaving them in control. 

Avoiding disruption to the core functionality : Juicy should not crowd out the work of reading data. Embellishment can aid memorabil-ity yet carries comprehension trade-offs [3], and Campos’s ethnography flags distraction and overstimulation as genuine risks [10]. Study 1 sharpens the point: the same features helped some participants and hindered others, so disruption is partly individual. Visually, cues should not obscure marks or controls; technically, they should not add latency; and when either occurs, the feedback should be reducible or removable, which loops back to adjustability. 

Preserving and sharing juicy interactive data visualization : Be-cause juicy feedback is fine-grained and unfolds in real time, it resists static description. Recording and sharing the underlying designs and parameters supports transparency and reuse. Our Juice Elements Palette (Tab. 2) is a step toward a reproducible, inspectable vocabulary for a space documented so far mostly by example. 

6.4 Limitations & Future Work 

We designed and refined the JuicyVIS prototypes and the considera-tions between ourselves, informed by theory, pilots, and our studies, but without validation by external design professionals or domain ex-perts; we therefore present them as considerations to be tested, and see structured experts critique and iterative refinement as the natural next step. Other limits temper our claims. Our online Prolific samples skew Western and English-speaking. Each study was a single session, leaving novelty decay untested. Our stimuli and intensity presets are one principled instantiation, not an optimum, and we did not vary content seriousness, so Sec. 6.2 stays a hypothesis. We also studied the three dimensions in isolation rather than their interactions. Each of these marks an opening for future work. 

7 CONCLUSION 

Interactive visualizations often ask users to act yet say little back. We introduced JuicyVIS , a theory-informed operationalization of juicy feed-back that translates game studies ideas into a visualization design space grounded in interaction type, feedback timing, and feedback intensity. Across 26 controlled prototypes and three exploratory mixed-methods online studies, juicy feedback generally improved engagement and aesthetic experience, showed a smaller task advantage and the strongest gains when feedback followed and an interaction as a reinforcing cue; stronger intensity alone did not reliably help, and accounts repeatedly revealed a tension between reinforcement and distraction. These results position juicy feedback as a design material for how interaction “ feels ,” more than mere embellishment, to be applied deliberately and with attention to timing, task, and interpretive load. We hope JuicyVIS offers a vocabulary, a set of exemplars, and an empirical starting point for visualization feedback that is not only functional, but experientially legible and alive.
