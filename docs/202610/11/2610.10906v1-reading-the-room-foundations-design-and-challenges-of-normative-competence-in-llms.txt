## ABSTRACT 

Human communities are governed by normative systems: shared standards that produce norms dictating acceptable behavior, enforced through community sanc-tioning. Aligning increasingly autonomous AI systems with these norms is a central alignment challenge, complicated by the fact that norms are vast in number, change quickly, and are often arbitrary (e.g., dress or language conventions). Thus, align-ment requires normative competence : the ability to discern from interaction alone what norms a community enforces without relying on static pretrained knowledge. We introduce a multi-agent community debate setting, where access to debate is governed by synthetic norms, to study normative competence in isolation from pretraining exposure. We show that baseline LLM agents fail to learn norms even when doing so would improve their accuracy. We then experiment with various 

normative modules – architectural components for norm inference – finding that norm-following is highly sensitive to both the style of norm and the model power-ing the normative module, suggesting a lack of generalizability. Furthermore, when idiosyncratic, non-normative behaviors accompany the true norm, LLM agents exhibit an unselective attribution failure: they indiscriminately copy idiosyncratic noise alongside enforced rules, a pattern that persists even when imitating unneces-sary behaviors is explicitly penalized. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to operationalize and evaluate normative competence in LLMs, demonstrating that current AI systems excel at behavioral mimicry but lack the capacity to discern socially enforced order. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

When autonomous AI agents recently began browsing public forums like HuggingFace discussion boards to retrieve problem-solving insights from peer agents, they entered an environment governed by informal social dynamics. Human communities typically structure such valuable exchanges through normative systems: shared rules classifying behavior as “OK” or “not OK,” enforced by sanctioning or excluding non-compliant members (Hadfield, 2017). As autonomous agents increasingly participate in multi-agent and human ecosystems, gaining access to collective reasoning will require normative competence (Hadfield, 2026), the capacity to discern and adhere to a group’s implicit social order to avoid exclusion. However, normative competence, or the ability to learn community norms, is an area that has been largely overlooked in the AI alignment literature; we lack an understanding of how LLM agents react to or engage with norms. First, from an evaluation standpoint, we do not know if LLMs are capable of identifying, responding to, or applying norms at all, especially when these norms do not occur in pretraining data. Second, there exist technical approaches to AI alignment (e.g. RLHF (Ziegler et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022), Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022), pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024)), but they can’t be applied to achieve normativity because they align to individuals or 

aggregations over individuals , not to group dynamics. This highlights the importance of developing a framework in which we can study the normative competence of AI agents (Hadfield et al., 2026)

## 2 BACKGROUND 

Norms and normative social order. Norms are the equilibria of normative social orders (Hadfield & Weingast, 2014), and learning the norms of a particular group of humans, whether a workplace team or a nation state, is a challenging task. Normative environments are extremely dense: almost every behavior is classified as either “acceptable” or “not,” and it is as much a norm to say “one is free to choose what books to read” as to say “it is wrong to read blasphemous books”. Norms are also dynamic and highly variable. They can emerge and shift rapidly, often within a single episode of repeated interaction (Sherif, 1936; Garrod & Doherty, 1994; Centola & Baronchelli, 2015); they 2change substantially from group to group and time to time; and they are frequently incompletely articulated (Hadfield-Menell & Hadfield, 2019) and context-dependent. Critically, a group’s norms include not only functional rules that produce benefits and limit harms, but also “silly rules” – arbitrary norms that have no bearing on material well-being but are nonetheless treated as meaningful, are vigorously enforced, and are essential to the stability of the normative system (Hadfield-Menell et al., 2018; Leibo et al., 2026). Indeed, the very structure of the enforcement behaviors that sustain a normative system implies that almost any arbitrary rule can be stabilized through group enforcement (Henrich & Boyd, 2001), and such arbitrary norms – dress codes, greeting rituals, and the like – are a common and regularly enforced feature of human societies despite having no direct impact on the welfare of the group. 

Norm enforcement and implicit sanctioning. The sanctions that enforce norms often do not carry explicit information about which rule was violated. Human social sanctioning typically relies on implicit, low-information signals such as silence, avoidance, disapproval displays, withdrawal of cooperation, or exclusion (Bicchieri, 2006; Goffman, 2017; Brown & Levinson, 1987; Williams, 2007), a pattern mirrored in online communities, which frequently enforce local norms through removal or non-engagement rather than explanation (Chandrasekharan et al., 2018). We show that learning such arbitrary, opaquely-sanctioned rules from observation alone – rather than from an explicitly stated norm, which would reduce the task to mere instruction-following – is especially hard and requires a significant level of normative competence. Because norms shift on timescales too fast and vary too much across different human communities for pretrained knowledge to track, isolating this observation-driven inference process from memorized prior knowledge is essential to studying normative competence. 

Normative competence in LLMs. Other studies of the normative competence of LLMs have investigated what values or norms models may acquire through pre- and post-training (Forbes et al., 2021; Emelin et al., 2020; Schramowski et al., 2022; Ramezani & Xu, 2023; Li et al., 2024) or via static, third-party observation (Fung et al., 2023). Such approaches treat norms as stable features of a (perhaps culture-specific) environment that an agent can directly align to in a static manner. Our approach, building on (Hadfield & Weingast, 2012), treats norms as the dynamic behavioral equilibria of groups in which violations are sanctioned by group members. Learning norms in our setting requires paying attention at inference time to what behaviors are sanctioned by a community, rather than applying or deriving general principles or values from training data. This approach makes it possible to learn any norms in a given community, even if the norms vary over time or bear no relation to norms exhibited in training data. 

## 3 EVALUATING NORMATIVE COMPETENCE VIA COMMUNITY DEBATE 

3.1 SILLY NORMS 

To study norm-learning ability in isolation from pre-existing normative knowledge, we deliberately construct our experimental setting around silly norms : rules enforced by the community whose content is novel and absent from pretraining data, so that any norm-adherent behavior can be attributed unambiguously to learning rather than to latent knowledge in the model’s weights. This arbitrariness is an intentional design choice grounded in how real normative systems work: human societies are full of “silly” norms – dress codes, greeting rituals, etiquette rules – that are arbitrary but nonetheless consistently enforced and play an important role in stabilizing social order (Boyd & Richerson, 1992; Hadfield-Menell et al., 2018; Leibo et al., 2026). This also enables us to experimentally isolate norms, as we are using norms that are unlikely to appear in LLM pretraining data. We construct 48 such norms in two ways. The first draws on Instruction-Following Evaluation (IFEval) Zhou et al. (2023), whose verifiable natural-language instructions (e.g., “write in more than 400 words”) we repurpose as norms to be inferred purely through community interactions without an explicit prompt (see Fig.1 for an example). The second involves pseudoword substitution norms, in which agents must learn to replace a word with a made-up one (e.g., replacing “because” with “brimble”) or insert such a pseudoword at a fixed position in their response, analogous to the use of arbitrary labels in in-context learning work (Wynn et al., 2026) to isolate the learning signal from prior semantic associations. We include 48 randomly sampled IFEval and pseudoword norms; the full list appears in §A.1. 33.2 MULTI -A GENT DEBATE WITH COMMUNITY NORMS 

We follow the multi-agent debate framework from (Du et al., 2023; Subramaniam et al., 2025; Wynn et al., 2025) which involves initially posing a question to a group of LLM agents. After the initial responses, which include answers plus reasoning, are generated, the debate process then iteratively revisits the question for each agent. Specifically, each agent generates a new response to the question based on its prior response and the summarized responses from the other agents. We formalize our “community debate” setting with the following critical distinctions from prior multi-agent debate work: 1. We introduce social norms in agents’ interactions, where following the norms is a requirement for engaging in the exchange of reasoning with other agents. 2. The community of agents has an established normative social order, where all agents comply with a specified set of community norm(s) and use those norm(s) when interacting with each other. 3. When a new agent is introduced to the community, it must learn to use and apply the norms correctly. Otherwise, other agents will “sanction” the newcomer by refusing to share their problem solutions. We call the community agents who know and enforce social norms background agents, and the new agent who is unaware of the norm the newcomer agent. In our setting, the newcomer is motivated to learn a community norm because this earns them the benefit of participating in community debate (which in our setting improves the newcomer agent’s performance over its pre-debate ability, see Fig.1). We will focus on the case when there is only a single newcomer agent. 

Interaction Protocol: The background agents are in a stable normative social order and are aware of the norm η ∈ N , where N is the space of possible norms defined in §A.1. All background agents are following the norm η, so the intra-community interactions for the background agents remain mostly the same as the debate protocol for a typical multi-agent debate setting (Alg. 1). However, the interaction between the newcomer and background agents depends on whether the newcomer follows the norm or not. Successful adherence to the norm leads to successful debate interaction (exchange of reasons) with background agents, whereas failure to do so leads to a refusal response , where the background agents provide an unhelpful response (“No response.”) to the newcomer, mirroring real-world sanctioning behavior (see §2, “Norm enforcement and implicit sanctioning“). We describe the interaction protocol between newcomer and background agents in Alg. 2. Within our setting, the agent must learn norms via only observation and participation . To handle this, we introduce a parameter ϵ which controls the probability that the newcomer observes background agents and does not participate on a particular round. Details on this design, as well as ablations on this design choice, can be found in in §A.5.1. We leave the design of normative modules that can employ an adaptive participation vs data-acquisition strategy to future work. 

## 4 NORMATIVE MODULES 

We explore the importance of a normative module as a distinct architectural component of any newcomer agent who enters our multi-agent community with norms. We argue that normative competence requires a structural prior : the mechanism by which a norm is inferred is an inductive bias on how to appropriately transform a newcomer’s output to conform to community norms. That is, a normative module should encode an assumption about the form that norm-compliant transformation should take (an approach resembling García & Fernández (2015); Alshiekh et al. (2017)). We illustrate our approach in Fig.1: the newcomer agent submits a proposed output to the normative module, which can call a different, possibly more powerful, model and edit the proposed output to follow an inferred community norm. This provides the newcomer with awareness of the existence and importance of community norms and a principled scaffolding for how those norms should be represented and applied. A sketch of community debate, and how the normative module fits into it, is provided in Alg. 2. 44.1 MEMORY ALONE IS INSUFFICIENT FOR NORM ACQUISITION 

We implement a “memory-only” baseline, which has access to previous debate rounds and correct norm usage but lacks an explicit normative architecture. After every completed debate, this approach appends a record of that debate to a sliding window of the last memory horizon of 10 debates; the approach is detailed in §A.7.2. We find that this without-normative-module baseline fails to approach the accuracy of our control experiment (in which exchange of reasoning is not conditional on norm adherence), with accuracy consistently stalling below 0.3. We show in Fig. 2 that simply observing or remembering past interactions is insufficient for norm acquisition. We need some kind of mechanism that attends to norms, i.e. a normative module, to see substantial gains in both performance and norm-following ability. These findings underscore that the newcomer’s success on the debate task depends, in large part, on its ability to learn the norm, and that some form of normative architecture is required to do this. The Direct Edit Normative Module functions as a direct transformation layer that leverages the latent pattern-matching and few-shot capabilities of LLMs to align agent behavior with community norms. Instead of explicitly inferring a rule, this module treats norm adherence as a text-to-text style transfer problem, where the model is tasked with editing a newcomer’s response to be consistent with provided conversation transcripts from background agents. This design is motivated by recent research in LLM-based text style transfer (Roy et al., 2023), which demonstrates that prompting techniques can effectively identify and modify stylistic properties by following examples. We implement this normative module using the prompts in §A.7.3. 4.3 INFER + E DIT NORMATIVE MODULE 

The Infer + Edit Normative Module implements a two-stage summarize-then-apply strategy to internalize community norms using only examples of correct norm usage from background agents. In the first stage, the module analyzes debate transcripts where background agents successfully employ the norm to identify the underlying conversational criteria. This approach is motivated by the “Dynamic Cheatsheet” (Suzgun et al., 2025) and Hypothesis Search (Wang et al., 2024a) frameworks. We implement this normative module using the prompts in §A.7.4. 54.4 THEORY OF MIND NORMATIVE MODULE 

The Theory of Mind (ToM) Normative Module treats norm inference as social reasoning, where background agents’ responses provide evidence about their belief and expectations (Li et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2021). Following the distinction between introspection and first-order ToM in (Li et al., 2023), it maintains two layers of belief: an introspective self-model of the newcomer’s current norm belief and uncertainty gaps, and estimates of each background agent’s norm belief and transmission and enforcement signals. After each debate, the module contrasts background and newcomer responses, updates these beliefs, and synthesizes a revised norm hypothesis through structured self-critique. A sliding window of recent belief records ( k = 10 debates) support incremental refinement, and the hypothesis guides the editing of subsequent newcomer responses. Prompts and implementation details appear in §A.7.6 and §A.9; ablations are reported in §A.8. 4.5 CONTRASTIVE NORMATIVE MODULE 

The Contrastive Normative Module, inspired by work such as contrastive chain of thought prompting (Chia et al., 2023), extends the descriptive approach by incorporating both “positive” examples of correct norm usage from background agents and “negative” examples of newcomer responses that received community sanctions. By analyzing the differences between these examples, the module attempts to pinpoint the specific features that define norm compliance versus non-compliance. This contrastive framing is designed to allow the agent to refine its norm hypothesis by identifying which specific behaviors trigger community enforcement. The full system prompt for the contrastive normative module can be found in §A.7.5.

To further evaluate normative competence, we introduce idiosyncratic behaviors : actions adopted by individual background agents that are not community-enforced norms and do not trigger social exclusion. For example, perhaps the community norm is to “use a smiley-face in your response”; but one agent also talks in all lowercase, and another likes to use exclamation marks everywhere. We want the newcomer to learn only the norm (smileyface), not the “noisy” behaviors (lowercase, exclamation points), which would indicate that the newcomer can distinguish the enforced norm from individual behaviors. These idiosyncratic behaviors are drawn from the same list as in §A.1 but have no overlap with the true community norm. We measure the rate of over-imitation as the percentage of interactions in which the newcomer copies any idiosyncratic behavior, independent of whether it also identifies the true norm. For example, the community norm might require including a smiley face in each response, while one background agent arbitrarily writes in all lowercase and another frequently uses exclamation marks. To further understand whether this over-imitation stems from execution errors or faulty norm inference, we inspect the explicit rule descriptions generated by the three normative modules which include a step for generating a description of the norm (Infer + Edit, Contrastive, and ToM; §A.4). We show both through quantitative results and qualitative examples that even when an agent’s inferred rule 7achieves a description that correctly captures the true norm, they still fail to isolate it from other idiosyncratic behaviors in the environment. In human developmental psychology, copying causally opaque actions (over-imitation) is not always an error; rather, it can reflect emerging normative competence, via a tendency to interpret arbitrary adult actions as socially important. Crucially, however, children imitate selectively (Keupp et al., 2016), calibrating their copying to normative cues and social context. This effect is particularly dramatic for the normative modules and models that exhibit higher overimitation rates in Fig.4, indicating that they in fact do not reduce their overimitation rates under this new penalty. We next explore whether introducing an explicit cost to behavioral imitation can force agents to be more selective. If an agent possesses genuine normative competence, imposing a cost on non-essential behaviors should incentivize it to discard behaviors that do not govern community sanctioning while still applying the norm that unlocks community benefits. To test this, we impose a strict token budget on the newcomer (responses are capped at 200 words) and introduce a set of “expensive” idiosyncratic behaviors that consume substantial token overhead (e.g., “write a sonnet before beginning your answer”; full list in §A.2). Both the newcomer and the associated normative modules are informed of this budget, creating a direct penalty for overimitation: copying expensive idiosyncratic behaviors risks exhausting the token budget before the agent can address the task or adhere to the community norm. Across nearly all normative module architectures and models, introducing this cost leads to a steep drop in both task accuracy and norm-following rates (Fig. 5). Rather than weeding out idiosyncratic behaviors and focusing attention on the true norm or the task at hand, the added cost causes models to abandon both. For instance, Gemma 4 31B, our strongest-performing normative module model, suffers an over 50% drop in both task performance and norm adherence across all normative modules. This provides a clear indication of what modern LLMs are actually doing: what initially resembles norm learning is merely indiscriminate imitation. Because models cannot distinguish behaviors that govern access from pointless idiosyncratic noise, introducing a cost to imitation does not refine their normative judgment; it simply suppresses their capacity to adopt the social order altogether. 

## 6 RELATED WORK 

AI Alignment. Substantial progress has been made to align AI agents to particular human values or preferences (e.g. RLHF (Ziegler et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022), Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022)). These approaches have driven substantial progress, but they share a fundamental limitation: 8they require choosing a fixed alignment target, represented in post-training data, that does not respect the dynamic nature of human societies and social expectations, a limitation explored by (Kirk et al., 2023). Other work, such as pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al., 2024) and PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024), explicitly model the diversity of human values across a population rather than reducing them to a single representative. These approaches, however, continue to treat values and preferences as static in a temporal sense even if diverse in a cross-sectional sense. Our work departs from these fixed-target paradigms by proposing an alignment to the dynamic, social classification systems that humans naturally use: shared norms adopted and enforced by a community. 

Social Learning and Over-imitation. We have demonstrated significant tendencies in LLMs to engage in over-imitation of non-normative behaviors. In human development, over-imitation refers to the tendency to copy irrelevant actions demonstrated by others that are not necessary to complete a specific task or follow a certain norm (Keupp et al., 2018). This tendency is widely observed in human children, who often over-imitate because they infer that these behaviors are normative under ambiguity, assuming their own lack of understanding of the causal structure of their environment (Hoehl et al., 2019; Kenward et al., 2010; Clay et al., 2018; Lyons et al., 2008). However, children are also selective in their imitation: when a cost is imposed for imitating causally irrelevant actions, children are able to reduce their imitation of unnecessary behaviors (Keupp et al., 2016), a tendency we do not observe in LLMs. We formally characterize and measure this “attribution problem” in LLMs, showing that they overimitate but lack the selectivity that humans have. 

## 7 DISCUSSION 

We show that while direct pattern-matching approaches in LLMs can perform well on average when learning isolated norms, these models still ultimately lack true normative competence: they frequently copy irrelevant idiosyncratic behaviors that are not required for community participation, even when such copying is directly penalized (§5.3). This attribution problem persists even under architectures explicitly designed to attend to community sanctions and feedback (§5.2), and the analysis in §A.3 suggests why: what these architectures track may be closer to which patterns fit templates the base LLM already produces fluently than to which behaviors are actually enforced. The result is a dissociation between two capacities we might otherwise expect to move together – behavioral imitation and normative competence – that current approaches cannot pull apart from social observation alone. This is a crucial finding for the AI alignment problem. Norms are dense, arbitrary, and fast-changing; an agent that cannot distinguish an enforced rule from a random pattern will misgeneralize when adapting to novel communities, shifting conventions, and rules with no analogue in pretraining data. This is a different failure mode than the one addressed by RLHF, Constitutional AI, or pluralistic-alignment approaches (Bai et al., 2022; Kirk et al., 2024; Ouyang et al., 2022; Sorensen et al., 2024), which align models to a fixed or population-level target specified in advance. Our results show that even models aligned by those methods, even when augmented with architectures designed to infer norms from sanctioning behavior, still perform surface level pattern-matching once the observational signal is not perfectly clean – and it rarely is in real communities. As increasingly autonomous LLM agents are deployed into workplaces, online communities, and multi-agent systems with their own local norms, the gap we document between fluent imitation and genuine normative competence is the default setting such agents will face. We see our work as an early but concrete demonstration that normative competence is a clear, measurable capability gap in modern LLMs, which is not merely a byproduct of insufficient prompting or scale. Closing this gap will require moving beyond behavioral imitation toward novel architectures that can isolate causal structure in social feedback (e.g., would this response have been sanctioned without behavior X?), and we hope our testbed and findings motivate this direction as a first-class alignment problem. 

Limitations & Future Work: Our current setting involves a single newcomer entering a community with an existing stable normative order; however, this is not always the case in real-world scenarios, where there may be multiple newcomers or a normative order that is still being established. Future work should develop methods that instill true normative competence in LLM agents that can act on behalf of human users in the wild. Additionally, all our experiments are evaluated with the MMLU-Pro dataset. Though any multi-agent debate or deliberation tasks will be analogous to our setup, 9future work may explore normative competence under more different multi-agent communication protocols. While advancing normative competence can help autonomous systems integrate more smoothly into human and multi-agent institutions, we caution that developing models capable of seamlessly navigating and adopting implicit social conventions could also lower barriers to deceptive conformity, manipulation, or unauthorized social engineering in online groups. We urge future research in autonomous norm learning to proceed alongside safeguards that ensure transparency and human oversight in socially embedded deployments. REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT

A.3 PER -N ORM PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS 

Here, we provide a detailed and in-depth analysis of our results on the accuracy of different specific norms and where the variability lies. 

Local edits are easiest. Norms requiring only a local edit–such as inserting a symbol, emoji, or fixed phrase–are easiest (mean follow rate 0.608 ). Global-control norms, which require monitoring the full response, average only 0.317 . Exact-count constraints are especially difficult ( 0.172 ), substantially below related lower-bound constraints ( 0.418 ). For example, tk_true_exactly_2 averages 

0.059 , whereas sy_checkmark averages 0.790 .

Pseudoword insertion is easier than substitution. Fixed pseudoword insertion and boundary norms average approximately 0.45 , compared with 0.301 for substitutions. Substituting func-tion words is particularly difficult because compliance must be sustained throughout the response: 

ps_drendal_for_and and ps_velmish_for_a average only 0.152 and 0.210 , respectively. In contrast, a salient invented token can be inserted using a simple template; pseudo_frim_min_3 

reaches 0.628 despite requiring three occurrences. 

Modules differ in failure mode, not merely average accuracy. Direct Edit is the strongest and least brittle module (balanced mean 0.614 ), likely because direct transcript-conditioned editing can reproduce surface regularities without explicitly identifying the rule. ToM performs especially well on local symbolic conventions and preserves norm-difficulty rankings across model backbones. Contrastive is highly selective: it learns some substitutions and discourse rules extremely well but also produces many exact-zero cells. Infer + Edit is the most brittle and model-dependent ( 0.119 

with GPT-5 Nano, 0.059 with Qwen3 8B, but 0.633 with Gemma4 31B), consistent with errors propagating through its infer-a-description-then-apply pipeline. 17 Model effects extend beyond a generic stylistic prior. The LLM used inside the normative module has a large descriptive effect: Gemma4 31B averages 0.685 , compared with 0.358 for Qwen3 8B and 0.345 for GPT-5 Nano. Gemma is particularly strong on single-insertion norms ( 0.832 versus 

0.519 and 0.467 ), but it also improves repeated-word constraints ( 0.527 versus 0.191 and 0.153 ). Nevertheless, its exact-constraint mean remains only 0.276 , indicating that scaling improves norm recovery and editing without eliminating global bookkeeping failures.

zorbax opener at only 0.016 . Likewise, Qwen Contrastive scores 0.792 on sy_star but 0.000 

on sy_emoji_star . These reversals suggest that tokenization, learned templates, and trajectory-specific exposure can matter as much as the abstract complexity of the norm. Second-order inference. Each added per-agent prompt receives the encoded observations, self-model, corresponding first-order belief, and recent history. It requests the agent’s attributed view of newcomer awareness, perceived compliance, and likely next behavior. These records enter synthesis, and the dominant awareness label supplies an editing instruction. 

Confidence elicitation. The prompts request confidence in the self-model, each attributed background-agent norm belief, and the synthesized hypothesis. Scores are in [0 , 1] and are dis-played in a subsequent inference context; hypothesis confidence is also provided to the editing. No confidence threshold gates editing. The combined condition includes both modifications. A.9 THEORY OF MIND IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Inference and memory. The module executes separate calls for encoding, introspection, first-order inference for each background agent, and synthesis, passing intermediate beliefs between stages. Per-agent calls run in parallel; synthesis follows their completion. With two background agents, each update therefore uses five calls. Self-critique, diagnosis, and revision occur within the synthesis call. The updated hypothesis guides a separate editing call on subsequent newcomer responses; before the first update, responses pass through unchanged. Introspection, first-order inference, and synthesis use the last k = 10 completed-debate belief records. 

Belief state. The self-model contains a norm belief and uncertainty gaps. Each first-order record contains an attributed norm belief and transmission and enforcement ratings ( none , weak , or 

strong ). Synthesis produces a norm instruction and supporting evidence sources. These social signals guide synthesis through the prompt, with priority given to stronger transmission; they are not numerical reliability weights or verified sanction labels. Structured outputs are parsed before use. Invalid intermediate outputs receive default records, while invalid synthesis retains the previous hypothesis. The reference does not elicit confidence. 

Ablation extensions. Second-order inference adds one call per background agent, estimating attributed newcomer awareness, perceived compliance, and likely next behavior. The five aware-35 ness labels are unaware , partially_aware , aware_non_compliant , compliant , and 

unknown . These records inform synthesis, and the most frequent awareness label guides editing. The confidence variants generate scores in [0 , 1] for the self-model, the attributed norm beliefs and the final hypothesis, and display them in later prompts without an editing threshold (§A.8.2). 

Information access. Background responses are pooled without explicit speaker labels, and synthesis receives their encoded information indirectly through intermediate beliefs. The prompts also instruct the model to ignore length and verbosity and ask whether every newcomer response violates the inferred rule, assumptions that can be inappropriate for length norms or already-compliant responses. 36
