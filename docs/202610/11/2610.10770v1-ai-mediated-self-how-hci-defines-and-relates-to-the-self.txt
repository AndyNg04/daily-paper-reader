## Abstract 

How might AI alter how we understand and experience the self? This scoping review analyzes 102 papers to examine how the self is defined in the field of human-computer interaction (HCI), how AI– self relationships are conceptualized, and what risks emerge when AI becomes entangled with selfhood. Our synthesis makes three contributions. First, we define AI-mediated self as a conceptual um-brella that connects dispersed work across education, workplace, health, and creative practices. Second, we consolidate six framings of the self with four domains of ethical risk—agency/autonomy, identity/authorship, relational capacity, and meaning-making—into a conceptual map that provides a reusable vocabulary across con-texts. Third, we introduce the Inclusion of AI–Self framework, which situates AI–self relationships along a spectrum of proximity. To-gether, these contributions position selfhood as a central design space in HCI.

## 1 Introduction 

The self stands as one of humanity’s most complex concepts. To be human is to continuously construct narratives into coherent ac-counts of who we are, how we connect, and who we might become. If our sense of self is constructed from our memories of the past and plans for the future, what will happen if AI distorts them? Today, Generative AI represents the latest, perhaps the most invasive, technology in reshaping the self. Personalized AI systems now have the potential to generate content that allows users to integrate into their self-narratives, blurring the boundary between self-generated and system-generated expression. These interactions extend into increasingly personal parts of people’s lives. For exam-ple, the emerging cases of AI psychosis highlight how LLM chatbots are changing people’s self-perception and awareness [18]. Beyond the individual, people are developing intimate bonds with AI com-panions like Replika or interacting with AI “clones” of deceased loved ones [2, 94]. As a consequence, the self increasingly emerges through interaction with technical systems, part autobiographical, part computational, continuously co-constructed with algorithmic artifacts. This subtle but pervasive shift has raised attention in both public discourse (e.g., discussions on AI existential risks) [24, 46] and academic research (e.g., the call for papers on Agency and Iden-tities for ACM CUI 2026 [1]). With the explosive growth in this area, it is clear that AI is reshaping people’s sense of self in unprece-dented ways. As HCI researchers, we urgently need to establish better understanding of AI-self interactions. In this paper, we raise three central research question: 

• How has HCI research defined self in HCI? 

• How has HCI research conceptualized the relationships between AI and self? 

• How has HCI research identified risks in AI-self interactions? We presented a scoping review of 102 publications from ACM SIGCHI venues, the International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction, and the Human-Computer Interaction journal from 2013 to 2025, and our analysis of these publications identified six distinct framings of the self, four types of AI-self relations, and four categories of risks that appear across the literature. We made three contributions: 

• A glossary of self-related definitions in HCI; 

• A framework mapping AI-self relations, including how AI sys-tems shape, interact with, or participate in self-related processes; 

• A set of design recommendations for system designers, ethics researchers, and behavioral scientists based on observed risks and harms. We concluded the paper with opportunities for future research, where we see understanding the self in relation to AI systems as essential for building resilience against GenAI’s tendency of distort-ing the self. To shape a more desirable future, we need to understand how researchers currently study and design these systems, map the existing and emerging design approaches, and provide researchers and designers with frameworks to navigate this space thoughtfully. arXiv:2610.10770v1 [cs.HC] 7 Oct 2026 Fu et al. 

## 2 Background 

The study of self is fundamental to HCI because it shapes how users interact with, interpret, and integrate technology into their lives. From avatar customization to social media filter selection, HCI de-sign decisions directly impact users’ sense of agency. As interactive systems become more personally adaptive and socially embedded, understanding self-concept becomes crucial for designing ethical experiences that empower users’ psychological resilience and iden-tity coherence. We clarify our motivation in Section 2.1, offer our definition of AI in Section 2.2, and provide a review of the HCI tradition on self research in Section 2.3. 

## 2.1 Why Self? Why Now? 

Our scoping review examines the nature of research and identifies gaps in the existing HCI literature on AI and self, which at its broadest, refers to an individual’s internal concept of who they are [31]. Self plays a critical role in motivation, emotion regulation, and social interaction. It provides continuity across time, helping individuals make sense of their past, plan future actions, and build a coherent identity. The self-concept supports essential psychological functions, including life story continuity, value-consistent behavior, and the foundation for social relationships. Since the study of self is inherently multifaceted, to develop a comprehensive search strategy that would not be constrained by any single disciplinary lens, we surveyed these related fields to identify key constructs and these theoretical references directly informed our search term selection (Section 3.2). AI and self is an emerging area in HCI. Previous work in per-sonal informatics, robotics, and XR shows how technologies me-diate people’s self-understanding, and current GenAI applications has shifted interactive systems from tools to co-authors, moving questions of ownership and agency from theoretical considerations to immediate design challenges. Public discussion of terms such as “AI psychosis [45]” and “rent a human for your AI [92]” indi-cates growing attention to these systems, and people are already discussing the unintended harm and consequences. Research ad-dressing these phenomena has emerged across multiple domains, including education, healthcare, and creative practice, but findings remain fragmented across different subfields and theoretical tra-ditions. This review synthesizes 102 studies to assemble shared terms and map recurring understanding of the self. Our position is that how HCI characterizes AI-self relationships will influence both research and the systems that are built. 

## 2.2 What is AI? 

Defining AI is difficult as its boundaries shift from yesterday’s breakthroughs to today’s infrastructure. While “AI” was once a relatively niche technical term [11], it is increasingly being adopted into different forms of user-facing interactive systems [96]. Ad-vances in large language models have catalyzed widespread public interest in AI, drawing researchers beyond the traditional AI re-search community to include practitioners and researchers from philosophy, design, and social sciences. HCI, as an inherently in-terdisciplinary field, is particularly positioned to engage with this complexity. Previous work in human-AI interaction has noted this challenge: the technical boundary of AI is disputed even within AI research communities and continuously evolving [77]. 

2.2.1 Scope of AI in This Review. For this review, rather than adopt-ing a universal definition, we scope “AI” functionally. AI refers to in-teractive and user-facing systems that participate in self-related ac-tivities. We consider both implemented systems and explicitly envi-sioned systems discussed in conceptual or design-oriented research. Their relevant functions include generating content, conducting dialogue, making predictions or classifications, and recommending or personalizing information. This scope includes generative AI and large language models, conversational agents, recommender sys-tems, and intelligent assistants supporting cognition and creativity. We exclude backend automation and AI used only for infrastructure. Thus, our conclusions concern the literature identified within this operational scope and the selected publication sources. The search strategy and its coverage limitations are reported in the Methods. 

## 2.3 HCI tradition on Self Research 

HCI’s study on selfhood has grown through waves of technologies, with each wave importing different theories, units of analysis, and design goals. In the sections below, we trace each tradition to surface what HCI collectively knows about technology and the self. The beginning of HCI research on the self emerged from stud-ies of early online communities and virtual environments. Goff-man’s theory [39] of self-presentation became a foundational frame-work, with researchers examining how interface design shapes the “stages” on which users perform different aspects of selfhood. Turkle’s work [87] on online multiplicity revealed how users exper-imented with different aspects of identity across digital contexts, challenging assumptions about unified, stable selfhood. Research on multi-user dungeons and early social platforms demonstrated that digital environments afforded new forms of identity play and self-exploration that were previously unavailable in offline contexts. These early-stage works ground the foundation of design lessons on how different modality enables selective self-presentation, and designers should provide users with flexible tools to experiment with their identity. The emergence of personal informatics and self-tracking tech-nologies marked the progression in HCI’s engagement with self-hood. Research in this domain has examined how self-monitoring technologies shape self-awareness, behavior change, and personal meaning-making [28, 30]. The quantified self movement introduced technologies that promised to reveal users’ “true self” through data collection and analysis. However, studies of fitness trackers, mood trackers, and life-logging systems have also revealed more complex dynamics [3, 19, 78]. Rather than straightforward self-improvement, these technologies often generated anxiety and self-criticism. To support better self-awareness in context, personal informatics re-searchers have used design principles such as personalized visual-izations for self-tracking systems, social feedback to enable com-munity engagement, and embedded tracking practices to foster collective support. At the same time, another strand of HCI research has explicitly positioned self-development as a design goal. This approach drew on positive psychology and self-determination theory to create AI-Mediated Self: How HCI Defines and Relates to the Self 

frameworks for evaluating whether technologies support or un-dermine psychological well-being and authentic self-expression. In Zimmerman’s work, “design for the self” introduced a design focus on helping people become the person they desire to be through product interactions. Zimmerman [103]’s approach is grounded in product attachment theory, which describes how people develop emotional connections to possessions through meaning-making and identity construction. Unlike static objects that people select to represent who they are, interactive technologies can actively partic-ipate in identity formation through their behavioral responses and adaptive capabilities. Drawing on findings to support a better self, HCI researchers have designed artifacts to support better self-paced learning experiences by aligning course design to users’ values, and customization in mixed-reality. In the critical and humanist HCI space, researchers drew on Fou-cault [34]’s “care of the self” to examine how digital technologies mediate self-reflections and therefore shape who they are. Critical HCI theories challenged previous HCI assumptions about stable and coherent selfhood by demonstrating that technology and de-sign are not neutral, and they create power and exacerbate existing societal issues, thus producing impacts that transform users’ iden-tities For example, Bardzell et al. [9]’s research on Second Life revealed how avatar design functions as a “technology of the self,” where users engage in ask¯ esis (labored self-consideration) through practices such as gender exploration and intimate role-play that blur the boundaries between the user and their digital representa-tion. Åhman [104]’s critical analysis of “Conceptualizing the Self” draws on theories by Mark C. Taylor, using poststructuralism to reframe technology from a user-tool dynamic to what Taylor calls “existential co-constitution,” where interactive technology becomes an “existential arena” and interaction itself an “existential process” that constitutes selfhood. To surface power dynamics and trans-late insights into practices, researchers leverage design methods, including participatory design, fieldwork, and longitudinal obser-vations, to closely examine how users navigate their agency in sociotechnical ecosystems. While this diversity reflects genuine differences in technological affordances and design contexts, it creates a practical problem as AI systems become increasingly general-purpose. To examine what HCI collectively understands about technology and selfhood, we are motivated to conduct the literature review presented in the current paper. 

## 3 Method 

We conducted a scoping review to map how HCI research defines the self, conceptualizes relationships between AI systems and the self, and characterizes risks arising from those relationships. To ensure our search terms were theoretically comprehensive and captured the full range of self-related constructs across different research traditions, we identified working search terms from social science and humanities literature. We report the review following the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines [85]. In the following section, we describe the information sources, the boundary of the review, eligibility criteria, screening process, data charting, and analysis. 

## 3.1 Information Sources 

We purposively selected publication sources that cover general HCI research and subfields in which user-facing AI and self-related ques-tions commonly appear, following the source-selection approach of prior HCI reviews [30, 70, 83]. This include 12 conference and pro-ceedings sources and 3 leading journals: CHI, CSCW, DIS, IUI, UIST, HRI, CUI, HAI, UbiComp, UMAP, Creativity & Cognition (C&C), and Interaction Design and Children (IDC), ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction (TOCHI), the International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction , and Human-Computer Interaction .We considered additional engines (e.g., Google Scholar, Scopus), but opted not to include them due to scale (e.g., “AI and Self” returns 6,760,000 articles on Google Scholar). This source selection creates a bounded map of HCI research and our findings should therefore be read as patterns in the selected HCI sources, not as estimates of all research on AI and the self. 

## 3.2 Eligibility Criteria 

We assessed eligibility along three separate dimensions: publication scope, AI involvement, and engagement with the self. Separating these decisions was important because a paper could concern a user-facing AI system without examining the self, or could mention a self-related term while using “AI” only for a backend or technical component. Table 1 states how we applied each criterion. Papers were retained only when they met all three. We applied the operational scope defined in Section 2.2.1. This scope allows comparison across changes in the technologies and terms used from 2013 through 2025. Inclusion in the review does not imply that rule-based chatbots, recommender systems, predictive models, and large language models have equivalent capabilities. It indicates that the source paper positioned the system within the review’s user-facing AI boundary and connected it substantively to the self. 

## 3.3 Search Strategy 

We developed the search query through three pilot rounds before conducting the final search. In the first round, we found a broad query combining “AI” and “self” returned 36,252 records in the ACM Digital Library and produced many technical false positives, including self-driving and self-supervised learning. In the second round, we specified the self-related terms using constructs drawn from cognitive science, psychology, and sociology [10, 23, 37, 39, 47, 50, 62, 63, 79]. We then validated this strategy through citation searches on key papers and confirmed adequate retrieval of rel-evant literature. This substantially reduced false positives while capturing the multidimensional nature of self as defined. We then combined the self-related terms using OR so that a paper could be retrieved through any one of the selected construct. Furthermore, we tested broader variations (e.g., adding “personhood,” “subjectiv-ity,” “autonomy”) but found they either yielded redundant results already captured by our existing terms or introduced false posi-tives falling outside our working definitions of self-constructs. In the third round, we expanded the AI-related terms to account for changes in HCI terminology over time. ## 3.4 Overview of the Final Corpus 

The searches identified 325 records before screening. Following the PRISMA-ScR guideline[86], we removed 45 records before title screening because they were duplicates, incomplete records, or papers for which the full publication could not be retrieved. We screened the remaining 280 titles and excluded 37 records that clearly used “self” in a technical sense, fell outside the selected publication scope, or did not concern user-facing AI. We then as-sessed 243 abstracts against the full eligibility criteria. The first and final authors reviewed these abstracts independently, discussed disagreements, and consulted the full text when the abstract did not provide enough information. This stage excluded 141 records. The first author then conducted a full-text confirmation of the remain-ing 102 papers, all of which were retained for analysis. The final corpus therefore consisted of 102 papers and spanned publications from 2013 through July 2025. Figure 2 presents the records at each stage. 

## 3.5 Data Extraction and Coding 

The paper was the unit of analysis. We aimed to analyze 102 papers consistently, despite the fact that many of these papers discussed the concept of ‘self’ and ‘AI’ abstractly and vaguely. We addressed that by (1) immersive reading and data extraction by the first author, (2) collaborative codebook development through iterative team discus-sions, and (3) thematic synthesis to construct analytic narratives. The first author read the full text of all 102 papers across three analytic passes. For each paper, the first author recorded descrip-tive information including research context, population, research question, method, and AI system, and wrote structured summaries addressing each review question. These paper-level summaries formed the initial coding material. Using the first version of the codebook, the first author coded a sample of 20 papers and brought the coded cases and supporting ex-cerpts to the full research team. The fram-ings direct attention to different aspects of this question: people’s perceived abilities, their social relationships, their expression of themselves, their practices of meaning-making, their values, and their life stories. 4.1.1 The Self as Psychological Needs (n=59). When a user com-pletes a difficult task with a AI system, what do they learn about their own capabilities? The most common framing conceptualized the self through psychological needs and motivation to act. To study the self as psychological needs, researchers use experimental behav-ioral science method like surveys and performance metrics. Papers also use psychological theory to guide system design. For example, to enhance self-efficacy, Jeong et al. [49] designed a conversational progress guide with visual feedback like progress bars and subtask markers. Similarly, by applying psychological theories to support sense of belonging, projects like Elwahsh et al. [29] showed how custom multilingual chatbots can support emotion regulation and mental health for individuals when they are navigating multiple contexts. Feeling more capable, however, does not necessarily mean as-sessing one’s capabilities more accurately. In a contrasting example, He et al. [40] found that participants who overestimated their per-formance on reasoning tasks tended to under-rely on AI advice and lead to poorer human-AI performance. Supporting confidence is dif-ferent from supporting accurate self-assessment, and this framing makes the instability raised in our introduction concrete: AI-related interactions can become part of how people assess what they know and what they can do. We relate these concerns to Self-Determination Theory’s account of autonomy, competence, and relatedness [25]. Albert Bandura’s self-efficacy theory complements SDT by emphasizing belief in one’s ability to achieve goals as a key motivational driver [7]. Re-lated constructs include self-awareness, becoming the object of one’s own attention, alongside self-disclosure and self-talk as regu-latory and developmental processes [6, 10, 55].

4.1.3 The Self as Social Construct (n=49). When AI helps people communicate, how do relationships and social expectations shape who I am? This framing views the self as formed through social relationships and cultural contexts, including how people are un-derstood and recognized by others. Researchers examine these processes through co-design, surveys, and qualitative analyses of interaction. For example, Choi et al. [21] developed AACessTalk to support conversations between minimally verbal autistic children and their parents. The system provides conversational guidance to parents and recommends vocabulary cards to children. Their deployment study described how parents reconsidered their com-munication strategies and became more attentive to their children’s expressions and agency. We connect the cultural dimension of this framing to Markus and Kitayama’s distinction between independent and interdependent understandings of the self [62]. This distinction directs attention to how social expectations shape people’s self-understanding. We also relate this framing to Barad’s agential realism, which offers a way to examine how possibilities for action take shape through relations involving people and technologies [8]. These theoretical connec-tions help us examine how AI participates in the relationships and social conditions through which selfhood is constituted. 

4.1.4 The Self as Presentation and Disclosure (n=48). How does AI change the way I represent myself? The fourth framing con-cerns how people manage representations of themselves and decide what to reveal to others. Combining computational social science methods and design research analysis, HCI research illustrates how different technologies mediate performances across contexts. For example, in social media, self-presentation practices are shaped by both aspirations for a positive impression and barriers such as stigma or accessibility challenges [95]. Generative AI introduces new representational practices, from stylized portraits that allow exploration of idealized or “wannabe” images [81] to digital legacies that extend curated identities into the afterlife [58]. Artists employ AI for self-portraits to navigate identity and vulnerability, raising concerns about originality, authenticity, and fear of judgment [59]. Goffman’s account of self-presentation is explicitly cited by Wu et al. [95]. We extend the connection across these examples by interpreting presentation and disclosure as practices of selective exposure. AI mediates both the form of a representation and the decisions through which people revise what becomes visible about them. 

4.1.5 The Self as Practice of Transformation (n=46). Who am I be-coming through my interaction with AI? In this framing, the self develops through ongoing reflection and practice. To study the transformative self, researchers use qualitative and mixed-methods designs. The most common approach is using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke) to trace how individuals articulate change processes through technology interaction. HCI researchers apply this framing to design systems to scaffold reflective practice. For example, consider a team leader learning to be a better manager. In this context. the opportunity for transformation lies in how they see themselves and what kind of leader they want to become Arakawa and Yakura [4]. Similarly, “Mind Probes” explores generative AI for therapeutic writing and self-expression, helping users construct new narratives and imagine future possibilities [14]. AI, in this framing, may influence both how people pursue change and which versions of themselves they learn to pursue. We connect this pattern to Foucauldian accounts of care of the self , as discussed in HCI’s “turn to the self” [54]. This theoretical connection helps articulate the framing’s emphasis on working on oneself through reflection and self-examination. For HCI, this framing suggests how repeated AI interactions can influence users’ goals and self-understandings and raises the question of whether users are working toward a future they have considered and chosen, or following one that the system has made convincing. 

4.1.6 The Self as Narrative Identity (n=30). When AI helps me tell my story, how does it shape who I think I am? The final framing conceptualizes the self as narrative identity: the evolving story people construct about their lives that integrates past experience with imagined futures. This perspective emphasizes how AI assis-tance can change how an experience is interpreted in the resulting story. Empirical studies reveal multiple ways AI systems become entangled with narrative identity. HCI researchers commonly use mixed-methods designs that pair writing prompts (e.g., past events, future selves, goals) with outcome measures such as well-being, connectedness to the future self, or sense of control. In educational contexts, studies of college application essays show how adolescents use writing to articulate values and aspirations through “deep self-reflection”. AI writing assistants have the potential to guide “deeper self-exploration” and help adolescents articulate their values and lived experiences [93]. Similarly, in healthcare, pain self-reporting technologies frame narrative as the “gold standard” for capturing subjective experience, with voice-based reporting not only doc-umenting symptoms but also sparking richer self-awareness of fluctuations and associations [26]. Beyond the clinical, there are projects that explore how AI is implicated in afterlife narratives, where digital legacies raise questions about how identities are ex-tended, remembered, and narrated across time [58]. We interpret these narrative practices through theoretical ac-counts of the relationship between storytelling and identity. Key theoretical influences amongst the work include Dan P. McAdams and Pennebaker’s expressive writing research [73]. Building on McAdams [63]’s framework of the self as actor, agent, and author, McLean et al. [65] propose a “process model of self-development” articulating how “selves creating stories creating selves” highlights the reciprocal relationship between narrative and identity forma-tion. Pennebaker’s research demonstrates that writing about trau-matic experiences leads to improved health outcomes by helping individuals construct coherent accounts from emotional reactions to stress and trauma.

## 4.2 Four Conceptualization of AI-Self Relationship 

In the previous section, we described six different ways HCI litera-ture understands the concept of self. In this section, we shift focus to the relationship between self and the AI. Our goal is to examine what are the relationship researchers are envisioning and how they are building towards these relationships. We organized the cate-gories directly from the artifacts HCI researchers build, study, and prototype. Across the corpus, we observed recurring differences in (1) what kind of self-relevant work the system supports, (2) how users interact with AI contributions, and (3) who the outputs are for. We found four types of AI-self relationship: AI as a bounded cognitive tool, staying outside of the concept of self (n=69), AI as collaborative partner that support the self, (n=88), AI as as an intimate mirror that reflects the self (n=54), and finally, AI as an representative extension that “clones” the self (n=24). 

4.2.1 AI as Bounded Cognitive Tool (n=69). In this relationship, AI is conceptualized as a cognitive instrument under human oversight. These systems automate tasks while deliberately avoiding entangle-ment with questions of identity or selfhood. Their primary value lies in improving efficiency while preserving human agency as the primary locus of control. We identified three separate variations on this theme. The first in-volves using as cognitive enhancement , where HCI researchers have designed AI as decision support tools that prompt reconsideration of evidence or highlight cognitive blind spots, directly engaging users’ self-assessment and metacognitive awareness [13, 40, 100]. For example, He et al. [40] targeted overconfidence with performance-feedback interventions that revealed discrepancies between per-ceived and actual ability, which allow users to recalibrate self-assessments and develop appropriate reliance on AI. The second variation is workplace automation , where in organizational contexts AI usage is extended to managing workflows and tasks [72, 101]. Finally, predictive analytics involves using forecasting models to extend human analytic capacity by anticipating outcomes in time-series data such as treatment responses and relapse risks [84, 97]. 

4.2.2 AI as Collaborative Partner (n=88). In this relationship, AI is conceptualized as a partner in shared creation and development rather than a bounded tool. Here, the self engages in active nego-tiation of authorship with AI systems that function as co-actors. Across the corpus, three domains illustrate this relational dynamic: creative co-creation, coaching and development, and reflective part-nership. We identified three variations on this collaborative theme. The first is creative co-creation , where AI serves as a collaborator in artistic contexts whose outputs require ongoing negotiation and reshaping by human creators [36, 44, 56]. For example, Huang et al. [44] found that poets often attributed minimal credit to LLMs yet reported that AI’s rigidity and repetition paradoxically spurred creative agency, with resistance becoming generative material. The second variation is coaching and development , where AI functions as a developmental partner that scaffolds reflection while requiring human interpretation [4, 48]. Finally, reflective partnership involves systems that explicitly cast AI as a mirror for self-understanding through ambiguity [69, 88]. Van Der Burg et al. [88] introduced Objective Portrait, in which designers trained an object-detection model on personal fascinations and used the AI’s outputs to reveal their implicit perspectives for self-examination. 

4.2.3 AI as Intimate Mirror (n=54). Beyond collaboration, HCI re-searchers have designed AI systems that deeply integrate into self-exploration. These systems act as reflective surfaces that actively shape how individuals articulate their own experiences. What dis-tinguishes these applications from collaborative partnerships is their integration into the self’s inner dialogue. We identified three variations on this theme. The first involves in-trospective artifacts , where systems generate speculative insights by reconfiguring personal data into reflective perspectives that prompt temporal self-examination [14, 43, 89]. For example, Brand et al. [14] introduced Mind Probes and Vision Shrine, a set of speculative artifacts that transform personal information into multi-sensory journal entries and real-time goal visualizations. The second varia-tion is emotional processing , where AI creates novel materials that facilitate emotional self-understanding by transforming personal expressions into different artistic modalities [71, 90]. Take Park et al. [71] for example, the researchers developed NoRe, a system that transforms journal entries into personalized music. By creating ef-fective feedback loops, users can uncover aspects of their emotional state that they had not consciously recognized. Finally, memory and reminiscence involves AI scaffolding reflection across personal history while helping users articulate internal experiences that might otherwise remain ineffable [82, 98]. For example, Sun et al. [82] created ReminiBuddy, which employed a dual AI persona. One younger agent evokes uplifting memories, and another older agent fosters contemplative dialogues and facilitates nuanced forms of reminiscence and emotional reframing across different life stages. 

4.2.4 AI as Representative Extension (n=24). In this relationship, AI transcends internal reflection to act as a representative in social con-texts. Unlike intimate mirrors that facilitate private self-exploration, these systems extend the self into public interactions, mediating how others perceive and interact with the user. This creates com-plex tensions around authenticity, agency, and identity ownership as AI begins to speak for rather than with the individual. We identified three variations on this representative theme. The first is mediated self-presentation , where AI increasingly shapes how individuals present themselves to others, raising fundamen-tal questions about authorship and authenticity in self-expression Fu et al. 

[33, 38, 51, 91]. For example, Fitzsimons et al. [33] demonstrate how reliance on AI in college application essay produces less meaningful narratives, leading students to internalize generic story templates derived from training data rather than developing authentic per-sonal voice. The second variation is advocacy and social action ,where AI systems enable users to extend their voice and values into social contexts while raising questions about representation fidelity [66, 74]. Mun et al. [66] explored AI tools for counterspeech against online hate, with participants seeking systems that could “write a response EXACTLY LIKE I WOULD” and positioning AI as an extension of their moral agency and personal voice rather than a separate entity. Finally, posthumous representation involves systems that position AI as permanent stand-ins that outlast or replace human presence entirely [43, 58]. One example is how Lei et al. [58] examined “AI Afterlife” agents that simulate deceased individuals for posthumous interaction. By creating digital legacies that persist beyond death, participants in their study debated fun-damental questions of identity curation, such as whether to exclude painful traits and who should control updates over time. 

4.2.5 Synthesis. The four clusters show that HCI research is not discussing a single type of AI-self relationship but four types of relationships: from bounded cognitive tools that sharpen reasoning while remaining external to self-understanding, to collaborative partners that engage in co-creation and negotiation of meaning, to intimate mirrors that fold into inner dialogue and shape personal reflection, and finally to representative extensions that project the self outward into social interactions or even digital afterlives. A system can serve several of these functions, and reflection can arise within more than one relationship type. In the discussion section, we proposed proximity as a way to further interpretive question about these relationships. 

## 4.3 Four Interrelated Domains of Risks in AI-Self Interaction 

In the previous sections, we described how HCI research conceptual-izes the self (RQ1) and the types of AI-self relationships researchers are building toward (RQ2). In this section, in response to RQ3, we will describe concerns about risks researchers have raised from their studies about AI’s impact on selfhood, aiming to provide a starting point for future research and reflection discussions in the field. We cluster these concerns as: agency and autonomy, identity and authorship, relational capacity and emotional regulation, and confidence and meaning-making. 

4.3.1 Agency and Autonomy (n=72). The first domain of concern centers on how AI systems undermine people’s ability to exer-cise meaningful control over decisions that shape their lives. This domain includes both material autonomy (having genuine decision-making authority and the practical capacity to exercise it) and per-ceived autonomy (experiencing a subjective sense of meaningful freedom). We identify three interrelated concerns that researchers raised about how AI systems undermine autonomy. The first is ero-sion of material autonomy , where automation can directly remove humans from consequential decisions, reducing opportunities to practice judgment and initiative [72, 76]. The second concern is 

manipulation of perceived autonomy , where AI systems create an illusion of freedom while subtly predetermining choices [12, 68, 80]. One example is when Sankaran and Markopoulos [80] describes users of recommender systems who felt controlled by a “puppet master,” with participants reporting that hyper-personalization left them feeling labelled in boxes with apparent choices subtly prede-termined. The third concern is over-reliance and moral passivity ,where gradual transfer of responsibility to AI systems fosters disen-gagement from moral and cognitive agency [27, 48, 66]. Mun et al. [66] show that in counterspeech contexts, users often deferred re-sponsibility for confronting harmful content to AI chatbots, raising concerns in cultivating passive moral attitudes and reducing their own accountability. 

4.3.2 Identity, Authorship, and Self-Perception (n=62). The second domain of concern focuses on how AI systems intervene in pro-cesses of identity construction, authorship, and self-perception. Here, the core ethical stakes are authenticity, ownership, and the in-tegrity of self-expression. When AI contributes to identity-relevant work, it risks substituting generic or distorted narratives for gen-uine self-authorship, particularly in contexts where individuals are actively defining or presenting who they are. We identify three concerns within this domain. The first is shap-ing self-concept , where AI involvement in identity-defining tasks can bypass formative self-exploration and embed generic narra-tives during critical developmental periods [33]. For example, Fitzsi-mons et al. [33] show how U.S. adolescents using LLMs for college application essays produced narratives that were less personally meaningful and less representative of their authentic selves than those written independently, with students perceiving AI-generated essays as “higher-quality,” creating insecurity and diminishing con-fidence in their own abilities. The second concern is authorship negotiations , where creative and professional workers express un-ease about outsourcing their voice to machines [13, 14]. The third concern is stereotyping , where AI’s influence on self-perception is shaped by bias, with algorithmic outputs not merely misclassifying but actively reshaping how people see themselves [20, 22, 38, 52, 99]. Zanardi et al. [99] showed children becoming distressed when AI-generated portraits misrepresented their gender or age, with these errors challenging identity affirmation and sometimes prompting children to blame themselves. 

4.3.3 Relational Capacity and Emotional Regulation (n=57). The third domain of concern centers on how AI systems reshape peo-ple’s relational capacities and practices of emotional regulation. When AI functions as a conversational partner, mentor, or emo-tional support, it can provide affirmation and availability but also risks dependency, masking, displacement of human bonds, and erosion of psychological safety. We identify three concerns within this domain. The first is de-pendency and manipulation , where constant AI availability fosters emotional dependence and narrows relational repertoires, mak-ing it harder for users to sustain authentic human relationships [16, 32, 102]. The second concern is social camouflaging , where AI systems actively involve identity-erasing strategies that weaken self-acceptance while shaping interactional styles in ways that un-dermine relational authenticity, with concerns particularly acute for neurodivergent individuals [17, 38]. For example, Carik et al. [17] report that autistic participants feared AI tools for negative self-talk AI-Mediated Self: How HCI Defines and Relates to the Self 

might inadvertently reinforce masking behaviors, adapting users to neurotypical norms at significant emotional cost rather than scaf-folding genuine expression. The third concern is displacement of human connection , where AI systems designed to provide relational support may erode the conditions that make authentic relationships possible by offering a seemingly safer alternative to human vulner-ability [35, 93]. Wise et al. [93] reveal that when emerging adults faced challenges collaborating with human support networks on identity-related writing tasks, they frequently resorted to AI for assistance, with this reliance risking becoming a "cop-out" that displaced valuable human conversations and undermined "the most important source of their resilience: their bonds with friends and family.” 

4.3.4 Confidence, Reflection, and Meaning-Making (n=48). The fourth domain of concern highlights how AI systems intervene in individuals’ confidence, reflective practices, and processes of meaning-making. In this category, researchers caution that AI systems often distort self-perception by inflating or undermin-ing confidence, amplifying regret, homogenizing creativity, or destabilizing temporal continuity. We identify three concerns within this domain. The first is con-fidence distortions , where AI unsettles the delicate calibration of confidence-creating unwarranted certainty or hollowing out self-trust-without necessarily improving skill [36, 40, 49, 53, 60]. For example, Kollerup et al. [53] found that conversational agents de-signed to encourage health self-reflection boosted participants’ self-efficacy but also created unjustified confidence, with users overestimating their diagnostic ability and delaying seeking medi-cal help. The second concern is over-introspection and regret , where AI-supported introspection can amplify regret and reinforce nega-tive self-perceptions, tipping reflection into rumination rather than fostering resilience [43, 71]. Huang et al. [43] introduced self-clones that served as “Probes” of alternative life paths, with participants describing how seeing speculative futures made them fear they would “regret more or blame [themselves] more” for paths not taken. The third concern is destabilized self continuity , where AI destabilizes the temporal continuity of meaning, raising questions of legacy and identity over time [43, 58, 98]. Lei et al. [58] exam-ine “AI afterlife” systems that simulate deceased individuals, with participants appreciating the potential but fearing that simulations would drift from the person’s essence and potentially prolong their grieving process. 

4.3.5 Synthesis. Across the corpus, we identified four clusters of concerns about risks and found that there is a field-level anxiety about AI’s impact on selfhood. Across various application domains and fields, researchers have highlighted that the most significant risks are not catastrophic failures, but rather gradually diminishing: the subtle manipulation of choice architectures, the infiltration of narratives into personal expression, the gradual displacement of social connections, and the miscalibration of confidence. 

## 5 A Proximity Framework for AI-Self Relationships 

The four roles identified in RQ2 describe how AI participates in self-related activities. Comparing these roles brought attention to a further distinction: the work a system performs does not fully describe how people understand its contributions in relation to themselves. This distinction prompted us to introduce proximity as an additional interpretive lens. We define proximity as the de-gree to which AI contributions become incorporated into people’s self-understanding. Similar design purposes can support different relationships, and the same role can therefore be considered through more than one proximity configuration. For example, in Jeon et al. [48], participants experienced future-self letters as representations of themselves, whereas chat-based agents were often perceived as friends or counselors. We interpret this contrast as showing why a system’s intended role and its experienced relationship to the self should be examined separately. To articulate this dimension, we draw on the overlapping-circle representation of Aron et al.’s Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) framework [5]. This application of IOS is our theoretical extension of the corpus synthesis. We found the IOS framework effective in characterizing the AI-self relationships in our corpus because it captures the relational dimensions central to how HCI researchers position AI systems relative to users’ sense of self. Using this lens, we use Separated , Coordinated , and Integrated as proposed configurations for interpreting these relationships. We also identify a fourth configuration, Merged , at the end of the AI-self spectrum where the boundaries between self and AI dissolve entirely. While underrepresented in current literature, early work on digital twins and AI clones suggests this trajectory: systems that do not merely support or reflect the self, but act as the self in social and digital contexts. 

• Separated : Users treat AI as a bounded tool and do not con-sciously integrate its contributions into their self-concept. Decision support systems and grammar checkers exemplify this pattern, where users maintained explicit control and clear attribution boundaries, though unconscious influences on confidence or competence may still occur. 

• Coordinated : Users engage AI as a workflow partner while maintaining awareness of distinct contributions. The AI is perceived as an agent to “work with” rather than a passive tool. Examples include brainstorming aids or tutoring sys-tems where human-AI division of labor remains explicit. Users often develop meta-cognitive awareness of their ver-sus AI contributions. 

• Integrated : Users consciously integrate AI into identity-relevant processes, where outputs directly shape self-presentation, memory, or narrative identity. Examples include adolescents adopting AI-generated college essays, AI photo reconstructions influencing personal memory, and afterlife agents representing posthumous identity. 

• Merged : The boundary between personal thought and AI suggestion collapses. This mode represents the dissolution of clear self-AI boundaries, this can include AI “clones” that act in one’s place, or reports of “AI psychosis”.

## 6.2 Design Considerations for HCI Researchers 

Our findings reveal a field-level convergence: across diverse appli-cation domains and theoretical orientations, HCI researchers are concerned about remarkably similar ethical risks. This convergence presents an opportunity for us to build shared frameworks for ad-dressing AI’s impact on selfhood. For example, if you want to design your system to support brainstorming by helping people to become more confident, you want to make sure the system is supporting the cognitive process rather than love bombing the users. Those who study AI as a collaborative partner, be cautious about the consequences of promoting anthropomorphism. The framing and design for a partner indicate a form of relationship that people will build on with interactions. In human-human behavior, we often start as colleagues and then maybe become friends, but the design of some agentic features suggests the opportunity of deeper relationships and more context and trust building beyond a single context. Those who study AI as an intimacy mirror, self-reflection is a valuable practice, but reflection can spiral into rumination when systems continuously prompt introspection without supporting closure or action. AI systems that engage users’ narrative identity or facilitate emotional processing should design mechanisms that recognize when reflection becomes maladaptive. This requires both technical affordances (detecting patterns of repetitive negative self-focus) and interaction design that supports users’ agency to resist persuasive prompts for continued engagement. Moreover, those who study AI as Representative Extension, if your proxy made mistakes, is the human responsible? As these systems become more autonomous, the gap between human in-tention and AI execution widens. Designers must establish clear boundaries around proxy authority, create mechanisms for mon-itoring AI representatives’ actions, and ensure users understand their responsibility for outputs attributed to them.

Such work would advance prox-imity from a conceptual lens toward an empirical and designable construct, introducing more robust tools for analyzing, comparing, and deliberately shaping AI-self interactions. 

## 7 Conclusion 

This scoping review establishes the conceptual foundations for an emergent field: AI-mediated self, defined as a self that emerges when AI systems modify, augment, or generate aspects of self-expression, self-understanding, or other aspects of concepts of self. Our analy-sis of 102 papers reveals that HCI researchers conceptualize the self through six distinct framings (transformation, narrative identity, so-cial/cultural situation, psychological needs, presentation/disclosure, and values/meaning-making), engage AI systems across four lev-els of relationships (from bounded tools to representative exten-sions), and identify four primary risk domains (agency/autonomy, identity/authorship, relational capacity, and meaning-making) that emerge when AI becomes integrated into identity processes. We propose AI-mediated self as the umbrella term for identity processes that unfold through interaction with AI systems, encom-passing both conscious collaboration and unconscious influence. We envision AI-mediated self as a distinct area of research within HCI, characterized by: (1) focus on self understanding processes rather than task performance, (2) attention to proximity as a de-sign space, (3) longitudinal and developmental perspectives, (4) integration of multiple theoretical traditions from psychology and philosophy, and (5) explicit ethical consideration of authenticity and agency. This field’s emergence reflects a fundamental shift in hu-man–computer interaction: from AI as external tool to AI as participant in the most intimate processes of self-construction. As AI systems become more sophisticated and pervasive, understand-ing their role in self formation becomes essential. The frameworks, constructs, and ethical considerations outlined in this review provide initial foundations for this necessary work. Our ultimate goal is not to eliminate AI’s influence on selfhood but to ensure that this influence serves human developmental needs rather than constraining them. This requires sustained collaboration between technologists, psychologists, and ethicists to create systems that enhance rather than diminish the practices through which people construct meaningful, coherent identities.
