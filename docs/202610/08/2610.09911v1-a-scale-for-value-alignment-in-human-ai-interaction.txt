## Abstract 

Value alignment is a central objective in AI and HCI research, yet no validated instrument measures how users perceive it. This gap hampers the comparison and accumulation of findings and limits the effectiveness of applications, where understanding users’ view-points is critical. We construct and evaluate a 13-item psychometric scale that measures perceived value alignment across two compo-nents: value understanding and value manifestation. It is based on a large item pool drawn from prior empirical studies, filtered by experts, and finally assessed by users (N=607) across diverse AI scenarios. Confirmatory factor analysis on an independent sample (N=259) confirmed the two-factor structure and high internal con-sistency for both subscales. Using optimization, we also derived a 6-item short form for quick administration. The scale is a reliable measure, providing HCI researchers with a common evaluation metric across contexts.

## 1 Introduction 

Suppose a digital assistant handles your correspondence while you are away. A student asks for a thesis extension after a family emergency. Because you value consistency and have declined such requests before, the assistant sends a polite refusal in your name, citing your rules. Yet you would have granted this extension, at this severity, care for the student outweighs the rule. The wrong email has been sent by an assistant that had your information and your precedents, but not the values behind them. To design for alignment, or to reduce misalignment of this kind, it must first be measurable. The above example illustrates value alignment : the problem of ensuring that an AI system’s behavior is congruent with what its users consider important [29, 43]. As intelligent interactive systems grow more autonomous, often acting without constant human approval, the problem is becoming an increasingly important one [9, 16]. These systems build persistent models of their users [39], make proactive suggestions [10, 45], and act on users’ behalf [58]. However, while value alignment has originated from AI research, HCI research has a central role in tackling the problem. Alignment is not only a property of a model, but it is also an essential emergent consequence of interaction [12, 41]. How a system elicits what arXiv:2610.09911v1 [cs.HC] 7 Oct 2026 Hegemann et al. 

matters to its user [56], how it presents the reasons for its actions, and when it asks rather than acts [42] all shape whether users perceive its behavior as aligned. In current systems, this is ensured during the interaction itself: users prompt a system for one task at a time, inspect each result, and can reject or revise it. The increasing agency and generality of systems changes what should be evaluated in human–AI interaction. AI systems will act in situations that no study can realistically cover, so task-level measures say little about behavior beyond what was tested. How-ever, the constructs HCI relies on are of this kind. Trust [24, 46], human–AI teaming [33, 38], and shared mental models [3, 28] cap-ture whether a system can be relied on for a task, whether the team performs on it, and whether both hold compatible representations of it. What they do not capture is whether the human and the sys-tem agree on what is worth pursuing. An agent can be trusted, team effectively, and share a task model, and still pursue outcomes its user would not endorse, as in the opening example. Whether users perceive an agent as acting in line with their values generalizes beyond the tested situations, and therefore complements task-level evaluation for systems that act across tasks and contexts. So far, researchers have had no reliable way to measure whether users perceive an AI system as aligned with their values. HCI has already begun trying to measure value alignment, but without a common instrument. Our literature analysis (see section 5 showed that recent user studies evaluate aspects of “ alignment ” in various senses, yet rely on self-developed, non-validated scales [17, 21, 60]. There is also no instrument to adopt from AI research, either. Al-though aligning models with human values has driven research and industry for years [44], the community’s understanding of values is often ill-defined [43], and neither a common understanding of the construct nor an established way to measure it exists. Neither field, therefore, offers an instrument whose scores can be interpreted as perceived value alignment. The measures in use leave open whether they are sufficient, capture alignment or an adjacent construct such as trust or teaming, and whether their scores are reliable. Since every study measures differently, it is equally unknown whether results can be compared. A validated instrument is therefore needed now, while evaluation practices for interactive AI are still forming. We argue that such an instrument must generalize across tasks and environments and apply to any human–AI interaction in which a user can observe an agent’s behavior. Because users can judge an agent only by the behavior they observe, we develop a scale to assess perceived value alignment. A system’s internal objectives are not accessible to its users, and it is their perception that shapes the interaction between human and AI. To develop the Perceived Value Alignment Scale, we followed established best practices for scale development [7], which sepa-rate item development, scale development, and scale evaluation. Following the deductive method, we generated the initial item pool from items and constructs used in prior HCI work to assess related aspects of human–AI interaction, such as teaming [2] and shared understanding, thereby grounding the scale in the accumulated knowledge of the domain rather than in ad hoc formulations. In an expert study, we selected the 106 items with the highest content va-lidity. 607 users then rated 6 scenarios that varied in alignment level, use case, and interaction paradigm. Exploratory factor analysis re-vealed a 2-factor structure comprising value understanding and value manifestation. We tested the scale on an independent sample (N259) using confirmatory factor analysis. The resulting subscales showed high internal consistency, convergent, discriminant and known group validity. The scale allows researchers to evaluate perceived value align-ment as a dependent variable in user studies and to compare systems and design variants. In summary, we contribute (1) a theoretically grounded definition of perceived value alignment of interactive AI, (2) a validated psychometric scale, and (3) evidence for its factorial validity and internal consistency. 

## 2 Related Work 

Work relevant to perceived value alignment spans three strands: validated HCI scales for adjacent constructs, evaluation practices for AI value alignment, and HCI studies that assess alignment in specific interactions. In the following, we review each. 

## 2.1 Psychometric Scales 

HCI offers established scales for assessing perceptions and disposi-tions relevant to human–AI interaction. The Trust in Automation questionnaire assesses trust and related perceptions, including reli-ability, competence, and predictability [23]. These perceptions may overlap with perceived value alignment, but confidence in a sys-tem’s capabilities does not by itself establish whether its behavior reflects what matters to its user. Usability instruments, including the Usefulness, Satisfaction, and Ease of Use (USE) questionnaire [15] and the System Usability Scale (SUS) [25], assess qualities of using a system; a system may be easy to use while pursuing outcomes that conflict with the user’s priorities. Teaming and cooperativity scales focus on how humans and AI work together. Attig et al.’s Perceived Cooperativity Scale and Teaming Perception Scale assess an agent’s perceived coopera-tive capability and practice, and the sense of working together through mutual contribution and support, respectively [2]. The Human-Machine-Interaction-Interdependence Questionnaire simi-larly assesses perceived interdependence in driver–vehicle cooper-ation [51]. While it is plausible that alignment supports a system’s ability to cooperate with humans and work with them in teams, perceived value alignment should also apply when users observe an agent’s behavior without directly cooperating with it. Other scales address users’ affective responses or general disposi-tions toward technology. The Perceived Creepiness of Technology Scale includes implied malice, undesirability, and unpredictabil-ity [52]. Creepiness is a plausible consequence of misalignment, but not all misalignment implies malicious intentions by developers or makes a system less predictable. An agent may consistently pursue outcomes that conflict with a user’s values without being perceived as creepy. The Affinity for Technology Interaction (ATI) scale and its short form assess a person’s general tendency to engage with technology [14, 50]. They therefore characterize the user rather than their perception of alignment with a particular system after interaction. Perceived shared understanding is more closely related to our construct. ## 2.2 AI Value Alignment: Concepts and Evaluation 

For HCI, value alignment raises the question of whether an AI sys-tem’s behavior reflects what matters to its users [29]. AI alignment is assessed through several kinds of evidence, from preferences over model outputs to judgments formed during interaction. In language-model training, reinforcement learning from human feedback uses human comparisons to fit a reward model, whereas Direct Pref-erence Optimization learns directly from preferred–dispreferred response pairs [32, 35]. These comparisons operate at the preference level of the values–goals–preferences distinction introduced above. At evaluation time, Chatbot Arena similarly aggregates anonymous pairwise preference votes to compare models [11]; LLM-as-a-judge methods automate output assessment, although judgments can be sensitive to response order and verbosity [59]. Criterion-based eval-uations instead assess responses against predefined standards, such as helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness or the moral judgments encoded in ETHICS [1, 18]. These approaches assess preferred out-puts or performance against specified criteria; they do not directly measure a particular user’s perception that an agent’s behavior reflects their values, goals, and preferences. Other approaches compare model responses with human opinion or value profiles. OpinionQA measures correspondence between a model’s response distribution and survey-response distributions from demographic groups [36]. Norhashim and Hahn combine GPT-3.5’s agreement with dataset-designated normative actions in Moral Stories with consistency across repeated judgments, then map the dataset’s norms to a human-value taxonomy [31]. Such profiles char-acterize agreement with a selected reference, but stated positions need not predict contextual choices: ValueActionLens identifies discrepancies between models’ stated values and their selections among value-informed actions [40]. Neither population-level cor-respondence nor consistency with a normative dataset establishes how an interacting user experiences the system’s alignment with their own values. The interpretation of these evaluations depends on what is meant by alignment. In their review of 94 AI value-alignment papers, Smart et al. found that 79% neither defined nor described values and 82% treated preferences as a stand-in for values [43]. This finding describes a pattern in their sample, rather than every approach reviewed here: ETHICS specifies moral criteria, while Norhashim and Hahn explicitly map judgments to a human-value taxonomy [18, 31]. Preference judgments can provide evidence about values, but the two are not interchangeable: context-specific choices do not exhaust the meaning or normative significance of what people value [61]. Following the measurement perspective of Wallach et al., interpreting a score as alignment requires an explicit construct and evidence supporting that interpretation [47]. This requirement applies equally to behavioral indicators and self-report instruments used to assess alignment in interaction. 

## 2.3 Perceived Value Alignment in Human–AI Interaction 

HCI research provides evidence at the level of interaction. Studies of AI-assisted programming combine suggestion-acceptance logs with retrospective labels of activities such as evaluating, verifying, and editing suggestions [30]. Decision-support studies measure whether a user’s final decision agrees with the AI’s suggestion and examine team performance separately for correct and incorrect AI recommendations [4]. These observations capture what users do, but cannot by themselves establish why users accept an output or whether it reflects what matters to them. Creative systems also elicit subjective judgments: ToMigo [17], PromptCharm [48], DuetUI [53] use Likert ratings of how well generated designs and design concepts align with creative intent. These ratings address perceived fit within a particular creative task; they do not establish a validated scale of perceived value alignment. Similarly, Yang et al. [54] use unvalidated questionnaires specifically designed to assess relationships between AI roles and trust. In online decision-making, Zheng et al. examine alignment be-tween user requirements and AI support through ContrXAI, a mixed-initiative system evaluated as a technology probe [60]. More directly, Yun et al.’s Value-Alignment Perception Toolkit (VAPT) offers a reusable, modality-agnostic methodology for examining how users perceive AI’s extraction, embodiment, and explanation of their values [56]. Their chatbot study combines interactive probes, interviews, and ratings with the validated PVQ-RR questionnaire as Hegemann et al. 

a baseline for participants’ values. VAPT therefore already addresses perceived value alignment; its contribution is a probe-based eval-uation methodology, not a psychometrically validated self-report scale of that perception. Taken together, these studies demonstrate a recurring need to assess whether AI systems understand and act on what matters to users. Existing instruments address neighboring constructs or particular relationships, while task-specific ratings and situated methodologies provide complementary forms of evidence. Our work develops a common self-report instrument explicitly grounded in perceived congruence in values, goals, and preferences. PVAS distinguishes Value Understanding, concerning whether a system appears to understand what matters to its user, from Value Man-ifestation, concerning whether its actions and outcomes reflect those values. We formulate items for use across agent roles and interaction modalities, and evaluate the scale using observed sce-narios that vary in embodiment, autonomy, and the values at stake. Through these scenario-based studies, we examine its factor struc-ture, reliability, and relationships with established measures of trust, creepiness, and affinity for technology interaction. This provides a basis for assessing perceived understanding and its manifestation in behavior separately within human–AI interaction studies. 

## 3 Method 

We followed the scale development process and best-practice rec-ommendations proposed by Boateng et al. [7]. This framework structures scale development into a sequence of stages covering construct definition, item generation and refinement, assessment of the scale’s latent structure, and evaluation of its psychometric prop-erties. Accordingly, section 4 through section 9 describe each stage of our scale development process and the corresponding method-ological decisions and analyses. After establishing the psychometric properties of the resulting scale, we further examined whether its length could be reduced while preserving its predictive perfor-mance. In subsection 9.11, we therefore apply the scale-shortening approach proposed by Yarkoni [55] to derive a more parsimonious version of the instrument. 

## 4 Construct Definition 

Scale development begins with specifying the construct to be mea-sured [7]. The definition delimits what the instrument is intended to cover, guides the generation of items, and serves as the reference against which their content validity is judged. Without it, items can only be assessed against an implicit notion of the construct, and the resulting scores cannot be interpreted [47]. Following McKinlay et al. [29], we define perceived value align-ment as the extent to which a human perceives congruence of values, goals, and preferences with an artificial agent after inter-acting with it and observing its behavior. The definition rests on a three-level hierarchy: 

Values. Adopted from Schwartz and Cieciuch [37], values are trans-situational, desirable end states or modes of conduct that vary in importance and serve as guiding principles for a human or artificial agent. They are abstract and motivational, organized as a system of relative priorities rather than a set of independent en-dorsements, and become observable only through their expression in specific contexts. 

Goals. Goals are situated manifestations of values: concrete, context-bound end states an actor pursues within a given task, constructed in service of one or more underlying values, where the originating values are not necessarily recoverable from the goal alone ([29]; consistent with goal-systems theory [22]). 

Preferences. Preferences are comparative priorities among op-tions, outcomes, or courses of action given an actor’s goals and values ([29]; in the decision-theoretic sense of preference as a com-parative ranking [34]). Following McKinlay et al. [29], value alignment is the process of understanding and calibrating values to achieve congruence between two agents. Perceived value alignment, then, is the extent to which a human perceives congruence based on their observation of the agent. Since values become observable only through their expression in specific contexts, the construct is assessed after interaction with an intelligent system. 

## 5 Item Generation 

We generated the initial item pool in three steps. First, we identi-fied questionnaires measuring constructs related to perceived value alignment through a literature analysis and extracted 1429 items re-ported in these instruments. Second, we generalized and unified the phrasing of the items so that they no longer referred to a particular system, task, outcome, or AI role. Third, we removed redundant, irrelevant, and out-of-scope items. The wording of the remaining 274 items was revised for linguistic naturalness. 

Literature Analysis. We approached perceived value alignment through a set of adjacent constructs that describe how people in-teract with other people or artificial agents, where value align-ment is essential. Based on our initial literature review and con-struct definition, three of the authors collaboratively identified the following keywords: collaboration , shared understanding , coopera-tion /cooperativity , teaming , value alignment , value-action gap , cohe-sion , partnership , integration , and symbiosis . For each keyword, we queried Scopus 1 in full-text mode following the pattern "Human-AI" AND "<key word>" AND "questionnaire" . The searches were con-ducted in July 2026. For search queries with more than 100 results, only the 50 newest and 50 most-cited results were viewed, to balance the representation of the different constructs. From the resulting publications, 79 used a unique questionnaire targeting a relevant construct. The retrieved papers were screened for questionnaires measuring a related construct. Items were extracted regardless of whether the instrument had been validated, as many constructs in human-AI interaction are still assessed with purpose-built scales. The resulting initial item pool contained 1429 items. 

Generalizing and Unifying Item Phrasing. The items were then rephrased as generalized statements: specific tasks (e.g., writing, driving) and domains were replaced with this task , deliverables (e.g., designs) with outcome , and human roles (e.g., doctor, colleague)

## 6 Content Validity 

We assessed its content validity in an expert study before testing the item pool with users. Following recommendations by Boateng et al. [7] we recruited domain experts to judged whether the items capture the construct.

## 6.2 Procedure 

Experts first provided informed consent and reported demographic information (gender, age, highest degree, current position) and their self-rated expertise in scale development and AI alignment on a 5-point scale. Experts read the construct definition of perceived value align-ment and then rated each of the 274 candidate items. For every item, experts saw the generalized item statement (e.g., “[This sys-tem/agent] understands my needs.”) and answered “How relevant is the statement above for measuring perceived value alignment?” on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (not relevant) to 4 (highly relevant), with an optional free-text field to comment on that item. After rating the full pool, experts had the option to answer whether they felt any factors of perceived value alignment were missing from the item pool. Finally, they were presented with two example sce-narios for scale administration and asked to comment what other use cases, interactions, and scenarios might be important to cover. 

## 6.3 Data Analysis 

For each item, we computed the mean relevance rating across the seven experts. On our 1–4 scale, a mean above the scale midpoint (2.5) indicates that, in aggregate, experts judged the item more relevant than irrelevant to perceived value alignment. Items below the midpoint were dropped. This retained 106 of the 274 items (38.7%) for the item-testing study. We analyzed the expert suggestions qualitatively and found a number of specific tasks and domains, from health care to design. In addition, specific types of values emerged (ethical, instrumen-tal preferences, moral, social, epistemic, privacy, and autonomy). One expert argued for varying scenarios along dimensions such as embodiment, from screen-based software to physically embodied agents, the distribution and level of agency, from responding with recommendations to full autonomy, duration of the relation, from one-shot to spanning a longer time, and stakeholder structure, from one user to balancing multiple user groups. 

## 7 Item Testing 7.1 Participants 

We recruited 673 English-speaking participants from the USA and the UK via the recruiting platform Prolific 2, of whom 601 passed the comprehension check and attention checks and completed the full questionnaire. The participants were compensated with £2.25 and took a median time of 12 minutes to complete the survey.

After giving informed consent, participants completed a task com-prehension test verifying that they engaged with the scenarios and comprehended the instructions: they watched a short video of a human–AI interaction and answered a question about it. Partici-pants who passed within two attempts were randomly assigned one of the scenarios and answered two questions about its content to ensure they had watched or read it. Correct answers were required to proceed but following Prolific guidelines, mistakes did not result in exclusion at this later stage of the survey. After observing and understanding the scenario, participants rated their agreement with each of the 106 items on a five-point Likert scale. 

## 8 Item Reduction 

We analyzed the data collected in the previous step to identify the latent structure underlying the item pool and reduce it to a manageable scale. This was done using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), which models the observed items as manifestations of a smaller number of latent factors. 

## 8.1 Data Screening 

Since careless responding is a prevalent issue in survey data [5, 49], we screened the data for failed attention checks and implausibly fast responses averaging less than 2 seconds per item. In addition, we computed longstrings: the longest streak of consecutive identical answers in each response. Based on the number of negative items and a plot of longstrings in the sample, we identified 20 as the threshold that careful item-based responses would not exceed. In total, 88 responses (14.5%) were removed, leaving 518 respondent-scenario rows for analysis. 

## 8.2 Item Pre-processing and Adequacy Testing 

Before conducting the EFA, we reverse-coded all negatively worded items so that higher scores consistently reflected the same under-lying direction across the pool. We then inspected each item’s mean, skew, kurtosis, and corrected item-total correlation, remov-ing three items whose corrected item-total correlation fell below .30 ( 𝑟 𝑑𝑟𝑜𝑝 < .30), indicating poor discrimination relative to the rest of the pool. This left 103 items for factor analysis. We then evaluated the suitability of the retained item set for factor analysis using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy, which compares each item’s raw inter-item correlations to its partial correlations after controlling for all other items (values closer to 1.0 indicate the item shares variance directly with the pool rather than only through intermediaries). The over-all KMO Measure of Sampling Adequacy was 𝑀𝑆𝐴 = .985, well above the conventional .60 threshold. ## 8.3 Exploratory Factor Analysis 

To determine how many factors to retain, we compared two quan-titative criteria (the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1), and parallel analysis) against visual inspection of the scree plot. The two crite-ria disagreed, suggesting 8 (Kaiser), or 6 (parallel analysis). With a large, semantically overlapping item pool, this is expected, since all three remain sensitive to numerous small, secondary factors that do not represent a stable or interpretable structure [8, 20]. The scree plot, by contrast, showed an elbow after the second factor: eigenvalues fell from 7.58 (Factor 2) to 2.57 (Factor 3) before leveling off. Given this inflection, we retained a two-factor solution. Factors were extracted using minimum residual (minres) estima-tion. For the rotation, we applied varimax rather than an oblique method. Varimax constrains the resulting factors to be uncorrelated, which is a stronger assumption than the data may fully satisfy. We selected varimax because it yielded a more balanced distribution of retained items across these facets. Item selection prioritized strong primary loadings and low cross-loadings. Orthogonality was treated as a simplifying assumption, acknowledging that the two perceptions may still correlate. Based on the rotated solution, we retained items with a factor loading of at least .60, a cross-loading no higher than .20, and a gap between these two of at least .30. Of the 103 candidate items, 32 were dropped for an insufficient factor loading and 56 for cross-loading, leaving a final scale of 15 items across two factors , with 8 and 7 items, respectively. ## 9 Scale Evaluation 

To evaluate the factor structure of the PVAS, we conducted confir-matory factor analysis (CFA). Further, we evaluated the internal consistency of the two factors and compared PVAS to scales on re-lated constructs to assess discriminant and convergent validity. We collected an independent sample and altered the set of scenarios to include two pairs of use cases, manipulating the level of alignment for one or both factors to support a known-group validation. We did not include a re-test in the evaluation, as perceived value align-ment is influenced by interactions rather than representing a stable characteristic of participants. Finally, we used a scale abbreviation method to develop PVAS short and re-ran internal consistency, dis-criminant, convergent, and known-group validity checks to ensure it maintained comparable performance. 

## 9.1 Participants 

We recruited 357 English-speaking participants from the USA and the UK via Prolific 3, of whom 296 passed the comprehension check and completed the full questionnaire. The participants were com-pensated with £2.25 and took a median time of 9 minutes to com-plete the survey.

## 9.2 Materials 

The scenarios comprised two complementary pairs (four groups in total) for known-group comparison: a more- and a less-aligned version of an autonomous car and a chat-based AI travel agent. ## 9.4 Procedure 

Participants provided informed consent and completed the task comprehension test from the item testing study (7). Those who passed were randomly assigned to one of the four groups, viewed the scenario video or text, and answered two attention check ques-tions about the content before completing the scales. To prevent order effects, the four scales (TiA, PC, ATI, and PVAS) were admin-istered in random order. 

## 9.5 Data Screening 

Following our EFA data screening procedures (8), we removed par-ticipants showing signs of careless responding based on attention checks, completion times, and longstrings per scale. We excluded participants who failed one attention check, averaged under 2 sec-onds per item, or produced longstrings with a probability under 0.1 given the proportion of reverse-coded items. Screening only the PVAS scale resulted in removing 41 samples for the CFA, whereas multi-scale validation required removing 75 samples that failed criteria on any evaluated scale. The sample used for validation con-sisted of 64 in the travel agent England scenario, 55 in the travel agent Ontario scenario, 55 in the risky car scenario, and 51 in the careful car scenario.

## 9.11 Short Form 

To reduce participant burden, we shortened the 13-item long form using the genetic algorithm method of Yarkoni [55] on the combined EFA and CFA samples ( N = 777). For each factor, we searched for the item subset that best reproduced the full-factor score under a loss function balancing model fit ( 𝑅 2) against a fixed item cost of 0.05 following [55]. The inclusion search plateaued at three items per factor: VM ( 𝑅 2 = .941, items 𝑉 𝑀 1, 𝑉 𝑀 3, 𝑉 𝑀 5) and VU ( 𝑅 2 = .894, 

𝑉𝑈 1, 𝑉𝑈 5, 𝑉𝑈 7), yielding a 6-item short form. To verify that item reduction did not compromise the psychome-tric properties of PVAS, we re-assessed the validation metrics with the same tests and samples as described in Subsections 9.7–9.10. The short-form factor scores correlated strongly with their long-form counterparts ( 𝑟 = .975 for VM, 𝑟 = .951 for VU, 𝑟 = .960 total), indicating low information loss. Internal consistency attenuated as expected with fewer items, but both subscales retained adequate re-liability (VM: 𝛼 = .843, 𝜔 = 0.844; VU: 𝛼 = .794, 𝜔 = 0.796). Conver-gent validity remained robust, with the short form displaying strong positive correlations with overall trust ( 𝑟 = .73) and its subscales, negative correlations with perceived creepiness ( 𝑟 = −.60), and low association with general technology affinity ( 𝑟 = .16). Discriminant validity was similarly supported, as HTMT ratios between short-form factors and external subscales remained below the conserva-tive threshold of 0 .85—ranging from 0 .138 to 0 .772 for the included subscales. Finally, known-groups validation confirmed that the short form maintained sensitivity to deliberate variations in system alignment: total scale scores differentiated between aligned and mis-aligned conditions in both the driving scenario ( 𝑑 = 0.618 , 𝑝 < .001) and the travel agent scenario ( 𝑑 = 0.807 , 𝑝 < .001). Subscale sensi-tivity was mostly preserved: VM scores showed large, significant differences in both the driving ( 𝑑 = 0.818 , 𝑝 < .001) and travel agent (𝑑 = 0.967 , 𝑝 < .001) scenarios, while VUscores captured moderate differences in the travel agent scenario ( 𝑑 = 0.417 , 𝑝 < .001). The difference in the car scenario became slightly smaller and was not significant ( 𝑑 = 0.200 , 𝑝 = .076). 

## 10 Discussion 

In this paper, we present the development and validation of a 13-item scale to measure value alignment in HAI. Through factor analysis, we identified a two-factor structure: VM, which measures the perceived manifestation of the system’s values, and VU, which measures the extent to which the system “ understands ” the user, that is, recognizes, interprets, and correlates with the user’s values and intentions. We abbreviated the scale further to a 6-item short-form. 

## 10.1 Using the Scale in Training AI Systems 

How do we train AI systems to reflect what their users value? Today’s training pipelines answer this question with stand-ins. A system is rewarded for what raters prefer, for what a grader marks as correct, or for what satisfies a rubric someone wrote for the task. The user is not asked whether the system understood what they care about or acted on it. The PVAS is a validated way to ask, and future work should test whether it can be used as a training signal. We see two ways to do this. In the first, a grading model reads a transcript of the interaction and answers the items on the user’s behalf, and the scores are used as the reward. Whether a grader’s answers match the user’s own is unknown; this can be tested by collecting user responses on the same interactions and comparing them. In the second, users complete the six-item short form after an interaction, and a reward model is trained to predict their responses instead of pairwise preferences. This is more expensive per label but measures the construct directly. In both cases the scale measures perceived alignment, and a system trained to raise its score may learn to appear understanding rather than to understand. A signal built from the scale should be paired with behavioral measures that do not depend on the user’s impression, and the validity of the scale should be re-examined once systems have been optimized against it. Since no normative data exist, scores are interpreted comparatively, between systems, conditions, or points in time, rather than against an absolute criterion. The six-item short form is scored identically, with three items per factor. Its scores track the long form closely at the person level (𝑟 = .975 for VM, 𝑟 = .951 for VU, and 𝑟 = .960 for the overall score), so the interpretation above applies unchanged. We recommend reporting the two factor scores alongside the overall score. The overall score shows how aligned a system is per-ceived to be, but analyzing the factors individually might shed light on how to improve an interactive AI system. A low VM combined with a high VU could indicate that the system understands the user but is not capable of performing the required actions. In such a case, understanding might be communicated, and the user might perceive it as this, while its manifestation in behavior diverges, for example, because the system lacks the capability or the permissions to act accordingly. A low VU combined with a high VM indicates the reverse: the system behaves acceptably but does not convey that it recognized what mattered, which concerns how it communicates, explains, and elicits. In both cases, the possibility to differentiate the two factors can guide further evaluation and system design. 

## 10.3 Factors and Relation to Related Work 

Our construct definition rests on a three-level hierarchy of values, goals, and preferences, but the factor analysis did not reproduce these levels. It separated whether a system apprehends what mat-ters to its user (VU) from whether its actions reflect it (VM). The mapping is asymmetric. All VM items are situated, referring to outcomes, actions, and preferences in a given context, which fol-lows from the construct stating that behavior can only be judged at the level of goals and preferences. The VU items address the level above, asking whether the system grasps what matters and why concrete choices are made. This is consistent with our definition, in which values become observable only through their expression in context, meaning that users cannot judge value congruence directly but need to infer it. 

## 10.4 Implications 

As agents gain autonomy, they take over trade-offs that users previ-ously made themselves. PVAS allows practitioners to compare how users experience different ways of delegating those decisions: an agent might infer priorities from past behavior, ask when priorities conflict, or let users revise its choices. The same assessment can compare models before and after fine-tuning, examining whether adaptation improves VU, VM, or both. Reporting these separately preserves a distinction that matters for implementation: users may feel understood while rejecting what the agent does. For researchers, PVAS enables experiments that distinguish the effects of how an agent communicates from those of what it does. Explanations might increase VU without improving VM, while decisions that reflect users’ priorities might improve VM without strengthening the feeling of being understood. PVAS could also sup-port the development of personalized models, such as digital twins intended to reflect an individual’s values, goals, and preferences. Ratings from that individual would provide an empirical basis for assessing whether the model is perceived as understanding and acting on what matters to them. These assessments could guide the evaluation of individual personalization and help determine whether successive model versions improve perceived alignment with the person they represent. 

## 11 Limitations and Future Work 

Several limitations of this work point to opportunities for future research. Our evaluation relied primarily on scenario-based vignettes such as videos and text rather interaction with functional systems. While vignettes allowed precise experimental control across modalities and alignment levels, perceived alignment in dynamic, multi-turn applications may introduce behavioral factors not fully captured in the vignette setups. Future work should validate the scale in naturalistic, interactive deployments. Our sample was limited to English-speaking participants from the US and the UK. Because personal values and expectations of agent behavior may vary across cultures, further research could validate PVAS across more cultural groups. Although the 6-item short form was derived using algorithmic optimization and demonstrated high statistical correspondence with the full scale, it was evaluated on the same datasets used for its construction. Independent empirical validation of the short form in standalone user studies would further increase confidence in the short form of PVAS. 

## 12 Conclusion 

We introduced the Perceived Value Alignment Scale (PVAS ), a psy-chometrically validated 13-item instrument, and its 6-item short form, to measure perceived value alignment in human-AI inter-action across two distinct dimensions: Value Understanding and Value Manifestation. By offering a reliable, domain-general metric grounded in observed agent behavior, PVAS enables researchers to evaluate interactive systems, compare design variants, and investi-gate alignment mechanisms in future work.
