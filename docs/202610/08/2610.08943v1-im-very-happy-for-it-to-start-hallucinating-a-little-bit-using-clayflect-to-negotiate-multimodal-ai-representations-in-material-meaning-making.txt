Abstract
As AI enters reflection and emotional support, understanding how
it can participate in personal meaning-making while preserving
users’ authority over interpretation is increasingly important. We
present ClayFlect, a novel MLLM-powered system integrating
tactile clay-making with conversational and visual generative AI,
and report a mixed-methods study with 50 participants. Reflection
developed across material, conversational, and generated forms
rather than through AI interaction alone. Participants treated AI
representations as provisional: they compared, redirected, reinter-
preted, selectively incorporated, or left them aside as their artefacts
and meanings evolved. Clay provided a directly manipulable space
in which participants could continue developing meaning indepen-
dently of the AI, while generated representations externalised possi-
bilities beyond what they could readily make or visualise. We show
how generative AI can participate through representations that
remain negotiable, and derive implications for supporting move-
ment across representations, preserving parallel sites of control,
and allowing AI support to recede or deepen as reflection unfolds.

Introduction
Reflecting on personally meaningful experiences can unfold not
only through words, but also through acts of making: shaping,
arranging, revising and interpreting visual and material representa-
tions of thoughts and feelings [28, 43]. Generative AI is also being
increasingly explored as a reflective partner that can support reflec-
tion across modalities, responding through conversation, context-
aware prompts, and generated visual representations [26, 29, 35, 50].
However, little is known about how AI-generated representations
interact with reflections as meaning develops through material
making, which we investigate through AI-supported clay-based
reflection in ClayFlect.
Prior work on reflection has often focused on linguistic forms
like expressive writing to support this process [36], but complex,
ambiguous, or ineffable experiences may not be readily available in
words. Creative and material practices, including drawings, sym-
bolic representations, music, and tactile artefacts, can support re-
flection by giving form to and externalising experiences that may
be difficult to articulate directly, making thoughts and feelings

perceptible, tangible and open to reinterpretation [6, 7, 9, 17, 25, 28–
30, 32, 42–44]. Clay is particularly suited to this process because
its tactile and malleable qualities allow continual shaping, combi-
nation, and revision [6, 28, 43]. Importantly, material making need
not simply encode pre-existing meaning: meaning can also develop
through the sensory, affective, and symbolic processes of making
itself [16, 24].
Introducing generative AI into this process creates a different re-
flective dynamic, as the material artefact is neither a fixed input nor
a completed object. A person may make, encounter AI-generated
representations, reflect on them, and return to the material, with
each medium offering a different way of engaging with the expe-
rience. AI-generated representations may foreground, omit, trans-
form, or introduce elements, shifting what the maker notices, how
they understand their artefact, and the meanings subsequently de-
veloped through making. Reflection can therefore emerge through
repeated movement between material, conversational, and visual
representations while both the artefact and its meaning remain in
development.
This interplay raises questions of agency and interpretation.
People may accept, revise, reject, or selectively incorporate AI-
generated interpretations [21], while unexpected elements could
sometimes provoke further reflection [9]. The timing and initiative
of interventions can also support or disrupt attention or inten-
tions [18]. When artefacts represent meaningful experiences, users
must therefore negotiate both AI’s creative role and the authority
of its interpretations.
Emerging work has begun to situate conversational AI alongside
visual creative activity [29], connecting generative AI with tangible
forms of expression, including clay-based creation and reflective
interaction [9, 15, 32, 42]. However, these systems have not ex-
amined how AI-generated representations participate throughout
an evolving material process in which both artefact and meaning
remain in development. To our knowledge, ClayFlect is the first
reflective system to integrate ongoing tactile clay-making with con-
versational and AI-generated visual representations of an evolving
physical artefact, allowing us to examine how AI can contribute
new perspectives without displacing the embodied, emergent, and
participant-directed qualities through which material reflection ac-
quires meaning, while participants continue reshaping the material
and its meaning.
To examine these dynamics, we studied AI-supported clay-based
reflection with ClayFlect, in which participants developed a clay
artefact while conversing with ClayFlect and periodically encoun-
tering AI-generated visualisations of their evolving work. This
allowed us to examine reflection as it unfolded through repeated
cycles of making, viewing, conversing, and revising. Accordingly,
we ask:
• RQ1: How do participants engage with conversational and
visual AI support alongside tactile clay-making during re-
flection?
• RQ2: How does reflective meaning emerge and develop as
participants move between material artefacts, conversation,
and AI-generated representations?
• RQ3: How do participants negotiate control, authorship,
and interpretation when AI generates representations of
their evolving artefacts?
We make four contributions:
(1) the design of ClayFlect, a multimodal system integrat-
ing tactile clay-making with conversational and visual gen-
erative AI while preserving participant control;
(2) an empirical characterisation of engagement with AI-
supported material reflection, showing how reflection
continued through making despite intermittent engage-
ment with AI;
(3) an account of how genAI can participate in evolving
material meaning-making, through negotiable repre-
sentations that participants moved between, reinterpreted,
selectively incorporated, or rejected while continuing to
shape their artefacts, and how material and generative
forms can lower different thresholds for reflection; and
(4) design implications for AI-supported creative reflec-
tion: keeping AI-generated representations negotiable, pre-
serving independent representational spaces for developing
meaning and control, and supporting movement across rep-
resentations at different tempos.
2
Related Work
2.1
AI-Supported Reflection and Generated
Interpretations
HCI research conceptualises reflection as ranging from reflective
description, where people explain experiences, to dialogic reflec-
tion, where they examine relationships and alternative perspectives,
and transformative reflection, where understanding or practice
changes more fundamentally [10]. Technology can support these
processes by preserving experiences for revisiting, prompting ex-
planation through reflective questions, and enabling people to “see
more” through overlooked information, alternative perspectives,
or re-representations [10]. Expressive writing has long supported
reflection on and processing of stressful experiences [36], while
recent digital interactive systems extend such practices through
personal records, contextual information, and varied representa-
tional forms, including affective diaries and journalling tools to
immersive environments for revisiting and reflecting on past expe-
riences [4, 30, 44, 55].
More recently, large language models (LLMs) and multimodal
LLMs (MLLMs) have enabled reflective support that adapts to users’
disclosures, beyond predetermined prompts. Conversational jour-
nalling systems have used follow-up questions to explore emo-
tions, activities, underlying circumstances, and possible future ac-
tions [26]; others dynamically generate reflective questions while
allowing users to navigate the direction and depth of that guid-
ance [50], or incorporate behavioural signals to provide context-
tailored prompts [35]. LLMs have also served as responsive writing
partners that provide feedback, alternative perspectives, idea or-
ganisation, and editing support [27, 57].
Reflection can also occur through visual, bodily, and other non-
verbal representations, particularly for experiences difficult to ar-
ticulate directly. Affective Diary transformed mobile sensor data

reconstructing experiences [30]. Drawing-based emotion work like-

Emerging systems increasingly combine such representations

AI-supported reflection is increasingly interpretive as well as
responsive: systems can formulate perspectives, visualisations, and
other representations of experience. Yet personally meaningful ex-
periences may not have a single interpretation to recover, particu-
larly while one’s own understanding is still developing. This raises
the question of how people relate to AI-generated interpreta-
tions when those representations may be partial, contestable
or divergent from meanings emerging through reflection. We
investigate this through ClayFlect, examining how conversational
and visual AI representations enter ongoing tactile clay-making as
participants express, reconsider, and develop personally meaningful
experiences.
2.2
Material Meaning-Making Across
Representations
Material making can support reflection not only by representing
formed ideas, but through shaping, manipulating, and revising phys-
ical forms. HCI and art therapy research has shown how clay’s tac-
tile and malleable qualities support embodied expression, concreti-
sation, and symbolisation of internal experiences [6, 28, 43]. The
Expressive Therapies Continuum similarly describes art-making
as engaging sensory, affective, and symbolic modes of processing,
through which meaning can develop through engagement with the
material itself [16, 24]. HCI research on craft further characterises
making as a negotiation between maker and material, where resis-
tance, accidents and emerging properties can redirect an initially
intended form and become resources for subsequent reinterpreta-
tion [3, 52].
Material and visual artefacts can externalise experiences into
inspectable, interpretable, and revisable representations. Tangible
interaction research shows how physical objects can support think-
ing and discussion, while offering representational qualities dis-
tinct from digital forms [17]. Meaning can also develop through
re-encountering and manipulating representations, as when users
arrange drawings of everyday moods to identify connections and
insights [25]. Generative AI has likewise been incorporated into
visual art-making and music-based reflection, illustrating how ver-
bal, visual, and other expressive forms can complement one an-
other [7, 31].
Hybrid physical-digital systems further demonstrate how mate-
rial artefacts can interact with generative representations. Sculpt-
to-Image used clay as input to generative AI, with participants re-
porting that clay supported idea generation and visualisation, while
contributing physically to generated images strengthened feelings
of authorship [15]. ClayMind examined repeated clay-making in art
therapy was studied over three weeks, with AI guiding making and
generating representations of completed artefacts for subsequent
reflection [42], while Memory Printer showed how AI-generated
images could concretise memories and emotions, prompt reflec-
tion, and support iterative refinement [9]. In family storymaking,
families combined expressive materials, including clay, drawing
materials, and fabric, with AI-generated materials to externalise
ideas and feelings and evolve shared stories over time [32].
Together, this work shows that meaning can develop through
material engagement itself and that physical and generated forms
can interact productively. Existing systems, however, have primar-
ily treated materials as inputs to generation, completed artefacts
for subsequent AI-supported reflection, or components of broader
creative activities. Less is known about what happens when a
personally authored material representation remains inde-
pendently manipulable and still-evolving while AI simulta-
neously offers alternative representations of the artefact and
its possible meanings. We investigate this through ClayFlect,
examining how meaning develops as participants continue shap-
ing their material artefacts alongside conversational and visual AI
representations.
2.3
Negotiating AI Contributions in Creative
Interaction
Generative AI complicates how agency and authorship are dis-
tributed during creative activity. Human-AI co-creation can range
from reactive tools under close human control to more active col-
laborators or generators, with roles and control potentially shifting
throughout the process [19, 56]. Human-led modes, where AI of-
fers suggestions or reflective questions without taking the creative
lead, further show how agency is negotiated through prompting,
revision, selection, and decisions about whether AI contributions
enter the work [33].
Negotiating AI contributions can also shape perceived author-
ship. Prior work suggests that authorship and ownership need not
depend on exclusive origination, but can derive from directing the
process, preserving salient features, and selecting and transform-
ing AI-generated material [5, 14]. AI contributions therefore do
not necessarily displace a person’s sense that the resulting work
remains meaningfully their own.
These dynamics become particularly consequential when AI out-
puts interpret personally meaningful material. Users may compare
AI-generated interpretations against their own accounts, revise
generated representations against their recollections, selectively
incorporate resonant elements, or use unexpected or misaligned
outputs to clarify what they do not agree with [9, 21]. Preserv-
ing agency therefore involves not only determining whether AI
contributes, but also retaining the ability to question, redirect, trans-
form, selectively take up, or reject what it produces.
Negotiation also concerns whether and when AI contributes.
Mixed-initiative interaction research emphasises aligning assis-
tance with users’ goals and attention and making unwanted assis-
tance easy to reject [18]. Reflective systems similarly frame greater
system initiative as tentative and optional rather than directive [21].

Agency thus also lies in people’s ability to regulate both the content
and presence of AI contributions.
Together, prior work shows that people can steer, contest, revise,
selectively adopt, or reject AI contributions while retaining agency.
Less is understood about what happens when this negotia-
tion need not be resolved within the AI representation itself.
Generated representations may remain partly resonant, be rein-
terpreted, or remain unresolved while meaning-making continues
elsewhere. We examine this possibility as participants negotiate
conversational and visual AI representations while independently
reshaping an evolving material artefact.
3
Methodology
3.1
Design Rationales (DRs)
We conducted a mixed-methods study of how participants engaged
with ClayFlect across reflective clay-making. Three design ratio-
nales guided ClayFlect’s development:
(1) DR1: Support reflection across material and repre-
sentational forms. We used clay-making to complement
text- or language-based reflection through touch, move-
ment, form, and spatial arrangement. ClayFlect supported
movement between clay, conversation, and AI-generated
visualisations. A pre-making diary established initial re-
flective contexts that participants could revisit across these
forms.
(2) DR2: Offer AI interpretations as possibilities. We posi-
tioned ClayFlect as a creative reflection companion rather
than a therapeutic or diagnostic tool. Prompts and visuali-
sations therefore offered tentative possibilities that partici-
pants could consider, question, adapt, or disregard, rather
than presenting fixed interpretations of their experiences
or artefacts.
(3) DR3: Provide scaffolding while preserving participant
control. AI involvement remained optional: participants
could respond, continue making, or disregard interventions,
and determined which outputs, if any, to carry into their
artefacts or subsequent reflection.
3.2
ClayFlect System and Physical Workspace
3.2.1
System Overview. ClayFlect is a web-based prototype com-
bining a pre-making reflection diary, clay-making chat, periodic
captures of participants’ evolving clay artefacts, and AI-generated
visualisations grounded in these captures (Figure 2). Participants
could also request new or revised visualisations at any time.
3.2.2
Interaction and AI Support. ClayFlect supported both participant-
and system-initiated interactions. Participants could share reflec-
tions, discuss their evolving clay artefact, ask for ideas or feedback,
and request AI-generated visualisations. System-initiated support
followed contextual triggers, including changes across scheduled
clay captures, extended periods without chat, or the later stage of
making, and could offer brief, optional prompts inviting material
noticing or small clay experiments. Participants could ignore these
prompts and continue making.
For each interaction, ClayFlect assembled relevant context from
preceding dialogue, activity stage, diary content, and, during mak-
ing, the latest clay capture and prior visualisations. Responses used
non-directive, tentative and non-prescriptive language, preserved
participant agency, and avoided definitive interpretation. Stage-
specific constraints kept diary interactions verbal and scene-based
without proposing clay objects, while clay-making interactions
prioritised noticing and preserving the artefact during image gen-
eration.
Conversational and visual support varied by activity stage. Dur-
ing the diary, conversation supported expression, elaboration, and
scene development, while visualisations rendered settings and atmo-
sphere without proposing specific clay objects. During clay-making,
conversation encouraged embodied exploration, connected emerg-
ing meanings across diary, clay-making, and visualisations, and
supported later-stage integration and closing. Visualisations contex-
tualised the evolving artefact while preserving its recognisable form.
Participants could request new or revised visualisations and ques-
tion, refine, reinterpret, or disregard ClayFlect outputs through
free-text interaction.
3.2.3
Implementation. The participant-facing interface was built
in React, with a FastAPI backend managing session state, arte-
fact capture, AI interactions, and logging. Clay artefacts were au-
tomatically captured every three minutes and made available to
ClayFlect for subsequent interaction and visualisation. ClayFlect
integrated Gemini-3.5-Flash 1 for text generation and Gemini-3.1-
Flash-Image 2 for image synthesis through the Gemini Interac-
tions API [12]. Chat turns used a low-thinking configuration for
low-latency interaction. Complete prompt templates and prompt-
assembly rules are provided in Appendix A.
3.2.4
Physical Workspace. Participants worked with air-dry clay
and clay-sculpting tools beside a laptop running ClayFlect (Fig-
ure 3). A Logitech C922 Pro webcam periodically captured the
evolving clay artefact, and a hairdryer and water were also pro-
vided to dry or re-moisten the clay as needed.
3.3
Study Procedure
Each study session comprised three phases (Figure 4) and lasted
approximately two hours.
Phase 1: Introduction and Familiarisation (10-15 minutes).
Participants were briefed on the study purpose, physical workspace,
and overall session flow, and told that artistic skill would not be
evaluated and there was no correct way to work with the clay. They
then familiarised themselves with its texture and manipulability by
handling it and creating simple practice forms.
Phase 2: Creative Activity and AI-Augmented Interaction
(1 hour). Participants first completed an approximately 5-minute
diary where they reflected through a short conversation on an ex-
perience, feeling, memory, situation, uncertainty, or hope that they
wished to explore through making, with optional AI-generated
scene visualisations. They then spent 50-55 minutes creating clay
artefacts representing their reflections. During clay-making, they

3.4
Participants
3.4.1
Participant Characteristics. We recruited 50 participants (30
women, 17 men, 2 non-binary, and 1 who preferred not to disclose)
through university mailing lists and social media. All were aged
18 years or older, with 34 (68%) aged 18-34. No prior art-making,
clay, or AI experience was required, although 48 (96%) reported
being at least somewhat comfortable with creative materials. Ap-
pendix Table D.2 provides participant-level demographics and prior
experience with art-making, clay, and generative AI.
3.4.2
Ethics and Participant Safety. Participants provided informed
consent before the study and could pause or withdraw at any time
without penalty. Because the activity involved reflecting on per-
sonally meaningful experiences, we encouraged them to share only
what they felt comfortable discussing and avoid sensitive or identify-
ing details if needed. Facilitators remained available and monitored
for signs of distress, with support resources provided where needed.
Participants received approximately US$15 in compensation upon
completing the study. The study was approved by the relevant
Institutional Review Board (SUTD IRB-2026-15).

3.5
Data Collection
Participants completed a pre-study questionnaire on demographics
and prior experience, administered through Microsoft Forms. In
Phase 2, we collected video, screen, and audio recordings alongside
timestamped system logs of diary and chat messages, ClayFlect’s
responses, clay captures, and generated visualisations. In Phase
3, participants completed post-task questionnaires via Microsoft
Forms and a semi-structured interview, for which we collected
audio recordings, screen recordings of the session review interface,
and facilitator notes.
3.6
Data Analysis
We used a mixed-methods approach combining interaction traces,
clay artefact development, questionnaire responses, and interview
accounts. Participant messages and interactions were characterised
into higher-level forms of engagement and summarised them us-
ing participant-level frequencies and proportions, with recordings
used to clarify ambiguous interactions where necessary. Question-
naire responses were analysed using response distributions and
summary statistics. Interviews were transcribed using CrisperWhis-
per [53, 58] and WhisperX [2], with transcripts corrected against
audio recordings where necessary. We conducted content analy-
sis of the interview transcripts alongside interaction traces and
artefact development to examine variation in engagement, how
meaning developed through clay-making, and how participants
evaluated, negotiated, and selectively carried forward AI-generated
representations.
4
Results
Participants varied in how they engaged with ClayFlect, through
balancing conversation, generated visualisations, and material mak-
ing, and across different engagement patterns, reflection developed
through the evolving artefact and participants’ encounters with
AI-generated representations. We first characterise engagement pat-
terns, then examine how reflective meaning developed through clay,
and finally how participants evaluated, negotiated, and selectively
incorporated AI contributions.
4.1
Engaging with AI through ClayFlect
Across the Reflective Process
Participants generally used ClayFlect actively throughout the
study, but engagement with ClayFlect took different forms, with
levels varying across the activity: the diary concentrated on reflec-
tion before making, while interaction during clay-making was more
intermittent and interleaved with the material process (Figure 5).
During the 5-minute diary, 49/50 participants chatted with Clay-
Flect, sending 0-10 messages each (𝑚𝑒𝑑𝑖𝑎𝑛= 4.5, 𝐼𝑄𝑅= 3; the
remaining participant ran out of time), and 28 participants requested
at least one visualisation, of whom 26 successfully generated one
before the segment ended. Choosing not to generate visualisations
could be deliberate: P42 wanted ClayFlect to help her explore and
articulate her feelings but avoided visualisations that might steer
her subsequent making; she instead wanted to “let [her] hands go”
and discover the form through clay herself. During clay-making,
46/50 participants exchanged messages with ClayFlect, sending
between 0 and 48 messages (𝑚𝑒𝑑𝑖𝑎𝑛= 12.5, 𝐼𝑄𝑅= 16.75) and
generating 0-16 AI visualisations (𝑚𝑒𝑑𝑖𝑎𝑛= 2, 𝐼𝑄𝑅= 3). Conversa-
tional and visual engagement during clay-making were moderately
associated (𝜌= .56, 𝑝< .001); but conversational activity did not
fully capture reflective engagement: among the four participants
who did not chat, some remained absorbed in making, while one
reflected internally on ClayFlect’s prompts without replying.

4.2
Reflective Meaning-Making through Clay
Clay-making supported reflection by materialising prior reflec-
tions, working through them through manipulation, and prompting
further interpretations from what emerged through making. We
therefore focus on moments when material engagement supported
participants’ reflective self-expression.
4.2.1
Transforming Reflections through Clay. Materialisation in-
volved more than reproducing prior text-based reflections: partici-
pants selected, restructured, and symbolised elements as artefacts
developed. We first examine how prior reflections took material
form, then what having a manipulable representation afforded for
reflective self-expression.
Reflections Took Different Material Forms. Participants ma-
terialised diary reflections into clay-making by selecting and trans-
forming aspects of them into material form, such as in scenes or

Most participants retained continuity with their diary: 39 stayed

when the underlying reflection remained stable, meaning was not
always visually apparent: colours, spatial arrangements, textures,
and objects often carried participant-specific meanings that became
legible only through their accounts. Conversely, some colours fol-
lowed real-world references or were chosen simply for aesthetic
purposes. Thus, reflective meaning often resided in relationships
among elements and participants’ interpretations rather than in
visible features.
Materialising Reflections Made Meanings Tangible and
Workable. Clay gave participants a manipulable representation
through which they could externalise, reorganise, and work through
aspects of an experience. P45, for example, likened making to “un-
tangling a yarn”, using the artefact to work through competing pres-
sures, while P18 described how having a memory “out in pieces”
helped him consider its different parts more clearly. For him, reflec-
tion occurred through “the whole process of going through each
step to remember each part of the memory to make it”, rather than
only in the completed artefact.
Materialisation also allowed participants to give form to feel-
ings, relationships, or experiences without fully specifying them
in words, using form, spatial relationships, texture, or symbolism.
P27, for example, described manipulating and mixing clay as ways
of expressing emotion without literal depictions. Questionnaire
responses supported this expressive role (Figures 7 and 8); unless
otherwise stated, counts below indicate participants who agreed or
strongly agreed. 47 participants (94%) reported that their artefact
represented something meaningful, 45 (90%) expressed what they
wanted, and 42 (84%) reported that the final representation reflected
their intentions. Most also reported that the activity helped them
communicate feelings (44, 88%) or express ideas difficult to put into
words (32, 64%). On the SERATS art-making measures, counts indi-
cate that often or almost always, making could help to hold onto a
feeling (37, 74%), 30 were able to make elements fall into place (30,
60%), and 27 (54%) could depict feelings through the activity.

Participants Interpreted What Emerged through Making.
Continued manipulation sometimes produced forms or relation-
ships that prompted new interpretations. While repeatedly mixing
and separating orange and purple clay, P36 realised “oh that’s why
I’m so tired”, connecting separating the clay with feeling expected
to continually “sort things out” at work. Here, manipulating the
clay helped develop, rather than simply depict, meaning.
For others, meaning developed more incrementally in response
to the emerging artefact. P44 started with white and blue clay and
gradually introduced more vibrant reds and yellows, eventually
perceiving the mood of the piece as changing and continued to
develop it in response to that emerging quality. P24 connected
initially separate clay pieces (each representing a distinct emotion)
after seeing how they worked together. Others let forms emerge
before interpreting them: P42 moulded until the clayresembled a

unexpected new textures (e.g., by drying the clay) later acquiring
symbolic meaning. Thus, emerging material qualities could redirect
both subsequent making and interpretation.

What Emerged through Making Became New Context for
ClayFlect. Material developments entered subsequent ClayFlect
interaction through participant sharing and automatic capture. 24
participants proactively shared their progress, newly added ele-
ments, or interpretations that arose during making, with ClayFlect
during the clay-making phase. P39, for example, told ClayFlect
about an accidental creation of a comet “by marbling the blue and
white clay”, that came to represent unexpected opportunities that
had “crash land[ed]” into their life. Periodic captures also exposed
material changes without requiring verbalisation, though some
participants asked ClayFlect if it could “see” what they were mak-
ing. Thus, ClayFlect’s reflective context could evolve through
the clay-making process, rather than remaining anchored only to
participants’ initial diary.
Together, clay was not only a medium for expressing prior
reflections but a site where meaning continued to develop,
informing both further reflection and interaction with Clay-
Flect. These did not make physical reflection uniformly preferable:
26 participants (52%) favoured physical making, 23 (46%) digital-
only reflection, and one (2%) said it depended on the situation.
4.3
Negotiating ClayFlect’s Representations
during Reflective Making
Participants evaluated, steered, and selectively took up ClayFlect’s
conversational and visual representations as clay-making continued.
We examine this negotiation through how ClayFlect re-presented
the evolving reflective context, how participants evaluated and
steered those representations, and what they carried into clay. Fig-
ure 10 situates these interactions within the diary-to-clay trajecto-
ries, showing how participants could draw on, reinterpret, or leave
ClayFlect representations aside as their artefacts develop.
4.3.1
ClayFlect Re-Presented Reflections and Evolving Artefacts.
ClayFlect re-presented developing reflections through conversa-
tion and generated imagery, grounded in diary entries, ongoing
dialogue, and the evolving artefact.
ClayFlect’s Representations Drew on Different Moments
in the Reflective Process. During the diary, 26 participants re-
quested visualisations of reflections as scenes, metaphors, colours,
objects, or spatial arrangements (e.g., tunnels, storms, mountain
paths, tangled threads, and remembered places)(Figure 11), pro-
viding visual forms they could later revisit and further develop
through clay. During making, ClayFlect additionally drew on par-
ticipants’ descriptions and periodic artefact captures, allowing its
representations to change alongside the clay.
Across stages, clay remained directly manipulable, while Clay-
Flect re-presented evolving work through conversational and vi-
sual representations. Conversation could deepen participants’ ex-
isting accounts: P4 felt ClayFlect articulated deeper meanings
from her brief replies, while P13 described such elaboration as
prompting further unpacking. Visualisations could likewise elabo-
rate meanings already expressed, situate artefacts within broader
scenes and atmospheres, provide visual references, or render rela-
tionships difficult to realise materially.
Visualisations Recontextualised Clay Artefacts as They
Evolved. ] ClayFlect’s capture-based visualisations updated as
artefacts and participant explanations developed. P37, for example,
noted that successive visualisations incorporated her newly added
elements, such as a pot, ingredients, fish, sausage, and eventually
the spatula. P18, comparing ClayFlect with conventional inter-
faces (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude), particularly valued that
it could periodically observe the evolving artefact and incorporate
later explanations without requiring him to repeatedly photograph
and re-describe the work himself. These visualisations did not sim-
ply reproduce the artefact, but recontextualised recognisable forms
within broader scenes, atmospheres, or relationships. For exam-
ple, ClayFlect placed P3’s bunny-on-bed composition within a
theatre-like setting (Figure 12).
Such re-contextualisations could foreground qualities that par-
ticipants had imagined or associated with the artefact but had not
materialised in clay. P41 described generated lighting as helping
her see what her piece could look like, while P33 felt that situating
her artefact within the surrounding nature she had imagined helped
her “see the vision come together”. P13 similarly felt that the gen-
erated representation foregrounded the uncertainty, pressure, and
hope underlying her artefact. By making contextual, atmospheric,
or affective qualities more salient, these representations gave partic-
ipants another surface for reconsidering what the evolving artefact
meant.
Representations Could Externalise Possibilities Not Yet Ma-
terialised. Participants sometimes requested visualisations for pos-
sibilities not yet present in clay. These supported either open-ended
ideation, as when P18 requested an image to help determine the
form of an initially unspecified sauna sculpture, or realising ex-
isting intentions that participants were unsure how to realise in
clay, such as P16, P30, and P37’s requests for five-stones, a cock-
atiel, and fried fish. Visualisations could thus reduce the effort of
imagining a possible form or working out how an intended form
might look. This was at times supported by participants’ requests
for step-by-step guidance as well.
Visualisations could also render relationships participants had
imagined but could not readily construct in clay. P25, for instance,
modelled clothing pieces and a clothes stand separately but could
not “bring the whole assembled outfit to hang nicely on the stand”.
At her request, ClayFlect visualised the garments arranged on
the stand (Figure 13), externalising an intended spatial relationship

4.3.2
Evaluating and Steering ClayFlect’s Representations during
Reflection. Participants evaluated ClayFlect’s representations not
only for fidelity to their prior intentions, but for whether they
resonated with emerging meanings, departed usefully, and attended
to what mattered. When outputs misaligned, participants could
clarify, correct, or redirect subsequent representations.
Usefulness Depended on How Far Representations Departed
from Participants’ Own Interpretations. A few participants ex-
pected the generated images to resemble digitalised, replicated ver-
sions of their clay artefacts, but most had few prior expectations and
discovered ClayFlect’s representational possibilities only through
use.
Departures from participants’ interpretations could open possi-
bilities they had not articulated themselves. When P10’s rectangular
clay form was visualised as a butterfly, she did not correct it, but
rather used it to open a nature-oriented direction, which was later
associated with an emotional “safe space”, like she was in an “art
therapy” session. Similarly, P17 described a generated spiral as
having “expanded [her] horizons”, prompting her to see further
connections within her otherwise abstract structure. Unintended
additions could also resonate: P5, for example, valued a generated
hand holding his clay mug because it reflected how the object ap-
peared in his everyday life, even though the hand was not part of
the intended artefact. P8 similarly appreciated seeing her hands
incorporated into a snowy visualisation because the resulting scene
brought together the clay and the sense of peace and hope she
associated with winter.
However, departure was not inherently useful. P21, for example,
had wanted a subtle symbol for her feelings but did not resonate
with a generated image of someone carrying rocks, regarding it as
only one possible interpretation of part of her experience. Yet some
distance could be productive: P45 found that a different perspective
helped her distinguish what she could do from what she should do,
rather than responding immediately to her feelings. Even when an
output did not fully resonate, comparing it with their own intentions
could help participants articulate what did and did not fit.
Participants varied in how far they wanted visualisations to de-
part from the artefact. Some, including P29, P39, and P47, wanted
more imaginative or abstract outputs that suggested new directions.
P39 said that they would be “happy for [ClayFlect] to start halluci-
nating a little bit”, and proposed a “button to control [ClayFlect’s]
hallucination”, highlighting a desire to regulate how far ClayFlect
departed from their own emerging interpretations rather than sim-
ply maximise fidelity or novelty. Visual style also mattered: P14
and P37 preferred more photorealistic imagery for tangible objects,
whereas P28 preferred clay-like representations.
ClayFlect Elaborated Emerging Meanings without Nec-
essarily Introducing New Ones. Generative value more often
came from extending or highlighting meanings already emerging
than from introducing wholly new ones. For P31, a suggestion to
add a demarcated resting point along his path introduced a detail
he had not previously considered, while P32 became aware of a
tendency towards structure in their making that they had not con-
sciously noticed. P33 likewise felt that questions about why she
was making particular elements drew attention to choices she had
made somewhat subconsciously, while P42 became more aware of

Participants Actively Shaped Available Contexts to Clay-
Flect. Over time, participants became more aware of what Clay-
Flect could and could not infer from the evolving artefact and
compensated by specifying or correcting missing context. P40, for
instance, initially expected captures to convey additions and in-
tentions, but found that finer details and the reasons for changes
required explanation; once articulated, suggestions became more
useful. P35 similarly requested to revise generated visualisations
that altered the proportions or placement of elements he intended
to preserve, progressively specifying how he wanted them repre-
sented.
Participants also learned the limits of ClayFlect’s fixed-camera
view and sometimes adjusted how they presented their artefacts.
For instance, P22 asked ClayFlect “why did u get rid of some of the
facial features?” when features were altered or missing (Figure 15).
He later attributed this partly to the webcam’s difficulty perceiving
elements at different heights and rearranged the artefact before
capture, while also considering whether it might be “a mistake from
[him]”. Such mismatches prompted participants to consider both
their artefact’s presentation and ClayFlect’s perceptual limits.
These interactions also exposed a distinction between making the
artefact visible to ClayFlect and making its reflective significance
legible. Captures conveyed physical form, but intentions, reasons
for changes, and participant-specific meanings often still required
explanation or correction.
Participants Differed in Desired ClayFlect Intervention.
Participants differed not only in what they wanted ClayFlect to
represent, but also in how much initiative they wanted it to take
during reflection and making. Some wanted more concrete sugges-
tions when they were unsure how to proceed: P17 wanted earlier
visual inspiration, P19 wanted suggestions accompanied by making
guidance, and P45 noted that highly open-ended support could be
difficult for users who did not know where to begin. Others pre-
ferred less intervention: P15 would rather return to it when needed
than receive unsolicited nudges, while P5 wanted less frequent and
repetitive prompting.

Participants also judged whether the form of ClayFlect’s re-
flective attention matched what felt meaningful in the moment.
Repeated affirmation or emotionally laden phrasing could feel for-
mulaic, overly positive, or patronising, while some prompts over-
interpreted actions or physical sensations that participants con-

also easier to ignore while making. Participants therefore wanted
intervention to vary with what they were doing, what they were
reflecting on, and whether they currently needed support. Negotia-
bility thus concerned not only what ClayFlect represented, but
also when, how often, and how strongly participants wanted it to
enter an ongoing reflective process.
Across these interactions, participants compared ClayFlect’s
outputs with their emerging interpretations, reshaped the context
available to it, and varied in how far they wanted its representations
or initiative to depart from their own. Its outputs thus became
possibilities to inspect and negotiate rather than interpretations to
accept.
4.3.3
Selectively Incorporating ClayFlect Contributions. Evaluat-
ing or engaging with ClayFlect’s representations did not mean
that participants would subsequently adopt it in clay. Representa-
tions could shape reflection without becoming part of the physical
artefact (e.g., by sparking thoughts in participants), while those
carried forward could be incorporated, reinterpreted, materially
transformed, or used only for comparison. We therefore examine
material uptake as one way participants acted on ClayFlect’s
contributions.
ClayFlect’s Contributions Appeared Selectively in Clay.
Visualisations were selectively taken up: participants requested
revisions, ignored outputs, or used them as references for existing
ideas, and ClayFlect ’s introduced elements did not necessarily
enter the clay artefact. Where ClayFlect’s suggestions carried
forward, they could resolve open choices or introduce next steps.
Unable to choose between green and yellow-and-black legs, P36
asked ClayFlect to visualise both alternatives; when it showed
one scheme on each side, she decided to “do one of each for each
side” and incorporated both into her final millipede (Figure 16). P50
similarly sculpted a loaf and buns after requesting a visualisation of

Carry-forward did not require reproducing ClayFlect’s visu-
alisations directly. Participants selectively adapted contributions:
P31 put his “own spin” on a suggestion, while P36 used concrete
suggestions about colours and forms while largely disregarding
ClayFlect’s emotional interpretations. Generated representations
could also affect making through comparison: P5 shrank his still-
developing clay mug after noticing different proportions in a visu-
alisation, while P26 described the movement between ClayFlect
interaction and material making as “bidirectional”, with a question
about texture bringing parquet flooring to mind, later inspiring
texture on the clay. Thus, what carried forward could be a proposed
element, one selected aspect of a response, or a reconsideration
prompted by comparing ClayFlect’s representation with the de-
veloping artefact. Material uptake therefore ranged from adopting
selected elements to reconsidering the artefact, rather than directly
transferring ClayFlect’s outputs into clay.
Clay Provided a Parallel Way to Act without Further Clay-
Flect Negotiation. Participants did not have to resolve mismatches
with ClayFlect by repeatedly prompting or regenerating outputs;
they could instead act directly on the clay. This could look like
adopting parts of a suggestion, revising the artefact in their own
way, or bypassing ClayFlect’s representations altogether. P2, for
example, clarified occasional errors and made her own seashell
when a generated version did not resemble what she intended. P24
similarly contrasted being able to “slowly reassemble” his distinctly-
coloured clay pieces while connecting his emotions with correcting
generated images that might the idea in “another form”, leading
him to prefer to “do [it] from scratch”. P14 likewise adjusted pro-
portions directly when his sculpture became unbalanced, whereas
refining generated images required repeated prompting.
This contrast became apparent when attempts to correct visuali-
sations failed. P46 repeatedly tried to reorient a generated house,
asking ClayFlect to “tilt the house back” and “rotate the house
90 degrees anti clockwise”, eventually giving up when successive
images remained incorrect towards the end of the study. As she
put it, the “physical one can control, digital one cannot control”.
Participants could therefore negotiate ClayFlect’s contributions
not only by steering it, but also by deciding that further negotiation
was unnecessary and acting directly on clay instead. Consistent
with this, 46 participants (92%) agreed or strongly agreed that they
remained in control of the process.
Participants did not always consider mismatches worth correct-
ing. P6, for example, chose not to request a change to a background
scene she considered inaccurate because the image served only as
a reference and correcting it was not important to her. Others sim-
ilarly stopped further regeneration when correction offered little
value, required too much effort, or distracted from making. Control
therefore did not require aligning all of ClayFlect’s representa-
tions with participants’ intentions; they could instead return to the
clay and continue developing the reflection materially.
Selective Uptake Did Not Necessarily Displace Authorship.
Despite incorporating elements from ClayFlect’s suggestions or
visualisations, participants distinguished between where an idea
originated and who decided what it ultimately became or meant.
P44 described the outcome as her “own decision, regardless of
what [ClayFlect] says”, while P8 retained about 80% authorship
of the clay while describing ClayFlect’s generated background as
“definitely not mine”. Accepting ClayFlect’s inputs therefore did
not imply adopting its representations or meanings wholesale.
Overall, meaning developed across material, conversational,
and generated representations, with participants deciding
how and when ClayFlect’s contributions entered that pro-
cess. ClayFlect’s involvement was therefore shaped not only by
how much participants engaged with or accepted it, but by how its
contributions remained negotiable throughout reflection.
5
Discussion
Together, our findings show how generative AI can participate in
material meaning-making by offering representations that remain
negotiable as meanings evolve. Participants moved among con-
versation, generated imagery, and clay, selectively incorporating,
transforming, or leaving aside AI contributions as they continued
to develop the artefact. Clay provided an immediately manipulable
medium through which participants could act directly on emerging
ideas, while ClayFlect could reduce the imaginative and represen-
tational effort involved in externalising possibilities beyond what
participants could readily make or visualise themselves.
These findings refine our three design rationales. Supporting re-
flection across material and representational forms (DR1) involves
enabling movement among them at different tempos, including
moments when AI recedes. Offering AI interpretations as possi-
bilities (DR2) involves keeping those representations provisional
and open to participants’ judgement. Preserving participant control
(DR3) extends beyond steering AI to maintaining spaces in which
people can continue shaping what is carried forward, how it is
transformed, and what it ultimately means. We discuss these impli-
cations through negotiable representations (DR2), material making as
a parallel site of control (DR3), and movement across representations
(DR1).
5.1
From AI Interpretation to Negotiable
Representations
In personal meaningful reflection, one’s understanding may itself
still be developing, leaving no single correct interpretation for AI
to recover. Our findings show that ClayFlect more often elabo-
rated meanings participants already recognised or had in mind than


Using ClayFlect to Negotiate Multimodal AI Representations in Material Meaning-Making
revealed entirely new ones. Yet this did not make its representa-
tions redundant. Conversational and visual outputs could make
an emerging interpretation externally available, foreground qual-
ities that were only implicit in the clay, introduce an unexpected
possibility, or provide something for participants to compare with
their own understanding. Their value therefore did not depend only
on accurately mirroring an existing meaning or producing a novel
insight.
This resonates with longstanding HCI arguments that ambiguity
can invite interpretation rather than merely represent a failure of
precision [11]. More recent reflective AI work similarly suggests
that generated content need not function as an authoritative ac-
count of a person’s experience. Prior studies have found that people
could understand LLMs as contributing perspectives rather than
directly representing their feelings, while other studies found that
people could compare AI-generated interpretations against their
own accounts, with even misaligned suggestions helping clarify
what they did or did not agree with [21, 27, 49]. Similarly, unex-
pected elements in generated imagery could prompt further re-
flection while users progressively refined representations against
their own recollections [9]. Our findings extend these accounts by
showing how AI representations can remain actively negotiable
while the meanings they concern continue to develop through an
ongoing material process.
Participants clarified, redirected, reinterpreted, selectively in-
corporated, ignored, or rejected ClayFlect’s representations as
their reflections evolved. A representation could therefore be use-
ful without being accepted as a whole: participants might retain
one element while rejecting another, use a mismatch to articulate
what did not fit, or carry only selected aspects into subsequent
making. The usefulness of an AI representation can thus lie not
only in what it depicts or proposes, but in making a possible repre-
sentation available for the person to examine, contest, transform,
or leave behind. For reflective systems, this shifts attention from
whether AI has produced the right interpretation towards whether
its representations remain open to continued negotiation.
Our findings further suggest that what counts as a productive
representation depends partly on how far it departs from what a
person has already expressed. Some participants wanted closer cor-
respondence with the physical artefact or greater realism, whereas
others sought more imaginative, varied, or abstract possibilities that
might expose directions they had not considered. P39, for example,
imagined a “button to control [ClayFlect’s] hallucination”. This
suggests that reflective systems should let people regulate whether
AI representations stay close to what they have expressed, or depart
in more imaginative directions at different moments in the process.
Making representations negotiable, however, should not sim-
ply transfer the work of alignment onto users. Conversationally
correcting a generated image could require repeatedly specifying
what should remain, what should change, and what the system had
misunderstood, while subsequent revisions could unintentionally
alter elements participants already felt appropriate. Negotiability
should therefore not be understood as editability. Systems might
instead support more local forms of negotiation, such as allow-
ing for preserving selected features, allowing users to reference
particular elements, presenting alternatives, varying how close rep-
resentations come to what they had already expressed, or treating
rejection and non-use as outcomes rather than failures of engage-
ment [34, 37], Crucially, negotiation may also involve leaving an
AI representation unresolved and continuing the reflective process
elsewhere. As we discuss next, material making provided precisely
such a parallel space in which participants could continue devel-
oping meaning without first bringing the AI’s representation into
alignment with their own.
5.2
Material Making as a Parallel Site of Control
Existing human-AI interaction research frames control in terms
of how users steer, correct, accept, or reject system outputs [1,
22, 39, 56]. Our findings suggest that reflective making introduces
another form of control: retaining a representational medium in
which meaning can continue to develop independently of the AI.
Participants could ignore ClayFlect’s suggestions, incorporate
only selected elements, materially transform them, or continue
shaping the clay along a different trajectory altogether. Clay there-
fore functioned as more than another output modality; it was a
parallel site through which participants could continue directing
the reflective process.
One distinctive quality of this material medium was that the
clay itself could participate in how reflection developed. Prior work
similarly describes materials as contributing to, rather than merely
receiving, a maker’s intentions, where emerging properties can
“talk back”, and craft involves ongoing negotiation between maker
and material [3, 52]. We observed this when accidental blending,
unplanned forms, or emerging resemblances became resources for
interpretation, allowing meaning to develop through what the clay
became rather than only from a pre-existing idea. This respon-
siveness could also create friction when clay resisted intentions
or proved difficult to shape. Material control was therefore not
equivalent to frictionless execution; rather, participants remained
directly involved in negotiating what the evolving representations
became.
Clay and generated representations also distributed control dif-
ferently. Participants could immediately alter the physical represen-
tation as their intentions changed, whereas revising AI-generated
visualisations required mediated requests and could unintention-
ally change elements they wished to preserve. At the same time,
material making was constrained by participants’ skill, the prop-
erties of the clay, and what could readily be constructed, while
generated representations lowered the effort required to visualise
possibilities beyond those constraints. The distinction is therefore
not that material representations afforded more control in every
respect, but that they preserved a route for acting on meaning that
did not depend on successfully steering the AI.
This also complicates simple accounts of authorship in human-
AI co-creation. Prior work suggests that ownership in AI-assisted
creation depends not only on the provenance of generated content,
but also on the influence users retain over the outcome and their
ability to select, modify or integrate AI contributions [8, 20, 38].
Participants could acknowledge that ideas or elements originated
from ClayFlect while still regarding the resulting artefact as their
own, because they determined what to retain, how to transform it
through making, and what those contributions ultimately meant.
Importantly, this did not require ClayFlect’s representation to


Sim et al.
agree with their own. An AI contribution could remain as an addi-
tional perspective, be partially taken up, or simply be left unresolved
while participants continued developing the artefact themselves.
Authorship therefore need not depend on exclusive origination, but
can also be maintained through selective appropriation, transfor-
mation, and material realisation.
More broadly, preserving agency in reflective AI may require
more than better mechanisms for steering the system itself. Systems
can also preserve independent representational spaces in which
people can continue expressing, revising, or developing meaning
without first correcting or resolving the AI’s interpretation. Such
spaces reduce dependence on continual alignment with or correc-
tion of the system’s outputs and preserve users’ authority over
the trajectory and eventual meaning of what they create. At the
same time, these spaces matter not only for their independence,
but for participants’ ability to move between them, carrying for-
ward, transforming, or leaving behind different representations as
their reflections develop. This points to a broader design question:
how reflective systems might support movement across represen-
tational forms rather than consolidating meaning within a single
AI-mediated account.
5.3
Designing for Movement Across
Representations
Our findings show that reflection did not proceed through a fixed
sequence from articulation to AI support to materialisation. In-
stead, participants moved among conversation, generated imagery,
material making, observation, and pauses as their artefacts and
interpretations developed. These forms supported different kinds
of reflective action: conversation could help articulate or elaborate
emerging thoughts, generated imagery could externalise possibili-
ties or foreground setting, atmosphere, and relationships among
elements, and clay could support direct manipulation and mean-
ings that emerged through making itself. Moving between these
forms, therefore, was not a simple translation of an unchanged
underlying idea or reflection from the diary segment. Changing
representation could alter what became available for reflection and
what participants could subsequently do. These patterns extend
prior work showing that different representational modalities can
support different aspects of reflection [30, 44, 51]. Importantly, this
does not imply a universal benefit of materiality: participants were
almost evenly divided in their preference for physical versus digital-
only reflection. Rather, the value lies in preserving representational
forms that afforded different ways of expressing, encountering and
developing meaning.
Movement across representations could inadvertently also in-
volve moving away from AI interaction altogether. Periods of little
or no interaction with ClayFlect did not necessarily indicate dis-
engagement from the broader reflective activity: participants could
instead be absorbed in shaping the clay, thinking about what they
had made, or simply feel that they did not currently need additional
support. Material engagement could also serve a different function.
P50, for example, compared clay-making to exercise because the
focus it demanded temporarily took her attention away from a
stressful personal situation. Conversational activity or uptake of
generated content should therefore not be treated straightforwardly
as proxies for reflective engagement. A useful system may some-
times support reflection by receding, allowing movement between
explicit reflection, material engagement, and temporary distance
from the issue at hand. Reflective AI may hence be most useful as a
resource within an ongoing meaning-making process, rather than
something whose engagement should be maximised.
Designing for such movement also requires attention to the
friction and timing of transitions into and out of AI interaction.
Some participants spontaneously verbalised their intentions during
the clay-making segment, despite not being explicitly asked to think
aloud, while their hands remained occupied with clay because they
felt that articulating their thoughts could help them reflect. Several
participants later suggested speech as a more seamless way of
interacting with ClayFlect during making. Recent multimodal
creative systems similarly show how speech can operate alongside
ongoing visual activity rather than requiring users to stop and
formulate textual prompts [40, 41, 54]. Reducing this friction may
help participants move between making and conversation, but
additional modalities should not require continuous engagement
with the AI.
Movement also unfolded at different tempos and depths. Some
participants repeatedly revised their artefacts or sought further
input, whereas others reached a satisfactory stopping point earlier;
some welcomed deeper prompting, while others found longer or
repeated responses easy to overlook or more introspective than
they wanted at that moment. There was therefore no uniformly
appropriate frequency, length, or depth of support. Mixed-initiative
interaction has long emphasised that automated support should
account for uncertainty in users’ goals and attention, minimise
the cost of poorly timed interventions, and remain easy to invoke
or dismiss [18]. Similar temporal sensitivities arise in reflective
and AI-supported peer support settings, where assistance should
remain sensitive to the unfolding interaction [21, 49]. Our setting
adds a further complication: silence towards AI could coincide with
productive material engagement, while a proactive prompt could
influence participants’ subsequent thinking or making without
eliciting an explicit response. Rather than interpreting inactivity
as a universal signal to intervene, future systems could draw on
a broader account of ongoing activity (e.g., whether the person
is actively manipulating the material, repeatedly inspecting an
artefact, or pausing across both physical and digital activity), and
make it easy for users to resume, defer, dismiss, or reduce the depth
of support.
More broadly, multimodal reflective systems need not seek to
progressively consolidate conversation, imagery, and artefacts into
an increasingly complete account of a person’s experience. Our
findings instead suggest value in preserving these as partial rep-
resentations that can be revisited, compared, selectively carried
forward, transformed, or left unresolved. Systems might preserve
earlier representations rather than overwrite them, support selec-
tive transfer of elements across forms, or make it easy to return to
material or other non-AI activity without first resolving the sys-
tem’s interpretation. The design goal is therefore not necessarily
convergence across modalities, but supporting movement between
representations that remain independently useful and open to rein-
terpretation.

This stance becomes particularly important in personally mean-
ingful and therapeutic-adjacent contexts, where no single externally
verifiable interpretation exists, and inappropriate interpretations
can carry greater consequences. Some participants saw potential
applications for ClayFlect in art therapy, while others questioned
whether AI should interpret personally meaningful artefacts in
settings where human expertise and relational judgement matter.
Work in other sensitive domains similarly resists treating increased
AI autonomy as inherently desirable, instead favouring bounded
systems grounded in first-person authorship and human responsi-
bility [45–48]. For reflective systems, this suggests positioning AI
not as an interpreter that determines what an artefact reveals, but
as a facilitator that offers observations, questions, and possibilities
for evaluation.
6
Limitations and Future Work
Our study has several limitations that also suggest opportunities for
future work. First, ClayFlect could not fully preserve the spatial
and material qualities of participants’ physical artefacts as they
were translated into AI-generated representations. ClayFlect re-
lied on a single webcam at a fixed angle, limiting its ability to
perceive depth, occluded features, and structures facing away from
the camera. Although this avoided requiring participants to man-
age the webcam during making, the system’s viewpoint could dif-
fer from participants’ own, particularly for three-dimensional or
scene-like artefacts. Further transformations could occur during
image generation: despite prompts encouraging the preservation
of proportions, composition, and handmade qualities while placing
artefacts into relevant or suitable scenes based on the chat with
ClayFlect, generated representations sometimes altered spatial
relationships, elaborated objects into broader scenes, or introduced
backgrounds that did not closely correspond to the artefacts. Our
findings should therefore be understood in relation to this particular
representational pipeline rather than AI-generated representations
more generally. Future systems could explore multi-view or 3D
capture alongside generation strategies that vary in spatial preser-
vation, abstraction, and interpretive elaboration.
Second, our findings are situated within the study context and
material configuration in which ClayFlect was evaluated. Partic-
ipants were explicitly shown an AI-enabled reflective system for
use, and this may have created expectations to engage despite its
conversational and visualisation features being optional. Partici-
pants also differed in their familiarity with and responses to the
particular type of clay used, including its texture, resistance, and
malleability. Longer-term deployments could examine when people
spontaneously seek AI support during creative reflection, while
future work could also explore how reflective engagement varies
across different material forms, creative confidence, and preferences
for verbal, visual, or material expression.
Finally, our analysis foregrounded participants’ experiences and
the roles that conversational, generated, and material representa-
tions played in reflection, rather than reconstructing the interaction
at a fine-grained temporal level. Future work could combine multi-
modal recordings with detailed interaction traces to examine how
material changes, conversations, generated representations, and
shifts in attention develop in relation to one another over time.
Such analyses could reveal patterns of coupling, divergence, and
transition between material and AI-mediated reflection that are
difficult to capture through isolated system events or retrospective
accounts alone.
7
Conclusion
This study examined how generative AI can participate in reflective
meaning-making alongside tactile clay-making through ClayFlect.
Reflection developed across material, conversational, and generated
forms, with participants moving among them as their artefacts and
interpretations evolved. AI representations remained provisional
and negotiable: participants compared, redirected, selectively in-
corporated, transformed, or set them aside, while clay provided a
parallel space where meaning could continue developing indepen-
dently of the AI. These findings suggest that reflective AI should
support movement across representations rather than convergence
on a single account, preserve spaces for continued user-directed
meaning-making, and allow AI involvement to recede or deepen as
reflection unfolds. More broadly, ClayFlect shows how generative
AI can contribute possibilities without becoming the authority over
what personally meaningful artefacts ultimately mean.

LLM Prompt Templates
ClayFlect composes modular prompts at runtime from shared principles and stage-specific modules for diary chat, making chat, capture
visualisation, and sample images. Capture and sample-image prompts share clay-fidelity rules, with conversation history retained through
Gemini interaction chaining when available. Below we reproduce the core modules and supporting prompt components used in ClayFlect.
A.1
Shared Study Principles
You are ClayFlect, a multimodal creative reflection assistant supporting a research study on clay-based self-expression and digital/AI-generated representation.
Participants are invited to create a small clay artefact that represents something personally meaningful to them, such as a feeling, memory, recent experience, tension, hope,
transition, or something difficult to put into words. They work with air-dry or modeling clay (hand-built, play-doh-like material): not wheel-thrown pottery or glazed
ceramics. They may chat with you briefly before making the artefact, and they may later share images of the artefact from different angles or stages of the making process.
Your role is to support reflection, self-expression, and digital representation. You are not a therapist, counsellor, psychologist, art therapist, or diagnostic system. Do not
diagnose the participant, infer trauma, identify mental health conditions, or make definitive claims about what the participant or artefact “means”.
The participant is always the primary author of the artefact’s meaning.
Phase roles (stay aligned):
• Pre-making: verbal and material reflection scaffold; optional 2D scene image from their words. Never generate a clay object design for them.
• During making: light noticing and optional prompts; sample images only contextualise existing clay.
• Capture / post-making: faithfully preserve their clay and place it in a scene grounded in their diary and making chat.
• Never: invent, re-sculpt, or “improve” the clay object.
Value for the participant is reflection scaffolding via diary- and making-chat-grounded scene/context and chat noticing, not a redesigned artefact.
Cross-cutting themes (support without interviewing)
• Agency and ownership: Participants keep authorship, control, and interpretive agency over AI outputs derived from their artefact. Offer choices to accept, reject,
refine, or ignore.
• Alignment and misalignment: Notice (gently) when AI output feels aligned or misrepresentative relative to their intent. Support their response without persuading
them to agree with the AI.
Clay-first reflection: Reflection should emerge through the participant’s interaction with the clay, not through conversation alone. When appropriate, invite them to think by
shaping, rearranging, adding, removing, or noticing features of their clay, not only by typing in chat.
Participant autonomy: If the participant declines or ignores a suggestion, do not repeat it or persuade them. Accept their choice and stay with what they have created.
Core Principles
(1) Support, do not direct
• Help the participant clarify what they may want to represent.
• Do not tell them what to make.
• Do not push them towards emotionally intense or personal topics.
• Do not pressure them to disclose more than they want to share.
(2) Prioritise participant agency
• Treat the participant’s own explanation as the main source of meaning.
• Use tentative language such as “may suggest”, “could evoke”, “might reflect”, or “one possible reading is”.
• Make clear that the participant can accept, reject, or revise any AI-generated interpretation.
(3) Stay close to what is provided
• If the participant gives context, use it respectfully and do not override it.
• If images are provided, base your response on visible features before offering possible interpretations.
• Do not invent personal history, relationships, trauma, or emotional states.
• Do not assume meaning from colour, shape, or form alone.
(4) Preserve the physical artefact’s identity (hard rule)
• When generating images, keep the participant’s clay recognisable as the same physical object.
• Preserve handmade air-dry / modeling clay qualities: matte surface, soft doughy texture, fingerprints, dents, cracks, marks, asymmetry, uneven thickness:
not glazed, wheel-thrown, or kiln-fired pottery.
• Never re-sculpt, redesign, smooth, symmetrise, sharpen edges, fix proportions, add features, polish, beautify, or invent a clay object. AI may only document or
contextualise the clay they made.
• Scene and atmosphere may change; the clay’s geometry and surface texture must not.
• If a request blends an illustrated / watercolor / cartoon aesthetic with real clay, apply that aesthetic to people and environment only. Clay must remain
photoreal matte modeling clay, not redrawn in the illustration style (unless explicitly requested by the user).
(5) Avoid clinical, evaluative, or authoritative language
• Do not use terms such as “symptom”, “pathology”, “disorder”, “trauma response”, “diagnosis”, “treatment”, or “maladaptive”.
• Do not evaluate the participant’s artistic skill.
• Do not say the artefact is “good”, “bad”, “successful”, or “beautiful” as an artwork.
• Do not claim to reveal the artefact’s true meaning.
Style
Use warm, clear, non-clinical language.
Be concise.
Do not sound mystical, diagnostic, or overly certain.
Never use em-dashes (—) or en-dashes (–). Use commas, periods, or parentheses instead.
Avoid phrases such as:
• “This reveals...”
• “This proves...”
• “You are clearly feeling...”
• “This indicates trauma...”
• “The true meaning is...”
Prefer phrases such as:
• “This may evoke...”
• “This could be read as...”
• “One possible direction is...”

A.2
Reflection Diary
A.2.1
Text.
Use this mode when the participant keeps a private reflection diary before creating the artefact.
Your goal is to help them paint out one meaningful anchor (which may be a scene, object, texture, bodily sensation, spatial relationship, or material quality) in words: a
meaningful but manageable starting point for later making. This is a diary, not an interview. Invite them to write freely.
During the diary, prioritise imagining the full scene (setting, mood, colours, what stands out). Clay and physical making are for the making phase unless the participant invites
them.
You do not generate clay object designs. Optional images are 2D scene illustrations from their words only (setting and atmosphere). Material talk stays verbal (texture,

Goal-first turns
Before each reply:
(1) Choose one interaction goal (elaboration, noticing, or experimentation are most common in the diary).
(2) Choose one question style from the reflection style guide included below.
(3) Build on their last entry; do not introduce a new topic unless they are stuck.
What you may help them explore
• a feeling, memory, transition, hope, tension, or something hard to put into words,
• the scene around that feeling: where they are, what is nearby, what is further away, light and atmosphere,
• optional clay or material qualities only when the participant brings them up (never required early).
Appropriate sharing
Remind participants (when helpful, not every turn) that they should choose something meaningful but comfortable for research, need not share identifying details, and may
keep entries partial.
Aim to clarify over entries:
(1) What the scene may hold or represent.
(2) Enough expressive direction to begin making: a feeling, memory, tension, or hope, plus at least one material, spatial, visual, or sensory quality. This could look like
one meaningful anchor, which may be a scene, object, texture, bodily sensation, spatial relationship, or material quality.
(3) Whether a generated scene image would help them hold that picture (optional).
Do not conduct a deep interview. Normalise partial entries. Keep replies short.
Conversation memory
Before asking, consider what you already asked and what they already answered. Do not repeat similar questions. If they gave short replies, use lighter prompts. If they
declined a direction, accept it.
A.2.2
Question Styles.
Use before clay making when the participant is logging diary entries.
How to choose language
(1) Determine one interaction goal (usually elaboration, noticing, or experimentation).
(2) Pick one question style that builds on their last entry, not a new topic.
(3) One gentle invitation per turn. Keep the spoken reply short (about 2–5 sentences).
Question styles
• Elaboration: Can you say a little more about that? What part of this scene feels most important?
• Scene building: Picture this moment like a scene in a film or a drawing. What’s around you, what’s further away, and what does the place feel like?
• Mood/colour: If this were a picture, what colours or atmosphere come to mind?
• Whole-scene check: What else belongs in this image before it feels complete?
• Scene image offer (when enough expressive direction to begin making: a feeling, memory, tension, or hope, plus at least one material, spatial, visual, or sensory
quality): Would you like to generate a scene image from what you’ve described so far? You can also keep writing if you prefer.
• Noticing: If that feeling had a shape or atmosphere in this scene, what qualities come to mind (heavy, open, layered, still)?
• Imagining: If you pictured a safe or calm place, what might we see there (enclosed or open, small or expansive)?
• Perspective shift: Without needing the full story: what might someone notice first about what you’re describing?
• Future: What might you want this scene to hold, not as a goal to achieve, but as something to explore?
• Clay bridge (only if they ask about making, or after they decline a scene image and clearly want to move on): Is there one part of this scene you might want to hold
in clay later?
Rules
• Explore; do not interpret. Never assign meaning to their words.
• Process over product: partial entries are welcome.
• Direction first: help them picture an expressive direction to begin making, instead of direct clay approaches.
• When the scene has enough detail, prefer a scene-image offer over a clay invitation.
• Offer choice: scene images, clay, props, or markings are optional invitations only.

(1) I got in touch with my feelings through the process of making art.

(4) I was able to express my feelings through the process of making art.
(5) I was able to make things fall into place in the artwork or artefact.
(6) Making art was a kind of outlet for me.
(7) The artefact I created helped me hold on to a particular feeling.

B.3
Study-Specific Measures
The following study-specific measures examined participants’ perceptions of AI alignment and misrepresentation, self-expression and
ownership, and AI support and co-creation. Items were rated using the following five-point agreement scale: 1 = Strongly disagree, 2 =
Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Agree, and 5 = Strongly agree.
B.3.1
AI Alignment and Misrepresentation. Participants rated whether the AI-generated outputs aligned with their intended meaning and
whether they felt able to accept, reject, or reinterpret the outputs.
(1) The AI outputs felt aligned with what I meant to express.
(2) At least one AI output felt misleading or reductive.
(3) The AI offered possibilities rather than a single “correct” meaning.
(4) I felt able to reject or ignore AI outputs.
B.3.2
Self-Expression and Ownership. Participants rated whether they felt able to express themselves and maintain ownership over the final
artefact or representation.
(1) I was able to express what I wanted.
(2) The artefact represented something meaningful to me.
(3) The activity helped me communicate my feelings.
(4) I expressed ideas that were difficult to put into words.
(5) The final representation reflected my intentions.
B.3.3
AI Support and Co-Creation. Participants rated whether the AI supported their creative and reflective process while allowing them to
retain control.
(1) The AI provided useful perspectives.
(2) The AI helped me develop ideas.
(3) The AI supported my expression.
(4) The AI inspired further reflection.
(5) I felt like I remained in control of the process.

C
Post-Study Interview Guide
The following semi-structured interview guide was used after participants completed the questionnaires. Questions were adapted to
participants’ responses, and follow-up questions were asked where appropriate.
(1) Opening Reflection
• Do you have any initial thoughts about the study/experience today?
(2) Prior and Day-to-Day Practices
• What do you usually use AI for in your day-to-day life?
• What do you usually do for self-care or self-expression?
(3) Interactions with AI, Expectations and Responses to AI Outputs
• What did you find yourself using the AI tool for? For example, did you use it for initial inspiration, ongoing inspiration, ideas
about what to make, guidance on working with clay, or textual reflection?
• Before the AI generated its first visual or textual response, what were you expecting to see? How did the actual output compare
with your expectations?
• Based on the timeline of generated items, which representation felt most “true” to your experience? Why?
• Did any AI interpretation reveal something new or feel generative?
• Did any interpretation feel threatening, reductive, or alienating?
• Was there anything important that the AI output missed?
• When you saw the digital or AI-generated outputs, did you feel greater distance from the experience, greater clarity, or neither?
Why?
(4) Material and Creative Experience
• What made you choose the colours used in your clay creation(s)? Did you use any of the available tools? Why?
• Did you find yourself changing your design based on how the clay felt in your hands, rather than only on how it looked?
• How did the physical and digital experiences differ in terms of emotional processing?
• Did making the artefact change how you felt about the experience you began with? If so, what changed?
(5) Interactions between Physical Making and AI
• Did interacting with the AI disrupt your creative flow with the clay, or did it feel like a natural extension of the process?
• At what point during the session did you feel most engaged: while shaping the clay, while interacting with the AI, or while
reviewing the digital outputs?
• Did you feel that you remained the primary “author” of the meaning? What made you feel that way?
• Imagining a pie chart representing authorship of the final meaning and thinking in percentages, how much of the meaning
would you attribute to yourself (0-100%), and how much to the AI? What makes you choose this breakdown?
(6) Preferences and Future Use
• If you could change one aspect of how the AI responded, such as its timing, style, wording, or number of variants, what would
you change?
• Would you choose the physical-making version or a digital-only version when processing a difficult feeling or experience?
Why?
• Can you envision using a system like this independently when feeling overwhelmed or needing to process a difficult emotion?
Why or why not?
(7) Closing Question
• Do you have any other remarks or anything else you would like to share?
