Abstract 

Conversational database interfaces face a crit-ical challenge: users naturally embed queries in conversational noise (greetings, politeness, off-topic remarks), which degrades intent clas-sification accuracy and wastes computational resources. Despite advances in orchestration and retrieval strategies, a fundamental question remains unanswered: which pooling strategy maximizes intent classification accuracy under realistic conversational noise in production lan-guage models? This work addresses this gap through 360 controlled experiments spanning four pooling configurations (mean, max, last-token, attention, and FFT-augmented variants) using Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct on BANKING77 and CLINC150 datasets under clean/noisy con-ditions with ten random seeds. Key findings reveal that attention pooling consistently out-performs alternative strategies under noisy con-ditions ( ≈ +2 .6–2.8 F1 over the default), while mean pooling degrades performance by up to 

∼ 5 F1 points. Frequency-domain filtering does not produce consistent accuracy improvements and functions primarily as a structural varia-tion rather than an accuracy-enhancing compo-nent. These results provide concrete, evidence-based guidance for building noise-robust con-versational classifiers: attention pooling is rec-ommended for noisy interfaces, mean pooling should be avoided, and last-token pooling is appropriate for clean-query scenarios. 

1 Introduction 

The rise of conversational database interfaces pow-ered by Large Language Models (LLMs) has trans-formed how users access structured and unstruc-tured data. In industrial settings such as manufac-turing procurement, users must retrieve part speci-fications, supplier availability, compliance records, pricing histories, and lead times across heteroge-neous databases, where even minor retrieval errors can have significant operational and financial con-sequences. Rather than writing Structured Query Language (SQL) queries, users increasingly inter-act conversationally (e.g., “Hey there! Can you pull up sales data from Q4? Thanks!” (Dai et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025)). This conversational paradigm introduces a criti-cal challenge: separating core database intent from conversational noise, including greetings, polite-ness markers, discourse fillers, and off-topic re-marks. In systems such as Universal Query En-gine (UQE) (Dai et al., 2024) and PlanGPT (Zhu et al., 2025), failure to isolate intent leads to re-trieval mismatches, misrouting across specialized models (Lee et al., 2024), increased Application Programming Interface (API) costs, and degraded user experience. Accurate intent classification is therefore the foundational step for reliable conver-sational database systems. Existing approaches address this challenge pri-marily through architectural orchestration, retrieval optimization, and confidence calibration. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020), instruction fine-tuning (Han et al., 2025), and multistep reasoning via Chain-of-Thought prompt-ing (Wei et al., 2022) improve downstream reason-ing but do not directly resolve representation-level ambiguity introduced by conversational noise. Despite extensive work on conversational database systems and routing strategies, a fun-damental question remains insufficiently studied: how should token-level hidden states be pooled to maximize intent classification accuracy under realistic conversational noise? While mean, max, last-token, and attention-based pooling are widely used, no systematic evaluation isolates their im-pact in noisy, low-resource conversational LLM deployments typical of industrial settings. This work addresses this research gap through 360 controlled experiments (10 seeds × nine exper-imental configurations × two datasets × two noise conditions), systematically evaluating standard pooling strategies and their variants augmented arXiv:2610.05394v1 [cs.CL] 4 Oct 2026 by Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) applied to trans-former hidden states. Contrary to widespread prac-tice, attention pooling consistently outperforms al-ternatives under conversational noise, while mean pooling degrades performance. Frequency-domain filtering yields no consistent accuracy improve-ments and does not enhance predictive performance relative to standard configurations. These findings provide production-ready guid-ance for practitioners building noise-robust con-versational database systems, particularly in indus-trial environments where classification accuracy directly translates to reduced API costs and fewer failed queries. Modular code is publicly available (Bogachov and Letov, 2026). The remainder of this paper is structured as fol-lows: Section 2 reviews related work on conversa-tional database interfaces, pooling strategies, and frequency-domain methods. Section 3 outlines the method, covering the proposed ablation configu-rations. Section 4 presents experimental method-ology and results. Section 5 provides conclusions and discusses the implications of this work. 

2 Related work 

2.1 Conversational database systems 

The proposed method targets conversational database interfaces, where natural language queries must be robustly mapped to structured opera-tions despite conversational noise. Recent sys-tems address this challenge through architectural and retrieval-based strategies. OrchestraLLM (Lee et al., 2024) routes dialogue turns between fine-tuned small language models and large language models via nearest-neighbor retrieval over expert pools, improving accuracy while reducing LLM calls, though primarily evaluated in dialogue state tracking. UQE (Dai et al., 2024) extends SQL with natural-language predicates for querying un-structured data, combining multimodal embed-dings, stratified sampling, and execution planning to reduce LLM usage while maintaining accuracy. For domain specialization, PlanGPT (Zhu et al., 2025) integrates domain-tuned embeddings, re-trieval, instruction fine-tuning, and orchestration agents for urban planning tasks. Additionally, SQL-aware confidence estimation methods (Maleki et al., 2025) derive reliability scores directly from token-level probabilities. 

2.2 Pooling strategies 

Pooling aggregates token-level representations into fixed-size sequence representations for downstream classification. Common strategies include: (i) 

special-token pooling , such as using BERT’s [CLS] representation for sequence classification (Devlin et al., 2019), (ii) last-token pooling , commonly used with decoder-only models, (iii) mean pool-ing (simple average across tokens) (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019), (iv) max pooling (per-dimension maximum) (Ennadir et al., 2025), and (v) attention-based pooling (learned weighted sum over to-kens) (Chen et al., 2023). While these pooling strategies are widely used, systematic comparisons remain sparse and domain-specific. BERT uses the final hidden representation of the special [CLS] token for sequence-level clas-sification (Devlin et al., 2019). Recent work reveals substantial variation across tasks: some show that mean pooling is highly effective for BERT-based sentiment classification (Xing et al., 2025), while max pooling outperforms average and attention pooling for biomedical relation extraction in Natu-ral Language Processing (NLP) models based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) (Suárez-Paniagua and Segura-Bedmar, 2018). Recent com-prehensive ablations (Ennadir et al., 2025) confirm that pooling choice critically impacts transformer performance across domains; however, these anal-yses do not explicitly target conversational intent classification or evaluate robustness under realistic conversational noise. 

2.3 Frequency-domain methods 

Frequency-domain filtering draws motivation from spectral bias theory (Rahaman et al., 2019), where neural networks prioritize low-frequency patterns. FNet (Lee-Thorp et al., 2022) replaced self-attention with 2D Fourier transforms, achieving 92–97% of BERT performance with 80% faster training. Recent work (Lao et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2024) applies frequency filtering to representations, inputs, and weights, demonstrat-ing low-frequency components capture semantics while high-frequency components often represent noise. While prior work examines frequency fil-tering of intermediate representations (Lin et al., 2023) and attention-based pooling under noisy con-ditions (Brothers, 2025), no prior studies combine 1D FFT low-pass filtering of hidden state sequences with systematic pooling strategy ablation, particu-larly in low-resource conversational settings. The current study evaluates 1D FFT low-pass filtering of transformer hidden states prior to pooling, a set-ting that has not been systematically examined in prior frequency-based work, particularly for con-versational intent classification. In summary, this research fills the identified gaps through systematic evaluation under industrial con-straints: limited labeled data, conversational noise, and deployment efficiency. 

3 Method 

3.1 Current chatbot architecture 

This work is done in collaboration with Axya Inc. (Axya), an industry partner of the subject research group. Axya requires a robust conversa-tional database system capable of retrieving engi-neering information from their databases. Figure 1 illustrates the current architecture of the chatbot pipeline. A user question is directly embedded using an LLM-based question embedder (AWS Titan) and compared against a set of predefined question embeddings. The top-k most similar pre-defined question-query-embedding triplets are se-lected, from which an LLM-based selector (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) chooses a single SQL query template. This template is then used to query a read-only replica of the main database. The retrieved results are passed to an LLM-based data compiler (Claude 3.7 Sonnet), which aggregates the information and generates the final response returned to the user. However, the embedding computation, similar-ity matching against predefined queries, and subse-quent selection introduce significant computational overhead; addressing this inefficiency motivates the proposed method described next. 

3.2 Proposed intent classification pipeline 

This subsection describes the lightweight intent classifier replacing the embed-ding+matching+selection pipeline (green blocks in Figure 1). The core contribution is a systematic evaluation of four pooling configurations with and without FFT filtering for transformer hidden state aggregation under conversational noise. Figure 2 illustrates the pipeline. Hidden states H ∈ RB×T ×d are extracted from the decoder of Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024). Table 2 presents comprehensive results across all nine experimental configurations and four datasets (Clean/Noisy BANKING77, Clean/Noisy CLINC150), reporting F1 scores (mean ± std dev over 10 seeds) and FLOPs. 4.3.1 Pooling performance under clean vs. noisy conditions 

Custom pooling heads consistently outperform the default head across all datasets, with particularly strong improvements under noisy conditions. 

Clean datasets systematically favor last-token pooling (C3: F1=90.79 on BANKING77 and 89.56 on CLINC150). This aligns with the intuition that clean conversational inputs preserve intent signals in final tokens, where discourse markers and po-liteness phrases minimally corrupt the represen-tation. The inferior performance of the default head ( C0: F1=88.80 on BANKING77 and 86.66 on CLINC150) despite using last-token pooling stems from two factors: (1) computing logits across 

all sequence positions before final-token selection introduces gradient noise during fine-tuning, and (2) suboptimal initialization of the default projec-tion layer relative to the custom linear head trained end-to-end on pooled representations. 

Noisy datasets , conversely, favor attention pool-ing (C4: F1=90.31 on BANKING77 and 89.25 CLINC150), achieving 2.64% and 2.83% gains over the default, respectively. The learned token weighting mechanism of attention pooling dynam-ically suppresses conversational noise (greetings, hedging phrases, discourse markers), prioritizing intent-carrying tokens regardless of position.

5 Conclusion 

This study examines intent classification in conver-sational database interfaces under user-generated noise. Across 360 controlled experiments (nine experimental configurations, four datasets – Clean/Noisy BANKING77 and CLINC150 – and ten seeds), it provides evidence-based guidance for practice. Results reveal that custom attention pooling per-forms best under noisy conditions (F1 = 90.31 / 89.25 on BANKING77 / CLINC150; +2.64 / +2.83 over default), learning to suppress greetings and dis-course markers while preserving intent-bearing to-kens. Last-token pooling dominates clean datasets (F1 = 90.79 / 89.56 on BANKING77 / CLINC150; +1.99 / +2.90 over default), benefiting from sta-ble end-position intent signals absent in the default head’s logit computation. FFT low-pass filtering yields no accuracy gains and degrades clean performance via discriminative signal loss. The findings have practical implications for in-dustrial applications: use attention pooling for noisy or multi-turn conversational inputs; reserve last-token pooling for short, clean queries; avoid mean pooling due to consistent degradation; ap-ply max pooling only when intent hinges on a few highly discriminative tokens; FFT filtering can be considered as a structural alternative, with ac-curacy differences remaining small and computa-tional costs comparable to standard configurations. The conclusions are derived from public intent benchmarks with synthetic conversational noise and a constrained model setting (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct with a fixed FFT cutoff), and may not fully capture all production noise patterns or domain-specific intent taxonomies. A detailed discussion of limitations and transfer considerations is provided in Appendix E. These findings address a key upstream bottle-neck for orchestration, retrieval, and SQL genera-tion pipelines, all dependent on accurate first-step intent classification. For manufacturing procure-ment platforms and similar systems, attention pool-ing is recommended as the default for noisy in-terfaces, with last-token pooling limited to clean-query scenarios. The modular classification head integrates with parameter-efficient adaptation (e.g., LoRA), and all code is publicly available (Boga-chov and Letov, 2026), establishing a reproducible baseline for noise-robust LLM classifiers. A Experimental details 

To emulate realistic conversational inputs, syn-thetic noise is applied to both BANKING77 and CLINC150 queries while preserving the original intent. The noise injection procedure augments queries with natural conversational artifacts, includ-ing greetings, contextual clauses, self-corrections, follow-up questions, soft urgency markers, and oc-casional mild typos. Distinct noise styles are ap-plied to each dataset as well as training and test sets to induce a controlled distribution shift. 

Example noise transformations: 

•“What can you do to unblock my pin?” →

“I’m receiving an error message – What can you do to unblock my pin?” 

•“I accidentally made a transaction to the wrong account.” → “I accidentally made a transaction to the wrong account. could you confirm what is happening?” 

This augmentation targets conversational realism rather than adversarial corruption.

This study evaluates pooling strategies for intent classification under synthetic conversational noise in a controlled experimental setting. Although the results are consistent across 360 runs, several limi-tations should be noted. The experiments rely on public benchmarks (BANKING77 and CLINC150) that serve only as proxies for industrial conversational database appli-cations. Domain-specific intent structures, alphanu-meric identifiers, and schema constraints common in real deployments are not fully represented in these datasets, so absolute performance and effect sizes may differ in production. The noisy condition is generated via rule-based augmentation and may not fully reflect real traffic, which can include code-switching, speech-to-text errors, domain jargon, incomplete fragments, multi-intent turns, and adversarial or non-cooperative in-puts. The relative ranking of pooling strategies may therefore shift under different noise distributions. All experiments are conducted using Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct with a linear classification head and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Results may not directly transfer to larger models, different archi-tectures, or alternative layer-aggregation strategies, and pooling is applied only to final-layer hidden states. FFT filtering is evaluated using a fixed 40% low-pass cutoff selected through preliminary sweeps. This simplifies comparison but does not explore adaptive or learnable spectral approaches that could behave differently across datasets, sequence lengths, or noise types. Finally, evaluation emphasizes offline F1 and training compute rather than end-to-end deploy-ment metrics such as inference latency, routing ac-curacy within orchestration pipelines, downstream SQL correctness, user satisfaction, monitoring overhead, or operational cost. While code is pro-vided, exact reproducibility may vary across hard-ware and software environments.
