Robots are entering human spaces faster than we can establish when they de-serve trust. We propose a framework for trustworthy physical AI that integrates Safety, Behavioral Intelligibility, and Perceptual Alignment across embodiment, control, cognition, and design. Trustworthiness emerges from aligning physical capabilities, observable behavior, and expectations people form during interac-tion. 

Robotics has long promised machines that can leave structured industrial settings and operate in the spaces where people live, work, heal, and receive care ( 1, 2 ). Yet this transition has been 1arXiv:2610.07382v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 slower than the field often suggests. Human environments demand robots that can perform useful tasks while remaining safe to interact with, understandable in their behavior, and clear about their capabilities ( 3–6 ). These qualities are closely connected: the way a robot is built shapes how it behaves, and both influence what people expect from it. We use trustworthiness to capture how these qualities come together in supporting appropriate human reliance. A robot designed to limit contact loads may still be difficult to anticipate, while predictable behavior can coexist with harmful contact. Appearance also matters, as an approachable form can shape expectations before the robot has demonstrated what it can do ( 7–10 ). This reveals a broader challenge, as properties usually studied separately come together in the same physical interaction. Understanding how they influence one another motivates our framework for trustworthy physical AI ( 2,11 ). The challenge is becoming commercially urgent as domestic and humanoid robots move from research laboratories into homes and workplaces. More than 50 million Roombas have already operated in private homes ( 12 ), while companies including Tesla, Figure, Sunday Robotics, and 1X are developing platforms explicitly intended for human-centered environments. In this context, trust and trustworthiness should not be conflated. Trust is a human-side relational variable, shaped by uncertainty, prior experience, context, and perceived reliability. Trustworthiness, by contrast, is a context-dependent system property: the extent to which a robot’s embodiment, behavior, and communicative cues support appropriate reliance by both direct users and bystanders during physical interaction ( 6, 13, 14 ). The design objective is therefore to maximize the robot’s trustworthiness across the intended interaction contexts, including for people with limited familiarity over their proximity to it. We propose an integrated framework for trustworthy physical AI that connects the qualities required for trustworthy interaction with the technical means through which they can be achieved, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We identify three system-side dimensions that we regard as necessary for trust-worthy physical interaction: Safety , Behavioral Intelligibility , and Perceptual Alignment . These dimensions address demands that become especially salient as robots enter human environments involving non-expert users and bystanders. 

Safety requires physical risk to remain within context-specific bounds and the robot to recover safely when nominal operation is disrupted ( 15, 16 ). Behavioral Intelligibility captures whether people can anticipate the robot’s actions and infer its goals. Perceptual Alignment concerns whether interaction supports an accurate understanding of the robot’s capabilities and role. The 2meaning and relative importance of these dimensions depend on the interaction context. Broader concerns, including accountability, remain essential and complement the physical-interaction focus developed here. Three technical pillars provide complementary means of shaping these dimensions. Embodied Intelligence captures how the robot’s physical structure and dynamics mediate interaction ( 17 ). 

Motor-Cognitive Intelligence connects sensing and decision making to controlled physical action. 

Communicative Intelligence concerns how the robot makes its state, intentions, and role available to the people interacting with it. Each dimension draws on multiple pillars, as detailed in Table 2. The dimensions define the desired outcomes of trustworthy interaction, while the pillars identify the means through which they can be pursued. Our position is that claims of trustworthiness should be supported by evidence across all three dimensions within the intended interaction context : bounded physical risk, behavior that people can anticipate, and expectations consistent with demonstrated capabilities and limitations. Their alignment should therefore guide design and evaluation from the outset. Achieving this alignment requires jointly considering how embodiment, control, cognition, and communicative cues shape physical interaction and human reliance. Our framework provides a common structure for examining these dependencies and translating them into actionable design recommendations and candidate evaluation criteria. # Safety 

Safety is the capacity to keep physical risk within a task- and user-specific envelope before, during, and after contact. Following established machinery-safety terminology, harm refers to physical injury or damage to health, and risk combines the severity of possible harm with the probability of its occurrence ( 18 ). Many interactive tasks require proximity or deliberate contact ( 1, 19 ), which means that robots should prevent unwanted or inappropriate contact when possible and bound the 4consequences of intended or unavoidable contact. Separation, collision avoidance, and speed limits remain essential in industrial collaborative operation ( 20 ), but applied too conservatively, they can preclude useful physical interaction ( 19 ). Failure can arise on either side of this trade-off. Avoidance-only systems may halt or withdraw so often that meaningful collaboration becomes impossible ( 19 ). Conversely, active safety may fail under loss of observability, inaccurate human-motion prediction, model mismatch, delayed feedback, or infeasible real-time safety filtering ( 21–24 ). If contact occurs, even a compliant-looking robot may generate harmful loads through high speed, reflected inertia, concentrated geometry, stored elastic energy, gravity, or aggressive feedback ( 3, 25, 26 ). Risk may also increase when ambiguous modes or poorly communicated hazards encourage unsafe proximity, hand placement, workspace entry, or timing ( 27 ). Safety must therefore address both the likelihood and severity of harm, including failure detection and recovery. When contact occurs, embodied and motor-cognitive intelligence jointly shape the magnitude, rate, and duration of mechanical loading ( 1, 3 ), while severity also depends on contact geometry, the exposed body region, and the person’s capacity to recoil ( 25, 28 ). Acceptable limits depend on the user and context ( 29 ); interaction near a premature infant’s head or airway, for example, requires evidence specific to that vulnerability. Safety claims should therefore specify intended users, task, environment, permitted contact modes, and assumptions about training or protective equipment. Evaluation must first delimit the interaction envelope and identify relevant hazards ( 18 ). To assess the potential severity of harm, the formalization below focuses on mechanical loading during blunt impact and sustained contact. Clamping, sharp or tool-mediated contact, and thermal, electrical, or chemical hazards require additional criteria ( 3, 25, 29 ). Industrial standards specify force-, pressure-, and body-region-specific limits ( 20 ); biomechanics also studies deformation, loading velocity, transferred energy, and acceleration ( 3, 25 ). Standardized methods measure and analyze contact force and pressure ( 30 ). The associated human-tolerance limits are commonly estimated from controlled impacts or quasi-static compression tests ( 28 ), providing reference bounds under those test conditions. Predicting the load generated by a particular robot requires a closed-loop contact model accounting for the robot’s mechanical properties, contact evolution, and controller dynamics. Embodied intelligence, through smooth geometry, low inertia, high compliance, and energy dissipation can reduce im-pact severity even when active collision avoidance fails ( 1, 3 ). Active compliance enabled by motor-cognitive intelligence shapes stable, yielding interaction through compliant and energy-aware control across rigid and soft robots ( 4, 37, 38 ). Their guarantees usually concern stability, passivity, or energy ( 37 ) rather than direct satisfaction of an injury threshold. They should therefore form the nominal interaction behavior complemented when necessary by explicit injury-aware or contact-force constraints ( 26, 32, 33 ). Motor-cognitive intelligence further extends to active risk control, beginning with human-state estimation and motion prediction to anticipate where and when contact may occur, the relative motion involved, and which body regions are exposed ( 19, 39 ). Confidence-aware and robust predictions can update planning and control constraints ( 22 ). Their uncertainty should inform conservative margins and task-appropriate clearance, speed ( 40 ), and interaction impedance ( 41 ). Information gathering can defer irreversible actions ( 42 ). Trials varying noise, occlusion, and conditions outside the training data should test these responses and fallback selection, including whether unnecessary caution subsides as uncertainty resolves. Safety-aware planning and runtime assurance seek to maintain these bounds through explicit safety constraints and filters ( 43 ). Safety should shape the nominal policy early: a filter acting only at the boundary may become infeasible or require fallback, intervene abruptly, or sacrifice performance when the planner repeatedly proposes unsafe motion ( 24 ). These approaches are increasingly being extended across robotic embodiments, including shape and contact-force constraints ( 26, 33, 44 ). Across embodiments, guarantees still depend on model fidelity, observability, real-time computation, and actuator authority ( 24 ). Robust margins should incorporate uncertainty in human motion and contact parameters, while runtime assurance should specify recovery when these assumptions fail or faults are detected ( 21, 45 ). During contact, tactile sensing and contact localization can support contact detection and appropriate reflex responses ( 46 ); learned tactile features can also help distinguish intended physical interaction from unintended collision ( 47 ). Communicative intelligence should provide people 7sharing the workspace with actionable cues about safety-relevant robot context, including intended contact and recovery ( 27 ). These cues should communicate robot intent and status early enough for people to respond ( 48,49 ). Together with speed and separation, they also shape perceived safety ( 5). Their effectiveness should be validated for the intended users and environment. By helping people remain within the robot’s actual interaction envelope, they complement mechanical and control safeguards ( 18 ). We therefore propose three linked design commitments: reduce hazardous contact loads through embodied intelligence ; maintain safety margins and support recovery through motor-cognitive intelligence ; and make safety-relevant context actionable through communicative intelligence . Their effectiveness should be evaluated jointly within the intended interaction context. People must also be able to anticipate what the robot will do and how it will respond to uncertainty. That requirement motivates behavioral intelligibility. 

# Behavioral Intelligibility 

Behavioral intelligibility is the system-side capacity comprising predictability , whether someone who knows the task and environment can anticipate the robot’s motion; and legibility , whether observed motion reveals its goal or target ( 7). Even if a robot is safe and capable of performing a task, it is not trustworthy if it requires continuous monitoring or moves unpredictably or abruptly. Failures can occur even when a robot executes its task correctly: an assistive arm may not reveal whether it will hand over or set down a cup; a mobile robot may not signal whether it will yield; and a humanoid’s gaze and reach may indicate different interaction partners or turn-taking roles. Intent may therefore become clear too late for coordination, while hesitation may reflect caution, yielding, replanning, or failure. Conversely, a legible goal does not guarantee predictable motion: abrupt replanning, jitter, or implausible recovery can still violate expectations. In soft robots, distributed deformation and stored elastic energy complicate motion tracking and surface following ( 38 ); hysteresis and recoil may cause motion at the contact surface to differ from the commanded motion. Reliability drops can reduce real-time trust ( 50 ), while close, rapid approaches may elicit involuntary startle responses ( 40 ). Trust is also shaped by error magnitude ( 51 ) and by sequences of errors, particularly near the end of an interaction ( 52 ). Evaluation should therefore 8account for rare but consequential events: a single unexplained lunge or force spike may outweigh many nominal trials. # Perceptual Alignment 

This section addresses how robots can shape what users perceive them to be, so that perception aligns with what they can actually do. We frame this as the alignment between a robot’s actual interaction 11 envelope and the user’s inferred model of it, drawing on work in perceived safety, transparency, ergonomics, and calibrated trust ( 5, 13, 59 ). Misalignment fails in two directions: under-reliance, when capable systems are avoided, and over-reliance, when appearance implies capabilities the robot does not possess, what Brooks calls a violation of the appearance promise (60 ). Soft robots make this tension especially visible. Their compliant bodies and approachable morphologies are often perceived as gentle, safe, and trustworthy ( 59, 61 ), yet softness alone guarantees neither bounded forces, robust control, nor transparent failure recovery. Where safety bounds physical risk and behavioral intelligibility makes ongoing action interpretable, Perceptual alignment ensures that users correctly infer a robot’s functional capabilities, reliability, compliance, safety limits, and intended role. This calibration has two coupled layers. The first is epistemic calibration : whether users accurately infer the robot’s capabilities, reliability, uncertainty, and appropriate conditions of use. The second is social-affective calibration : whether users adopt an appropriate interpersonal stance toward the robot, including comfort, approachability, and perceived social role. The layers are analytically distinct but practically coupled: the same appearance, motion, softness, or social behavior shapes both what users believe the robot can do and how comfortable they feel interacting with it. Miscalibration begins before interaction does. People read a robot’s appearance and demeanor quickly and largely automatically, and use these cues to infer its abilities, personality, and appropriate role ( 9). In design terms, these are perceived affordances: cues from which users infer what interaction is possible, whether or not the underlying system supports it ( 62 ). These attributions move trust independently of capability: the automation literature warned early that humanlike cues invite expectations a system cannot meet ( 13 ), and two decades of meta-analysis show trust in robots increasingly attaching to attributes such as form, personality, and anthropomorphism rather than to measured performance ( 63, 64 ). The resulting expectation gap fails in both directions ( 65 ). Overestimation produces reliance that observed faults do not correct: participants complied with irreversible and privacy-sensitive requests from a visibly faulty home robot even while rating it less reliable and less trustworthy ( 66 ). Underestimation is not mere caution but a self-reinforcing failure: low expectations suppress interaction, so capabilities remain undiscovered, and the inaccurate model persists ( 65 ); a capable manipulator that exposes nothing of its force regulation or active safety invites exactly this failure. The two failures are not symmetric. Overestimation in physical 12 interaction can end in contact the user did not anticipate, while underestimation leaves useful capabilities unused. The overestimation failure is now being engineered at scale. Home robot products, including several named above, lead with expressive faces, plush or textile surfaces, and curated demonstration videos, cues that build trust through first impressions and media exposure before any capability has been observed ( 65 ). These cues work because their meanings are inherited: decades of fiction have taught audiences to read large eyes and soft features as kindness, and an armored humanoid silhouette as competence. Exposure to fictional robots measurably raises expectations of what real ones can do ( 67 ), and cuteness raises the perceived trustworthiness of robot faces directly ( 68 ), a strategy already described as a dark pattern in home robot design ( 69 ). The two aesthetics divide the labor: companion forms target the social-affective layer of calibration, heroic forms the epistemic one. Little exposure is needed: in a recent caregiving study, a single brief interaction with a soft gripper was enough to significantly increase trust in robots ( 61 ). Trust acquired this way is borrowed, not earned. Expectations set high produce sharper disappointment when faults arrive ( 10 ), and trust lost to negative events recovers slowly ( 13 ). Whether these design choices survive the first year of deployment is an open and largely untested question. Evaluating perceptual alignment requires measuring both sides of the alignment. Lee and See provide the constructs for trust in automation: calibration , the correspondence between trust and actual capability; resolution , whether trust differentiates levels of capability; and specificity , whether trust tracks the right function, mode, and moment ( 13 ). A user with poor resolution trusts a robot the same whether it succeeds at grasping 60% or 90% of the time; a user with poor specificity extends trust earned in one task or mode to the whole system, assuming a robot that grasps reliably will also hand over safely. These constructs describe the user’s judgment, but the burden they imply falls on the robot: it is the system that must afford resolution and specificity, by making differences in capability observable across functions, modes, and time. The field of Human-Robot Interaction has measured the inferred side almost exclusively through single-shot self-report after brief encounters ( 63 ). This is insufficient for two reasons. First, attitudinal ratings dissociate from behavior ( 66 ): reliance, compliance, monitoring, and intervention rates are the more diagnostic signals. Second, calibration is dynamic. First impressions are dominated by appearance priors and novelty effects that decay over weeks ( 70 ), trust is history-dependent ( 13 ), and user mental models 13 drift as learned systems drift ( 71 ). The alignment target therefore moves on both sides: a learned policy may improve while the user’s model stays fixed at what onboarding showed, or hardware may wear while confidence grows through uneventful use, so the overtrusting user after many routine handovers is the case that matters most and the one that single session studies cannot see ( 11 ). Evaluation should therefore pair perceived and measured interaction envelopes, quantifying how accurately users infer interaction-relevant properties such as confidence, sensing limits, and contact tolerance, favor behavioral over attitudinal evidence, and span repeated interaction rather than a single point ( 65 ). Designing for calibration starts from matching rather than maximizing. Appearance, demeanor, and social cues should fit a robot’s actual role and capability ( 9), and setting expectations low pro-duces less disappointment and more positive appraisal than overselling ( 10 ). Calibration requires addressing both directions of misalignment: overtrust can be reduced by revealing the conditions under which the robot may fail, while undertrust can be reduced by demonstrating its capabili-ties ( 11 ). Limitations can be communicated through motion that expresses incapability ( 72 ), expo-sure to states where a policy is likely to fail ( 73 ), and explicit disclosure of partial reliability ( 13 ). Today, this learning is mostly left to chance: drivers of partially automated vehicles discover what the system can and cannot do by watching it fail in the first few drives ( 71 ). Capability onboarding would make it deliberate: structured first sessions in which users experience the robot’s boundaries before unsupervised use, treating calibration as a prerequisite rather than a byproduct of ownership. Uncertainty, mode changes, and requests for intervention should be salient in interaction-relevant terms, with the caveat that transparency cues must themselves be calibrated: how confidence is presented changes how it is used ( 71 ). Current approachable robot designs are converging towards a design language defined by fiction and anime. But it is a vocabulary of warmth and competence, not of capability and limitation, so the cues that would support calibration remain bespoke, rein-vented by every platform. The corrective is a deliberate vocabulary: shared, learnable capability and limitation signals, standardized in the way traffic signals are, so calibration transfers across robots rather than restarting with each one. Perceptual alignment closes the loop of our framework: a robot that is physically safe and behaviorally intelligible still fails as a trustworthy system if users cannot form accurate beliefs about what it can do, when it may fail, and what role it should hold. Engineering this alignment 14 across embodiment, control, and communicative intelligence is the subject of the outlook that follows. 16 Outlook: Toward a Science of Trustworthy Physical Interaction 

The value of this framework lies in treating trustworthiness as an emergent property of the whole robot. As Figs. 1 & 2 and Table 2 illustrate, safety, behavioral intelligibility, and perceptual alignment depend on how embodiment, control, and design work together. A change intended to improve one dimension can alter the others. A more compliant body may soften contact while making motion harder to regulate; greater adaptability may increase capability while reducing predictability; and a more expressive robot may be easier to approach while inviting expectations it cannot meet. Calibrating those expectations to demonstrated capability is a core design responsibility, especially when commercial incentives reward adoption, engagement, and perceived capability. We argue that these couplings should guide design and evaluation jointly. Robots with deformable components (e.g., soft robots ( 74 )) provide a particularly revealing setting in which to study these couplings, while the same principles extend to humanoids, collaborative manipulators, assistive devices, domestic robots, and systems governed by end-to-end learned policies. The three dimensions also suggest a research agenda for integrated evaluation, summarized in Table 1. Evaluation should characterize trustworthiness as a multidimensional profile tied to explicit users, tasks, and operating conditions, revealing how design choices shift safety, behavioral intelligibility, and perceptual alignment together. Future benchmarks could combine these measures during realistic physical tasks and examine how design choices shift the profile across dimensions. Evaluation should also move from one-shot impressions toward evidence of sustained appropriate reliance. Longitudinal studies will be especially important for understanding how initial impressions develop into stable patterns of reliance over repeated interactions ( 13, 70 ). Digital twins that integrate contact dynamics with models of human expectation and interpretation could further support trustworthiness assessment before deployment. Over time, insights shared across platforms could also form a recognizable language of physical interaction. Recurring ways of expressing confidence, adaptation, and the need for participation would allow experience with one robot to inform interaction with another. Such a language could connect robot design, evaluation, and standardization around a common understanding of how trustworthy behavior is physically expressed. Embodied Intelligence Motor-Cognitive Intelligence Communicative Intelligence Safety Passive harm mitigation. Mechanical compliance, damping, mass distri-bution, contact area, surface texture, and mechanical stops shape contact severity. Soft or compliant bodies can reduce peak forces, pressures, and transferred energy when perception, planning, or control fail. 

Active risk control. Human-aware sensing, human motion prediction, stable control, collision avoidance, safe motion generation, fail-safe recovery, and safety filters actively bound risk under uncertainty in closed-loop interaction. Controller gains, impedance/admittance design, latency, and passivity directly affect safety. 

Behavior-shaping safety cues. Mode indicators, motion previews, lights, sounds, displays, transparent warn-ings, and role-consistent appearance shape human behavior around the robot, including proximity, handover timing, workspace entry, and appro-priate reliance. 

Behavioral Intelligibility Intelligible embodied dynamics. 

Passive dynamics, compliant or un-deractuated morphology, soft contact, and visible shape adaptation create physical regularities that humans can learn. Hidden deformation, hysteresis, nonlinear material response, or os-cillation can make behavior harder to predict. 

Predictable and legible motion. 

Low-level stability and high-level motion policies determine whether actions are smooth, bounded, repeat-able, goal-directed, and consistent with human expectations. Perception failures, abrupt replanning, jitter, hid-den timing references, or unexplained pauses can make motion appear un-predictable. 

Observable state, intent, and up-coming action. Appearance, affor-dances, gaze-like orientation, body posture, lights, sounds, displays, trans-parent covers, and motion previews help humans infer the robot’s state, goal, mode, turn-taking structure, and upcoming action. 

Perceptual Alignment Physical compliance calibration. 

Visual, tactile, material, geometric, and scale cues should make perceived softness, stiffness, yielding, iner-tia, and contact tolerance match the robot’s actual passive mechanical compliance. This calibrates the em-bodied component of perceived safety: what the body itself can absorb or limit during contact. 

Competence, reliability, and active-safety calibration. Observable mo-tion and autonomy cues, such as smoothness, responsiveness, con-sistency, recovery, hesitation, inter-ruption, and failure handling, should make perceived confidence, com-petence, reliability, and active risk control match the robot’s safety-aware control capabilities. 

Aesthetic-social approachability. 

Appearance, morphology, expressive-ness, surface-material style, perceived cuteness, and behavioral congruence should make people feel appropri-ately comfortable approaching, being near, and socially interacting with the robot. These cues should match the robot’s intended role and actual social-interaction capabilities.
