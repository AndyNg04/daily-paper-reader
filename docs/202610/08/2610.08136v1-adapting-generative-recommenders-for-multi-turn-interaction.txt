Abstract. Generative recommenders decode items from a user’s in-teraction history, but offer no way for users to correct a recommen-dation that misses their current intent. Adding conversation is natu-ral since items and words share same output space, yet training the model to converse may overwrite the history-to-item mapping it relies on. We introduce INTEGER ( INTE ractive GE nerative Recommendation), which extends generative recommendation to multi-turn interaction with a learned routing token that lets the model decide when to recommend, history re-anchoring that conditions each item on both past behavior and the dialogue, and behavioral replay with instruction-data rehearsal that prevents forgetting during adaptation. Users can thus give feed-back on recommendations within the dialogue, while recommendations stay grounded in behavioral history and accuracy is not traded for flu-ency. On Amazon Beauty and Toys, INTEGER matches or exceeds the strongest baselines in accuracy with competitive conversation quality, improving Hit@10 by 13.3% on Amazon Beauty, and significantly out-performs the generative recommender it starts from. Our analyses show that INTEGER learns behaviors that naive adaptation fails to acquire, recommending once the user’s intent is clear and staying attentive to behavioral history at the moment of recommendation. INTEGER also learns an intent-agnostic replacement over the item space, which sup-presses rejected items but points to attribute-aware feedback as the next step. 

Keywords: Generative Recommendation · Conversational Recommen-dation · Dialogue Systems 

## 1 Introduction 

Generative recommendation has recently emerged as a promising paradigm for sequential recommendation [2,6,7,27,41]. Rather than retrieving items from an external index built over learned user and item embeddings [8,14,29], generative arXiv:2610.08136v1 [cs.IR] 6 Oct 2026 2 Den et al. 

recommendation decodes the identifier of the target item directly, represent-ing each item as a sequence of discrete codes derived from its content embed-ding [12,16]. This places items and words in the same output space and has proven competitive with other strong sequential recommenders [14,15,29]. However, these models rely on a fixed interaction history to infer user prefer-ences and generate recommended items, giving users no direct way to correct the system when its recommendation misses their current intent [27,36,41]. When the user’s preference changes or the recommendation fails to capture what they currently want, the model cannot receive that correction explicitly. Instead, the updated preference must first be reflected in subsequent item interactions before it becomes observable to the model, making preference correction indirect and potentially slow. Conversational recommendation systems (CRS) let users express preferences through dialogue [28,34], but they typically learn to recommend from the con-versational context itself. Generative recommenders, in contrast, learn a direct mapping from interaction history to item identifiers, capturing preference sig-nals accumulated from users’ past actions. When item identifiers are tokens in a language model’s vocabulary, recommending an item and generating a response become the same operation, so conversational ability can be added without ar-chitectural change. However, this shared output space also means that both be-haviors are carried by the same parameters: training the model to converse may overwrite the history-to-item mapping it has already acquired [33]. We therefore ask whether a generative recommender can learn to converse without losing the recommendation behavior it was originally trained on. 

We solve this problem with INTE ractive GE nerative Recommendation (IN-TEGER) , which empowers generative recommendation to interact with users through multi-turn conversation. Its design is grounded in a single principle: conversation acts as a control layer over a generative recommender instead of recommendation signals. Recommendation therefore remains in the history-to-item mapping acquired from interaction sequences, while dialogue governs when 

that mapping is invoked and how its output is presented and revised. Our contributions are threefold. First, we expose recommendation as a learned decoding action, where a routing token decides when to recommend and history re-anchoring re-emits the user history right before item generation. Second, we combine behavioral replay with instruction-data rehearsal to preserve recom-mendation accuracy during conversational adaptation. On Amazon Beauty and Toys [9], INTEGER is competitive with both end-to-end CRS and modular pipelines and significantly outperforms the generative recommender it is adapted from. Third, our analyses show how conversational and behavioral training erode each other and how re-anchoring and rehearsal counteract this, and that re-finement learns a single intent-agnostic replacement. ## 2 Background 

2.1 Multi-Turn Interaction 

In multi-turn conversation, a model converses with a user over multiple turns. We write the dialogue up to the t-th turn as Dt = ( u1, a 1, . . . , u t−1, a t−1, u t),where ui and ai denote the i-th user message and assistant response, and Dt ends at the pending user message ut. The response at is generated autoregressively conditioned on Dt. When the interaction serves recommendation, the model needs to identify the turns at which a recommendation is warranted and surface an item consistent with the intent expressed so far in Dt, while still generating fluent responses. Existing work approaches this task in three main ways. Conversational recom-mender systems (CRS) learn recommendation directly from multi-turn dialogue, jointly modeling preference elicitation, item recommendation, and response gen-eration [28,34,42]. Other work treats conversation as an additional feedback channel for an existing recommender, converting it into pseudo-interactions for sequential recommendation [36] or into preference signals [30]. A third line adopts a modular design, in which an LLM handles the interaction and delegates rec-ommendation to an external model or tool [5,11,17,43]. 

2.2 Generative Recommendation 

Conventional sequential recommenders [8,14,22,29] assign each item a unique ID and score items through a large embedding table. Generative recommendation instead represents each item as a sequence of discrete tokens and generates the next item by autoregressive decoding [2,7,27,41]. Existing work has focused on semantic ID design [13,20,46], training and decoding algorithms [6,10,19,35,44], and multimodal item representations [21,32,38,47]. In all of these settings, rec-ommendation is one-shot: the model produces an item from a fixed interaction history, and the user cannot revise its input except through further interactions. Formally, a generative recommender maps a user’s interaction history H =(h1, . . . , h T ) to a ranked list of item identifiers (ItemIDs). Each item is rep-resented by an ItemID z = ( c1, . . . , c L), a sequence of L discrete codewords obtained by RQ-KMeans [13] from the item’s content embedding, so that se-mantically similar items share longer codeword prefixes. ## 3 INTEGER: Interactive Generative Recommendation 

We start with a generative recommender [27] trained to predict an ItemID from a user’s interaction history H (Section 2.2). To apply this model to conversation, we place H in the system prompt at the beginning of the dialogue. At each turn t,the model receives this prompt and the dialogue context Dt, then decides whether to continue chatting or recommend an item. INTEGER makes recommendation a decoding action and places the interaction history next to the generated ItemID when that action is taken. Figure 1 illustrates the inference process. 

3.1 Recommendation as a Decoding Action 

We introduce a learned routing token <hist> into the model’s vocabulary. At the start of an assistant turn, emitting <hist> initiates a recommendation; other-wise, the model produces a conversational response. The decision to recommend is therefore made by the same model that generates the response, without an external routing classifier [1,25]. Internally, a recommendation turn follows the format <hist> H <rec> z′,where z′ is the recommended item’s ItemID. 3.2 Conversational Adaptation with Behavioral Replay 

We adapt the generative recommender with a mixture of three training sources: multi-turn recommendation dialogues, sequential recommendation examples, and general instruction-following examples. Each source teaches a different part of the desired behavior. The multi-turn dialogues teach the model when to emit <hist> , how to respond conversationally, and how to produce recommendations in the format shown in Section 3.1. Training only on these dialogues can weaken the history-to-item mapping acquired during generative recommendation training [33]. We therefore reuse examples from the original sequential recommendation training set during adaptation. We call this behavioral replay : the model continues to train on history-to-ItemID prediction while learning to converse. Behavioral re-play acts on the model parameters during training, whereas history re-anchoring places H in the decoding context when a recommendation is generated. We also include general instruction-following examples [3] to strengthen re-sponse generation alongside the recommendation-focused training data. Together, the three sources train the model to manage a dialogue while retaining the be-havioral mapping used for item generation. We train on their mixture with the 6 Den et al.

3.3 Item Refinement 

After receiving a recommendation, a user may reject it or ask for another item similar to one they accepted. INTEGER handles these follow-up requests within the same dialogue: the previous recommendation and the user’s feedback become part of Dt+1 , which let the routing and ItemID decoding procedure can produce a revised recommendation. We train this behavior using preference pairs constructed for two feedback types. For rejection , y− is the model’s erroneous top-1 recommendation and y+

is the ground-truth item. For variety , y− is the currently recommended item and y+ is its nearest neighbor, determined by cosine similarity between item content embeddings. Both pair types share the same structure: the preferred continuation replaces the current item rather than repeating it. We further apply Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [26]:

4.3 Ablation Studies 

Table 4 confirms that each component contributes to at least recommendation performance or conversation quality and that the two behavioral safeguards carry most of the weight. Training on dialogues alone collapses on both axes (Hits@10= 0.029 , Conv. Q= 2.39 ). Against the core configuration, removing his-tory re-anchoring or behavioral replay costs roughly 33% of recommendation accuracy (0.049 to 0.033 and 0.034), while removing the learned routing token costs comparatively little, since routing governs when the model recommends rather than how well. That re-anchoring and replay degrade to nearly identical points despite acting through different channels, one at decoding time and one on the parameters, indicates that neither substitutes for the other. Instruction tuning data, finally, is what converts the core recipe into a usable conversational system: it leaves accuracy essentially unchanged (0.049 to 0.051) while lifting conversation quality by 0.63, and removing replay from the core recipe raises conversation quality (4.14 → 4.37), consistent with behavioral data suppressing the language ability that rehearsal restores.

5.2 Two Directions of Forgetting 

Figure 3 shows the attention to the prompted history at the moment when the model recommends an item. The model trained on dialogues alone (Table 4, last row) attends slightly less to the history when it recommends than when it chats (0.86 ×), while ours attends 2.11 × more. Focusing on prompted history at the recommendation point is a learned behavior, and dialogue-only finetuning does not acquire it. The right panel shows why an adjacent copy H helps. Attention to the far prompt history falls off as the dialogue pushes it further behind the recommendation point ( 0.81 × for our model, 0.63 × for the dialogue-only model, first to last distance), while attention to the adjacent history recap H is flat (1.00 ×). The decay is therefore not the mechanical consequence of a longer prompt, which would move all three curves together. Re-anchoring works because it puts the same tokens on a channel that dialogue length cannot dilute, and it is the copied content that matters. 5.4 Case Study 

Figure 5 illustrates the routing difference from Section 5.1 on a single dialogue. The user opens without a clear goal "just browsing" and only gradually re-veals what they want. Bare replay recommends an item at every assistant turn, even before the user has expressed any preference. INTEGER instead responds conversationally, asking about product type and skin concerns, and withholds a recommendation until the third turn, when the user mentions patchy foundation. 

## 6 Conclusion & Future Work 

In this work, we presented INTEGER, a model that extends a generative rec-ommender to multi-turn interaction by exposing recommendation as a decoding action rather than learning it from dialogue. On Amazon Beauty and Toys, IN-TEGER is competitive with both end-to-end conversational recommenders and modular pipelines in accuracy and conversation quality, and significantly outper-forms the generative recommender it starts from. Our analyses show that replay, re-anchoring, and rehearsal together prevent conversational and behavioral train-ing from eroding each other. Our findings are limited by the use of synthesized dialogues and by refinement that recovers near-misses rather than satisfying stated preferences. Handling attribute-level feedback (e.g., too expensive , I want a black one ) would require conditioning refinement on item attributes, whereas the operation we learn is intent-agnostic and moves only within the embedding neighborhood.
