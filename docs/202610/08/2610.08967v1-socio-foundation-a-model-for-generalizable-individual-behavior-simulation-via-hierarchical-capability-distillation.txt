# Socio-Foundation: A Model for Generalizable Individual Behavior Simulation via Hierarchical Capability Distillation 

Liang Wang 1,∗, Wenxuan Xie 1,2,∗, Xinyi Mou 1,3, Yixin Luo 1, Zhongyu Wei 1,2,†

1School of Data Science, Fudan University, 2Shanghai Innovation Institute, 3Tencent 

> ∗

Equal contribution. , †Corresponding author. 

Simulating individual behavior requires large language models (LLMs) to preserve persona traits while adapting to dynamic social contexts. However, general-purpose LLMs often flatten distinct personas, while task-specific tuning suffers from fragmentation and generalization. To overcome these challenges, we organize individual simulation into the FONTS Taxonomy , comprising five complementary capability dimensions: persona fidelity (F), outcome realization (O), behavioral naturalness (N), trajectory coherence 

(T), and social grounding (S). Grounded in this taxonomy, we curate a standardized training corpus library of approximately 10 million instances across 14 representative datasets and present Socio-Foundation .Socio-Foundation decouples specialization from integration via a three-stage pipeline: learning task experts via DAPO, consolidating them into capability experts via off-policy distillation, and unifying them via multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). We also establish IndiEval , consolidating 29 metrics across the FONTS dimensions. Experiments show that Socio-Foundation outperforms its Qwen3-8B base by 11.0 points and approaches frontier models such as GLM-5.2 , with ablations and out-of-distribution evaluations further demonstrating the effectiveness and generalization of our model.

## 1 Introduction 

Individual simulation with large language models (LLMs) aims to reproduce how a particular person communicates, makes decisions, and responds across social contexts Mou et al. (2024a); Xi et al. (2025). It supports applications including persona-grounded dialogue Tang et al. (2024), user-behavior prediction Wang et al. (2026a), social-science experimentation Mou et al. (2024b), and multi-agent role-play Wang et al. (2025b). Classical social-behavior theories Lewin (1936); Rao and Georgeff (1995) establish that human action is a joint function of personal dispositions and changing environments. 1arXiv:2610.08967v1 [cs.AI] 6 Oct 2026 General-purpose foundation models inherently fall short of this objective. While their pretraining captures broad population-level linguistic regularities, contemporary post-training methods such as RLHF and DPO are heavily biased toward verifiable reasoning, safety, and goal completion tasks like coding and mathematics. Moreover, preference optimization often favors responses that align with the dominant preference distribution, which can drive the model toward behavioral homogeneity and reduce its sensitivity to distinctive, long-tail personas Slocum et al. (2025); Li et al. (2025b). As illustrated in Figure 1(a), this mismatch causes LLMs to produce homogenized, “flattened” behaviors that obscure genuine individual differences, fundamentally limiting simulation fidelity. To mitigate this homogenization, existing methods primarily rely on prompt-based conditioning or task-driven post-training Wang et al. (2026b); Liu et al. (2025); Zhang et al. (2025b); Ge et al. (2024). However, prompting merely supplies persona information at inference time but leaves the model’s underlying behavioral prior unchanged. Task-driven post-training provides direct behavioral supervision but suffers from limited transfer ability to unseen settings, as shown in Figure 1(b). Recent attempts endeavor to bridge this gap by jointly training on aggregated datasets from multiple scenarios, as shown in Figure 1(c) Lei et al. (2026); Zhou et al. (2026a). Yet, simply pooling heterogeneous corpora often triggers severe objective interference, as conflicting supervision signals from disparate tasks can corrupt task-specific behaviors Mueller et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2025a); Yuan et al. (2025). Resolving this conflict requires moving beyond ad-hoc data mixing: we must systematically disentangle the foundational capabilities of individual simulation and make a trade-off. To this end, we analyze over 40 widely adopted individual simulation datasets (comprising > 19M instances). We annotate the capability requirements of these datasets and manually group them into eight preliminary clusters. Guided by McAdams and Pals’ New Big Five framework (McAdams and Pals, 2006), we consolidate these clusters into the FONTS Taxonomy , comprising five complementary capability dimensions: persona fidelity (F), outcome realization (O), behavioral naturalness (N), trajectory coherence (T), and social grounding (S). These dimensions bridge fragmented task settings by formalizing their shared behavioral requirements. Guided by this taxonomy, we curate 14 representative datasets that maximize capability coverage and data quality, yielding a standardized, large-scale training corpus library of ∼10M instances. Building on the FONTS taxonomy, we propose Socio-Foundation , trained via a three-stage post-training pipeline designed to circumvent cross-task interference. Instead of forcing a single policy to optimize conflicting objectives directly, we decouple specialization from integration: we first train task-level experts in isolated environments with DAPO Yu et al. (2026) to mature divergent behaviors independently, distill them by capability affinity into five capability experts via off-policy distillation Kim and Rush (2016), and finally unify these experts into a single student through multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) Ma et al. (2026). Supervised by frozen experts on its self-generated rollouts, the model reconciles competing signals without policy drift, yielding a cohesive simulator that harmonizes macro-level trajectory coherence with micro-level persona fidelity. To rigorously benchmark individual simulation, we introduce IndiEval , a comprehensive evaluation suite consolidat-ing 29 task-specific metrics categorized under the five target capabilities while preserving their original evaluation protocols. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Socio-Foundation consistently outperforms its base model 

Qwen3-8B across all five dimensions, achieving an average score of 61.7 (an ∼11-point gain), surpassing other specialized models, and approaching frontier generalist models such as GLM-5.2 . Systematic ablations validate our three-stage hierarchical design: MOPD significantly outperforms the tested weight-merging and off-policy al-ternatives, and removing any single capability expert lowers the overall average, supporting the complementary contributions of the five capability experts. Furthermore, Socio-Foundation exhibits superior zero-shot transfer on three out-of-distribution benchmarks. Our contributions are: • We introduce the FONTS Taxonomy of five complementary individual-simulation capabilities and curate a ∼10M-instance high quality corpus library from 14 representative datasets. • We develop a three-stage post-training framework that learns task experts, consolidates them by capability, and integrates them by OPD into a unified simulator Socio-Foundation .• We present IndiEval , a 29-metric evaluation suite spanning the FONTS Taxonomy, and show our model outper-forms Qwen3-8B by ∼11 points and transfers zero-shot to three OOD benchmarks. 

## 2 Related works 

Individual simulation. Existing work on individual simulation asks how a LLM can generate behavior conditioned on a particular person rather than on population-level regularities. Persona descriptions Argyle et al. (2023); Park et al. (2023), interviews Park et al. (2024), memories, and interaction histories provide the conditioning signal, while prompting and retrieval, parameter-efficient adaptation, and reward-based post-training are used to encode and refine individual characteristics Argyle et al. (2023); Shao et al. (2023); Yu et al. (2024); Lu et al. (2024); 2Wang et al. (2024, 2025c). A remaining challenge is therefore to build a shared model that preserves persona fidelity while transferring across social contexts and behavioral requirements. 

Foundation models for individual simulation. Recent work has begun to learn reusable individual-level behavior across roles and contexts. Role-playing and user-simulation models learn persona-grounded dialogue and user-side interaction policies Shao et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2025b); Naous et al. (2026a), while cognition and behavioral foundation models learn transferable response patterns from psychological experiments, social-science data, and large-scale interaction corpora Binz et al. (2024); Xie et al. (2025); Gong et al. (2025); Kolluri et al. (2025). A common recipe first performs broad-domain adaptation and then task-specific post-training, consolidating the resulting specialists into a unified model via model merging Lei et al. (2026) or trajectory-level SFT distillation Zhou et al. (2026a). Recent on-policy and multi-teacher distillation methods further decouple specialist optimization from integration into a generalist model Agarwal et al. (2024); Ma et al. (2026); Wu et al. (2026c). Yet how heterogeneous experts should be organized around the cross-task capabilities required for individual simulation remains underexplored.

## 4 Socio-Foundation: Hierarchical Expert Distillation 

We organize the landscape of individual simulation into five capability dimensions through the FONTS taxonomy. Building on this capability framework, we design a three-stage post-training pipeline to develop Socio-Foundation, a unified individual simulator. Figure 2 provides an overview of the framework. Using this capability framework, we map the more than 40 collected datasets to the corresponding dimensions, with the detailed assignments provided in Appendix A. 

4.1 Five Dimension Capability Organization 

We organize individual-simulation capabilities in two steps. (i) Bottom-up: capability annotation and clustering. 

We examine the task formulations, interaction scenarios, and evaluation protocols of more than 40 individual-simulation datasets. For each dataset, we annotate the behavioral capabilities required by its tasks. We then manually group these requirements according to shared behavioral demands, yielding eight preliminary capability clusters. (ii) Top-down: theory-guided consolidation. We refine and consolidate these clusters with reference to McAdams and Pals’ New Big Five, which provides an integrative account of the whole person McAdams and Pals (2006). This theory-guided consolidation yields the five complementary capability dimensions of the FONTS Taxonomy :

Persona Fidelity (F). Captures the target individual’s experiences, preferences, values, and characteristic ways of communicating and making decisions. 

Outcome Realization (O). Concerns pursuing the individual’s goals and realizing intended outcomes across tasks and social contexts. 

Behavioral Naturalness (N). Concerns producing natural expression and interaction patterns that reflect the vari-ability and statistical properties of human behavior. 

Trajectory Coherence (T). Concerns maintaining behavioral consistency and ensuring coherent progression across turns and events, both causally and contextually. 4Social Grounding (S). Involves reasoning about beliefs, intentions, and emotions while respecting knowledge bound-aries and private information. These dimensions guide the consolidation of task experts into capability-level experts and provide a shared structure for evaluation. A task may contribute to multiple dimensions when its behavioral requirements and evaluation metrics address different aspects of individual simulation. More details of datasets can be found in Appendix A.

4.5 Implementation Details 

Training datasets. We collect more than 40 datasets for individual simulation, yielding over 19 million instances after processing and cleaning. We select training datasets based on three criteria: (i) sufficient scale for task-expert training; (ii) high data quality; and (iii) clear alignment with capability dimensions and complementary coverage of scenarios and capabilities. This yields 14 training datasets comprising approximately 10 million instances: LifeChoices Xu et al. (2025), AlignX Li et al. (2026), HumanLLM Lei et al. (2026), BehaviorChain Li et al. (2025a), CoSER Wang et al. (2025b), HUMANUAL Wu et al. (2026b) (four separately counted subtask datasets), UserLM-S3 Naous et al. (2026b), FANToM Kim et al. (2023), Social-R1 Wu et al. (2026a), SOTOPIA Zhou et al. (2024), and MirrorBench Hathidara et al. (2026). Training inputs for DAPO and OPD are sampled from this corpus library, with sampling details provided in Appendix C.4. 5Training details. All three training stages share a Qwen3-8B backbone. We optimize a separate rank-32 LoRA task expert with DAPO for each task, distill the resulting task experts into five capability experts, and train the final student against frozen capability teachers on student-generated trajectories. Training uses bfloat16, a 16,384-token context window, and disabled thinking mode, with DeepSeek-V4-Flash DeepSeek (2026) serving as both the auxiliary dialogue model and the reward model. Appendix C provides the stage-wise optimizer, sampling, and distillation settings. 

## 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental setup 

We evaluate individual simulation across five capability dimensions, examine the contributions of the training pipeline, and assess generalization on additional tasks. 

IndiEval. We construct IndiEval around the five FONTS capability dimensions. For each dimension, we specify concrete evaluation targets and select benchmarks whose task formulations and native metrics collectively cover these targets. (F) Persona fidelity evaluation examines preference alignment, consistency with assigned personas and personal states, and adherence to individual intentions. (O) Outcome realization evaluation assesses whether simulated individuals communicate their needs and achieve intended task and social outcomes, including goal com-pletion, relationship development, and knowledge or material gains. (N) Behavioral naturalness evaluation examines human-like expression and interaction, covering conversational style, utterance diversity, user-role adherence, and dialogue termination. (T) Trajectory coherence evaluation assesses behavioral consistency across successive decisions and coherent narrative progression beyond individual responses. (S) Social grounding evaluation tests reasoning about others’ beliefs, knowledge, and access to information, including the handling of private information. Figure 3 summarizes how IndiEval connects the five FONTS dimensions to concrete evaluation settings. We orga-nize these dimensions into 11 evaluation targets, operationalized through 29 native metrics across 13 benchmark entries. The mapping specifies which aspect of a capability each metric assesses within its benchmark’s task setting, allowing a benchmark to contribute to multiple dimensions through distinct metrics. To cover these evaluation targets, IndiEval brings together benchmarks spanning persona-conditioned decision-making, role-playing, user–assistant dialogue, narrative interaction, and social reasoning. Relative to Socio-Foundation’s training, these benchmarks form two components: (i) In-domain , covering the task domains of the training datasets listed in Section 4.5; and (ii) Out-of-distribution (OOD) , consisting of AgentSense Mou et al. (2025) and UserLM-LiC Naous et al. (2026b); Laban et al. (2026). IndiEval retains the benchmarks’ original evaluation protocols and organizes 29 task-specific metrics around these evaluation targets. We use DeepSeek-V4-Flash DeepSeek (2026) as both the auxiliary dialogue model and the judge model. Within a benchmark, different metrics may map to different capability dimensions, with detailed metric definitions and mappings provided in Appendix D.2. The overall Avg. includes both components and equally weights the five capability scores, each computed by averaging metrics within each dataset and then weighting datasets equally. Evaluation set sizes are provided in Appendix D.4. Figure 4 summarizes the composition of IndiEval’s 6,297 evaluation cases and their coverage of scoring types and capability dimensions. For the coverage summaries, each benchmark’s case count is divided equally among its associated scoring types or dimensions. These descriptive proportions are separate from score aggregation: IndiEval Avg. gives equal weight to the five dimensions after averaging metrics within each dataset and datasets within each dimension. 

Additional OOD evaluation beyond IndiEval. To further assess generalization beyond IndiEval, we separately evaluate Mistakes Ross and Andreas (2025), HiToM Wu et al. (2023), and τ -USI Zhou et al. (2026b). These three benchmarks supplement the OOD component within IndiEval and are excluded from Table 1 scores and Avg.

This may reflect the strong assistant bias introduced during general-purpose post-training, which can make faithful user simulation more difficult. 

Breadth versus specialized simulators. Compared with similarly sized specialized models, Socio-Foundation achieves the best or second-best result on 86% of the metrics and the highest overall average score. Existing special-ized simulators often excel in the tasks for which they were designed, but generalize unevenly to other tasks. For example, UserLM and USP perform strongly on dialogue-oriented simulation tasks but transfer less effectively to decision-oriented evaluations, with reported scores falling to zero on some metrics. We observe that these nar-rowly specialized models can fail to follow instructions and carry out the requested task when evaluated outside their training task families. This limited transfer may reflect overfitting to dialogue-centric training data, which could constrain their ability to adapt to new task requirements. Socio-Foundation’s more consistent performance suggests that it consolidates individual-simulation capabilities across heterogeneous task formats rather than optimizing primarily for a particular interaction setting.

Why use hierarchical integration and on-policy distillation? The full hierarchical pipeline achieves the highest Avg. in Table 2 (61.7). Replacing final-stage on-policy distillation with Capability-expert off-policy distillation 

reduces the Avg. to 59.3, while capability-expert averaging further lowers it to 56.8, supporting the benefit of learned on-policy integration over the tested alternatives. The intermediate capability level also provides a consis-tent advantage: under the same parameter-averaging strategy, capability-expert averaging outperforms task-expert averaging (56.8 vs. 54.5), while directly integrating through Task-experts SFT or Task-expert OPD reaches 59.5 and 54.2 but remains below the full hierarchy, suggesting that direct integration can encounter conflicting opti-mization objectives. Pooled-data SFT and Reverse KL OPD obtain 43.0 and 50.9, respectively. The latter retains on-policy prefixes but reverses the KL direction, providing an objective-direction ablation for our forward-KL design. Together, these results support both stages of our design: consolidating task-level expertise into capability experts and integrating these experts through on-policy distillation yields the strongest unified model. 

Why train task experts with DAPO? As shown in Figure 7, DAPO task experts generally outperform their SFT counterparts across tasks based on dataset-level scores aggregated over normalized metrics, motivating the use of RL for task-expert training. We compare alternative RL methods in Appendix C.3. We therefore adopt DAPO for task-expert training.

Additional OOD generalization beyond IndiEval. Beyond the OOD component within IndiEval, we evaluate Mistakes, HiToM, and τ -USI as three additional benchmarks. Socio-Foundation obtains 57.00 on Mistakes, 62.00 on HiToM, and 969.31 on τ -USI, outperforming both baselines on all three tasks and providing further evidence demonstrating the generalization and transability. 

Five-dimensional capability profile. Figure 5 presents five-dimensional scores aggregated from Table 1. The specialized models exhibit different strengths across dimensions. What is gained by integrating all five capability experts? 

As shown in Table 3, removing any capability expert lowers both the overall Avg. and the score on its cor-responding capability dimension. Removing the behav-ioral naturalness expert (N) causes the largest decline in both overall performance and the corresponding ca-pability score. Removing F, N, T, or S raises O while lowering the overall Avg., suggesting a trade-off between outcome realization and the other capabilities in these configurations. These results show that the full five-expert configuration yields the strongest overall capabil-ity profile, supporting the complementary contributions of capability-level supervision to the unified model. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce Socio-Foundation, a foundation model for simulating individual behaviors across di-verse social contexts. We organize the fragmented task landscape into five foundational capabilities and develop a three-stage hierarchical expert-distillation pipeline to consolidate task-specific expertise. We also introduce In-diEval to assess these capabilities while preserving task-specific evaluation protocols. Socio-Foundation improves over its Qwen3-8B base across all five capabilities, achieves the strongest overall performance among same-scale specialized simulators, and approaches frontier general-purpose models. Ablations show that on-policy integration outperforms direct expert merging and the tested off-policy alternative, while all five capability experts contribute to the strongest overall result, supporting the FONTS framework as an effective basis for organizing and inte-grating complementary behavioral capabilities. Gains on three out-of-distribution benchmarks further support generalization beyond the main evaluation suite. These findings show the value of capability-structured training for individual simulation.

13 A Dataset Survey, Capability Annotations, and Clustering 

This appendix documents the dataset-level evidence underlying the FONTS organization. The inventory contains 44 surveyed dataset/task entries and 10 supplementary benchmark or protocol entries used in the paper. Subsets and local derivatives are recorded separately when their simulated roles or task requirements differ; these 54 entries therefore do not represent 54 independent dataset families. This survey inventory is distinct from the selected training corpus. 

A.1 Annotation unit and organization 

For each dataset, we record the task formulation, the simulated or inferred subject, the relevant scenario, and the behavioral requirement suggested by its evaluation protocol. The annotation unit is a dataset–task–role configuration : the same source can support different requirements under different conversions. We represent these requirements with 19 labels (A01–A19) and organize them into eight semantic clusters (C1–C8). The theory-guided consolidation described in the main text maps C1–C3 to F, C4 to O, C5 to N, C6 to T, and C7–C8 to S. Table 4 specifies the complete mapping. The assignments describe capability demands rather than mutually exclusive task categories or independent scores for every label. A requirement can be inferred from a task’s scenario without having a dedicated native metric. Local transformations and role-specific interpretations remain qualified in the inventory. In particular, a multi-turn format alone does not establish T; maintaining a supplied user’s intent supports F, whereas achieving that user’s desired result supports O. Interpreting another person’s intent supports S.

## B Data Cleaning 

B.1 Cleaning Method 

We apply rule-based cleaning to the collected datasets, distinguishing between repairs to formatting or prompt– target consistency and filtering of unsuitable instances. The completed rules address construction artifacts, am-biguous supervision, role confusion, duplication, and excessive length. Dataset-level retained, removed, and trans-formed counts are reported in Section B.3. 

B.2 Cleaning Procedure 

Repairs. We apply three types of repair: 1. Spurious symbols and markers. Remove extraneous special symbols and noisy markers introduced during data construction. 2. Template inconsistencies. Resolve inconsistencies introduced by shared templates used to construct the data. 3. Prompt–target mismatches. Align targets with the requested output format, including removing chain-of-thought text when the prompt requests only an answer and reconciling numeric versus alphabetic answer labels (e.g., a prompt requesting labels 1–3 paired with a target using A–C). 

Filtering. We remove instances that meet any of the following seven criteria: 1. Role confusion. The target contains outputs that mix multiple speaker roles. 18 2. Profile-construction noise. An AlignX user profile contains artifacts such as Response 1 or Response 2 

introduced during profile construction. 3. Empty targets. The target is empty. 4. Unexpanded questionnaire templates. A SocSci210 instance contains an unexpanded template that makes the meaning of its target uninterpretable. 5. Conflicting or ambiguous answers. The target contains contradictory answers or does not specify a unique answer. 6. Duplicate instances. The instance duplicates another entry in the data. 7. Excessive length. The instance exceeds 32,768 tokens.

19 C Training details 

C.1 Task-level reinforcement learning 

Each task has an independently optimized LoRA adapter on the shared Qwen3-8B backbone. The task-expert launcher uses LoRA rank 32 and scaling parameter 64, a learning rate of 5 × 10 −6 with 10 warmup steps, and a budget of 200 optimizer steps. Each update samples 32 prompts with eight responses per prompt; the PPO mini-batch size is eight. The DAPO recipe uses lower and upper clipping thresholds of 0.20 and 0.28, a dual-clip coefficient of 10, token-mean loss aggregation, dynamic sampling of informative response groups, and overlong-response reward shaping. The configured context window is 16,384 tokens, with at most 6,144 prompt tokens and 6,144 response tokens. Validation and checkpoints are scheduled every 50 steps. 

C.2 Capability consolidation and final integration 

The five-capability hierarchy uses a shared Qwen3-8B starting checkpoint and bfloat16 training, with thinking mode disabled. We set the training random seed to 42. Each capability expert is a rank-32, scaling-64 LoRA adapter trained on filtered task-expert demonstrations. The hierarchy configuration allocates 200 updates per capability, a global prompt batch of 32, a learning rate of 10 −5, 10 warmup steps, and a top-64 teacher distribution for distillation. Rollouts use temperature 1, at most 6,144 prompt tokens and 1,024 generated tokens, within a 16,384-token context window. The final student is trained on its own generated prefixes against frozen capability teachers, with a global prompt batch of 32, a learning rate of 2 × 10 −6, 20 warmup steps, and a 200-step training budget in the reference hierarchy configuration. 27 E Repeated-Evaluation Stability 

E.1 Benchmark selection and evaluation pathways 

Small observed performance margins motivate a closer examination of evaluation variability. We first organize candidate benchmarks by the sources of variation in their evaluation pipelines, then select representative settings in which modest gains warrant careful interpretation. The resulting diagnostic subset covers three pathways: target-model generation with automatic scoring, target-model generation with LLM-based scoring, and interac-tive generation with both automatic and LLM-based scoring. This purposive selection characterizes evaluation repeatability; it does not constitute a representative sample of all IndiEval tasks or a selection based on a formal significance test. LifeChoices represents the first pathway: the evaluated model answers persona-conditioned choice questions, and the evaluator checks the selected option against the reference answer without an auxiliary LLM. HUMANUAL represents the second: the evaluated model generates a response without an auxiliary dialogue participant, after which an LLM judge assesses response and state alignment. MirrorBench represents the third: the evaluated model simulates the user, an auxiliary assistant participates in the conversation, and the completed interaction is assessed using both automatic lexical metrics and an LLM judge. Thus, the absence of an auxiliary dialogue participant in HUMANUAL does not imply the absence of an auxiliary scoring model. 

E.2 Repeated-run protocol and statistics 

We evaluate the same unified OPD checkpoint from training step 200 five times on fixed test sets: 500 LifeChoices cases, 600 HUMANUAL cases, and 200 MirrorBench cases per run. Every repetition starts a fresh evaluation and independently regenerates responses and scores. We hold the checkpoint, evaluation seed (20260824), prompts, test cases, and scoring configuration fixed. Temperature is zero for both the evaluated model and auxiliary LLM calls. LifeChoices enables thinking with a 2,048-token reasoning budget; HUMANUAL and MirrorBench disable thinking for the evaluated model. We retain the benchmark-specific response budgets and a 32,768-token context limit. All three benchmarks run concurrently with 64 case workers each and a shared target-model request limit of 192. DeepSeek-V4-Flash supplies the auxiliary LLM roles. These five repetitions assess a fixed checkpoint and are distinct from experiments that vary training seeds. All five runs complete all planned cases, and all metrics reported below have full availability. The experiment therefore comprises 6,500 case evaluations over 1,300 distinct test cases, rather than 6,500 independent test cases. 28 E.3 Evaluation Variability and Implications for Statistical Significance 

Across five repeated evaluations, LifeChoices accuracy, HUMANUAL response/state alignment, and MirrorBench GTEval exhibit limited run-to-run variation, with standard deviations below 0.6 percentage points on the 0–100 scale (Table 11). To characterize variation across evaluation pathways, we examine automatic scoring, LLM-based scoring, and interactive evaluation, and use a fixed-reference control to probe variability in the auxiliary scoring component. For HUMANUAL, response and state alignment have scale-normalized standard deviations of 0.585% and 0.587%, respectively. These measurements combine variation in generated responses with variation in LLM scoring. Mir-rorBench GTEval has a smaller normalized standard deviation of 0.396%, while PI and RNR vary more, at 1.690% and 1.723%. The automatic MATTR z-score also varies, with a standard deviation of 0.033. Stability is conse-quently metric-dependent even within one benchmark; a stable GTEval mean does not establish equal stability for all MirrorBench measurements. MirrorBench additionally provides a control based on the fixed human reference conversations. Its human-reference RNR scores across the five runs are 90.0, 92.0, 90.0, 90.5, and 91.0, giving a mean of 90.700 and a standard deviation of 0.837 on the 0–100 scale. Because this control does not use the generated conversation, its variation provides evidence of variability in the auxiliary scoring pathway under the fixed evaluation setup. It does not quantify how much of the main RNR variance comes from the judge: the control and generated-dialogue scores evaluate different inputs, and their variances cannot simply be subtracted. These results characterize observed repeatability on fixed test sets. They neither establish that a model improve-ment is statistically significant nor demonstrate that a modest improvement is absent. In particular, the observed run range is not a significance threshold. Assessing a difference between models requires matched comparison results and an analysis that respects repeated measurements and dependencies between cases. Isolating judge variability on model outputs further requires rescoring frozen responses or dialogue trajectories; the HUMANUAL and MirrorBench measurements above describe their complete evaluation pipelines. We therefore use this study to contextualize small score differences rather than to assign model-comparison significance from a single checkpoint’s repeated evaluations. 29
