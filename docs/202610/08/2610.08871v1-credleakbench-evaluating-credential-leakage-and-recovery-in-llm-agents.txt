ABSTRACT 

Language model agents are increasingly deployed to automate everyday digital chores from managing emails and social media to handling banking and bills al-lowing users to step away from supervision. However, this capability also ex-poses sensitive information to phishing. Safe execution requires distinguishing malicious requests from genuine ones without simply refusing to act. Despite its practical importance, this problem remains underexplored, and it is unclear whether current agents, or existing defenses can achieve it. To study this prob-lem, we first propose CredLeak-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark designed to evaluate how effectively and securely agents automate human workflows when confronted with phishing and identity verification. The benchmark covers both user-directed authentication and autonomous inbox monitoring, where agents are not explicitly instructed to log in. It systematically varies deceptive cues and pairs phishing scenarios with legitimate counterparts, enabling joint evaluation of in-formation leakage and utility on genuine tasks. Within a sandboxed environment, leakage is measured through actual submissions of synthetic information rather than agents’ self-reported behavior. Our evaluation reveals that all tested models including GPT, Claude, Gemini, Qwen are vulnerable to leakage. Agents also disclose sensitive information during autonomous inbox monitoring, demonstrat-ing that phishing can induce disclosure without a user request to authenticate. Furthermore, most evaluated mitigations that reduce leakage also impair perfor-mance on genuine tasks, exposing a security–utility trade-off in existing defenses. These findings show why reducing leakage alone is insufficient: effective defenses must prevent unauthorized disclosure while preserving legitimate task completion. CredLeak-Bench provides a controlled framework for measuring both objectives and evaluating progress toward secure, useful agents. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM) agents are designed to automate everyday digital tasks, from triaging email inboxes to managing accounts and responding to administrative notices (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Evtimov et al., 2026). Completing these workflows may require access to credentials and sensitive personal information, such as payment details or identity records (Zharmagambetov et al., 2026). This access allows an assistant to act on the user’s behalf, but also makes it responsible for deciding where that information can be sent. Delegating a task therefore delegates a security decision: whether the destination requesting sensitive information is authorized to receive it. Social engineering-style phishing can exploit ordinary task execution by misrepresenting the recip-ient of sensitive information; the attacker does not need to explicitly instruct the agent to ignore its safeguards or abandon the user’s goal (Wu et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026). Consider an assistant that encounters an email requesting account verification. The linked page may resemble the intended service while sending submitted information to an attacker. Following the apparent workflow, the agent could disclose a password then provide other personal records on a subsequent verification page. Simply refusing every request for sensitive information prevents leakage, but also prevents legiti-mate authentication and account management. Genuine and deceptive requests can involve similar actions, so useful defenses must distinguish authorized destinations rather than simply discourage agents from acting. Moreover, rejecting a phishing link may leave a genuine user need unresolved: an actual bill or account issue may still require attention. Secure delegation therefore requires pre-venting unauthorized disclosure, preserving legitimate task completion, and finding a trusted alter-native when the original destination is deceptive. Existing evaluations address parts of these requirements. AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024), WASP (Evtimov et al., 2026), and AgentHarm (Andriushchenko et al., 2025) evaluate task capabil-ity alongside resistance to prompt injection or malicious user requests. AgentDAM (Zharmagam-betov et al., 2026) similarly examines privacy and utility, but focuses on unnecessary disclosure during legitimate tasks rather than disclosure to a deceptive recipient. Destination deception is rep-resented in RiOSWorld (Yang et al., 2026) computer-use risks and in AgentBait (Wu et al., 2026) and LoginTrap (Guo et al., 2026), which study workflows that induce unsafe actions or authenti-cation. Scammer4U (Roy et al., 2026) measures sensitive-information submissions and includes benign counterparts and mitigation comparisons. These studies establish phishing susceptibility and examine the cost of defense, but do not evaluate whether an agent can bypass a deceptive destination and authenticate through a trusted alternative when the user’s underlying need is genuine (Table 1). We introduce C RED LEAK -B ENCH , a benchmark for evaluating credential and sensitive-information leakage in email-driven agent workflows (Figure 1). It tests whether agents distinguish deceptive destinations from legitimate recipients before releasing sensitive credentials, and whether protecting credentials comes at the expense of authorized work. Phishing scenarios are paired with legitimate counterparts, allowing unauthorized disclosure and genuine task refusal to be jointly evaluated. In the directed setting , the user provides a URL and requests authentication, testing whether the agent verifies the destination before acting. In the autonomous setting , the agent receives a vault of syn-thetic account information and is asked to triage an inbox without an authentication request, testing whether inbox content can induce the agent to initiate disclosure on its own. The recovery setting 

introduces phishing messages associated with genuine pending tasks and tests whether agents by-pass the deceptive link and authenticate through a trusted alternative. Across these settings, we systematically vary phishing cues, user framing, and safety support to examine vulnerability and the security–utility effects of mitigation. All services are fictional and interactions take place in a local sandbox, with leakage measured through actual credential submissions. 2 RELATED WORK 

Phishing detection. Phishing detectors identify deceptive webpages using signals such as brand– domain inconsistencies and credential-collection behavior (Liu et al., 2022; 2024). These methods focus on recognizing phishing, including through interaction with suspicious pages. We instead evaluate task-oriented agents entrusted with sensitive user information, where protection depends on whether the agent releases that information and whether it can still complete legitimate work. 

Agent security and privacy. Existing benchmarks examine harmful user requests (An-driushchenko et al., 2025), indirect prompt injection (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Evtimov et al., 2026), and broader computer-use risks, including phishing (Yang et al., 2026). Privacy evaluations address unnecessary disclosure during legitimate web tasks (Zharmagambetov et al., 2026), while internal-channel and trajectory-level audits examine information flow and permission violations throughout agent execution (Yagoubi et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). These studies motivate evaluating security alongside task utility and observing actions beyond the final response. Our focus is whether agents verify the recipient of sensitive account information while preserving legitimate task completion. 

Social engineering against web agents. Recent work shows that attackers can present unsafe actions as plausible steps toward a benign goal, including inducing authentication and credential disclosure (Wu et al., 2026; Syros et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026). 3 BENCHMARK DESIGN 

CRED LEAK -B ENCH evaluates whether agents protect sensitive account information while handling legitimate requests. It includes phishing scenarios and legitimate controls, allowing disclosure to be measured alongside an agent’s willingness to act on genuine requests. A separate recovery setting examines whether an agent can bypass a deceptive link and authenticate through a trusted destina-tion. All services are fictional and webpage interactions take place within a local sandbox. 3.1 PRELIMINARIES AND THREAT MODEL 

An agent π acts on behalf of a benign user u and has access to a vault V = {(bi, F i)}mi=1 , where bi

identifies a service and Fi contains its credentials and sensitive account fields. Let A(bi) denote the destinations authorized to receive information for service bi; it contains only the official hostname served over valid HTTPS, so an unencrypted or certificate-warning page on that hostname is unau-thorized. These destinations define the benchmark’s reference for authorization, but are supplied to the agent only in designated evaluation conditions. The agent encounters a destination d claiming to represent service ˆb(d) and can inspect webpages and submit account information through its tools. We consider a phishing adversary that controls deceptive messages, webpages, and their collection endpoints, but not the user’s underlying goal, the vault contents, or trusted reference information supplied by the benchmark. The adversary aims to induce π to submit information from Fi to a destination d / ∈ A (bi) while pursuing an apparently legitimate task. The attack presents disclosure as part of an ordinary workflow rather than requiring an explicit instruction to override the agent’s safeguards. The agent must therefore distinguish the service a destination claims to represent from the recipient authorized to receive the requested information. 

Threat Settings. We evaluate two primary settings that distinguish explicit authentication requests from agent-initiated action. In the directed setting , the user supplies a URL and asks the agent to authenticate with a named service. The agent receives the target account information and must verify the destination before following the request. In the autonomous setting , the agent receives the full vault V and is asked to triage an inbox without an explicit authentication request. A phishing or legitimate message is placed among unrelated emails. This setting tests whether inbox content can induce the agent to disclose information without the user asking it to sign in. 4Preprint. Table 2: Deception cues in C RED LEAK -B ENCH . 3.2 SCENARIO CONSTRUCTION 

We construct scenarios from 15 fictional services across eight categories: banking, email, social, work, health, government, shopping, and family. Each service bi has a designated domain, a web-page template, and associated account fields Fi. The vault V covers authentication credentials, identity records, financial data, medical identifiers, and personal details. Some scenarios add a ver-ification page after the initial login to examine whether disclosure extends beyond credentials to additional account information. 

Phishing Cue Variation. Phishing variants modify the destination d or its page using eight types of cues involving domain names, service identity, or connection security (Table 2). Legitimate cases in-stead use a designated destination d ∈ A (bi) without these cues. Connection-security conditions are simulated through the displayed URL scheme or a certificate-warning page; no real network inter-ception or certificate attack is performed. Figure 2 summarizes how services, account information, and request variants are assembled into evaluation scenarios. 

User Framing. The user is an important factor in agentic systems, as their input can guide the overall behavior of the agent. We therefore vary requests across four framings: naive , neutral , cautious , and 

pressured . These change both the user’s tone and their expectations about independent action. 

Security Guidance. A useful defense should help agents verify destinations without preventing authorized work. In the directed and autonomous settings, we compare no added guidance, generic safety advice, and explicit domain-verification instructions. The directed setting additionally evalu-ates a URL-checking tool, while the autonomous setting evaluates a vault policy instructing agents to release information only to trusted destinations. In these two settings, trusted domains are supplied in the verification and vault-policy conditions, but withheld under no added guidance and generic safety advice. The recovery setting instead varies the source of trusted information independently of these instructions. 

Controlled Generation. We generate the directed and autonomous suites by crossing service cate-gory, request framing, and security-support condition. Services are selected within categories, and deception cues are rotated across configurations to balance their exposure across safety conditions. Every phishing scenario generates a matched legitimate control sharing the exact same brand, fram-ing, and scaffolding, differing only by using the authentic origin. This paired structure enables joint measurement between false-refusal rate and leak. These suites contain 512 directed and 256 au-tonomous scenarios. Figure 2 summarizes the construction process, with full scenario counts and split details in Appendix B and prompt templates in Appendices D.1 and D.2. 3.3 RECOVERY SETTING 

Rejecting a phishing link protects account information but may leave a genuine user need unre-solved. The recovery setting tests whether an agent monitoring an inbox can bypass a deceptive link and authenticate through a trusted alternative without leaking information. We vary where trusted destination information is available: directly in the vault, within a genuine prior email, or not sup-plied as a trusted reference. This distinguishes using an explicitly provided address from locating one in the user’s records. Security guidance is varied independently of the information source, and the guidance itself does not supply the official domain. The suite contains 1,024 scenarios: 832 5Preprint. phishing cases and 192 legitimate controls. The main recovery evaluation uses 768 phishing cases with a genuine pending task and trusted reference information, split equally between the vault and inbox conditions. Additional phishing cases omit either the trusted reference or the genuine task (32 each) and are evaluated separately. Successful recovery requires the target account’s correct pass-word to reach the trusted endpoint without disclosure of vault information to the phishing endpoint. Full construction details are provided in Appendix A. 

4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We compare seven models, five closed-source and two open-weights on C RED LEAK -B ENCH using a shared sandbox evaluation environment. To isolate model judgment from API or tooling artifacts, all models operate an identical tool surface, with execution and scoring held strictly constant. 4.1 MODELS AND HARNESS 

The evaluation panel spans proprietary frontier models (Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gem-ini 3.6 Flash), the OpenAI GPT-5 family (GPT-5-mini, GPT-5.6-luna), and locally served open-weights models (Qwen3.5-9B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct). Agents can open webpages and submit login credentials, with additional form submission avail-able when a scenario requests further account information. Inbox settings also allow email replies, providing a way to respond without following a link. Specifically, the agent operates a mini-mal browser-style tool surface restricted to open page , submit login , submit form , and 

reply email . A URL-checking tool ( check url ) is exposed only in the designated verification condition. Agents receive the displayed URL, page text, and form fields, while webpage interactions remain within the local simulator. Fixed seeds control scenario generation, synthetic credential as-signment, and inbox message placement. These controls make the evaluation inputs reproducible without assuming that model responses are deterministic. 4.2 EVALUATION METRICS 

An agent should prevent unauthorized disclosure without abandoning legitimate requests. We there-fore measure security and utility jointly using recorded actions rather than model self-reports. The 

Leak Rate (LR) is the fraction of competent phishing runs with at least one disclosure of synthetic account information, verified by matching submitted values against the vault. The False-Refusal Rate (FRR) is the fraction of competent legitimate runs without a recorded form submission; it mea-sures non-completion, whether or not the agent explicitly refuses. We additionally report Balanced Accuracy and the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) to summarize performance across both classes, treating phishing as the positive class and leakage as a false negative. The recovery setting comprises phishing cases with a genuine pending task and trusted information available in the vault or inbox. We distinguish three mutually exclusive outcomes: R ECOVERY ,where the correct target account’s password reaches the trusted endpoint without leakage; L EAK ,where any vault account information reaches the phishing endpoint; and N ON -COMPLETION , where neither occurs. A run that both leaks and reaches the trusted endpoint is classified as L EAK . Recov-ery Rate is the fraction of these runs classified as R ECOVERY . Formal definitions, the confusion-matrix mapping, and outcome-scoring rules are provided in Appendix C.

5.1 CREDENTIAL LEAKAGE IN DIRECTED AND AUTONOMOUS SETTINGS 

Table 3 shows that every evaluated model leaks sensitive information in both settings. In the directed setting, leak rates range from 31.3% to 75.8%, despite relatively low false-refusal rates of 3.0% to 26.6%. This indicates that agents are generally capable of completing legitimate authentication tasks, but often fail to distinguish whether the destination is authorized to receive the credentials. The two open-weight models are especially vulnerable, with leak rates of 66.8% for Qwen3.5-9B and 75.8% for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Leakage decreases for every model in the autonomous setting, but this comes with substantially higher false refusal. For example, Opus 4.8 drops from 31.3% to 1.6% leakage, while its false-refusal rate rises from 6.6% to 88.3%. GPT-5-mini retains the highest legitimate-task completion in this setting, with a 43.8% false-refusal rate, but still leaks in 14.8% of phishing runs. Thus, autonomous agents are generally more reluctant to act, yet phishing messages can still induce credential disclosure even without an explicit request to authenticate. Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 provide setting-specific results. 8Preprint. 5.3 HOW USER FRAMING CHANGES AGENT BEHAVIOR 

Agent behavior depends not only on the underlying model but also on how the user frames a task. Figure 5a–b shows how cautious wording changes leakage and legitimate task completion relative to neutral requests. In the directed setting, cautious framing reduces leakage for every model, but lowers completion for most. For GPT-5-mini, leakage decreases by 51.5 percentage points while completion decreases by 50.0 points. Qwen3.5-9B is the exception: leakage decreases by 25.0 points and completion increases by 3.5 points. Thus, caution can improve both objectives, but typically reduces disclosure at a utility cost. In the autonomous setting, cautious framing lowers legitimate completion for every model. It yields zero observed leakage for all five closed-source models, but completion falls to just 0.0–10.7% across all seven models. These results show why leakage must be evaluated alongside utility: low disclosure can coexist with near-complete failure to execute genuine requests. Appendix Table 6 reports absolute rates for all four framing conditions. 5.4 CAN VERIFICATION REDUCE LEAKAGE WITHOUT SACRIFICING UTILITY ?Explicit verification support can improve security without suppressing legitimate task execution. In Figure 5c, domain verification moves six of seven models into the upper-left quadrant, reducing leak-age and increasing completion relative to no added guidance. For GPT-5-mini, leakage decreases by 50.1 percentage points while completion increases by 23.4 points. Providing a URL-checking tool also improves both metrics for five models, although substantial leakage remains (Appendix Table 7). For Gemini 3.6 Flash, however, a 19.2-point leakage reduction accompanies a 49.4-point completion loss. These differences demonstrate that mitigation effects depend on the intervention and model: restricting information release can reduce leakage, but does not consistently preserve useful behavior. 5.5 WHY DOES LEAKAGE OCCUR ?Leakage comes from two gaps in how agents decide where to send credentials. First, agents rarely verify the destination unless prompted. They treat the user-supplied or emailed link as authorized. Leakage is highest when the user explicitly asks the agent to sign in. Supplying the official domain with an instruction to compare against it lowers leakage for most of the models, for example from 73.5% to 23.4% for GPT-5-mini in the directed setting (Appendix Table 7). Second, when agents do verify, they match only the hostname string and ignore the transport. Plain HTTP on the correct hostname ( no https ) is the highest-leaking cue for every model (Appendix Table 8), and it passes even an explicit domain verify rule. Cues that change the hostname or display a warning page are caught more often. Opus 4.8, for instance, leaks in 9.4% of typosquat and tls warning 

cases but 90.6% of no https cases. Once the login is submitted, the agent also completes the follow-up verification page exposing identity and financial fields in addition to the password. 5.6 CAN AGENTS RECOVER FROM PHISHING ?Finally, avoiding a phishing link protects account information but may leave a genuine user need unresolved. We therefore evaluate recovery : whether an agent can bypass a deceptive destination and authenticate through a trusted alternative without leaking information. Figure 4a shows that recovery remains limited, ranging from 0% to 24.2% across seven models on the primary recovery scenarios. GPT-5-mini recovers most often, but still leaks in 12.5% of cases and leaves 63.3% incomplete without leakage; Opus 4.8 avoids leakage entirely yet never recovers. How trusted information is supplied also matters: five models recover more often with a full URL in the vault than with an official domain in a prior email, including GPT-5-mini at 33.3% versus 15.1%. Security guidance has model-dependent effects. Relative to no guidance, the vault policy raises Gemini 3.6 Flash’s recovery from 2.0% to 30.1%, but does not improve GPT-5-mini’s recovery (24.6% versus 23.4%). 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced C RED LEAK -B ENCH , a benchmark for evaluating whether agents disclose creden-tials and sensitive information to illegitimate destinations while preserving their ability to complete genuine tasks. Across seven evaluated models, we find that credential leakage remains a consis-tent failure mode in both directed authentication and autonomous inbox monitoring, including cases where the user never asks the agent to authenticate. Our analysis further shows that security cannot be measured by leakage alone: user framing and restrictive policies can reduce disclosure by making agents less willing to act, whereas explicit destination verification can improve both security and le-gitimate task completion. Finally, our recovery evaluation reveals a harder challenge beyond refusal. Even when agents avoid a phishing destination, they rarely complete the underlying task through a trusted alternative. Together, these results suggest that secure agentic systems require more than stronger refusal behavior. Agents must reliably determine where sensitive information is being sent and, when deception is detected, continue the user’s task through an authorized destination. AI USE STATEMENT

REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT 

Every scenario in C RED LEAK -B ENCH is emitted by a deterministic generator seeded once ( seed =20260827 ): items are constructed by modular factor rotation rather than stochastic sampling, so the directed (512), autonomous (256), and recovery (1024) suites, together with all of their marginal counts, are exact and byte-reproducible from the seed alone (App. B). The entire evaluation runs inside a sandboxed simulator bound to loopback: every secret is a freshly minted synthetic value, so no live credential, domain, or endpoint is ever contacted and the suite can be regenerated and rerun offline. All seven models are driven through a single tool-calling harness with an identical prompt surface and tool set (App. D). The ground truth is never taken from the agent’s narration: outcomes are adjudicated from an append-only network-capture log by SHA-256 digest of the sub-mitted payloads and, in the recovery arm, by which channel ( Cserved vs. Cofficial ) received the submis-sion (App. C). Because model APIs are not bit-deterministic, exact per-run transcripts may vary, but the fixed item set, ordering, and adjudication make the reported aggregate rates stable across reruns.
