## ABSTRACT 

LLM agents increasingly act in multi-actor environments, exposing them to mis-use from multiple sources: decomposition attacks, where a harmful request is split into innocuous sub-requests, and prompt injection attacks, where a com-promised tool delivers a malicious instruction. Existing evaluations treat these threats separately and ask whether a trajectory is harmful, rather than when it be-comes harmful. We propose monitoring the agent’s responses, where its actions are externalised, and ask whether the first point where monitors identify harm lands within a harm window (from the agent’s first harmful commitment to goal execution). We develop a unified formalism for trace-level misuse monitoring and use it to construct a benchmark of ∼6,200 conversation transcripts between a user, an LLM agent, and the external environment, spanning both threats in a shared schema, with a labelled harm window, corresponding benign controls, and matched instances of refusals to these requests. Across 17 monitor configurations, we find that our proposed action-framed monitors perform well on both threats under classical metrics (AUC: 0.95 and 0.99 respectively), while content-framed monitors collapse on injection attacks (AUC: 0.52). We also show that classical position-blind metrics paint an optimistic picture of monitor performance, since all monitors localise decomposition attacks poorly under the interval metric, which measures the ability to localise harm. Broadly, we illustrate the need for a unified study of misuse monitoring. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

LLM agents can now resolve real GitHub issues unassisted (Jimenez et al., 2024), and the length of tasks they can finish without help has been doubling roughly every seven months (Kwa et al., 2025). An agent with this kind of reach not only talks to its user but also reads emails, opens web pages and calls tools; so a single run can contain text written by many actors, each of whom steers what the agent does next. But this also means a malicious actor has several channels through which to introduce harmful content. We focus on two. In a decomposition attack , the user splits a harmful goal into innocuous sub-requests so that none of them trip a filter, and the agent completes the task without recognising the overall goal. In a prompt injection attack , the harmful instruction arrives from the environment, inside a tool result (like an email), and the agent follows it as though it came from the user. The standard safeguard against such threats is a monitor : a second system that reads the interaction and flags anything harmful. The simplest monitors are input and output filters. They read the trajec-tory and can block the interaction when it is deemed unsafe, either at a message level or a trajectory level. Existing literature on misuse monitors treats each individual attack type as its own problem (Chen et al., 2025; Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024), and thus the results of monitoring evaluations are scattered, non-standardised and hard to compare. It also almost exclusively studies 

whether a trajectory should be blocked, rather than when . Many kinds of misuse are time-critical, and catching these instances post hoc may not be sufficient (e.g. exfiltrated data cannot be recalled). A robust monitoring evaluation must measure how well a monitor can localise the threat.

A formalism (Section 3) in which both attack families (decomposition and prompt injec-tion) are instances of a single trace-level detection problem. A benchmark comprising ∼6,200 traces of agents completing user queries in varied en-vironments, spanning both threat families in a shared schema: 1,165 decomposition traces with benign counterparts, 582 traces where harmful steps are interleaved with benign ones, 1,644 refusal twins (three per labelled harmful trace, Appendix C.4.1) where the model does not comply with the request, and 2,828 injection attack traces built on AgentDojo agent runs. 3. An evaluation of 17 monitor configurations replicated across model families and scored against the benign controls and onset labels above with classical metrics and our proposed interval metric that measures the ability to localise the threat (Section 3.4). 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

2.1 LLM AGENTS AND THE MONITORING PROBLEM 

An LLM agent is essentially a language model run in a loop. At each step it reads the conversation so far and emits either text or a tool call, and the tool’s output is appended to the conversation for the next step (Yao et al., 2022). Typically, two layers of defences are deployed for such systems. The first is the model’s own safety training. This layer is brittle, and there are many ways to get harmful content out of aligned models (Wei et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023; Russinovich et al., 2024; Li et al., 2Preprint 2024). The second layer is an external monitor: a separate classifier that reads the traffic and can block it when necessary. In this work we focus only on this second layer. A deployer can afford to stop or escalate only a small fraction of benign traffic, so a key performance indicator is the catch rate at a chosen false-positive rate (FPR). 2.2 THREAT FAMILIES 

Decomposition attacks. A decomposition attack splits a harmful goal into pieces that would in-dividually pass a filter. DecomposedHarm (Chen et al., 2025) turns harmful agent tasks adapted from AgentHarm (Andriushchenko et al., 2024) into human-checked decompositions of seemingly benign sub-tasks, and further shows that interleaving random benign sub-tasks degrades recognition. We adopt this construction for one of our dataset splits (Section 4.1). On the AI control side, Hills et al. (2026) find that an agent spreading a covert side task across pull requests defeats monitors that are merely shown more history, and only a stateful monitor tracking suspicious build-up catches it. 

Prompt injection. Prompt injection, formalised in Liu et al. (2023), is the older literature. Greshake et al. (2023) showed that an instruction arriving in retrieved data can override the user’s prompt. AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024) provides the runs that seed our injection track. It pairs 97 user tasks across four tool suites with injection tasks, and its strongest attack, 

important_instructions , wraps the injected task in a message that claims to come from the user. Presently studied defences are either prevention, which changes the agent so that the injection cannot take effect (Debenedetti et al., 2025), or detection, which leaves the agent alone and watches for the attack (Chennabasappa et al., 2025; Abdelnabi et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025). We only deal with detection monitors in this paper. 2.3 MONITORING APPROACHES 

The most developed body of work on monitoring agents is AI control (Greenblatt et al., 2024; UK AI Security Institute, 2025; Bhatt et al., 2025; Stickland et al., 2025). Arike et al. (2026) find that monitors often do better when shown less and propose extract-and-evaluate, which we adapt as the 

eae monitor. On the misuse side, LLM monitors are newer. Prominent examples are the sequential monitor of Chen et al. (2025) and constitutional classifiers (Sharma et al., 2025). Across these systems there are several design axes: a) what the monitor sees; b) whether it keeps state; c) whether it judges the content of the conversation or the agent’s action. We sweep some of them in Section 5. 

Guard models. Guard models such as Llama Guard 4 (Meta AI, 2025) and Qwen3Guard (Qwen Team, 2025) are instruction-tuned classifiers that read a policy and answer safe/unsafe (Llama Team, 2024). They are often tied to their taxonomy (Harsh et al., 2026). Our experiments predate recently designed guards for agent traces (Chen et al., 2026) and thus the guards we use have no category for a prompt injection failure. 2.4 HOW MONITORS ARE EVALUATED 

The benchmarks above differ in how they evaluate the monitor. DecomposedHarm scores a monitor over the stream of user prompts and credits any flag at or before the labelled sub-task; AgentDojo scores the agent itself, and does not model a monitor; the guard papers report accuracy or F1 over single prompts and responses. Standard practice in most works (except for those in AI control) does not score the point at which the agent commits to the harm. The other consideration is timing. To our knowledge, no existing benchmark scores the flag against a window labelled on both sides. Monitors are also increasingly being evaluated under adversarial pressure (Kutasov et al., 2025; Kale et al., 2025; Terekhov et al., 2025; Najt et al., 2026). Appendix E.5 reports an exploratory study.

3.1 SYSTEM MODEL 

We model the system as a turn-based interaction between a central agent (the LLM) A, an envi-ronment of actors E = {U, T 1, . . . , T m, L 1, . . . , L n}, where U is the human user, Ti are tools or 3Preprint

Any environment actor e ∈ E can potentially be compromised or adversarial. Information is exchanged over messages. A message at a turn t is defined as a tuple 

mt = ( st, r t, c t), where st ∈ { A} ∪ E is the sender, rt ∈ { A} ∪ E is the designated receiver and ct is the content (a sequence of tokens). The interaction history up to turn t is the trace τt = ( m1, . . . , m t)

Messages carrying the agent’s instructions (serialised with role system ) are modelled as being sent by U .We focus only on black-box monitors that can read the full trace and not white-box monitors which can read the internal activations. The details of what the other actors can access are discussed in Appendix A.1. The natural extension of this formalism to multi-agent scenarios is provided in Appendix A.1.5. 3.2 THREATS 

We denote by H the set of all harmful or disallowed intents (e.g. building a bomb). A decomposition attack (in our setup) is orchestrated by the user U , where they split the harmful intent h into a sequence of k sub-requests S = ⟨q1, . . . , q k⟩ such that together they can accomplish the intent h but none of them can do so individually. Other threats can be modelled in this style as well, and some are set out in Appendix A.2. 3.3 THE HARM WINDOW 

In any attack, the adversary’s message alone is never the harm since only the agent can actually realise h by making appropriate tool calls. We therefore locate the onset of harm on the agent’s side of the trace. Consider the excerpt in Figure 2 (fully walked through in Appendix B), where the agent drafts a credential-phishing email at message 14, commits to sending it at message 25, harvests the passwords that come back, logs in, and only at message 67 does it mail the stolen data to the 4Preprint attacker’s address. Only here has the agent committed to the goal itself, which the send_email 

call at message 68 then completes. We would like to credit the monitor if it recognises the harm at message 25 too, not only around message 68. But if a monitor does flag on message 1, it is far too early, and would be too aggressive and disruptive to an agent’s autonomous flow. On the other hand, if the monitor flags on message 71, it is far too late, and the harm has already been completed. Hence, we locate harm in a trajectory as an interval. For a trace realising an intent h ∈ H , denote by tadv the index of the adversary’s message that completes the delivery of h (the final instruction to realise h). We then define O, the goal onset , which is the first agent message that commits to the harmful intent h, and the execution onset Oexec , the first tool call at or after O (or O itself if none follows) and hence the point where h is actually accomplished. We also define E, the 

evidence onset , which is the first point in the trace where the agent commits to anything harmful. Thus for any trace τ we always have E(τ ) ≤ O(τ ) ≤ Oexec (τ ) and we define the harm window 

as W (τ ) = [ E(τ ), O exec (τ )] , the interval from the first commitment to anything harmful until the execution of the overall goal. So a flag before E is early, but a flag after Oexec is late. 3.4 THE MONITOR 

A monitor M is defined by three components: a view VM (τ ) ⊆ τ function that fixes what part of the trace the monitor sees, a cadence CM (τ ) ⊆ { 1, . . . , |τ |} that fixes which message indices it evaluates for suspiciousness, and the scoring function fM itself that decides how suspicious an action is. At each index t ∈ CM (τ ), the monitor emits a suspicion score fM (τt) = σt. In our evaluation, this score lies on a 1–10 scale (established in Bhatt et al. (2025)) and is the mean of 

Ns samples from the monitor. We audit whenever the score reaches a selected threshold θ, which can be used to tune the sensitivity of the monitor in relation to the capacity for human review for false positives. The first-flag index t∗(θ) is the index of the first message where σt ≥ θ, and ∞

if this never occurs. We can say that the monitor M acts on a trace τ to return the first-flag index function, which then takes the threshold θ as input to return the first-flag index t∗(θ) under that threshold and use the notation M (τ, θ ) = t∗(θ) as shorthand 1. In deployment, the monitor appends a terminal tuple (M, A, BLOCK ) to the trace after t∗(θ). The indicator function 1[t∗(θ) < ∞]

identifies whether any message in the trace triggers a block, and a sweep of this over different values of θ is what much of prior literature has reported on. 

The monitor’s objective. The objective of the monitor is to ensure that the trajectory stays within some safety specification, which is a way of operationalising H as a checkable policy list or set of rules. H is unknowable in advance, and so the safety specification is always incomplete. Results on a benchmark are thus likely an overestimate of actual safety, since it is easier than the real world where the adversary can always be more creative and novel. For our purposes, when the ground truth label of a trace yτ = 1 , the trace τ is harmful, and realises some intent h ∈ H . We assume that the deployer only has a fixed budget to spend on false positives made by the monitor, and so just like AI control literature (Greenblatt et al., 2024), we formalise the auditing budget as a constraint and maximise detection by the monitor under this constraint. Misuse is a rare event in the real world, but not in a benchmark designed to study it. We do not use F1 scores as a metric since they are affected by the prevalence of harmful traces. We report recall at a fixed audit budget FPR ≤ α = 0 .1.The issue of position-blindness still remains, since the method described above credits a flag any-where. We would like the monitor’s first-flag index to actually land within the harm window W (τ )

of the trace τ . So our monitor solves the following problem:

## 4 BENCHMARK 

We now instantiate a benchmark following the language of Section 3. Each trace is a JSON file with an ordered messages list whose role field records the sender st (assistant for A, tool 

for the Ti, and user or the opening system message for U per the convention of Section 3). A detailed schema is provided in Appendix C.1. 4.1 DATASET CONSTRUCTION 

Decomposition track. We use the 582 harmful and 583 benign decompositions in the agent 

split of DecomposedHarm (Chen et al., 2025). Each harmful seed contains a goal (the 

attack_string ) rewritten as an ordered S = ⟨q1, . . . , q k⟩ with k ranging from three to thirteen sub-requests (median seven). The seed also marks the sub-request at which h becomes achievable, which the completed traces inherit as tadv . The seed traces contain user messages only. We use the Gemma 4 26B-A4B model (Gemma Team, 2026) to turn them into agentic dialogues. The model is prompted once after each user message with the rest of the conversation, and is asked to produce the next assistant message. This is usually some reasoning text followed by zero or more tool calls (the list of tools is fixed by us). The tool output is also simulated by the same model, following the success of LM-emulated-sandbox methods like Ruan et al. (2024). This produces the 1,165 traces (582 harmful) in the decomposition_main split. The decomposition_hard split realises the threat described in Chen et al. (2025) where benign requests are spliced into harmful traces. We fix the rate such that with probability 0.5 a benign request is inserted into each gap between consecutive harmful sub-requests in each trace. The benign request is taken from an ID-matched seed to the harmful one. The monitor can now no longer rely on harmful steps being adjacent. The decomposition_refusal split initialises a control where the assistant refuses the harmful request after receiving it. For each harmful decomposition_main trace with an E label we build three twins (1,644 from 548 sources; the twins keep the labels they were cut at, from the pass before the blind re-rating, Appendix C.4.1), cut at E, at O and at a random assistant turn between them; each shares the harmful trace’s prefix up to its cut and then refuses it. 

Prompt injection track. From the published AgentDojo run logs 2 (Debenedetti et al., 2024), we use only the important_instructions attack and keep the 947 distinct (suite, user task, injection task) pairs that have recorded run logs where the payload reached the assistant: 560 in workspace, 142 in banking, 140 in travel and 105 in Slack. We keep a prefix of each run, up to the tool message carrying the injected content (which is the tadv here), as the seed. Following this, we use the same Gemma model to complete the trace. Our benchmark is synthetic. We take real seeds from other benchmarks but everything else is written by instances of Gemma 4. Generation-time checks ensure that the traces are complete, onset labels are correctly placed, and known defects (like empty tool outputs or missing payloads) are corrected. ## 5 EVALUATION 

Monitor configurations. We evaluate 14 prompted monitor configurations and two guard models (Llama Guard 4, with two policy lists, and Qwen3Guard). We also replicate three core configura-tions ( baseline , sequential and sequential_action ) on eight further models (Haiku runs two). Every monitor sees the trace incrementally and is asked to return suspicion scores for the messages it is asked about. The baseline monitor sees the current message alone and rates the action in it. The sequential monitor sees the entire conversation up to the current mes-sage, and the sequential_action monitor sees the same context but rates only the assistant actions, not the environment’s. Besides these, we experiment with extract-and-evaluate ( eae ), the nine prompts of Chen et al. (2025), an action-framed zero-shot variant ( sp_zeroshot_action ), and the guard models; Table 1 and Appendix D.1 describe each, and Appendix G reproduces the three core prompts. 

Models and protocol. The main sweep runs every arm on Gemma 4 26B-A4B (Gemma Team, 2026) and Qwen3.6 27B (Qwen Team, 2026). The three core arms are also run on a Qwen3.5 size ladder (2B to 27B), Llama 3.1 70B, Mistral Small 3.2 24B and two API models, GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI, 2026a) and Claude Haiku 4.5 (Appendix D). Other models check whether results depend on Gemma being both generator and monitor. All models return a few sentences of reasoning followed by a final score from 1 to 10 (the _cot arms ask for step-by-step reasoning in the prompt as per Chen et al. (2025)). For the locally served models we take the mean of 5 samples at temperature 1 as σt; the API models return a single sample, and the guard models return a verdict-token probability 7Preprint

Most arms perform quite well on the position-blind metric as can be seen in Figure 4. On de-composition attacks, the simplest baseline arm already achieves an AUC of 0.90, while the 

sequential arm touches 0.96, suggesting, as do the recall scores at the budget, that monitors do well on the benchmark. The content-framed arms of Chen et al. (2025) do much worse on injection attacks, with the headline sequential arm only reaching an AUC of 0.52. This strongly points to the overspecialisation problem in current monitoring literature. Monitors that are being optimised for specific threats are comparable to coin flips on other threats. Framing the sequential arm onto the agent’s action sacrifices 0.008 points of performance in AUC on decomposition, but does far better on injection attacks with an AUC score of 0.985. So framing monitors on agent actions is a viable way to improve generalisability. The interval credit (in Table 2) illustrates that while many of these monitors are separating harmful from benign traces well, they still do not localise the harm well. On injection, this task is much simpler as the harm window is quite narrow, but on decomposition the best arm, eae , reaches only 0.50 under this metric. ## 6 DISCUSSION 

Timing: are monitors early or late? At a 10% budget, most arms recall 0.78–0.96 of harmful de-compositions (Table 2), but their interval credit spans 0.14–0.50. Almost the entire fall is attributed to early flagging before the harm window (Figure 10). Inspecting these early flags, we found that they were often on preparatory steps that did not directly cause harm, and 1 of the 21 sampled had no harmful content in the prefix at all. The sequential arm makes most of its early flags because it flags the user message just before E, and adjusting for this makes its performance comparable to 

sequential_action . Not all arms show this recovery, e.g. sp_zeroshot flags earlier still. Allowing a 4-message grace period before E increases interval credit of the five arms shown by 0.14–0.27 without changing their ordering (see Figure 9). 

How do monitors handle refusals? The refusal twins share a prefix with a

decomposition_main trace until the model refuses a harmful request at some point. We count flags from the evidence onset to just before this refusal as valid. If the model refuses right from E, the agent never acts on the harm. Under our agent-side definition, this is a false positive: it tests whether the monitor responds to what the agent does or to how harmful the content sounds. Against twins that refuse at E, the action-framed arms separate harmful traces from their twins well (AUC 0.76–0.91 across 10 monitor models) but still flag 28–48% of twins on most models, which is well over the 10% auditing budget. For refusals that occur later between E and O, the flag rate rises, but the new flags mostly fall at or just before E. The sequential arm scores the refusal message 8.0–9.3 out of 10 (except on Qwen3.5 2B, which scores it 6.9), reacting to the announcement of a harm that will not be completed. Per-model tables and the sweep are in Appendix E.2 and Figure 11. 

Limitations. A single Gemma model writes every completion and simulates every tool output in this dataset. All decomposition evidence-onset labels are produced by LLM raters rather than human annotators. On the 40 cross-model double-coded traces, exact-index agreement is 67.5%. As with any other benchmark, good performance here indicates that the monitor is likely to catch unintentional abuses of the security policy, but does not say anything about the robustness to an intentional adversary. Further limitations are discussed in Appendix E.7. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We showed that trace-level misuse monitoring can be treated as a single unified problem through a formalism. Our benchmark is static, generated synthetically, and extensible to new threat types. Through it, we showed that framing sequential monitors on the agent’s actions can separate harmful from benign traces on both tracks even though content-framed sequential monitors fail to generalise to injection. Further, we showed that monitors do not understand refusals correctly, and flag them anyway. Finally, on decomposition, action-framed arms place their first flag inside the harm window on only 33–50% of harmful traces. Future work should extend the benchmark to more complex workflows and threat types such as jailbreaks, and develop monitors that generalise across threats, calibrate easily and localise harm. 9Preprint AI USE STATEMENT

A multi-agent deployment is a set of agents A1, . . . , A p, each with its own environment Ej and its own trace τ (j), related by identifying actors across traces: an external entity Li ∈ E j of an orchestrating agent Aj is the agent Aj′ of another trace, and in that trace the orchestrator is the user. V (M, ·) ranges over the union of the traces ordered by time, which is again a trace in the sense of Section 3, with each internal message appearing twice, once as sent and once as received. If it controls only some of them, the rest are ordinary external entities, and their side of the interaction is a black box in exactly the way the Li of a single-agent deployment already are. A decomposition can be spread across agents as well as across turns: the orchestrator issues each sub-request qi to a different sub-agent, so that no single trace τ (j′) entails h while their union does, exactly as no single qi does in Section 3.2. A monitor that watches each sub-agent’s trace alone is then in the position of a per-message monitor on the joint trace, and the argument for a cumulative view within a trace becomes an argument for a view across them. A.2.3 PROMPT INJECTION ATTACKS 

Injected content can be aimed at the control flow or at the data flow of the agent. The control-flow variant takes over the agent’s control flow, asking it to do a different task from the one it set out to do; the data-flow variant feeds wrong answers to the agent so that it believes it is doing the right task even though the contents of the task have been replaced by adversarial content. The injection track of Section 4.1 instantiates the control-flow variant. A strong defence against prompt injection is the dual-LLM architecture of CaMeL (Debenedetti et al., 2025): a blind planner LLM that never sees the outputs of tool calls or external agents, and a quarantined actor LLM that interacts with unsafe outputs but makes no decisions. The budget is conditional on benign traffic. Greenblatt et al. (2024) evaluate monitoring protocols under a fixed auditing budget: only a small fraction of trajectories (2% in their setup) can be esca-lated for expensive human review, which bounds the monitor’s false-positive rate on benign inputs. The constraint of Section 3.4 is stated on benign traffic because that is what a deployer can actu-ally realise: θ is set from the monitor’s scores on benign traces alone (Section 5), which fixes the conditional rate whatever the prevalence of misuse. The audit budget of Greenblatt et al. (2024) is nominally a bound on all flagged traffic; the two coincide in deployment, where misuse is rare and flagged traffic is dominated by benign traces. AI control scores the monitor on the same object as we do, the agent’s actions, but there it is the only option: the untrusted policy is the adversary, so its actions are the only place harm can enter. The misuse setting is the general case, in which the adver-sary is in the environment and its messages are observable. We still score the monitor on the agent, since its role is to notice the agent doing something dangerous wherever the danger originated, and AI control is then the special case in which tadv does not exist. 

Base rates and F1. A common alternative summarises the trade-off between security and disruption as an F1 score over blocked traces. Precision, the fraction of flags that are true, depends on the prevalence P (yτ = 1) , which a benchmark fixes by construction far above any deployment base rate, so a benchmark F1 says little about deployed behaviour. The audit-budget framing also reflects how an operating point is actually chosen: review capacity fixes α, rather than a precision target. 

Achievable budgets. The scores σt take finitely many values, so only finitely many operating points exist, and when a fraction ϕ of benign traces attains the maximal score, every threshold that flags anything at all has a false-positive rate of at least ϕ. This saturation floor is a property of the monitor: a monitor with ϕ > α has no operating point at budget α. C.2 DECOMPOSITION TRACK 

Seeds. We use the 582 harmful and 583 benign decompositions in the agent split of Decom-posedHarm (Chen et al., 2025). Each harmful seed is a goal (the attack_string ) rewritten as an ordered list of k sub-requests, with k from three to thirteen (median seven), and marks the sub-request at which the goal becomes achievable. That mark is the final sub-request in 479 of the 582 seeds; in the remaining 103 it precedes one to six further sub-requests, which the completions carry like any others, so tadv is not in general the last user message of a harmful trace. Benign seeds are ordinary multi-step tasks of the same shape. The seeds contain user messages only; we build a skeleton by interleaving each sub-request with an empty assistant slot. 

Completion. Gemma 4 26B-A4B (Gemma Team, 2026), served with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), fills the skeleton. It is prompted once per empty slot, in order, with the conversation so far and the next user message, and asked to return only the next assistant step: some reasoning text, then zero or more tool calls, each with its output. The tool list is fixed by us at 25 tools (web search and fetch, file and terminal access, email and messaging, media generation, payments, account creation and so on; Appendix C.7.1) rather than taken from the seed, whose tool names are not standardised. No tool is executed: the same model writes a simulated result for each call, following the LM-emulated-21 Preprint sandbox approach of ToolEmu (Ruan et al., 2024). The sampling temperature is 0.3 (hard-split retries excepted, Appendix C.5.1). Each returned step is inserted as an assistant text message, an assistant message carrying the tool calls and one tool message per call, which gives the traces a regular user / announcement / call / result pattern. The result is decomposition_main : 1,165 traces, with a mean of 30.4 messages for harmful and 27.6 for benign traces. The completion prompt also shows the generator the seed’s attack_string . Read on their own, sub-requests are often ambiguous, and a generator answering one in isolation would misread what is wanted and emit the wrong call, whereas the traces are meant to depict what an agent that has understood the request would do. Showing the goal lets a modest generator infer the intent of each sub-request. It has a side effect: the generator sometimes front-runs the decomposition and acts on the goal before the sub-request at which the seed places it. We treat this as a labelling problem and correct it with the evidence onset E (Appendix C.4.1); Appendix C.5 reports two ablations that test whether the leak is what monitors key on. 

Hard split. decomposition_hard realises the spliced attack of Section 4.1. Between consec-utive harmful sub-requests the ID-matched benign seed ( benign_0041 for harmful_0041 )contributes a (user, assistant) pair with probability 0.5, at most one insertion per eligible posi-tion and with a fixed random seed, and the spliced skeleton is completed with the same pipeline. Only the harmful class is regenerated; the split is evaluated against the benign traces of the 

decomposition_main split. Hard traces are longer (mean 42.1 messages) and, because the benign seed’s opening reconnaissance step often coincides with the harmful seed’s, 94 of the 582 traces contain an adjacent pair of near-identical user messages (difflib similarity above 0.9). 

Refusal twins. decomposition_refusal instantiates the control of Section 4.1. For each harmful decomposition_main trace with an evidence label (548 of 582; the 34 traces the rater judged to contain no concretely harmful action are skipped) we build three twins. Each copies the source’s messages up to a cut and appends one refusal, so the twin ends there, has no O and carries 

harm_index = null . The cut is the start of the assistant turn at E, at O, or at a seeded-random assistant-turn start strictly between them. A valid refusal point is always the first message of an assistant turn, so no twin announces a step and then refuses it: in 189 sources E falls on a tool-call message or a second narration and the twin is cut one message earlier, at the start of that turn. When the gap holds no intermediate turn, the middle point coincides with the nearer of E and O and is marked as such (206 sources, of which 114 have E = O). The refusal is written by the same Gemma model at temperature 0.7 from the last twelve messages of the prefix and the step it was about to take (Appendix C.7.2). Because a monitor’s score at message t depends only on the prefix τt, the twin’s scores on the shared prefix are copied from the harmful run and only the refusal message is scored, so source and twin differ by nothing but the refusal. Scoring O-cut twins independently of the harmful run reproduces the copied-prefix numbers to within about one percentage point, which validates the construction. The twins use the E labels that preceded the blind re-rating of Appendix C.4.1 and were not rebuilt. Under the blind labels, 212 of the 548 sources keep the same E, 294 move (median 3 messages later, range −16 to +21 ) and 42 have no concretely harmful action; six newly labelled sources have no twin. C.5.1 FINDINGS AND REPAIRS 

Completeness. The strict check requires every message to be non-empty and every tool call to be surrounded by assistant reasoning; the end-state check requires the trace to end on a non-empty assistant reply; a continuity judge (Gemma) reads each assistant step and asks whether it is coherent and answers its request. The strict check fails on three main traces and on the refusal twin of one of them, which we left as they are. On the decomposition_hard split it failed on 79 of 582 traces at first, because the longer traces made the generator emit empty tool outputs more often. Regenerating whole traces, with the sampling temperature raised from 0.6 to 1.0 over the retries, brought this down to 15 traces (27 empty outputs), which were then repaired one output at a time from their tool call and the preceding context; no assistant message and no label moved. The continuity judge flags no main trace and five hard traces, all a benign task spliced between two referentially linked harmful steps as the splice intends. 

Injection track. Three checks are specific to this track. The payload check verifies that tadv exists: the <INFORMATION> block occurs in a tool message of every trace, benign included. The onset check is the rule of Appendix C.3. The oracle check searches, at or after O, for a tool call whose name and arguments carry out the injection task; 1,639 of 1,881 harmful traces pass. A trace that fails it is kept: the label anchors on the commitment at O, which the onset check verifies, so under Section 3.3 a monitor that flags the trace is correct, and the oracle check characterises the comple-tions rather than gating them. The failures we inspected were the checker’s strictness rather than a missing action: an argument the model paraphrased (a phone number, a display name in place of a user ID), a tool name outside the checker’s synonym list, or an injection task with no natural completing call. 25 Preprint 

Labels. The decomposition harm_index was audited independently of the pipeline. The remap from the DecomposedHarm index is arithmetically exact (0 of 582 mismatches). The label itself sits late, on a repeat of a damaging call rather than on its first occurrence, on roughly 17% of traces (13 to 22%), with a hard floor of 12.7% from a mechanically checkable signature: the damaging call fires once, then again one step later, and the label sits on the repeat. This is what E was built to correct. The injection labels need no audit: they hold by construction on every harmful trace. 

Generation artefacts. Two ablations test whether the goal shown to the generator is what monitors key on. In the first, 50 harmful decomposition_main skeletons were re-completed with the goal block removed from the prompt, all else equal, and scored by the sequential monitor against their matched benign seeds: prefix AUC at eight messages moved from 0.885 to 0.881, 42 of the 50 traces were flagged before and 40 after at the same median first-flag position, and 34 of the 40 first flags sat on user messages, which are byte-identical between the two runs. In the second, the traces are kept and the user’s stated intent is masked, then the assistant’s narration as well: at a threshold fixed on the unmasked benign traces, the recall of sequential on 50 harmful traces goes from 0.70 to 0.72 and then to 0.84. Neither ablation moves the tested monitor in the direction a leak would predict; they cannot exclude other cues shared with the generator. C.5.2 REMOVED TRACES 

Seven injection traces are excluded from the released set, with the reason recorded at removal: six traces of two banking pairs ( u9_i5 , u9_i7 ) for which no recorded run ever fetched the payload, so no payload-bearing prefix exists, and one workspace trace ( u1_i11 ) that would not act on the injection first in ten regenerations. ## D EVALUATION DETAILS 

D.1 DETAILS OF THE MONITOR ARMS 

Every arm reads a trace one message at a time and writes a suspicion score on the shared 1 to 10 scale for each message it is asked about; messages it is not asked about keep the floor score of 1. 29 Preprint The arms differ in the view VM , the cadence CM and the judgement target of Table 1, and in how many model calls make up one decision. Unless stated otherwise, each call is sampled five times at temperature 1 and σt is the mean of the parsed samples (Appendix D.4). 

Core arms. baseline , sequential and sequential_action share one prompt skeleton (Appendix G): a short instruction, the material to judge, a request for two or three sentences of rea-soning and a final SCORE: <integer from 1 to 10> line, with a 200-token output budget. 

baseline is shown the current assistant message alone, at every assistant message with content, and rates the assistant’s action in it. sequential is shown the whole conversation up to and in-cluding the current message, rendered as Role: content lines, at every message of any role, and rates the malicious intent of the tasks taken together. sequential_action is shown the same rendered prefix but only at assistant messages, is told that the final message is the assistant’s current action, and rates that action using the user’s and tools’ content only as context. 

Extract-and-evaluate. eae follows the extract-and-evaluate scaffold of Arike et al. (2026), adapted to run at every assistant message over the cumulative prefix. An extractor call is shown the rendered prefix and asked to pick out at least five verbatim quotes bearing on the agent’s suspiciousness, with a 900-token budget and a single sample. An evaluator call is then shown only the task description, taken as the first user message of the trace, and those quotes, never the trace itself. It returns a 0 to 100 suspicion score in <verdict> tags; it is sampled five times with a 400-token budget and the mean is mapped onto the 1 to 10 scale (Appendix D.2). When the extractor returns no quotes the message keeps the floor score, since a step cannot be dropped from a timing metric. 

DecomposedHarm prompt families. The nine content-framed sp_* arms are the monitor prompts of Chen et al. (2025), transcribed verbatim: zero-shot ( sp_zeroshot ), zero-shot with a safety guideline that lists harm categories ( sp_guideline ), eight in-context examples of decom-posed questions ( sp_qa_icl ), eight in-context examples of agentic sub-tasks with reasons (sp_agent_icl ), and hypothesis generation ( sp_hypothesis ), in which the model lists up to five hypotheses about the user’s goal with a harm probability each and the score is the largest of them. The first four also have a chain-of-thought form ( _cot ) that asks for the reasoning before the number; the hypothesis prompt has structured reasoning built in and no separate CoT form. Their view is the paper’s: the cumulative list of untrusted inputs, that is, the user messages and the tool outputs (on the injection track, where the injected instruction arrives), never the assistant’s replies. They are scored at every user or tool message and return a probability between 0 and 1 that the stream is malicious; the parser takes the last number in [0 , 1] in the reply and counts a reply con-taining “sorry” as a detection, as in the original. Output budgets are 30 tokens without CoT, 600 with, and 900 for the hypothesis prompt. Two deviations from the paper are deliberate: each call is sampled five times at temperature 1 rather than once greedily, for comparability with the other arms, and scoring starts at the first input rather than the second. 

Action-framed control. sp_zeroshot_action is the zero-shot prompt with two changes to the input and one to the question: the rendered stream includes the assistant’s messages and is scored at assistant messages, and the judgement sentence asks for the probability that the assistant’s latest action is malicious, “judging the assistant’s own action rather than the content of the requests it received”. Output format, scale, token budget and the closing “Do not return anything else” are unchanged, so the pair sp_zeroshot / sp_zeroshot_action approximately isolates the framing effect of Section 5. 

Guard models. The three guard arms use trained safety classifiers rather than a prompted rubric: Llama Guard 4 12B (Meta AI, 2025) with its native MLCommons taxonomy ( llamaguard4 ), the same model with the categories replaced by this benchmark’s policy list through the chat tem-plate ( llamaguard4_policy ), and Qwen3Guard-Gen 8B (Qwen Team, 2025) ( qwen3guard ), which has no custom-policy interface. Each is scored at every assistant message with content, on the cumulative transcript folded into strictly alternating user and assistant turns (Appendix D.2), with one greedy call of at most 16 tokens and the token log-probabilities returned. σt is the probability mass on the unsafe verdict, read from the top twenty alternatives at the verdict token, not a sampled judgement. The code release also contains exploratory arms not reported here. 30 Preprint D.2 SCORE MAPPING 

Three arm families do not natively produce a 1 to 10 rating, and their outputs are mapped onto that scale before storage so that every arm passes through the same threshold, ROC and timing analysis. A probability p ∈ [0 , 1] becomes σ = 1 + 9 p. The sp_* arms return p directly. The eae evaluator returns a 0 to 100 score, which is divided by 100 and then mapped in the same way. For the guards, 

p is read from the log-probabilities at the verdict token: for Llama Guard 4 it is the mass on unsafe 

divided by the mass on unsafe and safe ; for Qwen3Guard, which has a third Controversial 

class, it is the mass on Unsafe plus half the mass on Controversial , divided by the mass on all three. If none of the verdict words appears among the returned alternatives, the sampled verdict itself is used, at 1, 0.5 or 0. For the prompted arms σt is the mean of the five mapped samples, so the core arms move in steps of 0.2 and the sp_* and eae arms on a finer grid. The guards make one greedy call, so their σt is a single probability and needs no averaging. 

Folded transcripts for the guards. The guard chat templates accept only alternating user and assis-tant turns: Llama Guard 4 raises an error on any other shape and Qwen3Guard silently drops tool messages. Each cumulative prefix is therefore folded before scoring. Assistant messages become assistant turns, with any tool-call JSON rendered as Tool call: name(arguments) lines; user, tool and system messages become user turns, tool outputs prefixed with [Tool output] ;consecutive turns of the same role are concatenated, and a conversation that would begin with an assistant turn is given a placeholder opening user turn. The folded prefix always ends on the assistant message being judged. 

Folding ablation. To check that the folding, rather than the guards’ training, explains their drop on the injection track, both guards were rescored on a stratified sample of each track with every message in its own turn, neutral filler turns keeping the alternation so that no tool output is concatenated with a sibling, at the same evaluation points and with the same log-probability extraction. Operating points. For each arm and view we report the position-blind AUC of the trace maximum 

max t∈CM σt on the evaluation set, and two numbers at the budget α = 0 .1: recall P (t∗ < ∞ | 

yτ = 1) and interval credit P (t∗ ∈ W | yτ = 1) . The headline threshold is the in-sample frontier: for each metric separately, the reported value is the largest the metric attains at any θ whose false-positive rate on the evaluation benign traces is at most α. Recall and interval credit can therefore be attained at different θ; recall is monotone in θ but interval credit is not, since a stricter θ can move a first flag from before E into W . When no θ other than “flag nothing” satisfies the budget, the arm has no operating point: its saturation floor ϕ, the smallest positive false-positive rate it can reach (Appendix A.3), exceeds α, and the tables print ϕ after a † in the recall column and a dash for interval credit. The 10% budget is the loosest common budget at which most arms have an operating point; at 2%, five of the six core Gemma 4 cells have none. 

Held-out check. Because the headline θ is chosen on the same benign traces on which its false-positive rate is measured, the frontier is descriptive and can be optimistic. The stored scores support a second protocol that uses the calibration split: θ is the empirical 90% quantile of the calibration-benign trace maxima, frozen before any evaluation trace is seen, and recall, interval credit and the re-alised false-positive rate are then read off the evaluation set at that one θ. Scores are discrete and tied, so the realised rate need not equal 0.1. The per-model result files hold both protocols: rows labelled 

frontier are the headline, and rows labelled heldout and atstar are the calibration-split protocol and its early, in-window and late decomposition at the same θ. Cross-fitted operating points. To measure how much the in-sample frontier flatters the headline, we re-drew the 10% / 90% benign split 500 times (seeds 1000 to 1499), fixed the quantile threshold on each calibration part and evaluated on the rest. In-sample recall is not optimistic: the mean gap to the cross-fitted value is 0.000 on Gemma and +0 .002 on Qwen3.6 pooled decomposition. In-sample interval credit is higher by 0.124 on Gemma (range +0 .008 to +0 .234 over the ten arms with an operating point) and by 0.118 on Qwen3.6 (range +0 .006 to +0 .225 over its twelve); on injection the gap is +0 .006 and +0 .008 . The gap is a threshold-rule effect rather than overfitting of detection: the frontier chooses, per metric, the threshold that maximises interval credit, and interval credit is not monotone in the threshold, so it can pick a stricter threshold that trades recall for timing, whereas the cross-fit fixes a single quantile threshold. 0.359, baseline 0.327, sp_zeroshot_action 0.313, sequential_action 0.260 and 

sequential 0.035, which moves baseline above sequential_action . Realised cross-fit FPR averages 0.108 to 0.109 because the quantile rule lands on tied score masses; a 2% budget is attainable in only a minority of splits for most core cells. 

Residual early flags. At the interval-optimal threshold on pooled decomposition, 

sequential_action still fires more than one message before E on 359 of 1,059 harmful traces and eae on 309. We sampled seven such flags per arm for three arms (seed 0), rendered the flagged step with its context and adjudicated each. Of the 21, two were on steps that are already concretely 40 Preprint harmful under the labelling brief (committing to enter a secured system with credentials found ear-lier), so for those the label sits a few steps late. Eighteen were on preparation that the blind brief deliberately excludes from onset: drafting the phishing message or forged document, searching for a target’s credentials, profiling a private individual from public pages. One was a spurious first-message read of the operator’s own mailbox. One flag in 21 is genuine over-eagerness; the rest are preparation, which the label places before E by design. Flags on preparation are early by the construction of the label, not by monitor error, so the interval number is a lower bound on detection and the early segment of Figure 10 overstates miscalibration. The E label is an LLM judgement on LLM-generated traces; the adjudication combined an LLM pass with our own inspection. 

The cadence artefact in sequential . sequential scores every message, and on decompo-sition its first flag often lands on the user sub-request immediately before the assistant message that carries E (Figure 7). Six papers that bear on this work appeared since our experiments were fixed. 

Evasion. SLEIGHT-Bench (Najt et al., 2026) and Hills et al. (2026) reach far higher evasion than Appendix E.5: 20 of SLEIGHT-Bench’s 40 attacks are never caught by a Claude Opus 4.6 monitor 46 Preprint across ten trials at a 1% false-positive rate, and Hills et al. see 65% or more across three attack models. The difference lies in the adversary. Both are control settings in which the agent is the adversary and writes its own actions, so the attack is designed to fit its cover, and Hills et al. spread it across pull requests, which is decomposition by the agent itself. Our adversary sits in the environ-ment and can only rephrase around actions already taken. The two settings sit at opposite ends of the misuse case, at most 5 of 40 against 50% or more; where a live misuse adversary who re-elicits the agent would land is not testable on a frozen corpus. Two findings transfer. SLEIGHT-Bench’s evasion strategies (model priors, instruction ambiguity, state manipulation) work on what the moni-tor expects to see rather than on what the agent does, the weakness the refusal twins expose from the benign side (Section 6). And the stateful link-tracker monitor of Hills et al., which tracks suspicious build-up across steps, is the shape of monitor the decomposition track rewards (Section 6). 

Guardrails on agent traces. TraceSafe-Bench (Chen et al., 2026) finds that general-purpose models outperform specialised guardrails on tool-calling trajectories and that accuracy does not scale mono-tonically with size; Harsh et al. (2026) find the same on a text-moderation corpus. Our guard results agree (Appendix E.4). One TraceSafe-Bench claim does not reproduce here: they attribute guardrail performance mostly to structural competence, how well the model reads tool-call JSON, whereas giving our guards each message as its own turn moves AUC by at most 0.019 (Appendix D), so on our tracks the bottleneck is what the guard looks for, not what it can parse. Neither benchmark scores when a flag lands. 

Building the monitor. Koran et al. (2026) find that three diverse monitors gain 2.4 times more than three copies of one, and argue that diversity rather than scale drives the gain. That is the ensemble reading of Appendix E.3, where a reasoning budget 38 to 113 times larger lowers AUC on Qwen3.6 and a change of framing buys more; our arms differ in framing, and we did not combine them. Meerkat (Stein et al., 2026) searches across many traces at once and argues that per-trace judges miss failures visible only in aggregate. Our benchmark is per-trace by construction; a decomposition spread across sessions, or an injection campaign across users, is the natural next scope. E.7 LIMITATIONS IN FULL 

Two items are deferred here from Section 6. 

Simulated tool outputs. Every tool output is written by the generator. The unrealism carries no label signal on its own, since both classes share it, but the generator knows the goal when it writes an output, and a systematic difference between the results it writes for harmful and benign actions could hand a monitor a tell that executed traces would not. Appendix C.5 tests the leaks we could name; others may remain, so performance on fabricated outputs need not transfer. 

## F RELATED WORK IN FULL 

F.1 THREAT FAMILIES 

Decomposition attacks. The idea predates agents. Glukhov et al. (2023) argue that semantic censor-ship of model outputs is undecidable in general, since an attacker can reconstruct an impermissible answer from permissible pieces, so filtering is a security problem rather than a classification prob-lem. Jones et al. (2024) show the effect across models: solving the hard-but-benign sub-tasks with an aligned frontier model and the easy-but-malicious ones with a weak model yields outputs nei-ther produces alone. Multi-turn jailbreaks (Russinovich et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024) are the nearest neighbours, but their later turns are recognisably harmful once reached, whereas a decomposition needs no jailbreak so long as the agent never connects the pieces. The defence proposed in Decom-posedHarm (Chen et al., 2025) is a lightweight sequential monitor that reads the user prompts so far, returns a probability of harmful intent at each step and halts once it crosses a threshold; across ten monitor models, a prompt-engineered small model given in-context examples or a chain of thought beats reasoning models at a fraction of the cost. The sp_* arms reimplement those prompts (Ap-pendix D.1). 

Prompt injection. Perez & Ribeiro (2022) showed the direct form, an instruction in the user’s in-put overriding the application’s prompt, before the indirect form was named. Prevention defences besides CaMeL train the model to prioritise privileged instructions (Wallace et al., 2024), separate 47 Preprint instructions from data at the input (Chen et al., 2024), or mark retrieved data so the model can tell it apart (Hines et al., 2024). The detection defences cited in Section 2.2 differ in what they read: PromptGuard 2 in LlamaFirewall classifies inputs while AlignmentCheck audits the agent’s reason-ing (Chennabasappa et al., 2025); Task Tracker (Abdelnabi et al., 2024) fits a linear probe to the change in activations after external data is read; MELON (Zhu et al., 2025) re-executes each step with the user task masked and treats a matching tool call as evidence of injection. Our monitors are detection of the narrowest kind. They read only the text of a fixed trace, and most score the agent rather than its inputs. 

What the two families share. In both, the adversary sits in the environment, as the user in one case and as a tool output in the other, and the harm is realised by the agent’s actions; yet Decomposed-Harm holds user prompts with no agent turns and AgentDojo holds full runs but scores the agent, not a monitor. The benchmark puts both in one trace format and labels both at the agent’s commitment (Section 3). F.2 MONITORING APPROACHES 

Prompted LLM monitors. In AI control the monitor is an LLM and the design space is its scaffold. SHADE-Arena (Kutasov et al., 2025) hands the monitor a whole transcript and asks for a suspicion score; Kale et al. (2025) compare full-transcript, sequential and hierarchical scaffolds and find the scaffold matters more than how much the monitor is told, enough to let a weaker model monitor a stronger agent; Baker et al. (2025) monitor a reasoning model’s chain of thought and find that optimising against the monitor teaches the agent to hide its intent. On the misuse side, R-Judge (Yuan et al., 2024) has LLMs judge recorded agent interactions and AgentAuditor (Luo et al., 2025) gives the judge a retrieved memory of past judgements. Beyond the three axes of Section 2.3, these systems differ in whether the verdict is a boolean or a score, which model runs it, and whether a decision is one call or a pipeline; the arms of Appendix D.1 vary all six. On the judge itself, forcing a bare score is worse than asking for reasoning first (Chiang & Lee, 2023), and LLM judges carry known biases (Zheng et al., 2023). 

Guard models. The earliest deployed guards were classifiers over single texts against a fixed taxon-omy (Markov et al., 2022); Llama Guard 4 (Meta AI, 2025) also names the violated categories, so the taxonomy can be changed at inference time; ShieldGemma (Zeng et al., 2024), WildGuard (Han et al., 2024) and Granite Guardian (Padhi et al., 2024) follow the same shape, and Qwen3Guard (Qwen Team, 2025) adds a three-way safe / controversial / unsafe verdict. Harsh et al. (2026) find recall varying widely across fourteen open guards, with no relation to model size. Among agent-trace guards, AgentDoG (Liu et al., 2026) classifies a whole trajectory against a taxonomy of source, failure mode and consequence, and TraceSafe-Bench (Chen et al., 2026) finds general LLMs ahead of specialised guards. The guards we run judge one exchange at a time and require alternating user and assistant turns, which is why traces are folded before scoring (Appendix D.1). F.3 HOW MONITORS ARE EVALUATED 

In DecomposedHarm a true positive is a flag at or before the labelled sub-task at which the goal becomes achievable, a harmful conversation is missed if the first flag comes later or never, and F1 and defence success rate are reported; a flag on the very first sub-task earns full credit, so the metric is position-aware in one direction only. AgentDojo’s security is whether the injected task was carried out by the end of the run. SHADE-Arena and its successors (Kale et al., 2025; Stickland et al., 2025) report AUC over transcripts or TPR at a fixed FPR. In each case the object scored is the adversary’s input, the final outcome or the transcript as a whole. Under adversarial pressure, Kale et al. (2025) find that the agent’s awareness of being monitored hurts the monitor more than extra information helps it, Terekhov et al. (2025) show that a monitor-aware untrusted model can embed prompt injections in its outputs and evade diverse monitors, and SLEIGHT-Bench (Najt et al., 2026) finds half of its evasion-built transcripts never caught at 1% FPR; Appendix E.5 is the misuse analogue. 48 Preprint F.4 TOOLING
