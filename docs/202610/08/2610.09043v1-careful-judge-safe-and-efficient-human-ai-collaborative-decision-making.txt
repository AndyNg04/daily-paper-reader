## ABSTRACT 

In human–AI collaborative decision making, human review can prevent unsafe AI decisions, but each human judgment is costly. Treating human intervention as a one-off fallback misses the opportunity to improve future AI decisions, yet adaptively learning from human feedback changes the model and can invalidate previously calibrated safety guardrails. We approach this challenge with CARE —calibrated adaptive rectification and escalation—an end-to-end pipeline that combines AI models and human reviewers to guarantee safe, human-aligned decisions, while continuously learning from human feedback to achieve greater automation with fewer human queries. CARE is principled, general, modular, and works with any black-box AI model. We equip it with a rectification module that improves AI decisions by learning the structure of human–AI misalignment. Our novel adaptive calibration module guarantees risk control at every time step for any rectification module. Experiments on four safety-critical real-world datasets spanning lan-guage, vision, driving, and robotics demonstrate that CARE achieves human-aligned decisions while reducing human queries by 25–81% relative to baselines. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Human–AI collaboration is increasingly used in safety-critical applications, including data triage and evaluation (Hemmer et al., 2025; Mozannar & Sontag, 2020), autonomous driving (Luo et al., 2025; Sinha et al., 2024), robot monitoring (Sermanet et al., 2025), and model alignment (Christiano et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2022). In these settings, AI predictions represent an inexpensive, but potentially misaligned, way to scale reliable, but costly, human judgment. Deciding when to trust AI and when to call for human review creates a safety–efficiency trade-off (El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010). Existing methods address different parts of this trade-off. Some provide safety guarantees but may require frequent human intervention (Jung et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2023); others learn from human feedback to improve efficiency without providing such guarantees (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; Christiano et al., 2017). Naively combining the two directions may amplify their respective weaknesses and worsen the trade-off—splitting already scarce human feedback between model improvement and safety checks could make the former noisier and the latter more conservative, as is evident in Section 7. Still, we ask for the best of both worlds: how can a human–AI system continue to make safe decisions while learning from human feedback to reduce future intervention? 

A positive answer requires true collaboration: a human provides feedback when AI is uncertain, and AI becomes more confident when facing similar scenarios in the future. Such dynamic interaction should uncover the structure of human–AI misalignment and close this gap, making AI decisions safer and human intervention less frequent. However, this structure may be difficult to learn when human review is sparse and selective. Moreover, safety guarantees must keep pace with each model update. Specifically, queries selectively reviewed by a human can differ statistically from future ones,

We instantiate CARE in binary classification, using an exact risk decomposition (Section 2) to motivate its four modules: (Section 3) Residual Gauss rectifies AI predictions and uncertainty estimates by fitting a Gaussian process to the residual between human and AI decisions; (Section 4) Confident Jack calibrates the rectified model with statistical and computational efficiency; (Section 5) Exacting Gates escalates queries to a human using a scenario-specific threshold informed by calibration; and (Section 6) Active Max adaptively selects informative human queries. AI-accelerated human feedback. As AI models are applied to increasingly complex tasks, high-quality supervision is a key bottleneck. To alleviate this, some works consider using learned models to scale supervision. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) distills human preference data into a reward model (Christiano et al., 2017), while other techniques bring in human feedback at a higher level, in rubric or constitution design (Bai et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023). Such techniques have been instrumental in advancing open-world physical AI, with LLM/VLM rewards scoring decision-making in robotics and autonomous vehicles (Ma et al., 2024; Sima et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024; Sermanet et al., 2025; Rocamonde et al., 2024). However, ensuring align-ment between the AI feedback and what a human expert would provide remains critical, especially in safety-critical settings. Recent literature applies ideas from selective classification and abstention (El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010; Vardhan et al., 2026; Vishwakarma et al., 2023) to applications of AI feedback, allowing AI to escalate hard cases to human experts (Jung et al., 2025). We extend these ideas to allow for online adaptation, improving efficiency while maintaining alignment guarantees. Moreover, we find that the local online adaptation of CARE can mitigate the tendency of static selective escalation to exacerbate disparities among regions in the input space (Jones et al., 2021). 

Model-assisted inference. A related line of research explores combining cheap proxy measurements and limited expensive measurements in the context of variance-reduced statistical inference, where the cheap proxy can be a prediction model (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; Zrnic & Candes, 2024) or a simulator (Luo et al., 2025; 2026; Badithela et al., 2025). Angelopoulos et al. (2023) propose prediction-powered inference (PPI) that uses empirical risk minimization to correct the model’s systematic bias. While its fitted rectifier can capture some local structure, we model potential residual correlations explicitly and update each prediction’s confidence through its posterior. PPI instead provides a global confidence bound for its bias correction. Greater model capacity can reduce approximation error but may increase estimation variability, motivating prior refitting as human feedback accumulates. 

Conformal calibration. Sharing the goal of model-agnostic risk control, cross-conformal prediction (Vovk, 2015) and Jackknife+ (Barber et al., 2021) improve data efficiency over split conformal prediction (Papadopoulos et al., 2002; Lei et al., 2018; Jung et al., 2025), which splits the available human feedback into disjoint sets for training and calibration. To correct known or tractable covariate shifts, Tibshirani et al. (2019) introduce weighted exchangeability , and Prinster et al. (2022) apply it to obtain weighted Jackknife+. These methods do not directly handle the history-dependent covariate shifts induced by active acquisition in our setting, especially during the online period, when the weight calculation is intractable. Online model adaptation adds another layer of distribution shift. Existing online conformal methods (Gibbs & Candes, 2021; Gibbs & Candès, 2024; Bhatnagar et al., 2023; Feldman et al., 2023) often treat distribution shifts as adversarial and focus on time-averaged regret or coverage. Our goal is risk control at every time step without conservative fallbacks: calibration should track model improvement and reduce escalation as the model learns. 1.2 PROBLEM SETUP

Proposition 1 identifies four sources of risk to be addressed to align the AI model with human preferences and deploy it safely: • Aleatoric ambiguity encodes the intrinsic hardness of the query, which is the irreducible risk of the Bayes-optimal human-aligned predictor. When this risk exceeds the tolerance, we escalate the query to the human oracle to reveal the correct zero-loss decision. • Excess risk measures the gap between a learned predictor and the Bayes-optimal predictor due to finite data and model misspecification. We calibrate the escalation rule to control the ensemble’s risk despite imperfect learning. • Epistemic uncertainty captures training-sample-specific information, including the correlation structure revealed by the queried data. We adapt to this structure and prioritize informative queries that can improve future decisions. • Systematic bias is the population-level misalignment of the AI model . We rectify AI predictions by learning to correct this bias from human feedback. This motivates calibrated adaptive rectification and escalation ( CARE ), a pipeline that reuses every queried human label to adaptively rectify AI’s systematic bias and epistemic uncertainty, calibrate the rectified AI model, and escalate to human when the rectified AI decision is still risky. This pipeline improves efficiency while controlling the risk of the resulting ensemble. Formally, human queries inform a rectifier r in the score space (the range of a strictly increasing link function g : [0 , 1] → ¯R), an uncertainty-reduced confidence measure c, a calibrated escalation threshold λ, and an active acquisition function π. Remark 1. Proposition 1 offers a triage of gaps left by existing tools. Classical selective classification (El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010) and calibrated threshold-based escalation (Jung et al., 2025) do not learn to improve the base model, so a biased or uncertain model may escalate every query under a small risk tolerance, leading to huge cost. Population-level rectifier regression (Angelopoulos et al., 2023) targets systematic bias, but its uncertainty quantification does not adapt to locally revealed correlation structure. Finally, active acquisition (Zrnic & Candes, 2024) can target epistemically informative 4points, but does not by itself learn a rectifier or an acquisition function, nor does it certify the risk of accepted decisions. CARE closes all four gaps to achieve safety efficiently. Furthermore, we design CARE to support flexible offline–online model lifecycles. At offline check-points, CARE revisits accumulated human feedback in an order-invariant manner, refits rectification and rebuilds calibration, prioritizing query efficiency by making fuller use of queried labels. Between checkpoints, CARE processes samples sequentially and updates rectification and calibration online. 

## 3 RESIDUAL GAUSS : L OCALITY -AWARE BAYESIAN RECTIFICATION 

To close reducible gaps in Proposition 1, we use queries to learn the human–AI residual and rectify AI’s decision to align with human preference. AI’s uncertainty about human preference can be correlated across the input space. For example, an autonomous driving system that misjudges a pedestrian’s intention at one intersection may make similar errors at similar intersections (Sinha et al., 2024). These correlations allow feedback on one query to improve decisions on related inputs, improving alignment—and thereby reducing escalation frequency—over time. On the other hand, treating residuals as independent can lead to a query-all degradation when the initial uncertainty level is above the risk tolerance. Therefore, Proposition 1 suggests a learning model to capture both the global systematic bias and local epistemic uncertainty of the human–AI residual. We model this with a Gaussian process (GP) (Williams & Rasmussen, 2006). On the score scale of the link g, we place a GP( m, k ) prior on the residual r(x) = g(pH (x)) − g(pA(x)) , where pH is the Bayes-optimal human-aligned predictor, and use human feedback to obtain the Bayesian rectifier rt. The mean m captures global systematic bias, while the kernel k captures correlations that let feedback transfer across inputs. 

Initialization: parametric prior regression. AI’s raw confidence cA can offer useful information about uncertainty, even when it is not calibrated. We incorporate it into a learnable GP prior:

This adaptive prior lets human feedback determine how much to trust the AI’s confidence and how much additional residual structure to learn. 

Offline: nonparametric posterior inference. Given the queried labels, the GP posterior applies corrections at each input. At time step t, let Rt follow the (approximate) posterior distribution of the residual. The rectified posterior predictive probability Pt = g−1(g(pA) + Rt) gives a rectified point prediction pt as its posterior mean. We also rectify the confidence using the posterior risk estimate: 

1−ct = EY∼Bern( Pt)[L(pt, Y )] = EPt [PtL(pt, 1)+(1 −Pt)L(pt, 0)] = ptL(pt, 1)+(1 −pt)L(pt, 0) .

Thus, in binary classification, the same posterior mean provides both the rectified prediction and confidence measure. For example, ct = max {pt, 1 − pt} for the 0–1 loss and ct = 1 − pt(1 − pt) for the Brier loss. Since binary labels give a non-Gaussian posterior, we approximate it using expectation propagation (EP) or a Laplace approximation (Williams & Rasmussen, 2006). 

Online: assumed-density filtering. Between offline checkpoints, we keep the prior fixed and update the residual posterior after each instance of actively acquired human feedback using assumed-density filtering (ADF), the online counterpart of EP. ADF obtains the Gaussian approximation of Rt+1 by matching its first two moments with those of the conditional posterior tilted by the new observation. Each update requires only a one-dimensional integral and a rank-one covariance correction; the probit link gives closed-form updates. An online maximum a posteriori (MAP) update provides an alternative online counterpart to the Laplace approximation. See Appendix C for more details. ## 6 ACTIVE MAX : A CTIVE ACQUISITION FOR LONG -TERM EFFICIENCY 

An active acquisition module π in (1) independent of safety escalation is motivated by two needs. First, calibration requires replaying the acquisition policy on counterfactual histories to compute the weights in (4). If this acquisition policy coincides with the safety escalation policy, which is only known after calibration, then it creates a nested dependency and potentially exponential computational complexity. Second, active acquisition can potentially improve long-term data efficiency by prioritizing the human queries that can reduce future escalation frequency, which cannot be achieved by the safety escalation 7rule alone. This shares the same spirit as active statistical inference (Zrnic & Candes, 2024), but with a different objective: instead of maximizing inference precision, we aim to maximize efficiency. This double escalation mechanism separates the input and output of the calibration particle system and lets human review protect current decisions and guide learning for future ones. Specifically, between offline checkpoints, actively escalated queries ( 1 = At ∼ Bern( πt(Xt)) ) update the rectifier and calibration particles; queries escalated only by the safety threshold ( 1 = 1ct(Xt) ≤ λt(Xt))resolve the current decision, while their use for learning waits until the next checkpoint. We refer the reader to Appendix F for our active acquisition design.

CARE shares offline labels between rectification and calibration without online adaptation; and CARE 

with PPI replaces our GP rectifier with the PPI rectifier. The full CARE uses GP rectification with an offline–online learning and calibration schedule. Here we present some representative results demonstrating four properties that are observed consis-tently across all experiments.

## 8 CONCLUSION 

We introduced CARE , an end-to-end framework for safe and efficient human–AI collaborative decision making. By combining model-aware residual learning with model-agnostic adaptive calibration,  

> CARE

uses human feedback to improve future predictions while maintaining control of the final system’s risk. Experiments on real-world safety-critical tasks demonstrate substantial query savings across domains, AI models, and confidence measures. Together, these results provide a theoretical and empirical foundation for safeguarding continuously improving AI models towards greater autonomy.

Decisions, queries, and metrics. Each dataset comes with a binary decision task, with a fixed inexpensive predictor as “AI”, and a benchmark reference label as the correct “human” response. Ensemble risk divides final errors by all processed examples, including queried examples which contribute no error. Query rate divides all oracle calls by the same denominator and includes initial offline static preparation dataset (training and/or calibration) and online adaptive escalations. Reusing a purchased label for fitting or calibration does not incur a second query. Rectified-model risk instead evaluates the predictor before oracle intervention, including examples that are ultimately queried. 

Paired evaluation. Each study uses ten randomized trials, pairing the data stream within trial across methods, risk targets, and preparation budgets. Means and standard errors across the ten trials are reported. nuPlan and HateBench have reserved evaluation panels for assessing the rectified model. This evaluation/test dataset does not enter the data stream, is not used for training or calibration, and thus is not counted in the query complexity. Feature design may use covariate information, but no label information is revealed in this preparation stage. 

Methods. 

• AI uses the original prediction without queries. • PPI fits a linear rectifier on the static preparation data and then predicts without safety escalation. • SE calibrates the original AI confidence. • PPI+SE splits the static preparation data approximately equally between fitting the linear rectifier and calibrating escalation. • GPC+SE replaces the linear rectifier in PPI+SE with a GP classifier, but otherwise uses the same static preparation and calibration. • Static CARE uses GP rectification and cross-fitted calibration on the shared static preparation data, then freezes the fitted policy. • (Adaptive) CARE does not have static preparation data. It starts with a conservative confidence threshold and learns from labels obtained through escalation. • CARE with PPI replaces the GP rectifier in CARE with a linear one, but otherwise uses the same adaptive calibration and escalation. 

Prepare–offline–online schedule. Static methods reserves a preparation fraction of the entire data stream. This fraction is thus a hyperparameter for static methods, but not for (adaptive) CARE . Offline checkpoints are a hyperparameter for adaptive methods, and follow batches of 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024, and 2,048 newly queried labels, with the final interval repeated. 

Gap between theory and practice. Active escalation coincides with safety escalation in our experiments. Computational approximations include sampled counterfactual replays using truncated horizons and logged acquisition decisions, and shorter EP iterations for the particle system. Task and evaluation. We use nuPlan driving scenarios (Caesar et al., 2021) with its inexpensive “AI” predictor as the open-loop simulator and its “human” oracle as the closed-loop oracle. The binary task 13 is to collision prediction, with the label being 1 if the closed-loop simulation has a time-to-collision (TTC) of less than one second. The “AI” predictor’s probability of safety is a probit transform of the open-loop TTC margin, with scale 0.5 seconds and probabilities clipped to [10 −6, 1 − 10 −6].The population contains 49,763 scenarios from six intersection-related scenario types and 63 driving logs. A fixed, label-blind split reserves 9,953 scenarios, stratified by the six physical regions in Table 3; 39,810 remain eligible for streaming. Each of ten paired trials randomly orders this pool and processes its first 10,000 scenarios. Regional evaluation uses the same region-stratified panel of 2,000 reserved scenarios for every trial and method, with population region weights used for pooled snapshot metrics. Driving logs occur in both partitions, so this evaluates within-population adaptation. 

Feature space. The rectifier receives ten engineered open-loop scalars and eight joint principal components of 384 ego-, agent-, and road-embedding coordinates. The scalar and embedding blocks contribute 80% and 20% of normalized feature energy, respectively. Representations are fitted without labels on each trial’s first 2,000 covariates, frozen, and shared across methods.

When all left-out datasets are exchangeable and the learning method is permutation-invariant, the scores s−(k,k ′) are equivariant and thus conformal with the test score s−(K+1 ,k ). However, in our setting, the left-out datasets are drawn from different distributions due to the adaptive data stream, and thus they have different impact on the model particles. We thus need to weigh their conformity w.r.t. the test score. To do so, we need to delineate two things each data point specifies: a time slot and a realized sample value. Then, we consider the following conditioning: K + 1 groups of left-out time slots are realized, but the mapping from each slot group to the sample group is not revealed. Under this conditioning, each sample group has some probability of occupying the test slot group, and thus the scores should be weighed by the corresponding probability. Formally, define the following conditioning information D: the full redacted arrival slot log which records the escalation decisions is known; the grouped slots of all left-out datasets are known; all collected samples are observed; the slot-to-sample mapping of slots outside the union of the left-out datasets, including the online slots, is known; and the grouping and within-group order of the left-out samples, including the test point, are known. The only thing we mask—the mapping from left-out slot groups to left-out sample groups—is then an element of the (K + 1) -permutation group SK+1 .We can further augment D by restricting the mapping to be in a transitive subgroup G of SK+1 , e.g., the (K + 1) -cyclic group. Thus, the remaining assignments are exactly those obtained by applying G

to the realized assignment. Our results hold for any permutation subgroup. A smaller group reduces the number of permutations to consider, hence making likelihood calculation easier; however, it may produce more concentrated weights and reduce the diversity of our particle system. For example, if the subgroup is non-transitive and never maps a fold to the test slot, then the corresponding particle has zero weight and will not contribute. Let D◦
