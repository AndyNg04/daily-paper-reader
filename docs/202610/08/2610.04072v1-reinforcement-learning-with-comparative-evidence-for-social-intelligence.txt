## ABSTRACT 

Developing socially intelligent AI remains heavily dependent on human-annotated data, limiting the scale and breadth of social understanding that models can ac-quire. Methods that derive training signals from unlabeled data offer a path beyond this dependence, but social predictions lack the verification oracles available in domains such as mathematics and coding. Moreover, core social targets such as affect, intent, preference, and pragmatic meaning are often ambiguous. The same observed behavior can support multiple plausible interpretations, making it difficult to verify which is best supported. To address this challenge, we introduce 

REINFORCEMENT LEARNING WITH COMPARATIVE EVIDENCE (RLCE) ,a reinforcement learning method that learns social understanding from unlabeled training data without constructing rewards from ground-truth annotations. Given distinct answers in a rollout group, RLCE constructs evidence tests that identify observable evidence favoring an answer over another, validates these tests against the input sample, and aggregates test outcomes to determine the best-supported interpretation. Evidence tests are then regenerated as the policy produces new answers during training, enabling tests to evolve with the policy’s answers. Across four benchmarks spanning affect, pragmatics, communicative intent, and prefer-ence, RLCE attains the strongest performance among seven methods that use no ground-truth training labels for rewards, including consensus, policy LLM-judge verification, multimodal co-evolution, and rubric-based rewards. Gains over the strongest baseline are +3.58 on MOSEI, +4.50 points on UR-FUNNY, +1.15 on IntentQA, and +18.93 on PrefEval. Analyses further show that RLCE exhibits a larger share of reward variation between correct and incorrect predictions than compared rubric methods, can overturn erroneous policy-derived preferences, and benefits from pairwise test construction, compositional test aggregation, and on-policy test evolution. Code and models will be released after the review process. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Building machines with social intelligence, the ability to understand people, their affective and cognitive states, preferences, and behavior, has been one of AI’s longest-standing and most fun-damental ambitions (Picard, 2000; Breazeal, 2000). However, despite the remarkable progress of modern AI, socially intelligent models remains heavily reliant on human-annotated data (Zhang et al., 2026b), which is difficult to obtain at scale because human behavior is highly heterogeneous, context-dependent, and ambiguous to interpret (Zhang et al., 2026b; Ong et al., 2026a). This places a fundamental ceiling on social intelligent AI systems, limiting what models can learn and how well they generalize to new settings (Ong et al., 2026b). Raising this ceiling, we argue, requires self-evolving social intelligence : systems that derive learning signals from unlabeled training data rather than human annotations. Yet, this remains challenging as social predictions lack a verification oracle (e.g., a proof checker), unlike solutions in mathematics and coding (Zhao et al., 2026). Consequently, self-generated supervision risks reinforcing errors (Arazo et al., 2020).

However, social understanding poses a distinct verification problem: the social targets to be modeled - e.g., intent, sentiment, preference, or pragmatic meaning, are ambiguous, and manifest through observable language, expressions, cues, and behavior (Mathur et al., 2024; Vinciarelli et al., 2009). These observations can often be compatible with multiple plausible interpretations, although only one corresponds to the underlying social target (Vinciarelli et al., 2009; 2011). Existing proxy verification signals do not explicitly resolve among these interpretations. Rubric-based methods typically evaluate each response independently against general criteria, which may leave multiple interpretations similarly plausible (Gunjal et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2025). Additionally, consensus-based verification may favor agreement across predictions rather than what is actually observed in the sample (Chen et al., 2023; He et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a). To address this gap, we introduce REINFORCEMENT LEARNING WITH COMPARATIVE EVI -

(RLCE) , an RL method for learning social understanding from unlabeled training data. Our core principle is to verify ambiguous answers through comparative evidence: comparing answers pairwise through multiple evidence tests, each of which checks the sample for evidence (i.e., ob-servable support) favoring an answer over another, and then aggregating the test outcomes into a verdict. This allows verification to distinguish among different interpretations of the social target based on the evidence present in the sample. Given answers from a rollout group, RLCE constructs evidence tests that check for evidence favoring an answer over another. For example, if “Nice job” could mean praise or sarcasm , rather than checking a generic cue such as whether the speaker smiles, which may occur in either case, RLCE constructs a test around a distinction that helps resolve the two interpretations, such as whether the speaker’s tone aligns with or contradicts the positive words. Each test is then evaluated against the sample’s content to determine whether the specified evidence is present, and the resulting outcomes are aggregated to identify the best-supported answer, which receives the reward for GRPO. As the policy updates, RLCE regenerates evidence tests for its latest answers, keeping them specific to the policy’s outputs. We evaluate RLCE on four established multimodal and text benchmarks spanning core and diverse social understanding tasks: affective understanding (Zadeh et al., 2018), pragmatic reasoning (Hasan et al., 2019), communicative-intent (Li et al., 2023), and preference and personalization (Zhao et al., 2025); comparing against state-of-the-art methods that construct rewards from unlabeled training data, including consensus-based approaches, from self-consistency (Chen et al., 2023) to multimodal evolution (He et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a), policy LLM-Judge (Yuan et al., 2024), and rubric-based rewards (Gunjal et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). Our experiments show: (1) relative to baselines, RLCE achieves the best mean performance across benchmarks, with gains over the strongest baseline of +1.15 (IntentQA), +3.58 (MOSEI), +4.50 (UR-FUNNY), and +18.93 (PrefEval) points; (2) relative to compared rubric-based methods, RLCE exhibits a larger share of reward variation between correct and incorrect predictions, and more often overturns erroneous consensus and self-judgment preferences; (3) ablations of RLCE’s components show that pairwise evidence-test construction improves performance, aggregating multiple tests improves verdict accuracy, and evolving tests outperform fixed tests, particularly for free-form policy outputs.

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Self-generated verification signals. A central challenge in learning from unlabeled training data is constructing reliable learning signals without human-provided labels. Early approaches derive this signal from agreement across the model’s sampled predictions. Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022) and Universal Self-Consistency (Chen et al., 2023) use agreement across sampled answers, an approach extended to multimodal self-training by VisPlay (He et al., 2025) and Video-Zero (Zhang et al., 2026a). TTRL (Zuo et al., 2026) uses majority-vote pseudo-labels for policy updates, while SRLM (Yuan et al., 2024) relies on direct policy self-judgment. Recent work instead constructs explicit criteria for evaluating responses. Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) (Gunjal et al., 2026) uses fixed criteria, while subsequent methods construct or refine criteria adaptively from model-generated responses (Shao et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). By contrast, RLCE constructs pairwise evidence tests for candidate answers, checks whether the corresponding evidence is present in the input sample, and aggregates their outcomes across answers to identify the best-supported answer. Existing verification approaches have been explored less extensively for social understanding, where the underlying target is ambiguous and the same observed behavior can admit multiple plausible interpretations. This motivates RLCE’s focus on resolving ambiguity by testing which interpretation is more strongly supported by the observed input. 

Socially intelligent AI. Core to social intelligence is inferring latent affective, intentional, preferential, and pragmatic states from observable audiovisual and linguistic behavior. Existing work has largely relied on supervised annotations, ranging from sentiment and emotion labels in MOSEI (Zadeh et al., 2018) to human-authored reasoning traces in Social Genome (Mathur et al., 2025). Recent generalist models similarly learn social understanding through supervised training on annotated data (Ong et al., 2026a;b). Together, these approaches have advanced social understanding but retain a common dependence on human annotations for supervision. This limits how readily learning can scale to naturally occurring social data without training labels. Reinforcement Learning with Comparative Evidence

3.1 PRELIMINARIES 

Problem definition: Learning social understanding from unlabeled training data. We consider critic-free reinforcement learning (e.g., GRPO (Shao et al., 2024)) for social understanding tasks, where policy training and reward construction use no human-provided ground-truth annotations from the training data. Let πθ denote the policy to be learnt, instantiated as an LLM. Given a multimodal sample c and question q = ( u, A), where u denotes the natural-language query and A the answer space, we sample G rollouts ag ∼ πθ (· | c, q ), ag ∈ A , g = 1 , . . . , G . The goal is to assign rewards using only c and q, without access to the ground-truth answer for the training example. Here ag

represents an interpretation of a social target (e.g., sentiment, intent, preference, or humor) inferred from observable language, expressions, and behavior. Because these cues can be ambiguous, the same observation may support multiple plausible interpretations. 

Method overview. REINFORCEMENT LEARNING WITH COMPARATIVE EVIDENCE (RLCE) verifies candidate answers through three components. (i) Evidence-test construction : for pairs of distinct answers in a rollout group, RLCE constructs evidence tests that check for observable evidence in 

c favoring an answer over the other. (ii) Compositional aggregation : each test is validated against 

c, and the resulting test outcomes are aggregated across answer pairs into a verification score that determines the best-supported answer. (iii) On-policy evolution : as the policy updates and produces new answers, the evidence tests are regenerated so that verification stays specific to the policy’s latest answers. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Benchmarks & baselines. We evaluate RLCE on four core dimensions of social understand-ing: affect using the sentiment analysis task from MOSEI (Zadeh et al., 2018), pragmatics with UR-FUNNY (Hasan et al., 2019), intent with IntentQA (Li et al., 2023), and preference with PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025). We compare against seven methods that construct rewards from un-labeled training data: rubric-based methods, including Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) (Gunjal et al., 2026), RLER (Shao et al., 2025), and RLAC (Wu et al., 2026); multimodal evolution methods, Video-Zero (Zhang et al., 2026a) and VisPlay (He et al., 2025); self-agreement via Universal Self-Consistency (USC) (Chen et al., 2023); and a frozen Policy LLM-Judge baseline, adapted from the policy-as-judge formulation of self-rewarding language models (Yuan et al., 2024). 

Experimental setup. For MOSEI, UR-FUNNY, and IntentQA, we follow the splits, prompts, and metrics of the Human Behavior Atlas benchmark (Ong et al., 2026b); for PrefEval, we follow the evaluation setup introduced in the original work (Zhao et al., 2025) across its explicit, choice, and persona tasks. All methods use the same Qwen3.5-9B initialization, with no ground-truth training labels used for reward construction. From Table 1, we report the mean over three training runs with different random seeds. Full details of all baseline implementations and the experimental setup are provided in App. D. 4.1 RESULTS 

Main results. We first evaluate whether RLCE can learn from unlabeled training data to generalize to a held-out test split. From Tab. 1, RLCE achieves the best mean performance on all four benchmarks across three runs, with an average rank of 1.00. Its gains over the strongest competing baseline are +3.58 points on MOSEI, +4.50 on UR-FUNNY, and +18.93 on PrefEval, each exceeding the seed standard deviation of either method (Tab. 6 provides the standard deviation). On IntentQA, the +1.15-point difference is within one standard deviation ( 58 .48 ± 1.59 vs. 57 .33 ± 0.50 ), indicating comparable performance. Beyond this second-best baseline, RLCE outperforms the remaining baselines on IntentQA by at least 1.71 points beyond its reported standard deviation. Overall, across three runs, RLCE achieves the highest mean performance on the four benchmarks, with gains spanning affect, pragmatics, intent, and preference tasks. 

The magnitude of RLCE’s gains varies across benchmarks. The largest improvement is observed on PrefEval (+18.93 points), relative to the gains on IntentQA (+1.15), MOSEI (+3.58), UR-FUNNY (+4.50). Several benchmark characteristics may partly account for this difference. Preference-relevant evidence is often explicitly expressed in the preceding interaction (Zhao et al., 2025), PrefEval’s text-only format avoids the additional perceptual uncertainty present in the other multimodal benchmarks, and the lower base-model performance (44.72%) leaves greater room for improvement. We further examine the PrefEval result through controlled ablations under the same training setup. These matched comparisons highlight both pairwise construction and evolving evidence tests contributing to the PrefEval performance. Overall, benchmark-specific characteristics may partly explain the larger PrefEval gain, while RLCE also improves performance across the three other multimodal benchmarks (IntentQA, MOSEI, UR-FUNNY). A larger share of RLCE’s reward variation distinguishes correct from incorrect predictions. 

In GRPO, within-group reward differences determine the relative advantages that drive the policy update (Shao et al., 2024). Reward variation that separates correct from incorrect predictions therefore provides a learning signal more directly aligned with task performance. As shown in Fig. 2, on MOSEI, throughout training, roughly 65-85% of RLCE’s reward variation occurs between correct and incorrect predictions, compared with about 30-36% for RLER and RaR. This suggests that RLCE directs a larger share of its learning signal toward separating correct from incorrect predictions. 

RLCE more often corrects erroneous consensus preferences. A potential challenge for self-evolution is confirmation bias: when a model uses its own incorrect predictions as supervision, subsequent training can reinforce those errors (Arazo et al., 2020). We examine a related failure mode through USC, which derives supervision from agreement or consensus among the policy’s own rollouts. Among cases where USC’s consensus selects an incorrect answer, RLCE more often assigns higher reward to the correct answer relative to rubrics methods RaR and RLER. From Fig. 2 (Middle), RLCE does so in 56% of cases on UR-FUNNY, compared with 48% for RaR and 49% for RLER; on MOSEI, the corresponding rates are 51% (RLCE), 41% (RaR), and 37% (RLER), respectively. These results suggest that RLCE provides a stronger corrective signal than the rubric baselines considered when policy consensus is incorrect. 

RLCE can revise erroneous Policy-Judge preferences. We next consider the Policy LLM-Judge baseline, where the frozen policy directly judges the candidate answers. This comparison evaluates whether RLCE can overturn an incorrect direct judgment made by the same frozen policy used to validate its evidence tests. This tests whether RLCE’s verification procedure can yield a different preference from the policy’s own direct assessment. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduced R EINFORCEMENT LEARNING WITH COMPARATIVE EVIDENCE , an RL framework that learns social understanding from unlabeled training examples, with rewards constructed without ground-truth annotations. Across four core and diverse benchmarks spanning affect, pragmatics, intent, and preference, RLCE achieves the strongest performance among the baselines. Our analyses further show that a larger share of RLCE’s reward variation distinguishes correct from incorrect predictions than the compared rubric methods, and that RLCE can revise erroneous policy-derived preferences. Controlled ablations also highlight the utility of pairwise evidence-test construction, compositional test aggregation, and on-policy test evolution for verifica-tion. Overall, these results suggest that RLCE can provide a useful learning signal for improving social understanding from unlabeled training examples across core tasks spanning affect, pragmatics, intent, and preference. This work trains models to infer affect, humor, intent, and preferences from recordings of people, using existing public research benchmarks (CMU-MOSEI, UR-FUNNY, IntentQA, PrefEval) as released by their authors; no new human-subject data were collected. Automated inference of latent human states carries well-known risks. Such inferences can be wrong in ways that differ across demographic and cultural groups, and can be misused for profiling or surveillance. Label-free self-training adds a specific risk. Because rewards are derived from the model’s own judgments about what observable behavior means, the procedure can entrench priors of the base model rather than correct them, and removing annotators removes one safeguard against this. Our results should therefore be read as a study of learning signals on research benchmarks, not as evidence that the resulting models are fit for consequential decisions about individuals. Prompt. At evaluation, we hold the task content fixed across methods: every model receives the same input sample, dataset question, and, for closed-label tasks, the same answer options. To avoid introducing an evaluation-time format shift, we preserve the response format used by each method during training. Thus, methods may differ only in the requested output serialization (e.g., \boxed{} 

or answer/justification tags), while the task instruction, input evidence, question, and answer space remain unchanged. Figure 4 shows the common evaluation content and indicates where the method-specific response-format instruction is inserted. The user prompt contains the dataset’s question text with the <audio> line removed. For the two closed-label tasks, we append an additional line listing the task’s answer phrases in a fixed order. Evaluation Inference. For evaluation on the validation and test splits, we use greedy decoding via vLLM (temperature 0, no top-k truncation, no penalties) for validation and evaluation. One response per row and at most 512 new tokens. The prompt budget is 4,096 tokens. 

Answer extraction. Each method’s response is parsed according to the response format used during its training, after which the extracted task answer is evaluated identically across methods. For closed-label tasks, the extracted answer must equal one of the available task labels; we do not map synonyms or other alternatives to the label space. For free-text tasks, the extracted answer is passed to the same LLM-based evaluator. If the expected structured answer field cannot be extracted, we fall back to the response itself: for closed-label tasks, we check the full response and its final non-empty line for an available option, while for free-text tasks the full response is passed to the evaluator. Responses for which no valid task answer can be extracted are scored as incorrect. 13 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 

Consistency and Fairness of Baselines. Training prompts follow the requirements of each method, since several baselines rely on method-specific response structures during reward construction (Appendix D). At evaluation, we therefore keep the benchmark input, question, answer space, decoding configuration, and scoring procedure fixed across methods while retaining the response format used during each method’s training. The resulting responses are normalized to the underlying task answer before scoring. This avoids evaluating a method under an unfamiliar response schema while ensuring that the information available for solving each benchmark is identical across methods. 

The difference in prompts has little effect at evaluation . Every training prompt asks for exactly one of the task’s options, so the label vocabulary is shared, and the parser does not require the box: an unboxed response is scanned for an option or, on the free-text tasks, passed to the judge in full. Parse failures on the test split are accordingly negligible for every method, including the base model: none on IntentQA and PrefEval, exactly one row of 4,659 on MOSEI, and one to three rows of 224 on UR-FUNNY (at most 1.4%), with the same pattern on the validation split. The training format therefore does not worsen the baseline during evaluation. Video-Zero and RLAC additionally train at 2 fps with up to 32 frames to follow the method’s token budget; every other method trains at the evaluation setting of 1 fps and up to eight frames. For these reasons, the prompt differences are unlikely to account for the reported differences between the methods. The more reasonable factor would lie in the effectiveness (reward mechanism) of the methods. 

Compute. All experiments for the baselines and methods run on three H200-class GPUs. 

Checkpoint selection. For all methods, we follow the checkpoint-selection protocol used in es-tablished self-rewarding work (Yuan et al., 2024) and apply the same selection procedure across methods. As our focus is on learning without ground-truth training labels, validation labels are used only for checkpoint selection and are not used for training or reward construction. Every method, including RLCE, is selected in the same way: the checkpoint with the best score on the task’s validation split, computed with the task’s metric (Table 5), is the one evaluated on the full test split. MOSEI-Sentiment and UR-FUNNY use HBA’s validation splits, IntentQA the validation split from the official release 1 (2,044 questions), and PrefEval the validation split we build for each subtask (Appendix A). How often a checkpoint is validated follows each method’s own training schedule, which we preserve strictly from the original work (Appendix D). VisPlay and Video-Zero report a fixed iteration in their papers and do not validate intermediate checkpoints; we validate after every iteration and select on validation, which matches or improves on their published training recipe, and we also report the fixed-iteration checkpoints (Tables 11 and 13). This is to ensure that the methods that do not validate on checkpoints in their original work are not disadvantaged. RLCE is validated every second training cycle (Appendix C.2); the rubric, self-consistency and LLM-judge baselines every twelve optimizer steps; and the co-evolution baselines (VisPlay, Video-Zero, RLAC) after every iteration or round. Training ends at the method’s own budget or when the validation score stops improving. For VisPlay and Video-Zero, whose papers report a fixed iteration, selecting on validation matches or improves on the published protocol; Tables 11 and 13 also report the fixed-iteration checkpoints.

IntentQA. This task asks for the intention behind an action in a video. HBA’s question text asks for a one-sentence response, followed by the question (Figure 5). The reference answers are short phrases (median three words). The clip is the only input; there is no transcript. We score the open-ended responses with an LLM judge using HBA’s grading instruction and (LLM judge) prompt. The judge is gpt-5-nano through the OpenAI chat-completions API, called with a JSON schema for the verdict and a 2,048-token completion budget using the API’s defaults sampling parameters. Figure 6 shows the two user messages sent to the judge; there is no system message. The question sent to the judge is the text after Question: , the candidate is the extracted answer, and the reference is the row’s gold answer. The verdict is parsed as JSON and its correct field is read. A failed call or an unparseable reply is retried up to four attempts and a row that still has no verdict after these tries counts as incorrect. In our runs, all rows manage to receive a valid verdict. The final computed score is accuracy: the fraction of the 2,134 rows ruled correct by the judge. 

PrefEval. This task tests whether a model is able to follow the preferences of the user stated in an earlier conversation. A preference is introduced in one of three forms (we also denote these as subtasks of PrefEval): explicit , where the user states it outright; choice , where the user picks it from options the assistant offered; and persona , where the preference is implied or subtly revealed in a conversation. Unrelated conversations are inserted between the preference and the final question. The model is asked to answer that question in a way that follows the user’s preferences. We build our own splits from the released benchmark and score it with the PrefEval’s rubric. This task contains 1,000 preference–question pairs across 20 topics, with the same question posed in the three forms. For each pair and form, the conversation is the form’s preference-establishing turns (two turns for explicit, where the assistant’s acknowledgment is a fixed sentence; four for choice; and the same persona conversation in the benchmark for the persona subtask). This is followed by five to ten padding of turn-pairs sampled with a fixed seed from the benchmark’s own inter-turn corpus (pairs of 60 to 1,200 characters with the entire conversation capped at 13,000 characters). We provide the instructions and questions (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10) for judge checks. While the benchmark inserts the same padding into every example; we sample it per example so that it does not become a memorizable context during training. The gold answer of a row is its preference sentence. Because PrefEval does not provide fixed train, validation, and test splits, we split preference-question pairs 70/10/20 into train, validation and test, stratified within each of its 20 topics, with one decision per pair shared by all three forms. This produces 701/100/199 rows to train, validate, and test for each subtask in PrefEval. Following PrefEval’s procedure to score the task, we use the four binary checks in the benchmark’s released code. Each check is one user message to gpt-5-nano , with no system message with the default sampling parameters, the completion budget is 4,096 tokens, raised to 8,192 and then 16,384 on retries (up to four attempts in total). The checks see only the question (the text after Question: 

in the user turn), the candidate response and the gold preference. Per row, the checks run in the following order: the Acknowledge check asks whether the response mentions a user preference and quotes the sentence that states it; the Hallucination check runs only when acknowledge returned Yes with a non-empty quote, and asks whether the quoted restatement differs from or contradicts the gold preference; the Violation asks whether the response recommends something that contradicts the preference without acknowledging it; the Helpful check asks whether the response gives a substantive answer. Verdicts are extracted from the <answer> tag. We use all the exact prompts, including each binary check, provided in the released code 2 for PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025). After the checks, a row can carry one of four error types: unhelpful (helpful is No); preference-unaware violation (helpful is Yes, violation is Yes, and acknowledge is No); hallucinated-preference violation (helpful, violation, acknowledge, and hallucination are all Yes); or inconsistent (helpful, violation, and acknowledge are Yes, while hallucination is No). The latter denotes a response that correctly acknowledges the user’s preference but nevertheless violates it. A response is correct when it carries none of these error types. Thus, acknowledgement and hallucination help distinguish the source of a preference violation, while an incorrect restatement of the preference without a violation

## D BASELINE IMPLEMENTATION 

In this section, we detail the implementation for each baseline. We adapted five current methods for our baselines: Video-Zero (Zhang et al., 2026a), VisPlay (He et al., 2025), Evolving Rubrics (RLER) (Shao et al., 2025), Static rubrics (Rubrics as Rewards, RaR) (Gunjal et al., 2026), RLAC Adversarial Critic Wu et al. (2026), Universal Self Consistency (USC) (Chen et al., 2023), Policy LLM-Judge (as adapted from Self Rewarding Language Models (Yuan et al., 2024)). These methods were chosen to be comparable since they share this notion of learning with unlabeled training data. Appendix B detail the tables for the overall performance of the baselines reported as the mean and its corresponding standard deviation over three runs. D.1 UNIVERSAL SELF -C ONSISTENCY 

We implement Universal Self-Consistency (USC) following Chen et al. (2023), which determines the preferred answer from agreement among the policy’s own sampled responses rather than by evaluating those responses against external supervision. USC operates on the same rollout groups used for RL training and receives no ground-truth annotations. For closed-set tasks, where rollout answers can be aggregated directly, we compute the vote count over all parseable predictions and select the unique most frequent answer. Rollouts matching the selected answer receive reward 1 and all others receive reward 0. If the highest vote count is tied, the group receives no reward, since the rollouts do not determine a unique consensus answer. No additional model generation is required in this setting. For free-form tasks, exact-match voting is insufficient because responses with the same underlying answer may differ in surface form. Following the USC procedure of Chen et al. (2023), we therefore provide the question and all parseable responses in the rollout group to the frozen LLM and ask it to select the response most consistent with the majority of the candidates. The selector receives only the question and candidate responses; the underlying multimodal context, preference evidence, answer options, and ground-truth answer are not included. We use one greedy selection per rollout group. All rollouts whose response exactly matches the selected response receive reward 1, while the remaining rollouts receive reward 0. Thus, USC derives its training signal solely from agreement among the policy’s current rollouts. We provide the prompt that we utilized in Figure 15. USC uses the same RL training setup as RLCE, including the model initialization, rollout and data budgets, optimization and sampling hyperparameters, KL regularization, and auxiliary format reward. Only the task reward differs. USC derives it from agreement among the policy’s sampled responses.

In Self-Rewarding Language Models, the policy itself acts as an LLM-as-a-Judge: candidate responses are evaluated individually and assigned scalar rewards, which are subsequently 28 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 used to construct preference supervision. For our case, we utilize a frozen policy of the base Qwen3.5-9B model. Evaluating individual responses pointwise is also a standard LLM-as-a-Judge formulation (Zheng et al., 2023). We retain this direct self-judging setup while adapting the judgment to our task setting and binary GRPO reward. For closed-set tasks, each candidate answer is judged independently. The frozen policy receives the question, the available input evidence, and a single proposed answer, and is asked whether that answer is correct. The judge is not shown the remaining answer options. A parsed “Yes” assigns reward 1 to rollouts producing that answer; all other outcomes receive reward 0. Each distinct option is judged once per example and the resulting verdict is shared by all rollouts predicting that option. This pointwise construction follows the single-response LLM-as-a-Judge setup. For free-form tasks, we apply the same pointwise judgment to each distinct rollout response. The frozen policy receives the question, available input evidence, and proposed response and judges whether the response is correct. Duplicate responses are deduplicated before evaluation so that identical answers receive identical rewards. A parsed “Yes” receives reward 1; otherwise the reward is 0. For both closed-set and free-form tasks, the judge is a frozen copy of the base model (Qwen3.5-9B), which is the same as the validator in RLCE, and ground-truth training annotations are not provided during reward construction. This keeps the comparison focused on the source of the training signal: direct policy judgment for Policy LLM-Judge, agreement among policy rollouts for USC, and comparative evidence testing for RLCE. Policy LLM-Judge uses the same RL training setup as RLCE, including the model initialization, rollout and data budgets, optimization and sampling hyperparameters, KL regularization, and auxiliary format reward. Only the task reward differs. Policy LLM-Judge derives it from pointwise judgments by the frozen base policy.

Limitations of rubric rewards. In our experiments, we observed several limitations of rubric rewards, which motivate the use of evidence in RLCE. We observe that the Rubric Generator can occasionally produce imperfect or conflicting criteria in both the persistent and active rubric sets. Such inconsistencies can reduce the usefulness of the resulting GRPO signal. Figure 17 shows examples of conflicting criteria, with the corresponding reward behavior illustrated in Figure 18. When opposing criteria assign similar rewards to competing 33 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 responses, the resulting relative advantages can be reduced or neutralized. We refer to this behavior as criterion collapse (Figure 18). One possible explanation is the difference in task structure. RLER was introduced in DR Tulu for deep-research settings, where responses are substantially longer and may support a broader range of useful evaluation criteria. In our setting, outputs are comparatively short, typically consisting of a label and one to three sentences of evidence, which may provide fewer dimensions along which evolving rubrics can differentiate candidate responses. This may make criterion generation less informative or more susceptible to overlap and conflict. Static rubrics avoid changes in the active criterion set during training, but can still inherit limitations from the initially generated criteria. These observations motivate RLCE’s use of evidence derived locally from the observed input and the current candidate answers, rather than relying on an expanding set of task-level criteria.

D.5 VIDEO -Z ERO 

This section details how we implement the Video-Zero baseline. The method trains two co-evolving models, a Questioner and a Solver, both initialized from the same backbone (Qwen 3.5 9B), se-quentially over up to five iterations. The Questioner first learns to write questions about temporally localized evidence in unlabelled video clips, scored by the frozen Solver from the previous iteration (or the base model at initialization). For tasks without video clips (e.g., PrefEval), the Questioner instead writes questions about the transcript alone, and the parts of the method that depend on video are switched off. After training, the Questioner is then frozen to generate a new set of questions, which are pseudo-labelled by the same frozen Solver and used to train the next Solver. The training loop resembles St → Qt+1 → Dt+1 → St+1 , where t is the current iteration (starting from 0), St

the Solver, Qt the Questioner, and Dt the questions generated by Qt to train the Solver. Gold labels are not used during training. 

Training. We run one pipeline per task. Each run draws 600 clips at random from the task’s training split (seed 0) and reuses the same 600 clips in every iteration following the paper’s training recipe; This is standardized for both Questioner training (line 3) and for generating questions for the solver (line 4). Each sample carries the unlabelled video clip and the transcript (wherever applicable). The PrefEval tasks have no video, so the pipeline runs on the transcript alone: the Questioner template asks for a question about the transcript and outputs only the question and answer tags, the Solver template says Read the transcript and drops the grounding tag, and the evidence term of the Questioner reward is switched off (Figure D.5). The PrefEval transcript is the user-assistant conversation rather than a single speaker’s line. The answer space is set per task: a closed answer space constrains the Questioner’s proposed answer and the pseudo-label vote to the task’s labels (UR-FUNNY, MOSEI), while IntentQA and PrefEval use open-ended free text. After each iteration (lines 3–6) we save the Solver checkpoint and score it on the full validation split of the task with the same settings as the test-split evaluation. The checkpoint replaces the current best if it strictly improves on the best validation score so far. A run stops early when the validation score has not improved for two iterations in a row after the first, and otherwise runs the full five iterations. The best validation checkpoint is selected for evaluation on the test set of each task.

Ensuring a fair implementation for Video-Zero. In Video-Zero, the authors released the inference and evaluation code. We reconstruct training from their paper and re-use their training parameters. The reconstruction follows the paper’s Eqs. 1–6 and Table 6 term by term. The two terms the paper does not fully specify ( sevent and stemp ) are documented above, and the full training code will be released with ours so that our reconstruction can be verified. We also re-use the paper’s five-iteration schedule and its corpus size of 600 clips, drawn from the training split and fixed across the training iterations. The paper’s own scaling ablation claims that 600, 1000, and 2000 clips produce near-identical results. Where we deviate from the original work, we do so in Video-Zero’s favour. We give the transcripts to both roles (the Questioner and Solver), since several of our tasks depend on what is said. On the closed-label tasks we let the Solver answer in the label space and make the learnability frontier K-aware. That is, we adjust the frontier based on K classes. The motivation for this change was to adjust the difficult according to the number of classes for pseudo-labelling— with K = 3 

the paper’s fixed window would pass almost every noisy question. D.6 RLAC This method uses the same backbone model (e.g., Qwen 3.5 9B) for three roles: a Generator to be trained; a Critic, also trained, that attacks the Generator’s answers; and a frozen Validator that adjudicates between them. Unlike the other three baselines, both players are optimized. Their rewards are inverted, so the Critic is rewarded when the Generator is penalized, and the verifier adapts to the policy instead of staying fixed. The original method targets free-form factual generation and code, where verification is grounded in Wikipedia through FactScore or in executing a reference test case. We adapt it to social understanding tasks, where neither is available. Each round has two GRPO phases over the same clip corpus. In the Generator phase, the Critic and Validator are frozen and every Generator rollout is attacked. The Critic reads the clip, the question and the response, and returns the single claim the response most likely gets wrong. In the Critic phase the Generator is frozen and the Critic trains on pairs harvested from the previous phase. The loop is 

Ct → Gt+1 → Pt+1 → Ct+1 , where t is the round, Gt the Generator, Ct the Critic, and Pt the pairs harvested to train it. We do not include the gold label anywhere in this pipeline.
