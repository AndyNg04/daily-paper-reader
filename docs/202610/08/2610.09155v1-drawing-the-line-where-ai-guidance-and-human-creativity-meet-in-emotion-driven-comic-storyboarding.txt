Abstract—While generative AI models can produce visually
faithful artwork, they often fall short in conveying emotional
authenticity—a key driver of human expression. In visual story-
telling, particularly comic storyboarding, this gap becomes pro-
nounced: effective storyboards require both technical knowledge
(e.g., anatomical accuracy or scenic composition) and emotional
insight (from lived experience). We explore how human-AI
collaboration can support emotion-driven creativity, where the
user’s feelings guide generation and emotional resonance is the
goal. We present EMOTOON, a technology probe that helps
non-professional artists generate sketch-like storyboards and
iterate on visual ideas. In a controlled study with N = 25
participants, we find that AI assistance significantly improves
emotional expression, aesthetic quality, and exploration, but
reduces users’ creative ownership. Our findings offer broader
insights for human-AI co-creation in the domain of comic
storytelling, emphasizing balance between output quality and
user freedom, and raising new challenges for image generation
models in this domain.

I. INTRODUCTION
With current advances in generative artificial intelligence
(AI), models are capable of outputting highly realistic images
and videos in a variety of artistic styles. However, many artists
question the authenticity of AI-generated art in being able
to convey the human experience [1]. In particular, empirical
studies find that AI-generated art evokes less emotion and
narrative than human-made art [2]. Foundational creativity
research repeatedly observes that centering emotions yields
more creative outcomes, highlighting that emotion is both
a generative and evaluative component of creativity—artists
draw on their own emotions to create art, and viewers as
well as artists themselves appreciate art for the ways in
which a piece emotionally resonates [3], [4]. While models

may excel in knowledge-intensive tasks, such as anatomical
accuracy when generating a character [5], [6], they cannot
replace or replicate the rich array of emotions in people’s lived
experiences.
A central challenge for AI in the creative domain is to un-
derstand the tension between technical sophistication of gener-
ative models and emotional authenticity of human storytelling,
such that human-AI co-creation best leverages the “superpow-
ers” of both people and AI systems. In the human-computer
interaction (HCI) community, many researchers have identified
human-AI collaboration as an opportunity to help people co-
create art in the storyboarding process [7], [8], but few have
explicitly explored how AI systems can support emotion-
driven storytelling. We define emotion-driven creativity as a
process where the user’s feelings guide the creative generation
(input), and emotional resonance becomes a key goal of the
output [4]. In this work, we explore how generative AI tools
can be used to augment emotional expression in comic
storyboarding, using a technology probe and empirical
study to understand the dichotomy between providing
enough technical guidance to non-professional artists and
preserving human emotional authenticity.
We scope our work specifically to non-professional artist
participants as this population stands to benefit the most from
AI-assisted visual storytelling. Non-experts frequently possess
rich, emotionally resonant lived experiences and narratives
they wish to express, yet they typically lack the formal tech-
nical vocabulary, execution skills, or training in visual design
necessary to translate those feelings into robust visual assets.
Focusing on this user demographic allows us to investigate
how human-AI co-creation can make emotionally driven visual
storytelling highly accessible to everyday creators.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Human Perceptions on Creative AI
As AI increasingly permeates creative domains, its use
in fields such as painting [9]–[11] and music [12], [13]
has attracted growing attention. These tools enable creators
to explore new forms of expression that blend traditional
artistic practices with computational capabilities, and some
observers perceive AI-generated works as approaching human-
level artistry [14]. At the same time, evolving technologies also
reshape cultural understandings of art [11], and many artists
remain hesitant to fully embrace AI systems. Interviews with
artists reveal concerns that AI art relies on unconsented train-
ing data, lacks authenticity, and misses the human creativity
that defines artistic practice [1].
The broader public expresses similar skepticism [15]–[18].
People often rate AI-generated artworks lower than human-
made ones even when unable to distinguish between them [16].
When images are labeled as “AI-made,” viewers perceive them
as requiring less skill, talent, and creativity, and assign them
lower value [17]. Correspondingly, many respondents remain
doubtful that AI can truly create art [9].
Despite these concerns, AI is increasingly viewed as a tool
for supporting human creativity. Acceptance of AI-assisted
creation has grown in recent years [19]. For example, a survey
by Adobe found that three quarters of artists in the US, UK,
Germany, and Japan would consider using AI as a creative
assistant, though primarily for supporting tasks such as editing
rather than core creative decisions [20]. Interviews similarly
indicate that some artists see generative AI as expanding
accessibility and enabling new forms of collaboration between
humans and machines [1].
B. Human-AI Co-Creation Interfaces
A growing body of work explores tools that support human
creativity across domains such as music [21], data science
[22], calligraphy [23], engineering [24], animation [25], design
ideation, and creative writing [26]. These systems extend
creators’ capabilities by combining AI strengths in efficiency
and large-scale knowledge with human intuition, emotion, and
personal experience [27].
Some systems position AI as an active creative collabora-
tor. For example, Drawing Apprentice contributes unexpected
sketches during collaborative drawing to spark new artistic
directions [28], while creative sketching systems encourage
reinterpretation and iterative idea exploration [29], [30]. Other
tools instead scaffold technical skills or reduce labor, such
as ShadowDraw, which displays reference images during
sketching [31], assistive painting tools that manage visual
detail like color [32], or automation systems like FlatMagic
that perform repetitive drawing tasks [33].
AI has also been explored as a co-creative partner in
storytelling. Systems such as Talebrush visualize emotional
trajectories of narratives through interactive graphs [34], while
StoryDrawer transforms children’s stories into illustrative
scenes [7]. More recent work explores end-to-end AI-assisted
creative pipelines that lower the barrier to producing complex

visual stories [35]. However, while AI can support every-

[36]. This highlights co-creativity as a promising paradigm
that leverages complementary strengths of humans and AI
[27], [37]. Despite this growing ecosystem, relatively little

expressive visual storytelling, particularly for non-expert users.
We therefore turn to literature on technological support for
emotional visual expression.
C. Technology for Supporting Emotional Visual Expression
Emotion is central to both artistic creation and audience
engagement [3], [4], yet conveying emotion visually remains
challenging even for experienced artists [38]. Prior systems
have explored computational approaches to mapping emotion
to visual forms, though many focus on abstract or non-
figurative representations [39]–[41]. While these approaches
demonstrate how computational methods can encode affect,
they do not address narrative techniques such as character
gesture, camera framing, or scene composition that are central
to emotional storytelling in comics [42]–[44].
One notable exception is EmoG, an AI-assisted sketching
tool that generates emotional facial expressions for a drawn
character [8]. However, its scope is limited to individual faces
and the six-class basic emotion model, without incorporating
narrative context or broader visual strategies such as body
language, staging, or composition [8], [45]–[48].
In contrast to systems that focus primarily on emo-
tion recognition, EMOTOON aligns with affective loop and
emotion-as-interaction perspectives [3], treating user affect as
a generative input rather than a recognition target. We address
gaps with prior works through EMOTOON, a technology probe
that explores AI-assisted comic storyboarding for conveying
complex emotions in figurative form. Our system provides
suggestions that help non-expert creators translate emotional
narrative intent into visual panels, and our user study evalu-
ates its impact on (1) emotional expressiveness, (2) aesthetic
quality, (3) perceived creativity and ownership, and (4) ease
of learning storyboarding.
III. EMOTOON TECHNOLOGY PROBE DESIGN
To empirically investigate users’ creative expression during
AI-assisted comic storyboarding, we first introduce the design
of EMOTOON, a fully-functioning technology probe that is
available to users through a web application. EMOTOON is a
tool intended to guide the comic storyboarding process for
non-experts. Our system takes in character descriptions, a
comic script divided by panel, and an intended feeling and
takeaway of the story (all as text inputs), and generates one
storyboard panel per input panel. This probe was developed to
help us better understand how AI assistance affects emotional
expression in co-created comic storyboards.
A. Visual Design Elements for Emotion Expression in Story-
boarding
“As the old proverb says, ‘A picture is worth a
thousand words.’ If the storyboard image can tell the
story, then the writing of dialogues is less important”
[46].
It is well known that design elements can influence the way
art is perceived [49]. We begin the design of our technology
probe by drawing on visual design theories known to convey
emotions in the storyboarding process, focusing specifically on
visual elements within the comic panel. Through our review,
we identify two key storyboard elements specifically tied to
emotional expression: (1) composition [43], [45] and (2) facial
and gestural character expression [8], [44], [46]–[48].
Composition and character expressions have been studied
extensively through empirical studies conducted on subjects’
emotional responses to images [50]–[52]. Studies have shown
that emotions are aroused by shapes present in images, finding
that people felt higher valence emotions with rounder shapes
and more simplistic image compositions [45] and composi-
tional elements like harsh, sharp lines correlate directly to
emotional effects like despair or sadness [51]. In our study,
we define effective composition as the alignment between the
arrangement of visual elements within a storyboard panel
and the emotions that are conveyed.
When characters exhibit clear and relatable emotional ex-
pressions, it enhances readers’ empathy and engagement with

the story [44]. For example, animators will often push the
gesture or facial expression of characters to efficiently convey
an emotion. Our definition of effective character expression
focuses on how clearly facial expressions or gestures com-
municate the emotional states and intentions of characters.
B. EmoToon Design
a) Emotion-driven input: We include a “feeling and
takeaway” input (asking the author to explicitly describe the
intended emotion and takeaway they hope one would get)
to assist with the emotional element and anchors the entire
generation pipeline in the artist’s expressive intent. Storyboard-
ing literature emphasizes that the emotional intent of a scene
should guide the script and visual choices, meaning that the
feeling to be conveyed is typically clarified before detailed
storyboards are drawn [53], a workflow mirrored by our probe.
b) Idea Regeneration: We added a panel regeneration
function to support the exploratory and iterative nature of
storyboarding, where artists frequently experiment and revise
[54].
c) EmoToon System Pipeline: We construct the following
steps in the EMOTOON pipeline:
1) Generating a DALL-E prompt with GPT-4: Based on the
user’s comic script, character descriptions, and intended
emotion, we use a detailed prompt with GPT-4 to
generate the textual DALL-E Prompt, including specific
language about the camera angle, panel composition,
character expressions, and character poses—in addi-
tion to the user-inputted emotion.
2) Generating a Manga-style image with DALL-E: We
combine the generated DALL-E Prompt with the user-
inputted data (character descriptions, comic script, and
feeling and takeaway) as part of a larger image gener-
ation prompt, which is then fed into DALL-E to obtain
one manga-style image representing each panel of the
user’s comic script.
3) Converting Manga-style image to Storyboard Panel with
the Image-to-Line-Drawings model: We run the manga-
style images outputted by GPT-4 through an Image-to-
Line-Drawings model accessed via huggingface1.

IV. USER STUDY
To understand how people balance AI guidance with their
own creativity in constructing personal storyboards, we con-
duct a user study with N
= 25 participants. Our main
experimental manipulation is the use of EMOTOON to guide
storyboard drawing versus drawing completely FREEHAND.
A. Participants
We recruited N = 25 participants via mailing lists, focusing
on adults with an interest in art. The sample covered a range
of drawing and storytelling experience levels, but importantly
none were professional artists. Basic demographics and self-
reported art experience of our storytellers are summarized in
Table I. All participants were compensated $20 for completing
the study, which involved both creating storyboards and later
evaluating others’ storyboards.
B. Study Design and Procedure
Pre-Intervention. After providing consent and demo-
graphic information, participants joined a synchronous Zoom
session. A researcher introduced the concept of storyboarding
and demonstrated how to use the tool. Participants were then
asked to write two short personal stories, each specifying
a desired feeling and takeaway, and to create a 4-panel
storyboard depicting an important moment in their life.
Study Conditions and Tasks. Participants completed two
conditions: EMOTOON and a FREEHAND baseline where they


drew the storyboard from imagination. Critically, participants
generated two completely separate narratives during the pre-
intervention phase, assigning one distinct story to the EMO-
TOON condition and the other distinct story to the FREEHAND
condition. The pairing of stories and the execution order of
conditions were fully counterbalanced and randomized across
participants to guarantee that no single story prompt was
repeated or illustrated across both workflows. We used a
FREEHAND condition to isolate the effect of the technology
probe. More complex workflows (e.g., reference searching)
were excluded because differences in prior experience and
existing AI-assisted retrieval tools could introduce confounds.
This study design prioritizes internal validity over ecological
realism, consistent with prior work on creativity support tools
[31], [55].
Post-Study. After each task, participants completed a sur-
vey, followed by a 15-minute semi-structured interview after
both conditions. In total, participants produced 50 storyboards
(two per participant).
C. Hypotheses
We designed our study to test the following hypotheses:
• [H1] Users feel that EMOTOON helps them better express
the feeling of the story than drawing FREEHAND.
• [H2] Users will be more satisfied with the aesthetic of
their final drawings when using EMOTOON vs drawing
FREEHAND.
• [H3] Users will not perceive significant differences in cre-
ative freedom between using EMOTOON and FREEHAND
drawing.
• [H4] EMOTOON lowers the learning curve for storyboard
drawing compared to FREEHAND.
D. Data Collection and Analysis
To answer H1 - H4, we specifically asked storytellers
to self-rate (on a Likert scale of 1-7) the expressiveness
of their storyboards (EMOTIONALLY EXPRESSIVE, and ART
AND STORY MATCH), aesthetic appeal (AESTHETIC, CHAR-
ACTER EXPRESSIONS, COMPOSITION), creativity (HELPFUL
FOR EXPLORATION, EASE OF EXPLORATION, FINAL STORY-
BOARD CREATIVE, FELT CREATIVE, DEGREE OF FREEDOM,
and OWNERSHIP), and learning curve of the tool (LEARNING,
EASE OF USE, and CONFIDENCE).
In addition to survey, interaction, and artifact data, we
also recorded and transcribed 15-minute interviews with
all participants after interacting with the tool. Our interview
questions included participants’ specific feedback regarding
the interactive components and features of the EmoToon user
interface (such as the panel regeneration mechanism), as well
as questions around how the tool aided their own emotional
expression process or impacted their self-perceived creativity.

coded each interview utterance using the finalized themes and

VI. BROADER DISCUSSION, LIMITATIONS, AND FUTURE
DIRECTIONS
Our study examined how an emotion-driven, AI-assisted
storyboarding tool can support non-expert creators in vi-
sualizing personal narratives. Overall, EMOTOON improved
perceived emotional expressiveness (H1 partially accepted)
and aesthetic quality (H2 accepted) relative to FREEHAND
drawing, and increased ease of use and confidence (H4 par-
tially accepted), but came with costs to perceived creativity and
ownership (H3 rejected). These results suggest a core trade-
off: emotion- and composition-rich references can help novices
externalize affect and produce more polished storyboards, yet
the same high-level structural proposals may anchor authorship
and reduce perceived agency.
A key contribution of EMOTOON is treating emotion as a
driver of the co-creative workflow rather than only an evalu-
ation target. Conditioning generation on a “feeling and take-
away” helped participants translate emotional intent into con-
crete visual choices spanning facial expression, gesture, and
composition, extending prior work that emphasizes character-
centric cues or single-image emotion transfer [8], [45]. In
storyboarding, however, emotion is constructed across framing
and panel progression, not only within a face [57]. Our find-
ings motivate systems that treat emotional goals as dynamic:
interfaces could support lightweight per-panel affect tags and
rapid adjustment when intentions shift, and use interaction
traces (e.g., repeated regeneration or rejection) as signals of
misalignment that prompt clarification in an iterative workflow
[58].
Comics and storyboards impose sequential constraints that
current co-creative paradigms often under-address. Participants
reported that AI could assist per-panel emotion but sometimes
disrupted emotional coherence across panels via abrupt tone
shifts or melodramatic “spikes.” This suggests future story-
board tools should reason about emotion sequentially, coupling
text, layout, and affect across panels, potentially enabled by
data-centric approaches using large-scale comic datasets with
artist consent [57], [59]. Interaction designs could visualize an
emotional arc and offer compositional alternatives (e.g., insert-
ing a wider interstitial shot) to smooth or intensify progression,
extending sentiment-arc ideas from text to visual narrative
[60]. More broadly, composition and shape design remain
undersupported despite being central levers of affect; tools
could provide “composition palettes” that let users explore
framing, panel rhythm, and spatial weight as an abstract form
of creativity support [38], [61].
Consistent with prior co-creation work, participants ex-
perienced tension between exploratory benefits and reduced
ownership [1], [17], [28], [62], [63]. Notably, even though
EMOTOON outputs were sketch-like references rather than
polished renders, users still reported lower ownership and free-
dom, suggesting that high-level structural decisions (com-
position, pose) may shape authorship perceptions as much
as surface-level polish. Discrete proposal-based interaction
may further amplify anchoring relative to more continuous
steering interfaces [62], raising risks of fixation and reduced
divergence [32], [64], [65]. Future work should compare AI-
generated references to conventional references (e.g., photos
or other artists’ work) and develop measures that better capture
ownership in visual, spatial creation beyond attribution by
“idea proportion” [63].
Finally, EMOTOON lowered barriers for novice creators by
increasing ease of use and confidence, aligning with “low floor,
high ceiling” creativity support [66]. However, participants
also questioned whether AI support translated into actual
learning versus outsourcing. Concretely, future systems could
offer “guided” modes with explanatory overlays and prompts
to modify references, and “minimal” modes that restrict AI
to thumbnails, composition alternatives, or mood boards for
experienced artists [1], [67]–[69]. Transparency cues that
mark AI-proposed elements may further support reflection and
attribution norms in co-creative workflows [1], [63].
VII. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented EMOTOON, a technology probe
based on visual design elements that influence emotion, in
order to empower non-expert users to create their own comic
storyboards. Our user study with 25 users of EMOTOON
showed that our system aided in self-perceived emotional
expression, aesthetic quality, creativity exploration, and in-
creased user confidence compared to drawing FREEHAND.
However, we also found that participants felt significantly
lower ownership and freedom when using AI assistance in
storyboard creation, and suggest future directions in varying
abstraction of artistic support provided by systems to increase
the user’s own sense of creative voice for this medium. Our
work paves future directions in understanding the strengths and
limitations of AI tools for empowering users with diverse art
and storytelling backgrounds to create emotionally impactful
storyboards.


ETHICAL IMPACT STATEMENT
Because our system engages with emotional storytelling
and generative media, several ethical considerations arise
regarding human subjects research, privacy, potential misuse
of generative technologies, and the limits of generalizing our
findings.
Firstly, the study involved participants creating storyboards
based on personal experiences and emotional narratives. The
research was conducted under institutional ethics oversight
(anonymized for review), and all participants provided in-
formed consent prior to participation. Participants were in-
formed that they could skip questions, change their story
topic, or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.
Because recalling personal experiences can sometimes involve
sensitive emotions, we emphasized that participants should
only share stories they felt comfortable discussing and avoided
prompts that required disclosure of highly sensitive or identi-
fying information.
The study generated several forms of potentially sensitive
data, including participant drawings, interaction logs, gener-
ated storyboard references, and interview transcripts. These
materials may indirectly reveal personal experiences or iden-
tities. To mitigate privacy risks, we limited data collection to
information necessary for analysis, removed identifying details
during transcription and reporting, and stored all data securely
with access restricted to the research team. Any artifacts
reproduced in publications are screened to avoid revealing
identifying information.
As the system relies on generative image models, there are
also broader societal concerns related to generative media.
While EMOTOON is designed as a creativity support tool for
ideation rather than photorealistic generation, similar technolo-
gies can be misused for deceptive media or non-consensual
depictions. Additionally, generative models may reflect biases
present in their training data, potentially reinforcing stereo-
typical portrayals of emotions, identities, or social roles. Our
work does not attempt to resolve these broader challenges but
highlights them as important considerations for future systems
that build on generative storytelling technologies.
Finally, we recognize that our participant sample and study
design limits the generalizability of our findings. Norms of
emotional expression, visual storytelling, and comic conven-
tions vary significantly across cultures, which could influence
how diverse populations interact with an emotion-driven gen-
eration tool. Additionally, choice of a FREEHAND drawing
baseline prioritizes internal validity by avoiding confounds
related to individual differences in reference search habits.
Consequently, our claims regarding EMOTOON’s benefits are
framed relative to unassisted drawing rather than typical
creative practice involving reference gathering, which may
under-represent existing artists’ diverse workflows. Future
work should incorporate external assessments of emotional
and compositional elements to thoroughly validate these self-
reports, as well as ensuring a diverse, cross-cultural participant
base in order to validate broader applicability.
