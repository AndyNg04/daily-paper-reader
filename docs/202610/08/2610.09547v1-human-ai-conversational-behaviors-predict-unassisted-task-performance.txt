Assistance from AI tools has supported and improved human performance across domains. However, recent research suggests that these immediate benefits may entail future costs, including diminished performance when AI assistance is no longer available. We study how human-AI interaction behaviors correlate with immediate and future unassisted task performance across two game-based problem-solving user studies ( 𝑛 = 139 and 𝑛 = 111), using a dialogue act framework adopted from a tutor-student dialogue taxonomy. In our studies, verbalizing thought processes correlates with higher unassisted outcomes, whereas directly requesting solutions correlates negatively. Similar to these participant-side patterns, assistant explanations of the current problem state are associated with better subsequent unassisted performance, whereas directly providing the next action is associated with worse performance. Qualitative and subtype analyses further show that ostensibly similar reasoning turns can elicit different assistance. Our findings suggest that preserving users’ cognitive participation in problem-solving may support performance beyond AI-assisted interaction. 1 Introduction 

Assistance from AI tools can substantially boost user performance while assistance is available, improving both output quality and efficiency across tasks [18, 27, 62, 67, 84]. However, recent work shows that these immediate gains may carry long-term costs. Growing evidence links AI over-reliance [65] to cognitive activity decline [49], reduced perceived idea ownership and confidence [10, 53], lower memory accuracy for human-AI collaboration artifacts [92], and worse exam scores despite more efficient homework completion [80]. Yet as AI becomes increasingly embedded in education and knowledge work [9, 28, 30, 32, 34], abandoning the technology altogether is neither realistic nor desirable. The challenge, then, is not whether to use AI, but how to enable interactions that preserve human agency, learning, and critical engagement while still capturing its benefits.

This distinction is increasingly reflected in research on AI-supported learning. Unconstrained AI access may improve performance during practice while hindering unassisted outcome [80]; in contrast, installing pedagogical guardrails reduces damage to independent learning [12]. Other work similarly finds benefits from pedagogically-informed AI systems that promote generative sense-making, solicit explanations, or proactively guide learners rather than only providing solutions [58]. Engaging general AI assistants through Socratic dialogue has likewise been linked to better learning [60]. Together, these results suggest that learning with AI depends not only on the information an assistant provides, but on how reasoning work is distributed between the user and the assistant. This echoes longstanding literature on self-explanation: students benefit when they actively articulate and reason through problems themselves [24, 25, 38]. Similarly, effective tutoring systems often scaffold students through questions, explanations, and hints

In these systems, however, the pedagogical policy is typically imposed in a top-down manner, with education experts involved in the design process. General-purpose AI assistants allow substantially more flexibility, as they impose fewer pedagogical constraints [81]: the same assistant can act as an explainer, evaluator, tutor, or solution provider depending in part on how the user engages with it. This motivates a complementary question to prior work on system-imposed scaffolding: when users interact freely with an assistant that permits multiple modes of assistance, which conversational behaviors track the retention of performance when AI support is no longer available? 

We take an interaction-centered, bottom-up approach, examining the conversational actions that emerge when users freely interact with a less pedagogically constrained AI assistant. This perspective draws on pragmatics and speech-act literature, which treats utterances as action units during communication in addition to carriers of information [6, 76]. Dialogue acts provide a structured representation of the communicative function of user utterances, allowing researchers to aggregate, compare, and analyze recurring conversational behaviors and interaction patterns [4, 31, 79, 86]. This lens lets us examine how conversational moves distribute reasoning work between user and assistant, and which interaction patterns correspond to later independent performance. In this work, we focus on understanding the human-AI interactions that best support unassisted near-transfer , in which unassisted performance is measured on unseen, similar tasks [68], as opposed to far-transfer, where learned skills are applied to radically different domains. For example, after solving board positions with AI support, participants in our studies must independently solve a new board position with similar motifs from the same type of game. Specifically, our research aims to answer the following questions: (1) Which participant conversational behaviors are associated with subsequent unassisted near-transfer performance? (2) How do participant behaviors shape assistant responses, and which assistant response behaviors are associated with subsequent unassisted near-transfer performance? Across two studies, we find that articulating one’s reasoning correlates positively with unassisted performance (𝜌 = +0.36 , 𝑝 < .001), whereas directly requesting solutions correlates negatively ( 𝜌 = −0.22 , 𝑝 = .019). Mirroring these participant-side results, assistant explanations of the current problem state show a positive relationship with subsequent unassisted performance ( 𝜌 = +0.227 , 𝑝 = .017), whereas providing the next action shows a negative one (𝜌 = −0.243 , 𝑝 = .010). We then present qualitative case studies of conversations with participants who differ in their assisted and transfer performance, revealing the nuanced subcategories of the thinking aloud speech act. 

Our work offers three core contributions. First, we connect conversational behaviors during AI-supported problem-solving with later near-transfer performance without assistance, linking patterns in human–AI interaction to educational theories of self-explanation and help-seeking. Those theories were developed for students working with instructional material or with tutors that constrain what help is given. We show that the same distinctions hold for adults using a general-purpose assistant that supplies answers on request. Second, we show that users’ conversational behavior is systematically related to the form of AI assistance they receive, highlighting dialogue as both a trace of cognitive participation and a means by which users shape assistance. Third, we provide both qualitative and quantitative subtype analyses that offer a fine-grained view of different forms of the thinking aloud dialogue act, demonstrating that it is not homogeneous: utterances given the same label can prompt meaningfully different assistant behaviors. We outline design implications and possible future learning scaffolds that could teach less proficient AI users interaction strategies that best support unassisted near-transfer. Overall, our findings deepen understanding of what may enable effective AI use going forward and what may need to be built in to promote these beneficial behaviors in future interactions.

2 Related Work 2.1 Conversational Behavior and Reliance in Human-LLM Interaction 

User idiosyncrasies – request styles, monitoring efforts, and delegation habits – are reflected through their interactions with LLMs, which in turn influence task success and conversation dynamics. Recent work examines how conversational behaviors shape human-AI collaboration outcomes, measured by user satisfaction [47] and objective task success [7, 88], while broader analyses characterize recurring roles and divisions of responsibility between humans and LLMs [22, 93]. How users position themselves relative to the model, these findings suggest, shapes the collaboration. A particularly important dimension of these interactions is how much cognitive labor is delegated to AI. Over-reliance and over-delegation, often shaped by AI-related attitudes and beliefs [15, 48], can lead users to accept incorrect LLM outputs without critical examination [78, 89], and behavioral traces such as copy-pasting, limited task comprehension, and accepting information despite hesitation can reveal such reliance during conversation [55]. Crucially, cognitive forcing functions can reduce it [19]. Reliance is thus enacted through the interaction itself, as users continuously decide what reasoning to retain and what work to delegate. A smaller body of work connects these dynamics to outcomes beyond immediate metrics. Furniturewala et al. analyze human-LLM conversations on socio-political issues, finding that changes in political knowledge and self-efficacy do not emerge from LLM explanation quality alone but rest upon interactions between engagement quality and user traits [35]; in StudyChat [60] and Brender et al. [17], conceptually oriented engagement was linked to better outcomes than solution-oriented use. Nevertheless, most conversational analyses focus on immediately measurable outcomes of assisted tasks, including task success, reliance, and perceived helpfulness. Work that does measure subsequent transfer has also largely examined students in educational settings. This leaves open the question of how naturally emerging interaction strategies relate to retained performance among broader user populations. We address this gap by examining how conversational behaviors during AI-assisted problem solving relate to what users can subsequently accomplish 

without AI assistance, and how those same behaviors shape the forms of assistance they receive. 

2.2 Cognitive Engagement and Help-Seeking in AI-Assisted Learning 

Learning outcomes have been empirically linked to learners’ cognitive engagement with the material. The ICAP framework [25] specifies four modes of engagement – Interactive, Constructive, Active, and Passive – with deeper learning attainable as learners progress from Passive to Interactive. One well-established form of constructive learning is self-explanation: eliciting learners to explain their own reasoning can improve understanding and assist transfer [24, 73, 85], and a meta-analysis of 64 studies finds that prompting self-explanation yields moderate learning improvements across instructional contexts, with learner-generated explanations sometimes outperforming instructional ones [14]. A complementary literature examines how learners seek assistance, distinguishing instrumental help-seeking , in which learners request only enough information to continue solving a problem themselves, from executive help-seeking ,in which they seek to have the problem or next step completed for them [2, 44, 61]. Although executive help can facilitate immediate task completion, it accompanies poorer subsequent learning [2, 43]. Intelligent tutoring systems are therefore often designed to preserve cognitive engagement [46, 63]: Cognitive Tutors that prompt students to explain their problem-solving steps yield greater conceptual understanding and transfer than otherwise similar tutoring [3], self-explanation of worked examples in algebra tutors can further improve conceptual and procedural learning [16], and conversational tutors use prompts, hints, and scaffolding to guide learners toward constructing answers themselves [38, 87].

3 Methods: User Study Design 

Across our between-subject studies (Table 1), participants completed game-based problem-solving tasks both with access to an AI assistant and independently. The two studies share common components and design considerations: task design desiderata, prompting-proficiency screener, AI assistant, and procedure. As we focus on near-transfer as the outcome, the assisted task should precede the unassisted task, and they must be similar, which warrants deliberate curation and design (Section 3.1). We included the prompting-proficiency screener (Section 3.2) as a quality control for participants: they should ideally possess the required skills to interact with the assistant productively. During the assisted task, the AI assistance was engineered to improve experimental control and avoid confounding our results with variations in assistant response correctness (Section 3.3). The full study procedure included additional surveys to assess engagement and experiences (see Section 3.4). Experimental differences are described in their respective sections. 

3.1 Task Design Desiderata 

Our goal is to study how conversational behavior during AI-assisted problem solving relates to subsequent performance without AI. This required a task environment that was both experimentally controlled and rich and challenging enough to elicit meaningful interaction. Prior work has used game-like settings to promote engagement and high-quality participation in online tasks [33, 50], making games a useful setting for studying repeated task performance. In particular, we sought game tasks that participants could learn quickly, limiting the influence of specialized domain knowledge; that were sufficiently challenging to create a genuine need for AI assistance; and whose outcomes could be scored objectively against a known solution. The tasks also needed to support closely matched, unseen variants so that we could measure near-transfer after assistance was removed. Finally, because our analysis depends on participants meaningfully engaging with the assistant, we favored interactive, interesting tasks over purely mechanical ones. We operationalized these requirements through games, specifically Connect Four and Othello. They are popular games with simple rules, yet still challenging because of the sheer number of possible board positions [42, 82]. Since they are both solved games (i.e., the optimal move can be calculated), the quality of each move can be objectively assessed. At the same time, different board positions can represent similar strategic motifs, allowing us to select assisted and unassisted puzzles that require related reasoning without sharing the same solution. We used end-game puzzles rather than complete games to standardize task duration and reduce variation across participants. Participants played a

E.g., for Connect Four, both puzzles ( 𝐶𝐹 𝐴 , 𝐶𝐹 𝑈 ) require reasoning about play in the central columns and for Othello ( 𝑂 𝐴 , 𝑂 𝑈 1, 𝑂 𝑈 2), all puzzles involve strategic play around X-squares and avoiding tempting but strategically suboptimal greedy moves. We pre-computed optimal strategies for the opponent using Minimax [83]. During each game, the computer opponent followed the corresponding optimal strategy. Pre-computation both reduced system latency and provided a consistent basis for evaluating participant performance. Using gameplay simulations, we scored each participant based on the number of optimal moves they made given the state of the board ( Board Score ). For Othello, we focus onthe disc count metric, the number of discs of a specific color on the board; a participant with more than 32 discs wins for our specific Othello board size (8 × 8). 

3.2 Prompt Creation Screener 

We targeted participants who were sufficiently competent at interacting with AI in order to maximize participants’ chances of utilizing their AI interactions to boost subsequent unassisted performance. Because inquiring about usage frequency prior to the study can bias participants, and Prolific’s pre-screening can be incomplete, we instead used 

prompt-construction ability as a proxy for competency level. Prior work has shown that AI usage does not directly predict AI literacy [13], and that self-assessment of AI prompting skills may be inaccurate [91]. Participants completed a prompt-creation task based on a fictional scenario (Appendix C) in which they were asked to formulate an instruction for an AI assistant. Those who scored at or above the screener threshold (median score 3 out of 4) qualified. Prompts were automatically evaluated by a panel of LLM-based judges. We validated these scores against human annotations and observed substantial agreement between the panel’s mean score and the human’s (ICC(2,1) = 0.787; Pearson’s 𝑟 = 0.829, 𝑝 < .001; Appendix C). 

3.3 AI Assistant Design 

We designed the AI assistant to isolate variation in the form of assistance from variation in its underlying solution quality. We instantiated our AI assistant as GPT-5.5 with low reasoning effort to leverage a high-capability model while minimizing latency. We prompted the assistant to act as a coach and included the primary gameplay principles in the prompt, while explicitly permitting it to supply answers directly on request (Appendix J). The assistant was therefore less pedagogically constrained than a tutoring system with a top-down pedagogical policy. To reduce potential errors in the assistant’s responses, we provided the assistant with the participant’s next optimal move and an image of the board . We made this design choice deliberately to reduce variation in assistant correctness, but the assistant could still deliver the same fixed solutions in different ways depending on participants’ conversation strategies. Holding the underlying solution constant, therefore, allows us to study how conversational behavior shapes the assistance users receive and how those interaction patterns relate to subsequent unassisted performance.

5 Replication Study Results: Othello 

The Connect Four study provides some initial insights into how users’ behavioral patterns during human-AI conversa-tions can relate to their unassisted performance. We examined whether these results would hold in a second game-based task and addressed some limitations in the discovery study. First, most participants engaged with the assistant only briefly, limiting the available signals about interaction patterns. Secondly, unassisted performance was measured using only one puzzle with discrete, narrow score ranges, undermining the robustness of our correlation results. Third, we lacked a completely unassisted baseline to establish how unassisted performance after AI assistance compares with that without it. Othello directly addresses the low-resolution outcome space of Connect Four by offering a wider range of possible game states and final scores. Played on an 8 × 8 grid, two players take turns placing black and white discs. Whenever a newly placed disc traps a continuous line of the opponent’s discs between two discs of the player’s own color, all of the trapped discs are flipped to that player’s color. The game ends when no legal moves remain, and the player with the most discs of their color on the board wins. For outcome, we measured the disc count metric, which is the number of Black pieces on the board (participants all played Black). Every game played to the end filled all 64 squares, so a count above 32 is a win, 32 is a draw, and the count ranges from 0 to 64. For the two unassisted puzzles, we average the two counts. To address the aforementioned limitations, we therefore required at least five participant turns with the assistant during the assisted Othello round, measured transfer across two subsequent unassisted puzzles, and added a baseline condition in which participants completed all three puzzles without AI assistance.

5.5 Additional Validation Under an External Taxonomy 

Our taxonomy was adopted from D’Mello et al. and then enriched with task-specific dialogue acts, which raises a question about the generalizability of our behavioral coding. We therefore re-described the same conversations under TurnStyle, a taxonomy built by other authors, for other tasks, without reference to our data [7]. 

Annotation. We labeled every participant turn in the Othello study (616 turns across 111 conversations) with a TurnStyle subcategory, then mapped subcategories to the six top-level categories, following the original procedure. Labels were produced with TurnStyle’s published annotation prompts, so the categories are applied as their authors defined them; we did not re-validate them against human labels on our data. We treat this section as a convergent check, not as an independent measurement. 

The same behavior correlates with assisted and transfer outcomes in opposite directions. Assisted performance on 𝑂 𝐴 

and unassisted performance on 𝑂 𝑈 1 and 𝑂 𝑈 2 were themselves negatively related ( 𝜌 = −0.307, 𝑝 = .002, 𝑛 = 97). Against these two outcomes, the same behavioral measures pointed in opposite directions (Table 7). Task management, in our case mapping to asking the assistant to supply the move, was the strongest single covariate of doing well when assisted, and a significant negative covariate of unassisted performance. This is a replication of our Solution Request result. Reasoned agreement reversed direction, negative on the assisted round and positive on transfer, while information request was negatively correlated with assisted performance and unrelated to transfer. Scored against the assisted outcome, TurnStyle’s inquiry-loop finding replicated more strongly in our data: participants whose conversation contained an Information-request loop won the assisted round far less often ( 𝑂𝑅 = 0.12, 95% CI 

[0.01 , 0.91 ], 𝑝 = .020, 𝑛 = 101). However, other prior findings pertaining more to open-ended collaboration did not appear here. We attribute these to task structure rather than to either framework, as we focused on puzzles with fixed solutions.

7 Think-Aloud Subtypes Elicit Assistant Acts Differently 

Our case study highlights distinctions that our taxonomy cannot express: P2 engages deeply with the game state, and P3 criticizes the assistant; both instances are categorized as Think Aloud . While we could propose constructs based on the observed differences in the chosen dialogues, we observed that participants with successful transfer did not always follow the same conversational recipe. As a result, we decided to leverage a more quantitative, unsupervised approach to cluster Think Aloud .We leveraged ConvoKit’s PromptTypeWrapper tool to cluster all 193 Think Aloud utterances [21, 90]. The tool groups conversational utterances by the type of reply they elicit in a deterministic manner, making it the ideal instrument for our purpose. Out of 193 utterances, 139 received a type assignment, as the remainder carried no usable dependency arcs. The unsupervised tool separated Think Aloud into interpretable clusters. We report 𝑘 = 4 here for readability; robustness across 𝑘 is addressed below. The largest cluster ( 𝑛 = 44) consists of turns that ask why something on the board happened or what would follow from a move: “I chose a 7 but it was an illegal move, why?” , “I played c8 but [W]hite is in the a8 corner now?” , “[W]hat if I did g2? [W]ould [W]hite have a legal move?” We refer to these as diagnostic turns. A second cluster of comparable size ( 𝑛 = 39) names a candidate square and asks the assistant to provide a selection: 

“[I’m] thinking c8, is that any good?” , “I’m considering G2. Does this work?” , “Is a7 good? [T]hat’s my initial thought” . We refer to these as verification turns. A third cluster ( 𝑛 = 41) reports board state or intention without posing a question at all ( “[I] can’t choose between b6 and a7” , “[I] think my next move should be a7” ), which we call narration , and a small fourth cluster ( 𝑛 = 15) delivers a verdict on a move, sometimes with a reason attached ( “g2 looks good because [W]hite can’t play the corner next” ), which we call evaluative .These subtypes differed sharply in what they drew from the assistant (Table 10). 8 Discussion 

Across our studies, we find that participants’ performance without AI assistance tracks the distribution of reasoning workload between the user and the assistant. Interactions in which users articulate their reasoning correspond to better unassisted performance, and more instances of direct requests for the next best moves to poorer transfer. These participant-side behaviors drive assistant responses accordingly: elaborating thought processes elicits more tutor-like behavior, while soliciting solutions results in direct answers. Finally, qualitative and subtype analyses reveal substantial heterogeneity within the aggregate Think Aloud category. We discuss our findings in the context of cognitive engagement and delegation, the need for users to steer AI assistants towards pedagogical roles, and the limits of using coarser-grained dialogue taxonomies. 

8.1 Thinking Aloud as Cognitive Participation during AI Assistance  

> Think Aloud

tracks better unassisted performance in both Connect Four and Othello studies, while  

> Solution Request

is coupled with worse unassisted performance in Othello. These two modes of interaction with AI are instances of retaining vs. delegating cognitive work. Think Aloud is consistent with , albeit broader than, 

constructive engagement in the ICAP framework [25] and self-explanation [14, 24], because although Think Aloud

Our work differs from existing self-explanation literature: traditional self-explanations are directed toward students themselves, whereas we include a responsive interlocutor. Meanwhile, Solution Request maps neatly to executive help-seeking [59, 61]. Existing research on help-seeking distinguishes between two primary modes of help-seeking: executive help-seeking, where students expect the problem to be solved for them, and instrumental help-seeking, where students request only the help required to complete the task on their own [44, 45, 59, 61]. Executive help-seeking is known to improve immediate progress while producing worse learning outcomes [2, 43]; instrumental help-seeking, we hypothesize, is more likely to support future unassisted performance. Solution Request , the act of requesting the next optimal move directly, transfers the agency for game state interpretation and action selection to the assistant and is therefore consistent with the definition of executive help-seeking. As a result, the user may fail to learn skills required to produce good performance when AI assistance is no longer available. We emphasize that our results do not indicate that productive human-AI collaboration must avoid executive help-seeking. In our Connect Four study, Solvers still invoke Solution Request , but their requests co-occur with Think Aloud . Likewise, Solution Request appears in one of our strongest Othello case-study conversations, although Think Aloud dominates. In sum, these patterns are consistent with transfer being stronger when users remain involved in constructing and monitoring their own representation of the problem. Simon and Newell describe this representation as a problem space : the states a solver can be in, the operators that move between them, and the tests that recognize a goal [77]. In these terms, Think Aloud has users search the problem space themselves, proposing operators and evaluating the states they lead to, whereas Solution Request hands operator selection to the assistant, so users can reach a good state without building the space that produced it. A new puzzle requires that space again, which may explain why Solution Request accompanies better assisted performance but not better transfer. Importantly, the user’s ability to monitor and build mental representations is not necessarily visible in their im-mediate assisted performance, since assisted performance does not always reliably indicate strong unassisted performance . This is validated through the negative correlation in our Othello study, and further demonstrated in Section 5.5, where we re-code our collected conversations using an external taxonomy developed to identify in-task human-LLM interaction dynamics. Awasthi et al. find that concrete task management is beneficial to task success, which is affirmed through the positive correlation between Solution Request and assisted performance [7]. However, the same behaviors that improve in-task performance accompany worse transfer. This means that curricula targeting human-AI collaboration performance may overlook the retention of performance without AI assistance. We recognize that our data does not rule out the possibility that Think Aloud is a symptom of participants who already attend closely to the board and would be inclined to extract learnable principles from the game regardless. The skill measures we hold from before the assistant was available do not explain the association. They are uncorrelated with Think Aloud , and adding them leaves its coefficient intact in both studies (Section 4.4, Appendix F.3). But these measures barely predict unassisted performance even in our no-AI Baseline. A null adjustment on weak proxies does not settle the question, so we read every result in this paper as an association between conversational behavior and later performance, not as an effect of that behavior. To firmly demonstrate that articulating reasoning is a learnable user strategy that enables productive AI interactions to translate into retention, future empirical work should test whether interventions that increase the prevalence of Think Aloud also improve unassisted performance. We outline design guidelines for enabling such interactions in Section 9.

8.2 Users Shape the Pedagogical Behavior of Less Constrained Assistants 

We observe a symmetry between participant-side and assistant-side behavioral patterns that track transfer. In our Othello trials, Board Report is positively correlated with transfer outcomes, while this relationship is negative for  

> Provide Correct Answer

. Think Aloud tends to evoke more tutor-like behaviors, whereas Solution Request draws out direct answer provision. Users’ conversational choices therefore actively shape subsequent assistance received. This relationship is further validated by our counterfactual rewriting experiments (Appendix H.2), which establish that minimally changing the form of a user’s request can causally alter the assistant’s response behavior. More broadly, these results suggest that an AI assistant can be steered toward explanation, prompting, or direct solution provision through users’ conversational choices. 

8.3 Think Aloud Collapses Multiple Interaction Strategies 

Although Think Aloud correlates with stronger unassisted performance across our studies, our case study and subtype analyses indicate that the same label does not reflect a single interaction strategy. Prior work has similarly identified different types of self-explanations, ranging from principle-based explanations to elaboration of the problem situation [72]. Indeed, we can separate Think Aloud utterances collected in our studies into interpretable clusters, and its different subtypes exhibit distinct behavior during human-AI interactions. With increasing granularity in our subtype analysis, we identify stable subtypes of Think Aloud : verification and diagnostic. Verification subtype turns often offer move candidates for evaluation, whereas diagnostic turns ask the assistant to reason about why an outcome occurred or what would follow from a move. These differences matter for the assistance elicited, since only verification turns have substantially higher odds of eliciting Provide Correct Answer .Therefore, two utterances can both satisfy our Think Aloud definition while allocating different parts of the reasoning process to the assistant. We do not find that these subtypes themselves robustly predict transfer, so we do not interpret them as identifying intrinsically better or worse forms of Think Aloud in our setting. Rather, they show that the aggregate label collapses interactionally distinct behaviors and that frequency alone does not fully characterize the distribution of reasoning within the conversation. 

9 Design Implications 

Our study indicates that conversational behaviors during human-AI interaction are correlated with user performance when the AI is removed. The implications below are therefore hypotheses to test, not prescriptions. If Think Aloud 

marks users who are already engaged rather than making them so, nudging other users to verbalize would add effort without adding retention. To establish whether these relationships are causal, future work should design interventions that teach users to adopt behaviors linked to better retained performance, either before or during interactions. We differentiate this type of intervention from system-side scaffolding [29, 56] because its purpose is to help users develop behavioral patterns that they can carry into future AI use even when such scaffolds are absent. We propose several possible avenues for designing such interventions. 

9.1 Redirecting Solution Request Toward Active Reasoning 

Systems can detect when the user is repeatedly soliciting solutions and then nudge the user toward more explicitly stating their reasoning [40]. Repeated, context-sensitive interventions may provide opportunities for users to practice 

> Manuscript submitted to ACM

Human-AI Conversational Behaviors Predict Unassisted Task Performance 23 reasoning-preserving interaction strategies, with prior work suggesting that repeated metacognitive questioning can produce effects in learners that persist after intervention removal [11]. Future systems could therefore adapt and eventually fade these interventions as users begin to exhibit the targeted behaviors independently. Alternatively, a rephrased prompt could be presented to the user, similar to automated prompt enrichment [8] and recent LLM-based prompt enhancement approaches [36, 74]. Such systems could expose the reformulation to the user so that the intervention also teaches reusable interaction strategies. This type of intervention can occur in the input field or through another interactive companion. It is important to note, however, that the presence of Think Aloud alone may not indicate sufficient cognitive involvement, as our quantitative subtype analysis reveals. Thus, interventions should not simply teach users to verbalize more reasoning. Rather, future systems could attend to what role that articulation plays and teach users interaction strategies that more effectively expose their current reasoning, uncertainty, or misconceptions. Given that our fine-grained analysis shows that Think Aloud encompasses distinct conversational behaviors, these nudges could attend to what role articulation plays in user reasoning, rather than simply increasing its frequency. In particular, verification-style Think Aloud can still elicit direct answers from the assistant, illustrating that two turns labeled as reasoning can allocate very different amounts of cognitive work to the user and the AI. Therefore, simply increasing the frequency of all Think Aloud utterances may not be sufficient, and greater care should be taken to encourage forms of articulation that expose users’ underlying reasoning, uncertainty, or misconceptions. 

9.2 Teaching Interaction Strategies That Persist Beyond Scaffolding 

Users can also receive interventions that help them adopt behaviors consistent with higher cognitive engagement independently. This parallels AI-literacy interventions: model-specific tutorials can improve reliance calibration [26], and scaffolded auditing can prompt more critical scrutiny of outputs [69]. In our setting, boosting could explicitly teach users to propose candidate solutions, explain their reasoning, and seek principles or feedback instead of immediately delegating the next step. Interactive boosting can let users practice these behaviors, while lighter-weight approaches can scale via contrasting examples [16] or short videos. Cao et al. showed that a lightweight AI-literacy intervention, consisting of five 3-minute videos explaining AI and demonstrating effective vs. problematic ChatGPT use, is capable of improving self-efficacy and knowledge of AI versus a time-matched control [20]. This suggests short, example-based instruction can scale practical interaction strategies. Building on this, short trainings could contrast ways users participate in problem solving – for instance, repeatedly asking “What should I do next?” versus proposing a solution, justifying it, and asking for critique – highlighting reusable strategies for retaining more of the reasoning process. 

9.3 Making Reasoning Delegation Visible Through Peer Comparison 

Building on our findings, future systems could help users reflect on how they distribute cognitive work with AI by comparing their conversational behavior with that of peers who demonstrate stronger subsequent unassisted performance. For example, an interface might visualize the proportion of a user’s turns containing Think Aloud , 

> Solution Request

, and other dialogue acts alongside the corresponding distributions among relevant peers. Such comparisons could make otherwise difficult-to-observe interaction patterns more salient, helping users recognize when they rely more heavily on direct solution seeking or contribute less of their own reasoning than users who retain stronger performance without assistance. Rather than prescribing a fixed “optimal” conversational composition, this

10 Limitations and Future Work 

Our work has several limitations. First, we only measure immediate near-transfer to related puzzles of the same game type rather than delayed retention or far transfer, and therefore do not claim durable learning outcomes. Second, Connect Four and Othello provide controlled tasks with objectively optimal solutions, but may not generalize to open-ended domains such as writing or programming, where optimality of assistant answers is not guaranteed. We also deliberately supplied the assistant with the optimal next move to reduce variation in response correctness; this improves experimental control but differs from realistic interactions with AI models. The participants in our studies have been filtered to those capable of producing high-quality prompts; therefore, any generalization to all AI users may be constrained. Finally, our dialogue acts capture observable conversational behavior, and the relationships between participant behavior and transfer remain observational. We had no validated pre-test of game skill or of general engagement. The available pre-assistance measures (one unaided move, its confidence rating, and self-rated skill) did not account for the Think Aloud association, but they are coarse, range-restricted by our screening, and weakly predictive of unassisted performance. A pre-test puzzle and a randomized manipulation of Think Aloud are the natural next steps. Future work should test these patterns across longer timescales, less constrained tasks, and different assistant policies. Additionally, interventions for participants should be designed according to these patterns and examined for effectiveness on improving unassisted performance. 

11 Conclusion 

AI assistance is usually judged by what users accomplish with AI. We ask a different question: what can they do once the AI is gone? Across two controlled problem-solving studies, we find that conversational behavior is meaningfully associated with subsequent independent performance, and that users’ conversational choices shape the kind of assistance they receive. Users who maintained cognitive participation during AI assistance performed better independently afterward, though our design cannot establish that this participation produced the better performance. As AI becomes embedded in everyday work, users should learn not only how to succeed with AI support, but also how to retain their ability to reason and perform independently without it.

C Prompt Creation Screener Details 

To filter participants based on their prompt construction capability, which is our proxy for Generative AI interaction literacy, we filter participants through a prompt creation task. Specifically, we present participants with the following scenario: 

• Julie posts cooking videos to 1.2M followers who love her quick, healthy recipes. 

• Over the next three years she wants to grow online and publish a cookbook (torn on traditional vs. self-publishing). 

• She works four days weekly, limited time and budget. 

We instruct participants to help Julie devise a concrete three-year plan. They can construct a single prompt to a state-of-the-art AI agent that must produce a deliverable. After constructing this prompt, the final prompt is sent to a panel of three LLM judges for a more reliable assessment [54]. The prompt quality is scored on a scale from 1 (poor) to 4 (excellent). The three LLM judges are GPT-5.4-mini 

with chain-of-thought reasoning, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct [39], and Claude-sonnet-5 . When selecting models, we aimed for low latency and decent agreement with humans. The final score of the prompt is aggregated by averaging the individual scores. See the rubric below: Score 1 (Poor / off-target): Vague or minimal; reads like a quick search query; omits most of the scenario. Score 2 (Developing): Conveys the gist with several goals/constraints but loosely; incomplete coverage and little structure. Score 3 (Proficient): Captures essentially all key scenario elements clearly; a complete, competent brief, even if plainly formatted. Score 4 (Excellent): Proficient PLUS deliberate prompt craft — explicit structure (sections/roles/output spec) and/or meta-instructions (self-check, clarifying questions, reasoning guidance) beyond merely restating the scenario.

H LLM Simulations H.1 Agentic Simulation of Full Participant Trajectories 

We aimed to increase the number of analyzable conversations by directly simulating participants using LLMs. To achieve sufficient diversity in the simulated behavior, we use models from multiple providers and sample diverse personas from Persona-Hub [37], as conditioning on personas has been shown to influence model behavior. Specifically, we first perform k-means clustering with 60 clusters to identify the main archetypes among the 1M personas, and then select the cluster centers as the representatives. The simulated participants are instantiated as agents with three different tools: view_board , play_move , and 

consult_assistant . Similar to Othello phase participants, the agent must interact with the AI assistant for 5 turns. All other conditions are held consistent for the simulations, from the specific puzzles to the scoring mechanisms. Furthermore, to make simulated utterances more human-like to elicit similar distributions in assistant utterances, we incorporate 14 in-context human utterance examples spanning different dialogue acts. The in-context examples increase the stylistic similarity between real and simulated utterances from 0.88 to 0.97, as measured by cosine similarity using StyleDistance embeddings [66]. In total, we produce 270 conversations across different persona-model-prompt combinations and 1347 turns. We report odds ratios for the assistant dialogue acts of interest, given that specific simulated participant acts are present in the previous turn (Table 18). Consistent with human participants, Solution Request strongly elicits Provide Correct Answer. Similarly, the temporal orientation correlation with Board Report reproduces ( 𝑂𝑅 = 6.940 , 𝑝 < .001). There are, unsurprisingly, still discrepancies between human and simulated behaviors: Think Aloud co-occurs with a higher rate of Provide Correct Answer rather than suppressing it, and the presence of Knowledge-Deficit Questions appears to be tied to Board Report. This likely stems from qualitative differences between human and simulated participants: LLMs are more likely to include specific board coordinates in their queries, and there are fewer single-act turns by simulated participants. The simulation results therefore reproduce the Solution Request and temporal-orientation patterns we discover in human participants, but not the Think Aloud pattern. Because full simulated

H.2 Controlled Replay of Participant Utterances through LLM Simulations 

The previous studies focusing on human-AI dialogue revealed correlational relationships between participants’ and assistants’ speech acts, but do not establish causal relationships between them. As clean manipulation of human behavior is challenging [51], we leverage LLM simulations. Rather than simulate complete participant trajectories [5, 41, 64], which produced interactions that differed from human conversations (Appendix H.1), we use controlled counterfactual replay at the utterance level. Our approach involves intervening at turn granularity while fixing the conversation history, board state, and the assistant . We perform targeted modifications of natural participant utterances occurring during gameplay to produce minimal pairs using gpt-5.6-luna . We focus on the following rewrites: (1) Solution Request ↔ Move Candidate Evaluation (Common Ground Question). (2) Retrospective ↔ Prospective. Examples of minimal pairs are in Table 19. We constrain our pool of participant utterances to those made during the game to ensure that all four types of utterances we study are plausible. Both members of the minimal pair are then presented to the assistant, and we observe the resulting differences. 

Rewriting a single participant utterance shifts the assistant’s next act in the predicted direction (Table 20). Re-orienting a prospective question toward the move just played raises Board Report from 16% to 94% ( +78 pp, 

𝑝 < .001). Transforming a Move Candidate Evaluation into a Solution Request raises Provide Correct Answer from 46% to 94% ( +48 pp, 𝑝 < .001); rewriting in the opposite direction lowers it from 94% to 74% ( −20 pp, 𝑝 < .001). Because the outcome is a paired binary indicator — whether the assistant’s next turn carries the target act, before and after the rewrite — significance is by exact McNemar test on the discordant pairs. Over the full set of human-sourced stimuli the manipulations are close to one-sided: re-orienting toward the move just played adds Board Report in 86 pairs and removes it in 1 ( 𝑛 = 131, 𝑝 < .001); rewriting a candidate evaluation as a solution request adds Provide Correct Answer in 155 pairs and removes it in 10 ( 𝑛 = 343, 𝑝 < .001); the reverse rewrite removes it in 98 pairs and adds it in 14 ( 𝑛 = 384, 

𝑝 < .001). We find that these causal relationships replicate when we replay the same stimuli (system prompt, conversation, and board state) across four assistant models from three providers (Table 21). The magnitude of changes given a dialogue act, however, is constrained by the model’s general tendency to respond with that dialogue act (Table 22).

Participant dialogue-act annotation — instructions 

Given an utterance from a user talking to an AI assistant while solving an Othello endgame puzzle, first split it into clauses, then classify each clause into the dialogue acts that apply per the taxonomy provided. Output all applicable acts. Disambiguating the question-type acts (these are easily confused — read carefully): - "Solution Request": an OPEN request for the assistant to supply the move/answer, with NO specific candidate move proposed by the user. E.g. "what’s the best move?", "what now?", "any advice?", "just tell me what to play". - "Common Ground Question": the user proposes a SPECIFIC candidate move (or their own read of the position) and asks the assistant to confirm/evaluate it. E.g. "is b7 good?", "was my last move good?", "would a8 be better?". - "Knowledge Deficit Question": asks about a concept, rule, or the board (e.g. threats), or clarifies something the assistant said — NOT a request for the move itself. E.g. "why not c8?", "are there any threats?", "what do you mean by that?". 

The taxonomy itself is passed as a separate input, one line per act, in the form DIALOGUE ACT: <name>, EXAMPLES: <examples> . Fourteen acts are supplied: ten student moves from Table I of D’Mello et al. ( Think Aloud , Conversational Acknowledgment , Knowledge Deficit Question , Misconception , Common Ground Question , Vague Answer , Partial Answer , Social Coordination Action , Metacomment , Read Aloud ), three tutor moves that a participant may adopt (Forced Choice , Repetition , Prompt ), and Solution Request , which we added because the published scheme has no student act for an open request that the assistant supply the answer. The in-context examples for the Table I acts are the original biology-tutoring ones; only Solution Request carries examples written for this setting. Assistant dialogue-act annotation — instructions (fine-grained scheme) 

Given a reply from an AI assistant helping a user solve an Othello endgame puzzle, first split it into clauses, then classify each clause into the tutor dialogue acts that apply. Output every act that applies. Assistant replies are long and usually carry several acts; do not stop at the first one. The four content acts matter most and are easiest to confuse. Decide them by asking: WOULD THIS SENTENCE STILL BE TRUE AND USEFUL ON A DIFFERENT BOARD? - "Board Report": states what is on this board — which squares hold which colour, whose move it is, which moves are legal, the disc counts. Pure description, not true of any other board. - "Move Verdict": names the square to play with NO reason attached ("play h8"). If a reason is attached, code the reason separately too. - "Local Justification": the reason a move is right HERE, naming specific squares, files, rows or groups on this board ("h8 takes the corner and locks the h-file"). Would be false on a different board. - "Worked Line": traces a concrete sequence of consequent moves ("if you play g2, White must take h1, then b6 gives you the edge"). Two or more plies. One move named with no continuation is Local Justification. - "General Principle": a rule, heuristic or concept NOT tied to this position, which would hold on other boards ("corners can never be flipped", "mobility matters more than disc count in the midgame", "parity decides who takes the last move"). A principle stated and THEN applied to this board is BOTH General Principle and Local Justification. The scaffolding acts: - "Hint": points at where to look without resolving it. If the text states the conclusion, it is not a Hint. - "Prompt": a question the user is expected to answer or work out, including an easier sub-question handed back to them. - "Comprehension Gauging Question": checks the user followed. Not about puzzle content. Reacting to the user: - "Paraphrase": restates the USER’s own proposal or reasoning. Adding new board facts is Board Report or Local Justification. - "Positive Feedback" / "Negative Feedback" / "Neutral Feedback": endorses / rejects / hedges the user’s idea or move.
