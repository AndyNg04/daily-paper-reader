## Peer Influence across Heterogeneous AI Models 

FRIDA NØHR LAUSTSEN, IT University of Copenhagen, Denmark 

MARIE HAAHR PETERSEN, IT University of Copenhagen, Denmark

When two AI agents disagree, who persuades whom? As multi-agent systems increasingly combine language models of different families and sizes, the answer can determine which judgments survive interaction. Measuring persuasion as the probabilistic shift in an agent’s decision after a single exchange with a dissenting peer, we test seven open-weight models across three language understanding tasks. We find that persuasion is strong: when models disagree, receivers often abandon their initial judgment after seeing a peer’s answer and explanation. Surprisingly, however, neither standalone certainty nor model scale reliably predicts persuasion dynamics. Models producing almost perfectly consistent decisions in isolation can be among the most susceptible to persuasion, and small models can match larger ones as persuaders and resist their influence just as effectively. Furthermore, we show that the size of the shift depends more on the susceptibility of the listener than on the persuasiveness of the speaker. Persuasion patterns are therefore specific to each model pairing, with heterogeneity amplifying persuasion in some combinations and suppressing it in others, allowing a dissenting agent running a small model to overturn the judgments of a much larger one. These findings show that the behavior of interacting models cannot be inferred from their individual properties but must be evaluated in the combinations in which they will operate. 

1 Introduction 

Automated decision-making workflows are increasingly relying on decentralized multi-agent systems (MAS) of au-tonomous AI agents. Such systems self-organize to handle tasks collaboratively in ways that go well-beyond simple ensembling of models: populations of agents powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) execute subtasks locally and exchange their outputs for further processing or recombination, according to the adaptive topology of the commu-nication network linking them [39, 47, 52, 54, 55]. This new generation of systems is powering complex workflows across a widening range of domains, including software engineering, healthcare, scientific discovery, and the Internet of Things [24, 27, 38, 45, 50]. The core mechanism underlying decentralized MAS is the dynamics of agent conditioning through message passing. In its most essential formulation, a judge agent 𝛼 must generate an output from a memory state consisting of two components: the local context 𝐶 𝛼 that the judge holds about the task, and the output 𝑂 𝛽 previously produced by a peer agent 𝛽 .The judge agent 𝛼 thus effectively implements a kernel generator function 𝑓 𝛼 (𝐶 𝛼 , 𝑂 𝛽 ), in which the effect of 𝑂 𝛽 on the generation is the influence that the peer agent 𝛽 exerts over the judge. Quantifying this peer influence is essential for a statistical characterization of the outputs that agents generate in interconnected systems. Such characterization is key to estimating the likelihood of emergence of different system-level outcomes, and in turn to assess the stability, vulnerabilities, and alignment of the decision-making system [3, 18, 23].

Research that develops and tests these systems has been predominantly concerned with how architectural choices affect benchmark performance of the system as a whole, rather than characterizing microscopic influence dynamics [35, 55]. A separate and prolific line of work has examined influence between LLM agents through the lens of opinion dynamics [13], tracking the opinion states of interacting agents [6, 8, 9, 43, 48]. These studies have shown that LLM agents may indeed be highly susceptible to peer pressure depending on model type and the direction of the shift [5, 33, 58], and can exhibit conformity to group norms [4, 10, 12, 18, 41]. These patterns of influence can create group-level biases that are undetected in isolated models, and ultimately lead to misalignment and performance degradation [3, 14, 53]. Finally, recent results on agent priming show that behavioral policies of LLM agents may be extremely sensitive to alterations in memory context, suggesting that peer influence may be a nuanced and rich phenomenon [30]. However, studies on opinion simulations with LLMs have been limited in two ways. First, they often track shifts in agents’ stances on general opinion topics rather than how agents jointly perform tasks, constraining how readily their findings translate to decision-making systems. Second, they instantiate agents that may differ in assigned personas or communication strategies but are homogeneous in their underlying language model [56]. Model heterogeneity is an increasingly common condition in MAS, both in open systems, where different players contribute agents of different types to a shared workflow [1, 11, 20, 25, 31, 36], and in self-adaptive systems, where agents can spawn new agents with different characteristics depending on needs [15, 40, 49]. Very few recent studies have experimented with multi-agent systems of heterogeneous language models, either focusing on negotiation debates [17], or on the evaluations of the performance of a decision-making systems as a whole [16, 57], while disregarding the characterization of microscopic dynamics of influence. We contribute to fill this gap with an evaluation framework to measure peer influence among heterogeneous agents in joint decision-making tasks. To isolate the influence effect in the message-passing component of decentralized MAS, we propose a minimal dyadic setup with one judge agent and one peer agent, working over two rounds. In the first round, both agents perform the same task independently. In the second round, the judge agent repeats the task while considering both its own prior output and the peer’s output from the first round. This setup captures the most essential message-exchange dynamic of this class of decision-making systems [19]. To measure influence, we introduce a metric that compares the judge’s output across rounds in a way that accounts for both the stochasticity of the generations and the base model bias. This setup allows us to estimate how influence depends on the pairing of model types, relative model size, and baseline confidence of the model when executing the task in isolation. We run our framework with seven models spanning different architectural families and parameter sizes (Llama 8B and 72B, Gemma 4B and 27B, Qwen 7B and 72B, and GPT-OSS 20B) on three binary text classification tasks for a total of 238 , 682 text instances. We find that agents typically exert strong influence on one another, with large shifts observed across all tasks. The reciprocal degree of influence is generally asymmetric, with varying patterns across model families and a general tendency of smaller models to exhibit competitive persuasive power compared to larger models. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the observed shift is determined primarily by the inherent susceptibility of the judge model more than the influence power of the peer model, with some models being generally more prone to influence than others, regardless of the type of model of their peers. Surprisingly, we record frequent instances of backfire effects, in which models reinforce the certainty of their own judgment when exposed to an opposing signal. Contrary to prior work suggesting that opinion shifts are more likely when an agent is initially uncertain [12], we find that some models remain highly susceptible to influence despite expressing high confidence in their initial judgments. Finally, model heterogeneity has a non-uniform effect on influence Peer Influence across Heterogeneous AI Models 3dynamics: some combinations of models lead to more pronounced shifts than their respective homogeneous systems, while others lead to the opposite outcome. Overall, our work helps enrich the toolkit for assessing and enhancing security in multi-agent systems [2, 7, 26, 46] by providing a methodology to test the reciprocal influence of AI agents in heterogeneous decision-making systems, while highlighting patterns that demonstrate the effect of model heterogeneity on the output stability of these systems. 

2 Experimental Setup 2.1 Decision-making framework 

In the first decision-making round, a judge agent 𝛼 and a peer agent 𝛽 are independently prompted to generate a binary classification label ˆ𝑦 for the same text sample 𝑖 , along with a natural language explanation 𝑒 motivating their decision. Because our interest is in measuring influence, we focus on cases where the labels of the judge and the peer diverge (the more trivial case of convergent labels is discussed in Section S6). In the second round, the peer’s output is passed to the judge, which is asked to reconsider its round-1 decision in light of the opposing label and explanation. The two agents can be instantiated from any combination of models, supporting the study of both homogeneous and heterogeneous peer interactions, and they can swap roles for a second run of round 2, so that each pair of models acts as both judge and peer on exactly the same set of samples. Comparing the judge decision in round 1 ( ˆ𝑦 𝛼 ) with its decision in round 2 after peer conditioning ( ˆ𝑦 𝛼 |𝛽 ) reveals the influence of the peer on the judge’s decision-making. We make this comparison probabilistic by repeating each round 𝑀 

times and measuring how much the probability of the judge generating label 𝑦 in isolation ( 𝑝 𝑦 𝛼 ) shifts after the interaction (𝑝 𝑦 𝛼 |𝛽 ). The resulting influence score Δ𝛼 |𝛽 ∈ [ 0, 1] quantifies the influence of the peer over the judge across all samples, with Δ = 0 denoting no effect and Δ = 1 a maximal probability shift. 2.2 Datasets 

We experiment with three binary classification datasets in the domain of natural language understanding, spanning different levels of difficulty. The binary labels are reasonably balanced in all datasets, in the 50% –54% range. Data sampling and preprocessing are detailed in Section S1. The first dataset is the Sentiment Analysis corpus from the SocKet repository [29], consisting of 91 ,964 statements paired with human-assigned Positive /Negative sentiment labels. Assessing sentiment from text is a relatively easy language understanding task, as the text often carries explicit signals of the underlying sentiment. The second is CommonsenseQA 2.0 [42], consisting of 9,264 factual questions paired with binary Yes /No answers. Although the questions are intentionally designed to be challenging, they are unambiguous and can often be answered through common-sense reasoning. 2.3 Models and parametrization 

We evaluate a diverse set of open-weight models spanning different parameter sizes, vendors, and geographical regions of development: Google’s gemma-3-4b and gemma-3-27b ; Meta’s llama-3.1-8b and llama-3.3-70b ; Al-ibaba’s qwen-2.5-7b and qwen-2.5-72b ; and OpenAI’s gpt-oss-20b . For brevity, we omit version identifiers and refer to the models by the model-size naming convention. We use 4-bit bitsandbytes quantized versions of all models. We consider all pairwise configurations of the largest models from each family and, to test effect of model size, we separately pair large and small models within the same family. We adopt default sampling parameters when available, or values reported as suitable by previous work as alternatives (details in Section S2). Instruction prompts are kept short and with neutral tone, and are detailed in Section S3. In all experiments, we set the number of repetitions per sample to 𝑀 = 10 .

3.1 Peer Influence 

Figure 1A reports the influence scores Δ𝛼 |𝛽 for all pairs of the largest models. The marginal means of the matrices represent the susceptibility to influence when a model acts as a judge (columns) and its persuasiveness when it acts as a peer (rows). Figure 1B reports the backfiring scores Δ−

𝛼 |𝛽 instead. The results support five main findings. First, peers exert a substantial effect on judges. Opinion shifts are generally large across models and datasets, with influence scores exceeding 0.5 in most cases and frequent peaks above 0.75 . Regardless of the judge’s label probability distribution in round 1, the probability mass of assigning the peer’s suggested label in round 2 concentrates heavily toward 1 (see Section S5). This finding is in line with prior observations that the judgments of LLM-based agents are typically highly sensitive to external input [12, 51]. Second, influence tends to be directional. Within a fixed model pair, an agent that is an influential peer is likely to be less susceptible to influence when assuming the role of judge. For example, when pairing qwen-72b with llama-70b ,

qwen-72b consistently achieves higher influence scores as peer and lower scores as judge. At the aggregate model level, however, high persuasiveness is not necessarily associated with low susceptibility: a model may exert strong influence 6 Nøhr Laustsen et al. as a peer while remaining highly susceptible as a judge. Gemma-27b is the clearest example, exhibiting the highest susceptibility values while also scoring high in persuasiveness. Third, high certainty does not imply lower susceptibility. The judges with the highest susceptibility are also those with the highest rate of consistent classification labels when evaluating samples independently. In the Sarcasm dataset, 

gemma-27b produces consistent labels for 99 .95% of samples, yet its susceptibility as a judge is 0.89 . The pattern holds across the board, as reflected by a positive Spearman rank correlation between certainty (i.e., the proportion of samples labeled consistently in round 1) and susceptibility, when pooling all model pairs and datasets ( 𝜌 = 0.61 , 𝑝 = 0.036 ). Fourth, the final classification outcome is driven more by the characteristics of the judge than by those of the peer, as indicated by the higher standard deviation of susceptibility values across all model-dataset combinations ( 𝜎 = 0.19 ), compared to persuasiveness values ( 𝜎 = 0.10 ). Fifth, the likelihood that a peer-judge interaction backfires is low but not negligible, averaging at about 15% in Sarcasm 

12% in Commonsense and 7% in Sentiment, and with peaks exceeding 40% for some combinations of models. The full set of values is reported in Section S7. Backfiring is also milder overall than positive influence, with peak values rarely falling below −0.5.

Model overview. Individual models exhibit distinct influence profiles. Gemma-27b is the most susceptible judge across all datasets ( 0.78 to 0.89 ) and near-zero backfiring throughout. Its shifts are not only frequent but close to complete: post-interaction certainty is pushed near 1.0 across the full range of baseline probabilities (see Section S5 for detailed statistics). Despite this, it is also a highly effective peer, scoring consistently high against every judge. Gpt-20b is the only model that combines strong persuasiveness with stability under influence. The contrast is sharpest on Sarcasm, where it reaches influence scores of 0.53 to 0.89 as a peer and the lowest susceptibility of any model ( 0.28 ). Its backfiring scores are close to zero except on Sarcasm ( −0.23 ). Llama-70b behaves similarly, though it is somewhat more susceptible, less persuasive, and backfires more strongly. Qwen-72b is a more ambiguous case. Judging by positive influence alone, it appears resistant, with marginals below those of gemma-27b and llama-70b . The backfiring scores add nuance to this interpretation: qwen-72b is the judge with the strongest backfiring effect on all three datasets ( −0.27 , −0.41 , and 

−0.24 for Sarcasm, Commonsense, and Sentiment). Much of the influence it receives surfaces as movement away from the peer opinion rather than as stability, most prominently when interacting with llama-70b (details in Section S5).

3.3 Impact of model size 

To investigate the role of model size in persuasion outcomes, we focus on interactions between models of different size within the same family. On aggregate, larger models consistently achieve higher persuasiveness, as shown by the right marginal means in Figure 3A. This advantage, however, is driven mostly by their higher influence scores in homogeneous decision-making systems, and restricting the analysis to heterogeneous combinations makes the systematic size advantage disappear. In heterogeneous systems of gemma or llama agents, smaller models achieve comparable or higher influence. Smaller qwen models, by contrast, are not very effective peers against large qwen judges. Backfiring follows a different pattern (Figure 3B): smaller peers trigger stronger backfire overall. This is compatible with the intuition that the poorer justifications produced by smaller models are read by judges as evidence for reinforcing their prior opinion. The effect is clearest in gemma and qwen systems, and weaker in llama systems, where the gap between sizes is less pronounced.

Explanation swap. We restructure the prompt so that the label-explanation pairs are swapped between the peer and the judge. This tests whether the content of an explanation drives the final classification more than the identity of the agent presenting it. We pair llama-70b with gpt-20b , as gpt-20b achieved by far the highest persuasiveness scores on Sarcasm, suggesting higher-quality explanations. The results indicate that content plays the major role. Exposed to its own reasoning as a peer opinion, llama-70b becomes less susceptible, implying that its own explanations are less compelling as external input than gpt-20b ’s. Symmetrically, gpt-20b becomes more susceptible and less prone to backfire when presented with its own reasoning. Both models therefore assign greater weight to gpt-20b ’s label-explanation pairs regardless of the role from which they are presented, suggesting that gpt-20b produces more persuasive reasoning. A comparative analysis of the two models’ explanations is provided in Section S10. 

4 Discussion 

Our findings confirm the strong effect of message passing on the decision-making outcomes of multi-agent systems. Influence patterns in heterogeneous systems depend on the specific combination of underlying language models and cannot be inferred from relative model size alone: small models can be effective persuaders and as impervious to influence as larger ones. The magnitude of the opinion shift is determined mostly by the model receiving the message and only to a lesser extent by the one exerting influence. It also depends on the properties of the target task, with larger shifts observed in harder tasks where decisions must rest on limited informative signal. Shifts can also occasionally reinforce the pre-existing agent opinion, similar to backfiring effects in human opinion dynamics [10, 34]. Relative to their homogeneous counterparts, heterogeneous systems can either reduce or increase the variability of the final outcome, depending on model choice. In line with previous work emphasizing the gap between individual and collective agentic behavior [3, 14], we find that the classification certainty of an isolated agent does not imply stronger resistance to influence when the agent is moved to a multi-agent setting. This result runs against the widespread intuition that model certainty, estimated either from logits or from empirical generation probabilities, is a good proxy for confidence in the assigned label or for the correctness of that label [28, 44]. In this work, we deliberately focused on estimating influence rather than on classification performance against the ground-truth labels available for all datasets. For completeness, we report the difference in F1 scores between round 2 and round 1 in Section S11. Three main findings emerge. First, peer interaction improves performance mostly on harder tasks, with Sarcasm detection benefiting the most, while performance on Sentiment generally declines in Round 2. Second, homogeneous judge-peer pairs perform worse than heterogeneous pairs. Third, systems with smaller models as judges benefit more from interacting with larger models as peers than the reverse. 10 Nøhr Laustsen et al. Our findings come with three main experimental limitations. First, our framework considers only single-turn agent interactions on binary classification tasks. The single-turn setup isolates message-passing dynamics from the confounding factors that arise in larger and more articulated systems, and binary classification allows for an unambiguous quantitative definition of opinion shift. These constraints nevertheless call for broader exploration of emergent decision-making patterns in large, complex systems of self-organized AI agents [23] working on open-ended tasks. Second, our results rest on a limited pool of open-source, quantized models tested over a limited range of sampling parameters. Despite efforts to maximize variety, the findings are not guaranteed to generalize to other LLMs or to very large commercial models. Quantization might also affect the outcomes in way that might be hard to predict [21]. Last, our analysis weights all configurations of influence equally in the final estimate, a deliberate choice to examine every configuration in equal detail. However, the empirical distribution is concentrated in a subset of configurations, as shown in Section S12. Despite these limitations, our results carry concrete implications for the design of multi-agent decision-making systems. The choice of which combination of models to use emerges as a first-order design decision requiring systematic evaluation. The model mix determines not only the system’s inference capabilities [57] but also its stability, affecting its propensity to overturn the decisions that its agents would reach in isolation. Instability also increases with intrinsic task uncertainty, making system evaluation necessarily task-specific: model combinations that are stable on easy tasks provide no guarantee of stability on harder ones. Such instability can also become a security liability. In open application scenarios, where multiple parties contribute resources to a shared workflow, the system’s outcome can be reversed effectively through the injection of a contrarian agent powered by a relatively small model [22]. In other words, adversarial capability is not gated by model scale, and the properties that make a system robust to attacks cannot be estimated from the performance of its components in isolation. Design practices that select agents on individual capability alone (such as their individual confidence or performance on the task) therefore leave a substantial attack surface exposed, and should be revisited in light of these findings to build more reliable and robust multi-agent systems.

The complete set of cases that enable disagreeing interactions in round 2 is 𝐵 :1, 𝐵 :0, 0:𝐵 , 1:𝐵 , 1:0, 0:1, and 𝐵 :𝐵 , and every instance 𝑖 falls into exactly one of them. For 1:0 and 0:1 we test a single direction of influence on all 𝑀 available instances. In all other cases, we upsample the label-explanation pairs to 𝑀 for each available direction, so that every case yields 𝑀 repetitions in round 2 (Figure 4B illustrates this construction). Upsampling, rather than simply increasing the value of 𝑀 , is necessary because the label distribution is inherently skewed for some model-sample pairs. It also keeps the number of repetitions consistent across cases, ensuring that the influence measure rests on the same number of interactions. In a homogeneous setting, where both judge and peer are instantiated from the same underlying language model, disagreeing output pairs can be constructed only in the 𝐵 :𝐵 case, in which the model produced mixed labels in its round-1 predictions. Finally, to weight all valid label combinations equally when estimating the influence between a model pair, we compute the influence score of Equation 7 separately for each match case and then macro-average across cases. Only for the match cases 𝐵 :1 and 𝐵 :0, Δ𝛼 |𝛽 (𝑖 ), as defined in Equation 6, is strictly lower than 1 by definition, since the judge’s certainty in isolation is <1 and the peer can steer the decision in only one direction (toward 1 for 𝐵 :1, toward 0 for 𝐵 :0). We therefore min-max normalize Δ𝛼 |𝛽 (𝑖 ) to bring it into the desired [0, 1] range. To keep computational cost manageable, we cap each match case at 7,000 samples. Given 𝑀 = 10 , this amounts to 70 ,000 generations in total for each combination of model, match case, and dataset. The exact cardinality of all match cases is reported in Section S12. As a robustness check, we also test agreeing interactions where both the judge and the peer output the same label in round 1, and find that almost no opinion shifts occurs, as expected (Section S6).

SUPPLEMENTARY INFORMATION S1 Data preprocessing 

We preprocess the Sarcasm and Sentiment datasets to remove low-quality samples. The Commonsense dataset is already well curated and required no additional steps. The preprocessing scripts are published in the project repository (src/preprocessing.py ). 

Sarcasm. We omit all claims shorter than 6 words to ensure sufficient semantic context. We also apply a keyword-based filter to remove claims touching on sensitive topics such as firearms , holocaust , and suicide , so that model outputs are not affected by guardrails. The full keyword list is provided in the project repository. 

Sentiment. Because of its tree-based structure, the Sentiment dataset contains many semi-duplicate instances sharing most of their text, with only a few additional words appended. We remove semi-duplicates that carry the same label and retain those in which the added text changes the sentiment. Consider the following three labeled samples: (1) [positive] The film boasts at least a few good ideas and features some decent performances. 

(2) [positive] The film boasts at least a few good ideas and features some decent performances, but, 

(3) [negative] The film boasts at least a few good ideas and features some decent performances, but the result is disappointing. 

The first two express the same sentiment and are treated as semi-duplicates with identical labels, so we keep only the longer text. The third is retained, since the added phrase flips the sentiment from positive to negative.

S10 Comparison of Model Explanations 

We examine explanation quality more closely, motivated by the swap sensitivity analysis, where the opinions of the two agents are presented from reversed roles. The results of the analysis suggest that gpt-20b consistently produces more compelling explanations than llama-70b . Regardless of whether an explanation is framed as a peer opinion or the model’s own prior, both models show a systematic preference toward gpt-20b ’s reasoning. From a quantitative perspective, gpt-20b produces considerably longer explanations than llama-70b , with an average of 23 words per explanation compared to 7. This difference in length is also reflected in the quality of the explanations each model produces. With longer explanations gpt-20b is able to construct more elaborate arguments, whereas llama-70b tends to produce brief statements that assert a label without providing a supporting explanation (see examples below). This pattern is consistent across all interaction outcomes observed in the swap analysis. This suggests that explanation quality is a contributing factor to a peer’s ability to influence a receiver.
