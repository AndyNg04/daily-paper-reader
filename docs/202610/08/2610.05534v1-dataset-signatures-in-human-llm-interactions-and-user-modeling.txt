## ABSTRACT 

Human–LLM interaction datasets shape our understanding of AI use and provide a foundation for downstream research, including training and evaluation of user models. In recent years, a growing number of datasets have sought to capture a representative picture of human–LLM interactions. But how different are the pic-tures these datasets provide, and what do those differences mean for research built on them? We study these questions across seven conversation datasets, spanning in-the-wild chat logs and human preference data. We begin by revisiting the dataset classification experiment of Torralba & Efros (2011) and find that neural network classifiers identify the source of a conversation from user messages alone well above chance, indicating distinctive dataset signatures . This separability persists after matching datasets on the dimensions of human-designed taxonomies, imply-ing subtle differences that these taxonomies do not capture. We then examine the implications for user modeling: how dataset signatures propagate to the outputs of user models trained on these datasets; how dataset choice influences evaluations of user model quality and subsequent evaluations of LLM assistants paired with these user models; and how dataset classifiers can guide data selection for training user models. While each dataset is meant to capture a slice of “real-world” interactions, our findings reveal the extent to which these slices diverge, and the consequences of those differences for research built on these foundations.

1arXiv:2610.05534v1 [cs.CL] 4 Oct 2026 Preprint What we learn about how humans use LLM assistants depends on which conversations we observe. Large-scale human–LLM interaction datasets (Zhao et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2025) have made it possible to observe real-world user requests and conversational behavior beyond small, controlled studies. They also support a growing approach to LLM assistant development: training user models that simulate the user side of a conversation to help train and evaluate assistants (Kong et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Naous et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Tack et al., 2026). Interaction datasets therefore serve two connected roles: they provide empirical evidence about user behavior and training data for models that reproduce it. In both roles, the dataset choice determines which interactions become the foundation for research. Datasets collected from real interactions, however, need not provide the same picture of AI use. Voluntary use of free chatbots in WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024), participation in the model comparison platform Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2024), public sharing of chat history in ShareChat (Yan et al., 2025), and paid interaction under task instructions (Bai et al., 2022) capture data through different processes. Data collections differ in recruitment, incentives, filtering practices, and more. Taxonomy-based analyses describe some of these differences through human-designed categories, such as topics and patterns of use (Mireshghallah et al., 2024; Shelby et al., 2025; Longpre et al., 2026). Yet two questions remain: how much do these datasets differ beyond these summaries, and what do those differences mean for models trained on them? 

Computer vision offers a useful starting point. Torralba & Efros (2011) asked whether an image’s source dataset could be identified from the image itself. Their “Name That Dataset!” experiment showed that individual images carry signatures of their source dataset. Revisiting this with modern image datasets, Liu & He (2025) found that datasets remained distinguishable by neural classifiers, even though their differences were less apparent to human experts. In NLP, web-text datasets have likewise been shown to carry distinctive signatures that propagate through pretraining (Mansour & Heckel, 2025). Here we investigate signatures in a new domain, human–LLM interaction, where signatures may be particularly consequential because of the dual roles datasets play. When taken as evidence about user behavior, a dataset’s signature may be mistaken for a property of human users in general; as training data, it may be learned and reproduced by user models, and in turn shape how assistants are trained (Suh et al., 2026) and evaluated (OpenAI, 2026). We examine these signatures in two parts. In the first half, we test whether conversations can be traced to their source across seven human–LLM interaction datasets (Section 2.1). Even after removing dataset-specific markers and masking assistant responses, classifiers identify a conversation’s source well above chance, reaching 53% accuracy on a seven-way task where chance is 14% (Section 2.2). Figure 1 shows examples with three datasets; we invite readers to guess their sources. Strong classification performance persists after rebalancing the datasets to match each other on human-designed taxonomy dimensions (Longpre et al., 2026), which describe what users discuss, what they ask assistants to do, and how they follow up (Section 2.3). Thus, persistent and subtle differences exist between the datasets, but what are their consequences? In the second half, we turn to these consequences for user models. Dataset signatures propagate into user models: a classifier identifies a user model’s training dataset from the conversations it generates well above chance, even when user models interact with the same assistant and are conditioned on the same conversation intents (Section 3.1). Dataset choice also affects evaluation: when evaluating LLM assistants with user models, assistant performance and gaps between assistants vary substantially depending on the user model’s training dataset (Section 3.2); furthermore, evaluations of the user model’s own quality vary depending on the choice of held-out dataset (Section 3.3). Beyond exposing these problems, dataset classification also offers practical tools. Greater separability between two datasets correlates with a larger transfer gap, the drop in performance when a user model trained on one dataset is evaluated on the other, so separability can anticipate transfer before training. We also show that dataset classifiers can help select training samples that improve user model performance on a target dataset (Xie et al., 2023; Albalak et al., 2024) (Section 3.4). Each dataset offers a slice of human–LLM interaction. Our findings show that differences between these slices are subtle enough to escape taxonomy-based summaries, yet persistent enough to carry over into user models trained on them. These differences also influence downstream conclusions, such as about user model and LLM assistant quality. Our results call for awareness of these differences, caution when interpreting conclusions, and testing across datasets; only then can we clarify which conclusions generalize beyond the particular slice of interaction each collection captures. 2Preprint 

## 2 DATASET CLASSIFICATION 

The dataset classification task (Torralba & Efros, 2011) is an N -way classification task with N datasets where each sample has a label corresponding to its source dataset. While the task was originally introduced for object recognition datasets, we apply it to human-LLM conversation datasets. 2.1 DATASETS 

We study human–LLM conversation datasets, using three criteria. (1) Data records interactions with LLMs, excluding human–human dialogue such as Reddit discussions (Baumgartner et al., 2020). (2) The dataset is large enough to sample 50K conversations after filtering, allowing us to allocate training, validation, and test splits; this excludes smaller datasets such as PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024) and ThoughtTrace (Jin et al., 2026). (3) We prioritize general-purpose collections over those centered on a specific population or topic, such as students completing coursework in StudyChat (McNichols et al., 2026). Datasets differ in recruitment, participant incentives, assistant models, collection periods, and more. HH-RLHF, for example, contains conversations elicited from crowdworkers, whereas ShareChat collects conversations that users chose to share publicly. Public sharing may favor particular users, and particular conversations from each user. Prior work has documented specific differences: WC-4.8M skews toward more engaged users than Bing Copilot’s user base (Hicke & Tomlinson, 2026), and datasets differ in topic distribution (Mireshghallah et al., 2024). Rather than isolating individual differences, we quantify how far the resulting conversation distributions differ overall. 

Preprocessing. We keep English conversations, remove duplicates within and across datasets (e.g., WC-4.8M contains WC-1M), and filter out boilerplate user prompts. Dataset construction can leave artifacts that can act as classification shortcuts (Gururangan et al., 2018). We therefore inspect user messages for dataset-specific markers , such as name placeholders in LMSYS and privacy-redaction tags in WildChat and ShareChat (Table 1, Markers column). We delete each marker and collapse the surrounding whitespaces into a single space. Finally, we sample 50K conversations per dataset and split them into 40K training, 3K validation, and 7K test conversations. 2.2 CLASSIFIERS IDENTIFY THE SOURCE DATASET 

We observe surprisingly high accuracy by neural classifiers in this dataset classification task. 

Setup. We train classifiers to predict which dataset a conversation comes from. In addition to removing dataset-specific markers, we replace every assistant response with the placeholder [assistant response] to hide the assistant model’s identity. Each classifier is a linear probe (multinomial logistic regression head) on frozen Qwen2.5-7B representations (Qwen et al., 2025). We select the pooling method, layer, and regularization strength by validation accuracy; details at Appendix B. 3Preprint

The classifiers therefore appear to learn generalizable dataset-specific features, rather than memorize or overfit to training examples (Liu & He, 2025). 2.3 SEPARABILITY BEYOND HUMAN -D ESIGNED TAXONOMY SUMMARIES 

Human-designed taxonomies make conversation datasets easier to interpret by assigning each conver-sation to categories, such as its topic or how the user follows up (Shelby et al., 2025; Shaikh et al., 2025). Prior work compares datasets by how often each category occurs, for example, the share of conversations about information analysis (Mireshghallah et al., 2024); we refer to these proportions as a dataset’s category distribution. How much of the dataset separability in the previous section do these categories explain? We test this in both directions. First, we match datasets on their category distributions, so that the categories alone cannot tell them apart, and ask whether classifiers can still separate them. Second, we match datasets on classifier score distributions and ask whether their category distributions still differ. 

Human-designed categories. We annotate WC-1M, LMSYS, and ShareGPT conversations with gpt-5.6-luna (medium reasoning effort) using three facets of the AI Observatory taxonomy (Longpre et al., 2026): ‘Function’ (what the user is trying to do, e.g., Content generation, Info analysis), ‘Topic’ (the subject matter, e.g., Technology, Health & Relationships), and ‘Multi-turn’ (how later turns relate to earlier turns, e.g., Extend/deepen prior task). We summarize each dataset by its category distribution on each facet, along with the distributions of two structural features: the number of turns and the number of user message tokens. Annotation and matching details are in Appendix C. 

Matching categories only modestly reduces separability. For each dataset pair, we use integer linear programming (Huangfu & Hall, 2018) to select equally sized subsets with matching category distributions. We perform this matching separately within the training, validation, and test splits, and then train and evaluate a classifier on the matched subset. As shown in Table 4, matching lowers accuracy by only 3.0 – 6.0 points, leaving it at 74.9 – 83.2% (chance is 50%). ## 3 IMPLICATIONS FOR USER MODELING 

User models are increasingly used to elicit LLM behavior at scale by simulating human–LLM interactions (OpenAI, 2026). To improve simulation fidelity, they are often trained on recorded interactions: Kong et al. (2024) train on ShareGPT, Wang et al. (2025) on LMSYS, Naous et al. (2026) on WC-1M, and Wang et al. (2026) on PRISM. Grounding user models in real data gives them an empirical basis, but leaves open a central question: how much does the choice of dataset shape the user being simulated? Section 2 showed that user messages carry substantial information about their source, including differences that taxonomy-based summaries miss. A user model trained on such conversations may absorb these dataset-specific regularities, so that models given the same simulation goal produce different interactions depending on their training data. We examine this possibility through four connected questions. First, do user models carry their training dataset’s signature into the conversations they generate (Section 3.1)? Second, when user models are used to evaluate assistants, does the choice of training dataset change conclusions about the assistants (Section 3.2)? Third, how well do user models trained on one dataset perform on others, and does their ranking depend on the evaluation dataset (Section 3.3)? Fourth, can dataset classifiers help select training data that improves a user model’s fit to a target dataset (Section 3.4)? Together, these questions cover how user models generate conversations, evaluate assistants, are themselves evaluated, and are trained. 

User model training. Following Naous et al. (2026), we train intent-conditioned user models. We first annotate each conversation with a high-level intent summarizing the user’s goal. We then fine-tune one model per dataset, starting from the same base model, to generate the next user turn or end the conversation given the intent and the preceding dialogue. Every user turn serves as a prediction target, with earlier user and assistant turns as context. This formulation trains the user model to initiate, continue, and terminate synthetic conversations. We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3-8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) as base models; training and sampling details are in Appendix D. 3.1 DATASET SIGNATURES PERSIST IN SYNTHETIC CONVERSATIONS 

Method. For each dataset pair, we condition both dataset-specific user models on the same set of conversation intents, drawn from a third dataset used to train neither. Each user model converses with the same assistant (Qwen3.5-9B with reasoning; more results in Appendix E), so the two resulting synthetic conversation sets differ only in the user model. We then apply the dataset classification task in two settings. In the synthetic-to-synthetic setting, the classifier is trained on the train split of synthetic conversations. In the real-to-synthetic setting, it is trained on the train split of original datasets but tested on the synthetic conversations (Mansour & Heckel, 2025). Both settings use the same held-out test split of synthetic conversations and the probe setup of Section 2.2, using Qwen2.5-7B embeddings with a trainable logistic regression head. 6Preprint 

Results . As shown in Table 5, all ten pairs we tested are distinguishable well above chance in both settings. Synthetic-to-synthetic accuracy ranges 62.9% – 91.5%, and real-to-synthetic accuracy 59.3% – 75.1% (chance is 50%). The synthetic-to-synthetic results show that user models produce distinguishable conversations even when given identical intents and paired with the same assistant. Real-to-synthetic accuracy is lower, but remains well above chance: a classifier trained only on real conversations can still tell which dataset a user model was trained on, so the differences reflect characteristics of the original datasets. Synthetic conversations thus inherit the signatures of their user model’s training dataset. User models trained on different datasets do not simulate interchangeable users; each mirrors the dataset it was trained on. 3.2 DATASET SIGNATURES AFFECT THE EVALUATION OF ASSISTANTS 

User models are increasingly used to evaluate assistants in interactive settings, either on tasks with verifiable answers (Laban et al., 2026) or in open-ended conversations (Johnson et al., 2026). If user models inherit the signature of their training dataset, the evaluation of an assistant may depend on which dataset trained its simulated users. 

Tasks with verifiable answers. We take 100 math problems from GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and 100 programming problems from HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021). Each user model receives a problem as its conversation intent and simulates five conversations per problem with each of two assistants, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. We apply the user model generation guardrails of Naous et al. (2026): among other constraints, each user turn must contain 3 – 25 words and must not copy the problem or an earlier turn, so that the original problem is revealed across multiple user turns. Conversations last at most eight assistant turns. At the end of simulated conversations, a verifier compares assistant responses with the reference answer (GSM8K) or the unit test (HumanEval). Results. Table 6 shows that measured task success depends on the dataset used to train the user model. Even with the problems fixed, each assistant’s success varies by up to 2.8 × across user models: from 9.6% to 26.8% for Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct on GSM8K, and from 7.2% to 14.0% for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on HumanEval. We attribute this variation to differences in how user models simulate conversations, such as how models approximate users in intent expression and terminology use (Piantadosi et al., 2012), which are inherited from the training data. The user model also changes the overall takeaway: whether the larger assistant (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) appears meaningfully stronger than the smaller one (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) or if their gap is trivial (Chang et al., 2025). On GSM8K, the two perform almost identically with the WC-1M user model (9.6% vs. 10.0%). With the ShareGPT user model, however, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct outperforms Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (24.8% vs. 14.4%). Across both benchmarks, the ShareGPT user model separates the two assistants the most (10.4 and 11.4 points), whereas the LMSYS user model yields some of the smallest margins (0.6 and 4.0 points). Which assistant appears better, and by how much, depends on the dataset used to train the user model. 3.3 CROSS -DATASET GENERALIZATION

## 4 RELATED WORK 

4.1 DATASET SIGNATURES 

Dataset classification experiments and cross-dataset evaluations have exposed source-specific regular-ities in vision datasets (Torralba & Efros, 2011; Tommasi et al., 2015), including modern large-scale collections (Liu & He, 2025). Such regularities make within-dataset performance an unreliable guide to generalization. NLP has analogous findings: annotation protocols introduce predictive shortcuts (Gururangan et al., 2018), and corpus filtering changes which content and populations are disproportionately represented (Dodge et al., 2021). Mansour & Heckel (2025) identify fingerprints of web-filtered corpora and show that they propagate through LLM pretraining. However, human–LLM dataset releases often emphasize diverse real-world interactions (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024) yet dataset separability in this setting is less understood. We address this gap by examining three aspects: dataset signatures beyond human-designed taxonomy categories (Shelby et al., 2025), their persistence through user model training, and their relationship to cross-dataset generalization. 4.2 HUMAN -LLM I NTERACTION DATASETS 

Human–LLM datasets support both descriptive research and model development. They inform studies of AI use, such as Anthropic Economic Index (Handa et al., 2025), and taxonomies of user behavior (Shelby et al., 2025; Shaikh et al., 2025). For example, AI Observatory examines how descriptions of real-world AI use vary across conversation sources (Longpre et al., 2026). For assistant development, recorded queries and model responses provide supervision for assistant fine-tuning, as in Vicuna (Chiang et al., 2023). Additionally, preference alignment uses conversation logs paired with preference labels, motivating HH-RLHF’s collection (Bai et al., 2022). For evaluation, WildBench constructs a real-world task benchmark from the WildChat collection (Lin et al., 2025). Recently, user models (also called user simulators) have gained attention (humans&, 2026) as a scalable way to train and evaluate assistants through adaptive multi-turn interactions (Kong et al., 2024; Naous et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). They support evaluations of assistant behaviors emerging over extended conversations (OpenAI, 2026) and assistant training through simulated interactions (Suh et al., 2026). We therefore focus the second half of this paper on user modeling: it directly transforms recorded user behavior into simulated interactions used for downstream experiments. This opens up the possibility that the choice of training dataset is consequential for both the simulated user behavior and the conclusions about assistant behaviors drawn from simulation. 

## 5 CONCLUSION , L IMITATIONS , AND FUTURE WORK 

By revisiting the dataset classification experiment of Torralba & Efros (2011), we show that human-LLM interaction datasets contain signatures that survive marker removal, assistant masking, and matching on human-designed taxonomy summaries. Then, we investigate the implications of dataset signatures for user modeling. Dataset signatures persist in conversations generated by user models; the evaluation of interactive assistants and user models depends on which dataset we use to train user models. Dataset classifiers also have a practical use: classifier-guided selection improves a user model’s fit to a target dataset. Together, these findings demonstrate that dataset choice is consequential both for the user behavior being simulated and for the evidence used to assess it. Our results motivate documenting collection context and testing research findings across datasets. Our study has several limitations. We study English conversations from public datasets, so application of our findings to multilingual, cross-cultural domains is an open problem. We also note that dataset separability neither identifies one ‘representative’ dataset nor isolates the causes of source differences: recruitment, incentives, collection periods, and more can vary together. Matching selected taxonomy summaries does not control for all semantic or joint distributional differences. Perplexity measures predictive fit, rather than human judgments of simulation fidelity which should be verified by a separate user study process. Future work could disentangle collection effects, extend evaluation across languages and populations, and test whether better predictive fit yields more realistic simulators or reliable assistant evaluations. 10 Preprint ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

## A DATASET CURATION 

A.1 DATASETS 

Datasets are ordered chronologically. 

HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) is a human preference dataset collected by Anthropic through crowd-workers’ interactions with AI assistants under two task conditions: (1) helpfulness and (2) harm-lessness. (1) In the helpfulness condition, participants engaged in open-ended conversations, such as question answering, writing, and planning. At each assistant turn, participants compared two candidate responses and selected the response that is considered more helpful. (2) In the harmlessness condition, participants were instructed to adversarially probe the models to elicit harmful responses and select the more harmful candidate. Conversations continued from the selected response, allowing participants to produce multi-turn interactions. 

ShareGPT contains user-shared ChatGPT conversations collected from ShareGPT.com. ShareGPT conversations were subsequently used to train Vicuna (Zheng et al., 2023). Data filtering included converting HTML content to Markdown, filtering inappropriate or low-quality examples, and splitting lengthy conversations into segments compatible with the model’s context window. Because multiple snapshots and processed derivatives circulate under the ShareGPT name on HuggingFace, we identify our input as the two HTML-cleaned JSON files in anon8231489123/ShareGPT_Vicuna_unfiltered, containing 77K conversation records before our curation. 

LMSYS-Chat-1M (Zheng et al., 2024) was collected from voluntary user interactions on the Vicuna demonstration and Chatbot Arena website between April and August 2023. The platform provided 14 Preprint free access through three interfaces: a single-model chat, a comparison between two randomly selected anonymous models, and a comparison between two user-selected models. The dataset comprises one million conversations with 25 models, originating from 210K unique users. Before release, the researchers removed conversations containing personally identifiable information (PII) and added message-level moderation annotations. The released corpus contains conversation logs, without the human preference labels available in a separate Chatbot Arena dataset. 

Arena Human Preference 140K extends the collection setting of LMSYS-Chat-1M by pairing user–model interactions with explicit human preference judgments. Whereas LMSYS-Chat-1M contains conversations collected across single-model and comparison interfaces in 2023, Arena contains model comparisons collected between April and July 2025. Users submitted their own prompts and voted for either anonymous model, a tie, or dissatisfaction with both. The updated interface allowed users to continue a conversation after voting, with a newly sampled model pair receiving prior conversational context. Each record therefore represents a preference judgment within a potentially longer session, rather than an independent conversation. The release contains 135,634 vote records with paired transcripts, model identifiers, and session metadata. 

WildChat-1M (Zhao et al., 2024) was collected through publicly accessible chatbot services hosted on HuggingFace Spaces and powered by GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4. Users received free access after explicitly consenting to the collection and sharing of their interactions. Conversations are based on users’ requests, not on researcher-assigned topics. The collection spans April 2023 to May 2024 and includes both single-turn and multi-turn interactions. Individual conversation records were linked into conversations using conversation histories, IP addresses, and request headers. Before release, the researchers applied automated procedures to redact PII, hashed IP addresses, and derived coarse geographic metadata. After reserving a subset for benchmark development, the release contained approximately 1 million conversations. 

WildChat-4.8M (Zhao et al., 2024) extends the WildChat-1M collection through continued logging of voluntary interactions with publicly accessible chatbot services, with coverage extending through July 2025. The expanded collection incorporates additional OpenAI model families, including GPT-4o, GPT-4.1-mini, and the o1 reasoning models. Release preparation included automated privacy redaction, scanning for exposed secrets, and content moderation. The collection originally comprised approximately 4.8 million conversations; the public release named WildChat-4.8M applies toxicity filtering and contains approximately 3.2 million conversations. 

ShareChat (Yan et al., 2025) was constructed by retrospectively collecting publicly shared conver-sations from five commercial chatbot platforms: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, and Grok. The researchers identified shared conversation URLs through the Wayback Machine using platform-specific URL patterns, then employed automated browser interactions to retrieve rendered pages and extract structured conversation records. Users had independently conducted and shared these conversations, so the collection did not involve researcher-assigned conversational tasks. Extraction preserved ordered user–assistant exchanges and available platform-specific features, including cita-tions, visible thinking traces, code artifacts, timestamps, and model identifiers. ## B NEURAL CLASSIFIER 

All dataset classifiers share one recipe: a linear probe on frozen representations of a language model. 

Input to the language model. We format each conversation as alternating lines of the form User: ... and Assistant: ... , separated by newlines. Dataset-specific markers (Table 1) are re-moved beforehand. Unless stated otherwise, every assistant response is replaced with the placeholder 

<assistant_response> , so the classifier sees only user messages and the turn structure. 

Representations. We encode each conversation with the frozen Qwen2.5-7B and read its hidden states at layers 6, 12, 18, and 28 (the final layer). From each layer, we form two representations: the mean of the hidden states over all tokens, and the hidden state at the final EOS token. This yields eight candidate representations per conversation. For the model size comparison (Figure 3), we apply the same procedure to other Qwen2.5 family models, using four layers that span the network’s depth and include the final layer (e.g., layers 12, 24, 36, and 48 for Qwen2.5-14B which has 48 layers). 

Classifier head. On each representation, we train a multinomial logistic regression head. We standardize features with the mean and standard deviation of the training split. The loss is cross-entropy with class weights inversely proportional to class frequency, plus an L2 penalty on the weight matrix. We optimize it with Adam (learning rate 0.05) for 300 epochs from a zero initialization, computing validation balanced accuracy every 10 epochs and keeping the best checkpoint. 

Model selection and evaluation. For each classification task, we select the representation (layer and pooling), the regularization strength λ ∈ { 10 −4, 10 −3, 10 −2}, and the checkpoint by validation accuracy. Each dataset contributes 40K training, 3K validation, and 7K test conversations, so test sets are balanced and accuracy equals balanced accuracy. In the real-to-synthetic setting, training and model selection use only real conversations, and synthetic conversations are used only for testing.

## D USER MODEL TRAINING 

Intent Generation. For each conversation, we generate a high-level user intent summary, following Naous et al. (2026). Specifically, we provide the full human–LLM conversation trajectory to Qwen3-32B and instruct it to produce a short summary of the user’s high-level intent. We refer the reader to the “Prompt Template for Intent Generation” in Naous et al. (2026) for the exact prompt. 

Supervised Fine-Tuning. We perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the user-side utterances of the training split. Below, we present a training sample from WC-1M formatted with the Qwen-2.5 chat template, where highlighted spans indicate the tokens over which the loss is computed:                                              

> <|im_start|>system You are auser chatting with an assistant language model to convert awebsite into aPDF format.<|im_end|> <|im_start|>user To make the following website as an PDF<|im_end|> <|im_start|>assistant To convert awebsite to aPDF, you can follow these steps: ...(truncated) ...but not its dynamic or interactive elements.<|im_end|> <|im_start|>user Can you do that for me?<|im_end|> <|im_start|>assistant Apologies for the confusion, but as atext-based AI, ...(truncated) ...to assist you.<|im_end|> <|im_start|>user <|endconversation|><|im_end|> <|endoftext|>

We explain some details. First, the prepared intent (“You are a user chatting. . . ”) is placed in the system prompt to condition generation. Second, the loss is applied not only to user-turn content but also to the turn-ending special token ( <|im_end|> for Qwen2.5-family models), so that the model learns to produce delimited turns. Third, following Naous et al. (2026), we introduce a special token <|endconversation|> to signal that the user has terminated the conversation: a real human–LLM conversation with 2n turns is structured into 2n+1 turns with n+1 user-side utterances (the last one always being <|endconversation|> ) and n LLM responses. We initialize the embedding of the special token with the pretrained EOS token embedding. 

Synthetic Conversation Generation. The fingerprint experiments use a fixed Qwen3.5-9B assistant with thinking disabled. Only the user model sees the intent; the assistant receives the generated dialogue without an additional system prompt. Each intent produces one conversation per user model. The initial user request is generated, rather than copied from the seed conversation. Train, validation, and test splitting follows the seed intent’s original split, and the two synthetic conversation datasets share the same intents within each split. User sampling uses temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9, and no top-k restriction. Assistant sampling uses temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8, top-k 20. The served user-model context is 8,192 tokens. A conversation stops at the user termination token, four completed assistant replies, or the context limit. A user utterance identical to the immediately preceding user utterance is resampled up to seven times, increasing temperature by 0.05 per retry to a maximum of 1.0. 18 Preprint
