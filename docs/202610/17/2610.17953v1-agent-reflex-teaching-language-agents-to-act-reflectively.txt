Large language models have evolved from single-turn chatbots into interactive agents capable of long-horizon reasoning. In real deployments, MCP tool services can return noisy feedback and users may intervene to correct the agent, but existing methods provide limited supervision for reflection learning and failure recovery. To bridge this gap, we propose Agent-Reflex, a general training framework for incentivizing proactive reflection in agents. Specifically, Agent-Reflex comprises three stages. First, Environment Reflection Skill Discovery (RSD) injects grounded tool-feedback perturbations into environment scaling, samples a capability-diverse LLM pool, mines matched success–failure pairs, and summarizes reusable reflection meta-skills. Building on this skill library, we then apply Reflective Supervised Fine-Tuning (ReSFT) that converts each skill into supervision for self-reflection, skill recovery, and user-centric correction, strengthening complementary reflective behaviors. Finally, we propose On-Policy Reflective Policy Optimization (ORPO) to combine agentic reinforcement learning under noisy interactions with reflection-guided on-policy distillation to further elicit proactive reflection. Built on Qwen3-8B, Agent-Reflex reaches 58.6 overall on five long-horizon tool-use benchmarks and 29.1 on AgentNoiseBench, surpassing strong environment-scaling and reflection baselines. Experiments on six challenging agent benchmark suites demonstrate the effectiveness of Agent-Reflex. Further analyses show that Agent-Reflex improves agents’ robustness to noise while maintaining stable training dynamics, supporting scalable agent training.

1arXiv:2610.17953v1 [cs.CL] 13 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have extended their applications from natural language tasks to practical workflows that require long-horizon reasoning (Yao et al., 2023; Patil et al., 2025; Trivedi et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2026b). Meeting this demand requires agents not only to master tool interfaces but also to accumulate transferable interaction experience from dynamic environments. Building diverse, stateful environments that faithfully capture real-world interaction logic has therefore become the foundation for scaling agent capability. Recent studies to scale agent training environments can be roughly categorized into two groups. One group, exemplified by Simia (Li et al., 2025) and GenEnv (Guo et al., 2025), treats LLMs as implicit textual world models that generate feedback from tool specifications and interaction histories. These approaches substantially reduce environmental engineering costs, but the feedback generated can be hallucinated. Instead, a complementary family explicitly reproduces real interaction logic, synthesizing executable tools and environment databases: it extracts databases from real-world service themes, generates executable tools, and synthesizes tasks with verifiable results (Song et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Tu et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026a). As a result, these executable environments give agents a basis for learning interaction patterns that transfer across environments. Although environment scaling improves interaction diversity and out-of-distribution generalization, existing agent-training paradigms still rely on an idealized assumption: agents interact with stable, well-controlled environments under minimal user intervention. This mismatch with real-world deployment reveals two inherent challenges (as shown in Figure 1):  

> (1) Lack of noisy feedback.

Real-world tool services are unreliable, frequently returning timeouts and service errors. Existing training environments usually assume stable and noise-free feedback, leaving agents underprepared for failure detection and recovery (Wang et al., 2026a; Gupta, 2026).  

> (2) Lack of user-guided correction.

Real users actively intervene when an agent deviates from their intent, correcting its reasoning and actions. Existing user simulators often rely on standardized templates, with limited coverage of corrective instructions and diverse interaction styles during execution (Barres et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025; Suhr and Artzi, 2023). Together, these challenges reveal that agents still lack proactive reflection under noisy external feedback. Ideally, a robust agent should continuously perceive environmental noise, task failures, and user correction intent, then promptly revise its actions. Existing reflection methods explore inference-time reflection, correction-trajectory learning, and noise-augmented training (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2025; Bi et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026), but most are trained in stateless or simplified environments, leaving one question open: How can we elicit proactive reflective capability in agents from execution failures in real-world environments? 

To address this gap, we propose Agent-Reflex, a general training framework for incentivizing proactive reflective thinking in agents. It models reflection as a learnable meta-skill: transferable meta-skills are explicitly mined from execution failures and progressively internalized into the policy through verifiable reflection learning. Specifically, Agent-Reflex comprises three components:  

> (1) Environment Reflection Skill Discovery (RSD).

We anchor 557 high-usage real-world environment themes and automatically construct database-backed executable environments and tasks, injecting diverse tool-return failures to emulate noisy conditions. From a diverse LLM agent pool, we sample trajectories covering varied task types and response styles, and matched success/failure pairs are then mined into 1,170 reflection meta-skills across 557 environments.  

> (2) Reflective Supervised Fine-Tuning (ReSFT).

Given the skill library, we design three auxiliary and verifiable reflec-tion sampling strategies: Self-Reflection (SR), Skill Recovery (KR), and User-Centric Reflection (UR), which strengthen reflection at different levels. Supervised fine-tuning then internalizes these explicit skills into the agent policy.  

> (3) On-Policy Reflective Policy Optimization (ORPO).

To further elicit proactive reflection, ORPO combines Noisy Agent RL with reflection-guided on-policy distillation. For each task we match its environment’s reflection meta-skill and rollout in the noisy environment, optimizing the policy by reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards. The same action tokens are then re-scored under a privileged skill context, and a confidence-weighted OPD objective (Wu et al., 2026) turns this contrast into dense supervision for reflection decisions. 2Experiments on six long-horizon tool-use and noisy-interaction benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of Agent-Reflex. Further analysis shows that it improves noise robustness and keeps training dynamics stable, supporting scalable general-agent training. Therefore, our contributions are threefold: • We identify two gaps between idealized synthetic environments and real-world deployment in agent interaction, namely lack of noisy environment feedback and lack of user-guided correction during execution , highlighting the importance of reflection learning for noisy interactions. • We introduce RSD, which systematically builds a scalable noisy-environment ecosystem by incorporating real-tool perturbations and mining reflection meta-skills from large-scale success/ failure trajectory pairs sampled by an LLM Agent Pool. • We further propose ReSFT and ORPO. ReSFT builds multi-level reflection data through three sampling strategies and internalizes it by supervised fine-tuning; ORPO adds reflection-guided on-policy distillation to Noisy Agent RL for token-level supervision of failed executions. 

# 2 Related Work  

> Agent Reflection Learning.

Agent reflection differs in where the corrective signal lives. Inference-time methods leave parameters untouched: Reflexion and Self-Refine append a verbal self-critique, and ExpeL retrieves cross-task insights at test time (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). Because the critique comes from the policy that failed, a well-formed but wrong tool return is rarely contradicted. Training-time methods move the signal into a per-trajectory repair: Agent-R, ReflectRL, and LoongReflect learn from that repair (Yuan et al., 2025; Bi et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026), and NoisyAgent exposes the policy to perturbed interaction under only a scalar return (Chen et al., 2026). Either way, nothing accumulates into an environment-level statement a later episode can reuse. Agent-Reflex mines cross-model success/failure contrasts under the same injected noise into environment-indexed meta-skills and internalizes them through two-stage training.  

> Scalable Environment Synthesis for Agent Training.

Diverse, stateful environments underpin tool use and long-horizon decision making. One line of work simulates interaction feedback with language models, treating tool specifications and dialogue history as an implicit world model (Li et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). A second makes the environment itself executable: EnvScaler and ScaleEnv synthesize tools with verifiable tasks (Song et al., 2026; Tu et al., 2026), Agent-World scales database-backed services over hundreds of real-world themes and verifies outcomes through state transitions (Dong et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b), and EnvACE internalizes environment dynamics through world rehearsal (Xu et al., 2026). All of them scale the supply of solvable environments, whereas Agent-Reflex keeps the database-backed paradigm but turns the failures inside those environments into supervision, mining diagnosis and recovery rules from cross-model success/failure contrasts under perturbations derived from real tool errors. Appendix A gives an extended discussion of reflection learning, environment synthesis, and agentic RL.

3.1 Stage 1: Environment Reflection Skill Discovery (RSD) 

3.1.1 Noise-Grounded Environment Scaling 

To construct realistic environments with tool failures, we select 557 high-usage MCP server specifications from Smithery 1, each defining an environment topic m ∈ M . Following Agent-World (Dong et al., 2026a), a research agent equipped with search, browser, coding, and operating-system tools builds and iteratively enriches a topic-grounded database. A coding agent then generates candidate tools and unit tests from each topic and its database. We retain only tools that compile, pass all tests, satisfy state-transition constraints, and support executable task verification. Thus, each environment Em consists of a database, a validated tool set, and corresponding verifiers. The resulting ecosystem is E = {E m : m ∈ M} , with 60 verifier-backed tasks per topic; the resulting task set Q supplies the ReSFT data. 

Tool-Failure Mining and Injection. Because real deployment is unstable, we ground perturbations in observed failures rather than invent them. We replay agents across nine public agentic benchmarks whose environments expose production-grade tool ecosystems and log every tool return (Appendix D.2). In total we collect 177,700 tool returns, of which 24,510 are error returns, and group them by surface form into eight actionable failure families (Table 4). We then inject these failures into tool returns probabilistically to emulate real deployment:

3.2 Stage 2: Reflective Supervised Fine-Tuning (ReSFT) 

ReSFT converts explicit reflection skills into policy behavior. Starting from a frozen behavior policy π0, we construct three complementary forms of reflective supervision for local repair, global recovery, and user-guided correction.  

> Self-Reflection Sampling (SR).

For an erroneous trajectory τ −, let t⋆ be its first erroneous turn, st⋆ the environment-state snapshot taken before that turn is executed, ht⋆ the deployment-visible history up to that turn, and κ the matched reflection skill. Let x ⊕ z denote appending z to context x, and let Ver s(x, y ) indicate whether continuation y

successfully completes the task after restoring the environment to state s. To teach failure awareness and self-reflection, we restore the environment to st⋆ and continue after appending the corrective skill: 

ySR ∼ π0(· | ht⋆ ⊕ κ), DSR = {(ht⋆ , y SR ) : Ver st⋆ (ht⋆ , y SR ) = 1 }. (2) The skill triggers diagnosis and replanning during generation but is removed from the stored input, so the policy learns to recover from its own error state.  

> Skill Recovery Sampling (KR).

Many failures originate from an incorrect initial plan rather than a local tool error. To teach global recovery, we reset the environment to its initial state s0 and restart from the original task with the matched skill: 

yKR ∼ π0(· | q ⊕ κ), DKR = {(q, y KR ) : Ver s0 (q, y KR ) = 1 }. (3) As in SR, the privileged skill is removed before storage, encouraging recovery without an explicit skill prior.  

> User-Centric Reflection Sampling (UR).

When an agent deviates from the user’s intent, users may intervene and correct its reasoning. To teach user-centric reflection, a user simulator U converts the matched skill into a natural-language correction: uκ = U (κ, h t⋆ , s t⋆ ). We then restore the environment to st⋆ , insert the correction after the erroneous turn, and continue: 

yUR ∼ π0(· | ht⋆ ⊕ uκ), DUR = {(ht⋆ ⊕ uκ, y UR ) : Ver st⋆ (ht⋆ ⊕ uκ, y UR ) = 1 }. (4) Because the user message is deployment-visible, uκ remains in the stored input, whereas the privileged skill κ is removed. Let Dπ0

4.1 Experimental Setups  

> Datasets.

We evaluate our method on two groups of benchmarks. (1) Long-horizon tool use covers general function calling and interactive workflows: BFCL-v4 (Patil et al., 2025), ACEBench (Chen et al., 2025), 200 stratified ToolSandbox tasks (Lu et al., 2025), τ 2-Bench (Barres et al., 2025), and VitaBench (He et al., 2025). (2) Noisy interaction uses AgentNoiseBench (Wang et al., 2026a), instantiated on the τ 2-Bench and VitaBench tool- and user-noise settings. Following the EnvScaler protocol (Song et al., 2026), all evaluations use the standard MCP format and task templates, and each benchmark is scored with its official metric. Appendix B specifies every benchmark.  

> Baselines.

Every trained method here, including Agent-Reflex, starts from the same Qwen3-8B backbone under one fair experimental setting. We compare three families: reflection methods (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026), environment-scaling methods (Li et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026a), and trajectory-level RL algorithms (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). Appendix C specifies every baseline and Appendix D the implementation. 

4.2 Main Results  

> 4.2.1 Results on Long-Horizon Tool Use.

Table 1 shows that Agent-Reflex performs well on five long-horizon agent benchmarks, achieving the highest overall score among strong baselines. We further make the following observations.  

> (1) Internalizing Reflection beyond Inference-Time Guidance.

Agent-Reflex outperforms Reflexion-8B and ExpeL-8B on VitaBench, ACEBench, and ToolSandbox. On VitaBench, Agent-Reflex achieves 17.5%, compared with 6.8% for Reflexion-8B. While these methods rely on self-generated critiques or retrieved experience at inference time, Agent-Reflex trains directly on verified recovery behavior. This may help the policy translate reflection into corrective actions more reliably, reducing its reliance on interpreting textual advice during execution.  

> (2) Reusable Recovery Knowledge beyond Individual Trajectories.

Agent-Reflex surpasses Agent-R-8B and NoisyAgent-8B on VitaBench, BFCL, ACEBench, and ToolSandbox. On ACEBench, it reaches 78.0%, compared with 52.0% for NoisyAgent-8B. These gains suggest that environment-level meta-skills provide useful recovery guidance beyond individual correction trajectories or episode-level rewards.  

> (3) Reflective Supervision Complements Environment Scaling.

Agent-Reflex improves over EnvScaler-8B and Agent-World-8B on all five benchmark averages, e.g., 62.1% vs. 54.6% on τ 2-Bench. On ACEBench, the gain comes mainly from multi-turn interaction: Agent-Reflex reaches 73.3% at the turn level while matching the baselines at 85.0% step-level accuracy. This suggests that reflective supervision complements broader tool and task coverage.  

> 4.2.2 Robustness under Interaction Noise.

To assess whether Agent-Reflex remains effective when tool feedback or user interactions are perturbed, we evaluate it on AgentNoiseBench. In detail, every foundation model degrades markedly.  

> Generalization of Agent-Reflex to Unseen Perturbations.

Table 2 shows that Agent-Reflex achieves the highest Overall score among the compared 8B methods, reaching 29.1% compared with 25.9% for NoisyAgent-8B and outper-forming the other strong baselines overall. These results indicate that its effectiveness extends to noisy interaction. Appendix E.1 provides a fine-grained attribution of the observed noise-robustness gains.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present Agent-Reflex, a general training framework for incentivizing proactive reflective thinking in agents. In detail, RSD mines reusable reflection meta-skills from matched success–failure trajectories under grounded tool-feedback perturbations. Building on this skill library, ReSFT turns each skill into complementary supervision for self-reflection, skill recovery, and user-centric correction, and ORPO further elicits proactive reflection by combining agentic reinforcement learning under noisy interaction with reflection-guided on-policy distillation. Across six challenging agent benchmark suites, Agent-Reflex improves long-horizon tool use and robustness under tool and user noise with stable training dynamics, supporting scalable agent training.

A.1 Agent Reflection Learning 

Agent reflection studies how interaction feedback can reveal errors and guide subsequent behavior. The methods differ along one axis that matters for our claims: where the corrective signal lives : in the context at inference time, in an offline trajectory rewrite, or in the parameters.  

> Inference-time reflection.

The first family leaves parameters untouched and reflects in context. Reflexion appends a verbal self-critique to an episodic buffer that later attempts read (Shinn et al., 2023), and Self-Refine iterates generation, self-feedback, and revision within a single episode (Madaan et al., 2023); ExpeL instead accumulates cross-task natural-language insights and retrieves similar past trajectories at test time (Zhao et al., 2024). These methods bound what prompting alone recovers, and that bound is informative: because the critique is produced by the same policy that failed, it is only as good as that policy’s model of the environment. When a tool return is well-formed but wrong, there is nothing in the context to contradict the failed plan, so the critique tends to restate it. Our Table 1 shows this concretely: both Reflexion and ExpeL fall below their own backbone on the longest-horizon benchmark.  

> Learning from correction trajectories.

The second family moves correction into training. Agent-R combines MCTS with model-guided selection of the revision point to construct recovery trajectories, then learns timely correction by iterative self-training (Yuan et al., 2025). ReflectRL transfers reflection elicited on golden negative trajectories into direct reasoning (Bi et al., 2026), and LoongReflect combines privileged reflection supervision with outcome-based RL (Zhang et al., 2026), the closest prior work to our ORPO stage in supervision type , though its privileged context is an episode-level hindsight rewrite rather than an environment-level artifact. The shared limitation is that the correction is synthesized per trajectory: each repair is fitted to the episode that produced it, so nothing accumulates into a statement about the environment that a later, differently-failing episode can reuse.  

> Robustness to perturbed interaction.

A third line attacks the problem from the environment side. NoisyAgent perturbs user instructions and tool responses, mixes clean and noisy rollouts, and adaptively raises noise difficulty, giving a complementary route to recovery and ambiguity handling (Chen et al., 2026). This exposes the policy to failure states, which trajectory-level RL alone does not, but the supervision remains a scalar return: it can express that a perturbed episode was harder, not which recovery the specific failure admitted.  

> Positioning.

Agent-Reflex shares the goal of learning from failure and differs in what it learns from and what it stores. Before any policy training, it consolidates cross-model behavioral contrasts (matched success/failure pairs on the same task under the same injected noise) into explicit, environment-indexed reflection meta-skills. A skill is therefore reusable across episodes and mined once per environment, rather than rewritten per trajectory. These skills then supply supervision for autonomous recovery and user-directed correction (ReSFT) and a privileged teacher context for token-level distillation (ORPO). Because the skill is privileged only during training and stripped from every stored input, inference needs no skill prompt and no auxiliary reflector, unlike the inference-time family above, where the critique must be produced online at deployment. 

A.2 Scalable Environment Synthesis for Agent Training 

Existing approaches split into two families. The first uses language models as implicit textual world models: Simia generates environment feedback conditioned on tool specifications and interaction histories, and GenEnv couples generative simulation with a capability-adapted curriculum (Li et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). This removes most environment-engineering cost, but the fidelity of a simulated observation is bounded by the simulator’s ability to keep state consistent, so long rollouts can drift away from any realizable environment. The second family makes dynamics explicit through executable tools, databases, and programmatic verifiers. EnvScaler, AWM, and ScaleEnv construct environments whose transitions execute and whose outcomes are checkable (Song et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Tu et al., 2026); Agent-World mines databases from real service themes, validates generated tools against them, and lets environments and policies co-evolve through continuous task generation (Dong et al., 2026a); EnvACE instead internalizes dynamics by world rehearsal , with one policy emitting both tool actions and the responses they induce (Xu et al., 2026); and TOUCAN takes a data-centric route, synthesizing tool-use trajectories from real MCP services (Xu et al., 2025). These efforts are why a large pool of executable environments can be assumed today. What they do not address is the 17 status of failure inside those environments: a verifier returning zero says an episode was hard, not which of dozens of tool calls broke the plan, what evidence exposed it, or which recovery the situation admitted. Agent-Reflex adopts the database-backed executable paradigm from this line and targets that residual. 

A.3 Agentic Reinforcement Learning 

GRPO removes the value network by normalizing rewards within a sampled group (Shao et al., 2024), and DAPO and REINFORCE++ improve stability and sample efficiency through clip asymmetry, dynamic sampling, token-level loss aggregation, and variance reduction (Yu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025). Applying these to tool-using agents introduces a difficulty single-turn reasoning lacks: a long episode’s return is one scalar attached to a decision sequence of unknown length. One line of work therefore reshapes the rollout or the credit assignment rather than the optimizer, through hierarchical rewards with a self-critic stage (Dong et al., 2025b), entropy-triggered partial rollouts with soft advantage attribution (Dong et al., 2025c), and balanced exploration across the agent and environment branches (Dong et al., 2025a). A complementary route leaves the reward untouched and densifies the gradient instead: on-policy distillation scores the student’s own sampled tokens under a teacher context, which SEED instantiates for agents by re-scoring the same action token with and without privileged information and gating a stop-gradient log-likelihood shift through a sigmoid (Wu et al., 2026). ORPO is orthogonal to the first route and reuses SEED’s estimator unchanged in the second; what it contributes is the content of the privileged slot, an environment-level skill card mined once from empirical tool failures rather than a larger model or a per-episode hindsight rewrite. 

# B Datasets 

All evaluations use the standard MCP interaction format and each benchmark’s official metric, reported by domain. 

B.1 General Function Calling 

• BFCL-v4 (Patil et al., 2025) extends the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard from single-call accuracy toward agentic evaluation. We report Web, Memory, Multi-Turn, Non-Live, Live, and Irrelevance, and the average follows the official BFCL scoring procedure rather than a simple mean of the six categories. It is useful here because it separates call formatting from execution: a model can be near-saturated on Non-Live while failing the multi-turn categories. • ACEBench (Chen et al., 2025) evaluates normal, special, and agentic tool use, emphasizing when to call a tool and how to handle underspecified requests. We report step-level and turn-level accuracy, and the average follows the official ACEBench scoring procedure rather than a simple mean of the two; the turn-level metric credits a turn only when every call in it is correct, making it sensitive to error propagation. • ToolSandbox (Lu et al., 2025) is stateful and conversational, with implicit tool state dependencies, an LLM user simulator, and deliberately unsolvable tasks that require asking rather than acting. We use a stratified 200-task subset and release the task identifiers so the subset is reproducible. 

B.2 Long-Horizon Interactive Workflows 

• τ 2-Bench (Barres et al., 2025) is dual-control : agent and simulated user both act on shared state. We report Retail, Airline, and Telecom and their macro-average. Each domain supplies a policy document the agent must respect, and success requires the final database state to match the target, so partially correct trajectories score zero. • VitaBench (He et al., 2025) covers delivery, in-store, and OTA domains plus composite cross -scenario tasks spanning domains within one episode. We report the four domains under official Avg@4 and take the benchmark average as the macro-average of the per-domain scores. 

B.3 Noisy Interaction  

> AgentNoiseBench

(Wang et al., 2026a) injects perturbations into both sides of the interaction loop on the τ 2-Bench and VitaBench environments. Tool noise covers faulty, incomplete, inducing, redundant, and wrong returns; user 18 noise covers ambiguity, topic shift, conflicting instructions, redundancy, and boundary probing. We score with the official Stability-Gated Success metric, which credits a task only when the whole trajectory stays valid and therefore excludes episodes derailed by noise that answer correctly by chance. The benchmark is out of distribution for us: our perturbations are summarized entirely from large-scale real tool-call failures, were designed without any reference to AgentNoiseBench, and are injected by a different process; no AgentNoiseBench environment is used in training. 

B.4 Training Task Pools 

Two training pools must not be confused with the evaluation sets. The RSD ecosystem supplies the SFT data: 557 Smithery-anchored environments, each with a mined database, validated tools, executable verifiers, and 60 tasks (Section D.2), from which the ReSFT corpus is synthesized. EnvScaler tasks supply the RL data: ORPO directly reuses 2.5K EnvScaler RL tasks (Song et al., 2026) rather than RSD-synthesized tasks, and every algorithm in Figure 4 trains on this same pool, isolating the objective from the Stage 1–2 data.

# D Implementation Details 

D.1 Training Guideline 

Our aim is to test whether reflection can be mined, supervised, and internalized as a meta-skill, not to maximize a leaderboard number. All stages follow the cold-start-then-RL paradigm (Dong et al., 2025b) so RL does not begin from a policy that cannot complete a trajectory, and every stage and every trained method shares the Qwen3-8B 

backbone, with supervised fine-tuning implemented in LLaMA-Factory (Zheng et al., 2024) and reinforcement learning in VERL (Sheng et al., 2024). All RL baselines and ORPO use the same experimental settings and hyperparameters. Tokens belonging to tool returns and simulated user messages are excluded from the loss; only agent-generated tokens contribute gradient, which is the mask χq,i,t,k in Equations 8 and 11; the same exclusion applies to the ReSFT loss of Equation 5. 

D.2 Stage 1: Environment Construction and Skill Discovery  

> Environments.

Environment topics are 557 high-usage deployed MCP servers in the Smithery registry, ranked by the registry’s cumulative useCount . Following Agent-World (Dong et al., 2026a), a research agent with search, browser, code, and OS tools mines a topic-grounded database and expands it for N = 3 complexification rounds; a coding agent then generates candidate tools with unit tests, and a tool is retained only if it compiles, passes its tests, respects state-transition constraints, and admits an executable verifier. Each environment contributes 60 verifier-backed tasks. Both agents are GPT-OSS-120B (OpenAI et al., 2025), matching the Agent-World setting so that environment construction is not advantaged by a stronger builder.  

> Failure mining and injection.

Running agents across the nine public benchmarks listed below yields 177,700 tool returns of which 24,510 are errors. An analyzer model groups them by surface form into the eight families of Table 4; the families are multi-label, so one return may land in several counts. During construction each tool-return position is perturbed independently with probability pinj = 0 .2 per Equation 1. Both draws are balanced uniform: the family uniformly over the eight rather than in proportion to mined frequencies, and the realization uniformly among those compatible with the invoked tool. A clean task is kept only if it admits a verifiable solution, and a perturbed task only if it admits a verifiable recovery.

Skill record schema. A skill is a structured record rather than free prose: a trigger over visible history with its supporting and contradicting evidence, a diagnosis naming the failure family, a recovery template stating when to retry, switch endpoint, ask the user, or abort, and reversibility and partial-effect flags. Provenance and replay-validation fields travel with each record so that a skill remains auditable. 21 D.3 Stage 2: Reflective Supervised Fine-Tuning 

The three sampling strategies of Section 3.2 share one actor for continuation rollouts, π0 = Qwen3-8B, the same backbone that is being trained, and one user simulator for UR, U = GPT-4.1, following the τ 2-Bench setting (Barres et al., 2025). A sample enters the dataset only if the environment verifier accepts the resulting episode. The final corpus contains 54K trajectories, combining verified Qwen3-8B successes with reflective-correction samples spread over SR, KR, and UR. Two kinds of privileged information are kept out of every stored input. The simulator state snapshot is used to restore the environment before a rollout resumes and never appears as tokens in the context, so the policy sees only what it would see at deployment. The skill text is removed from the stored input in SR and KR, and in UR it never enters the context at all, since the simulator rewrites it into a natural user message uκ; those synthesized user messages are retained, because a user turn is visible at deployment whereas a skill prompt is not. The UR simulator is prompted to cover direct correction, partial hints, ambiguity, and deliberately incorrect advice, so compliance is not always the right response. 

D.4 Stage 3: On-Policy Reflective Policy Optimization 

ORPO starts from the ReSFT checkpoint with the skill library and reference policy frozen. ORPO directly reuses 2.5K EnvScaler RL tasks rather than RSD-synthesized data, which keeps the comparison against EnvScaler and the other RL algorithms direct. For the OPD hyperparameters, we follow the standard settings of SEED (Wu et al., 2026) to ensure a fair comparison. Each update samples a task group, rolls out G = 8 ordinary trajectories of which a fraction 

ν = 0 .25 runs in perturbed environments, normalizes advantages over the whole mixed group, and re-scores the recorded action tokens under the skill-augmented context. The skill branch is a forward pass only: it never queries the environment and its gradient is detached, so the marginal cost over plain Agent RL is a forward pass rather than a rollout. The confidence weight γ is a soft token weight: it emphasizes tokens with a positive skill-induced shift and down-weights those whose shift is weak or negative, while every trajectory still contributes through the outcome term. We further conduct an ablation study on the perturbation fraction ν. Across the tested values, the performance remains relatively stable on the evaluated benchmarks, with only minor fluctuations and no consistent advantage for alternative settings. We therefore empirically set ν = 0 .25 .

Meta-skill selection and environment alignment. The EnvScaler RL task pool is entirely distinct from the RSD task pool, but the two are aligned at the environment level: the more than 100 environments used to construct EnvScaler’s RL tasks are covered by the 557 high-usage MCP environments collected in Stage 1. Each environment and synthesized task is assigned a canonical environment identifier, allowing direct lookup of the corresponding skill: 

κq = Lookup(env _id( q), K).

Thus, skills mined from RSD trajectories are transferred from the environment level to different RL tasks instantiated within the same environment, without semantic retrieval or task-level overlap. This directly tests the reusability and transferability of the proposed environment-level skills. At inference time, the skill prompt is removed, so the deployed policy does not require access to the skill library.

Following EnvScaler and Agent-World (Song et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026a), every score we measure is the mean of three independent runs, and decoding settings, maximum turns, and tool-call limits follow each benchmark’s standard setting. # E Supplement Experimental Results 

This section reports the fine-grained noise attribution deferred from Section 4. 

E.1 Does Agent-Reflex Act Reflectively Under Noise? 

Task success alone cannot show whether an agent repaired for the right reason. We therefore split the AgentNoiseBench noise settings into finer diagnostic slices (seven tool-side and six user-side axes, which Table 2 aggregates into its tool-and user-noise buckets) and score each with the benchmark’s Stability-Gated Success ( SGA = Itraj ×Itask ), which credits a rollout only if its trajectory was never derailed by the injected artifact, discarding turns pulled off course that answer correctly by luck. Each axis is normalized by its own three-model mean so noise-family difficulty cannot dominate the shape, with 95% paired bootstrap intervals over 165 tasks per axis. As shown in Figure 5, Agent-Reflex leads on all thirteen axes, with the margin widest exactly where each part of the design is meant to act. 

Tool side. The two axes clearing the interval against both baselines are misleading lure (13.9, the largest tool-side margin) and incomplete information (10.9), the families whose returns are well-formed, so no schema check fires and only the evidence inside the payload reveals the fault. This is what RSD contributes and environment scaling cannot: more clean environments add successful trajectories, never a record of which well-formed return silently lied. On the four hard-failure templates, where the status code already names the fault, gains over the backbone are uniform (12.3–13.5). 

User side. The largest margin is cognitive overload (12.9), the only user-side axis also clearing the interval against the stronger EnvScaler-8B, and the one ReSFT’s user-reflection supervision targets directly: when the instruction itself is the defect, the repair is to re-scope the request rather than re-plan the tools, a behavior no tool-side reward can teach. Pooled over both sides, Agent-Reflex is derailed on 28.5% of rollouts against 44.8% for EnvScaler-8B and 56.1% for 23 the backbone. Because the metric gates on trajectory validity, none of this is reachable by recovering an answer after being derailed: the gains are repair, not tolerance. 

E.2 Why AgentNoiseBench Is Out of Distribution for Agent-Reflex 

AgentNoiseBench is never consulted while RSD is built: neither its taxonomy nor its injector informs our perturbations, and the two noise designs are constructed in entirely different ways. The noise used by Agent-Reflex comes entirely from summarizing large-scale real tool-call failures: the eight families of Table 4 are counted from 24,510 error returns observed while replaying agents on nine public agentic benchmarks, and are injected into tool returns of our own synthesized environments during construction and training (Equation 1). AgentNoiseBench instead derives its categories from its own empirical analysis of interaction noise and uses a separate injector that rewrites tool returns and user utterances of the τ 2-Bench and VitaBench environments at evaluation time, covering seven tool-side and six user-side diagnostic axes. The two therefore differ in their source of noise, in the environments they act on, and in the injection process, and no AgentNoiseBench environment, task, or injector is used anywhere in training. Performance here therefore measures whether a reflective policy transfers to unseen perturbations, not whether a seen noise distribution is recalled.

# F Limitations 

Our reflection skill library is mined offline from a fixed snapshot of a large collection of executable environments. Consequently, it does not automatically adapt to changes in tool interfaces, service behavior, or environment dynamics. Such changes may require periodic re-mining or lightweight maintenance to keep the library aligned with the current environments. Online updating, however, does not necessarily yield broadly generalizable skills. When the available environments or tasks lack sufficient diversity, skills summarized online may remain specific to individual environments or local failure patterns. The broader environmental coverage used for offline skill discovery may therefore help explain the stronger generalization of Agent-Reflex relative to SEED, although isolating this factor would require further controlled experiments. This highlights the importance of balancing update frequency with sufficient environmental coverage for skill generalization. Future work will investigate online skill summarization and incremental updating across larger and more diverse environment collections, allowing the library to incorporate new environments and emerging failure patterns while supporting cross-environment generalization. 

# G Broader Impacts and Future Directions  

> Scope of the current recipe.

Agent-Reflex is validated on 557 executable environments anchored in live MCP services, eight empirically mined tool-failure families, and six evaluation suites spanning long-horizon tool use and noisy interaction, so the recipe is exercised well beyond a single benchmark. Its three stages are also deliberately modular: the skill library, the reflective supervision, and the token-level objective can each be replaced without touching the others, which makes the extensions below incremental rather than redesigns.  

> Future directions.

Three extensions follow naturally. First, the failure taxonomy is textual, mined from what an agent 

sees when a service fails; extending it to temporal signatures such as latency, concurrency, and partial writes would 26 widen the range of deployment faults a reflective policy can anticipate. Second, the library is mined once at 8B scale, and testing whether it transfers across capacities, and whether skills compose across environments rather than only within them, would turn it from a per-recipe artifact into a reusable resource. Third, the privileged slot in ORPO accepts any environment-level artifact, so targets richer than reflection rules, verified sub-plans or tool contracts for instance, can be distilled with exactly the same estimator.  

> Broader impacts.

An agent that recovers from failure is more useful and also more consequential when wrong, since it takes more actions in the world than one that halts. Our skill records therefore carry explicit reversibility and partial-effect fields, and recovery templates distinguish asking the user from acting unilaterally. The ReSFT user-reflection data deliberately includes incorrect advice so the policy learns evidence-sensitive replanning rather than obedience. All environments are synthesized from public service specifications and contain no real user data, and because training perturbations are mined from real error logs, we release failure counts and families rather than per-service identities.
