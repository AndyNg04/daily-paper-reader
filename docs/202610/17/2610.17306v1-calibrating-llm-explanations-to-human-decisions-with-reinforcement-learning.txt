# ABSTRACT 

Explanations of LLM outputs are considered an important way to make model be-havior legible, helping human users make good decisions on downstream tasks. However, default training approaches for improving explanations target model task performance or human preference rather than human decision quality. To bridge this gap, we introduce HD-RL , a reinforcement learning framework for optimizing LLM explanations for human decision making. Our approach cali-brates a human decision simulator on observed human decisions, applying a proxy reward model to optimize the LLM to generate explanations that help people to make good decisions. We apply this framework to AI-generated text detec-tion. In our initial data collection phase, we observed that while LLMs possess strong autonomous detection capabilities, the explanations they generate for users yield near-random human accuracy (51.3%). By applying the HD-RL frame-work to the open-source Qwen 3.6 model, we generate optimized explanations that improve human performance, achieving a GLMM-estimated human accuracy of 63.7% in a randomized between-subjects experiment with repeated measures and outperforming RLVR, RLAIF, and other baselines by over 8%. Full code and data are available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Human_ Decision_RL-2471/ .

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly powerful, people rely on them more heavily for complex, everyday decision-making tasks (Kim et al., 2025; Duenas & Ruiz, 2024). When collaborating with an LLM, users frequently look to the model’s explanations or reasoning traces to help them assess how it might have arrived at a particular conclusion. Even when explanations are not fully faithful to the model’s reasoning, they play an important role in people’s decisions by mediating trust (Ribeiro et al., 2016). However, default approaches to improving LLM explanations, such as CoT prompting (Wei et al., 2022) or standard preference alignment (Christiano et al., 2017; Rafailov et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026), target objectives that are only indirectly related to human users’ decision quality, such as the model’s autonomous task performance or ability to produce explanations that appeal to human preferences. These approaches can result in distinct failure modes for human use of explanations. For example, optimizing an LLM to maximize its own autonomous task accuracy (e.g., via verifiable-reward RL) only ensures the reasoning trace is logically legible to the model itself. It does not guarantee decision utility to an end-user. Similarly, optimizing for subjective human preference conflates preference with decision utility, because people frequently favor intuitively plausible explanations over practically useful ones Palta et al. (2026); Oliveira et al. (2025). Recent literature demonstrates that plausible-sounding LLM explanations often degrade human judgment rather than enhance it (Wang et al., 2026; Cheung et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Statistical decision theory (Berger, 1987) provides a natural framework for formulating the goal of explanations intended as human decision support. A decision-maker observes information about an uncertain state of the world, including an explanation, and chooses an action under a utility function. 1arXiv:2610.17306v1 [cs.HC] 12 Oct 2026 Preprint. Under review. The quality of the explanation is evaluated by whether it helps the decision-maker select a good action under the utility. Hence, to train explanations for good downstream decision use, methods should target the distribution over actions of human decision-makers who use that explanation. We contribute a decision-theoretic framework for explanation post-training that distills this intuition to a measurable target. Our approach first trains a decision simulator on a large-scale human deci-sion dataset to predict the probability distribution of human decisions conditioned on a given text and explanation. We then use this simulator as a proxy reward model to optimize an LLM policy via reinforcement learning. To construct the reward signal, we evaluate the simulator’s predicted probability distribution over the human’s actions against the objective ground-truth label, generat-ing a logarithmic surrogate reward. By optimizing the policy to maximize this reward, the LLM is explicitly trained to generate explanations that help people to make good decisions. We demonstrate the HD-RL framework on an AI text detection task. We collect and release a large-scale dataset comprising 21K real-world human decisions on 2712 unique texts, where half are organically written by human. In a randomized between-subjects experiment with repeated measures, Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) results show that explanations generated via HD-RL achieve an estimated human detection accuracy of 63.7%, outperforming RL with verifiable rewards, RLAIF, and other baselines by 8 to 12%. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Recent efforts to improve the quality and reliability of LLM explanations have primarily relied on RL paradigms based on subjective preference, most notably through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Christiano et al., 2017), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023), or their structural variants. However, an explanation’s perceived quality and its effect on decision quality are distinct evaluation targets. Buc ¸inca et al. (2020) found that subjective mea-sures of trust and preference for different explanation styles did not predict performance on the same decision-making tasks. Buc ¸inca et al. (2021) showed that the most beneficial decision-support de-sign received the least favorable subjective ratings. Moreover, optimizing for user preference can degrade human oversight, because users tend to favor fluent and agreeable outputs (Mitchell et al., 2026). These findings suggest that optimizing preferences can fail to lead to effective decisions. Prior work also developed explanations targeted at impacting human decisions. Fukuchi & Yamada (2024) select combinations of explanations to encourage agreement with AI recommendations, using a predictive user model to estimate which combination will minimize the discrepancy between the user’s anticipated decision and the AI’s output. Similarly, Li & Yin (2024) use learned behavioral models to modify explanations toward targeted human decisions, strictly relying on the manipu-lation of static, score-based feature attributions. However, both frameworks lack an optimization objective that genuinely improves the user’s task comprehension. Because they manipulate expla-nations merely to select a targeted behavioral response, rather than clarifying the underlying ground truth, they yield mixed empirical results and fail to improve independent human decision-making. ComplLLM (Guo et al., 2026) post-trains an LLM within a decision theoretic framework to extract signals that complement an existing recommendation, providing a direct precedent for optimizing language-model outputs according to decision value. Building on this perspective, we propose a decision theoretic RL-framework for explanation generation.

We construct a dataset of mixed human and AI authored texts. Each instance in the dataset comprises a short text and a binary ground-truth label, where 1 indicates AI-generated content and 0 indicates human-written content. To collect the human-written source data, we initially scraped 10,000 texts from Reddit published prior to November 30, 2022 (the release date of ChatGPT). To maintain par-ticipant engagement during the human decision collection stage, we applied a length filter, retaining only English texts containing between 50 and 250 words. This filtering process yielded a final-ized set of 2,260 human-written texts. To construct the parallel AI-generated texts, we employed a two-stage LLM pipeline using GPT 5.4. For each human-written text, the model first generated a concise summary. We then prompted the LLM to rewrite the text based on the summary, requiring it to match the original word count. This process yielded a balanced final dataset comprising 4,520 total texts, which we partitioned into three subsets using a 60/25/15 split at the source-pair level. The original human text and its AI rewrite were always assigned to the same split. A summary of the dataset is given in the Appendix C.1. 4.2 EXPLANATION GENERATION 

LLM explanations often contain a prediction, which can lead users to accept the system’s conclu-sion rather than evaluating the text themselves. To prevent this, the primary goal of our candidate explanations is to guide the user’s attention to specific features without making the final decision for them. Inspired by the visual scaffolding techniques used to help humans spot AI-generated images (Kamali et al., 2024), we format our explanations as a structured set of localized feature indicators: • Look for [specific linguistic or semantic feature]... • Look for [structural, rhetorical, or thematic pattern]... We collected human decisions on a public research website that hosts several media-forensics ex-periments and draws a general audience. Participants were unpaid volunteers who opted in from the site’s landing page and gave informed consent before their first trial. Participants were identified only by an anonymous browser token. We collected no demographic information. Each trial t presents a human decision-maker with a text xt ∈ X alongside a designated explanation 

et ∈ E ∗(xt), and selects an action at ∈ { 0, 1}, where 1 denotes AI text and 0 denotes human text. Upon submission of the participant’s decision, the system reveals the ground-truth state yt ∈ Y 

as feedback before moving to the next trial. We used session-based progress tracking to strictly guarantee that no participant was ever exposed to the same text twice, even across multiple visits. Furthermore, the interface prioritized serving instances (x, e ) with the current lowest total number of collected human decisions. More detailed task information is in the Appendix E. 

Dataset Summary Human data collection was conducted over a four-month period. As shown in Figure 2 (a), this stage collected 21,263 binary decisions from 2,452 unique participants for 2,712 unique texts (1,356 AI-generated and 1,356 human-written). Participants completed as many trials as they wished and could stop at any point. On average, each participant completed 8.7 decision trials; 5% completed more than 25. Each text-explanation pair received 3.9 decisions. Overall human decision accuracy using the unoptimized explanations was near-random at 51.3%, where participants achieved a 61.2% accuracy when identifying human-written texts, and 41.2% when identifying AI-generated texts. 4.4 TRAINING DECISION SIMULATOR 

For any given text-explanation pair (xi, e i), we obtain a set of human actions Hi =

We train an ensemble of five shallow MLP probes as a human decision 5Preprint. Under review. simulator that takes an (x, e ) pair and predicts the human decision distribution PH (a | x, e ). Each MLP is trained with distinct random weight initializations and data permutations. Conceptually, we need to learn a mapping function that generalizes from the observed (x, e ) pairs in our dataset to arbitrary new (x, e ) pairs generated during RL. Because x and e are natural lan-guage, this function requires a robust representation of the pair from which human responses can be accurately predicted. A standard supervised approach might train a text encoder from scratch or fine-tune a language model to map the text directly to human actions. However, training a language model on 21K decision data risks overfitting. We use Qwen 3.6 as a frozen feature extractor. We extract the representation hi ∈ Rd of the (x, e ) pair from the LLM’s last layer residual stream, where d = 5120 denotes the hidden dimensionality. We then train a lightweight simulator to pre-dict human decision probabilities based on the hi. Because this simulator only updates a small set of parameters, it converges quickly, avoids overfitting, and effectively generalizes to arbitrary new explanations (Jahanparast et al., 2026). As shown in Figure 2 (b), each individual MLP projects hi

into a lower-dimensional hidden space ( 256 dimensions), applies Layer Normalization to stabilize the input vectors, and uses a Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation followed by Dropout (p = 0 .2). A final linear projection and sigmoid activation yield the pre-ensemble probability. The simulator’s final predicted probability, ˆpi = Sϕ(a = 1 | xi, e i), is the unweighted average of the outputs from the 5 MLPs. Decision Simulator Performance Before evaluating the decision simulator, we first establish its maximum achievable performance on our dataset. Because the model has no information about respondent identity, for any fixed (x, e ) pair, the best it can do is predict the population action distribution. Therefore, we compare our simulator’s performance to the best metrics that could be achieved by a model knowing only the (x, e ) pair. Using the response probability for every observed pair gives an in-sample minimum achievable Log Loss (0.49) and Brier Score (0.17). Similarly, predicting the majority response for each pair establishes the maximum individual-decision accuracy (72.9%) for a model with access only to (x, e ). To assess out-of-sample generalization, we evaluated the simulator on a 20% held-out test set, partitioned at the text level. Benchmarked against these limits, our simulator performs very well, as shown in Figure 2(c), achieving a predictive accuracy of 71.0%, a Log Loss of 0.50, and a Brier Score of 0.18, alongside an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.04. To confirm this predictive power stems from the explanations rather than the source text, we compare the simulator against a text-only ablation in Appendix F.1. 4.5 REINFORCEMENT LEARNING 

Our RL architecture comprises a trainable explanation policy πθ , a frozen reference model πref , and the frozen decision simulator Sϕ. Both πθ and πref are initialized from the Qwen 3.6 27B model. The reference model πref serves a dual purpose: it enforces KL-divergence penalties to prevent policy collapse and acts as the representation extractor for the reward pipeline. In this stage, we use 25% of the pre-split dataset, ensuring the policy optimizes its explanation generation on a completely unseen distribution of texts. We optimize the policy πθ using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For a given input text x, the policy samples a group of G = 8 candidate explanations

# 5 EXPERIMENT 

We conduct a human decision experiment on AI text detection, comparing decision quality with HD-RL to that of three baseline post-training methods. 5.1 BASELINES 

Direct Preference Optimization on AI Feedback (DPO-RLAIF) The first baseline evaluates standard preference-based performance based on AI feedback. Following the explanation genera-tion stage, each text x is paired with two explanations, e1 and e2. While gathering large human pref-erence labels is expensive, recent research on Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) demonstrates that AI-generated preference labels can achieve alignment performance highly compa-rable with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Lee et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). Leveraging this insight, we use Qwen 3.6 as an LLM judge to evaluate the pairs and select the preferred explanation, yielding a dataset of (x, e chosen , e rejected ) tuples. We then optimize the policy model on these preferences using DPO. 

Verifiable-Reward RL (VR-GRPO) The second baseline evaluates outcome-based RL perfor-mance. During training, we prompt the policy model to generate a joint output: an explanation 

e ∈ E followed by an autonomous action aLLM ∈ A . The reward is computed by comparing the LLM’s action aLLM directly against the ground-truth state y ∈ Y . We assign a reward of +1 .0 if 

aLLM = y and a penalty of −1.0 if aLLM ̸ = y. The policy is optimized using GRPO. 

LLM Decision Calibration The third baseline serves as a direct ablation of the Human Deci-sion Collection stage, designed to test whether LLM simulations can be substituted for real human decision-making data. It replicates our framework exactly but replaces human decisions with syn-thetic LLM decisions. We prompt Qwen 3.6 to act as a human decision-maker, generating 21 , 263 

synthetic decisions over text-explanation pairs. The decision simulator Sϕ is then calibrated entirely on this synthetic distribution. 

Implementation Details All models, including the proposed framework and the respective base-lines, are initialized using the Qwen 3.6 27B model (Qwen Team, 2026). To enable parameter-efficient training, we apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to the policy models, configured with rank r = 16 and scaling factor α = 32 . Training is conducted across 4 NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs. We optimize the models for 3 epochs using a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5. The batch size is set to 8 per device, with a gradient accumulation step of 4, yielding an effective global batch size of 128. During the GRPO exploration phase, the generation temperature is set to 1.0, and the policy model samples a group size of 8 candidate generations per input state. We apply a KL divergence penalty with a coefficient of β = 0 .04 . A length penalty of γ = 0 .001 per token to encourage concise explanation. The LLM decision baseline serves as an ablation of the human decision collection phase, training the decision simulator entirely on synthetic LLM decisions. While this approach performed better than other baselines (yielding a raw overall human accuracy of 55.3% and a GLMM-estimated accuracy of 55.1%), it still fell over 8% short of HD-RL framework. To understand this performance gap, we compare the underlying decision-making patterns present in the human and synthetic decision datasets. As detailed in Appendix C.6, when an LLM acts as a proxy participant, it achieves an initial detection accuracy of 76.4%. Human participants, on the other hand, achieve near-random baseline accuracy. We hypothesize that this discrepancy exists be-cause the LLM already possesses the latent knowledge required to identify AI-generated text. While human decisions are inherently noisy and occasionally conflicting, they encode critical behavioral cues regarding which specific explanatory features help people to make better decisions and which do not. For instance, as demonstrated in Appendix C.5, human participants achieve higher accuracy when explanations focus on specific, class-appropriate lexical phrases. 6.3 ASYMMETRIES IN EXPLANATION GENERATION AND SELECTION

Although our explanation format strictly avoids revealing explicit predictions, the wording can still imply a human versus an AI origin by using terms more strongly associated with perceptions of humans versus machines. For example, the following example is an AI-generated text: 

“Starting a new embedded systems job soon and honestly I’m pumped but pretty nervous too. Feel a bit underqualified and could really use some advice from anyone who’s been there...” 

An explanation such as, ‘‘Look for neatly balanced emotional contrasts like ‘pumped but pretty nervous too,’’’ highlights structural symmetry, implic-itly suggesting AI authorship. Conversely, an explanation stating, ‘‘Look for informal emotive language like ‘pumped’ and ‘nervous,’’’ focuses on conversational imperfection, implicitly suggesting human authorship. During the initial explanation generation stage, we observed that LLMs frequently generated explanations focusing on human informality, regardless of the text’s actual label. Furthermore, in the DPO-RLAIF baseline, where an LLM acts as a judge to select preference pairs, this asymmetry is amplified. As detailed in Appendix C.4, even when evaluating AI texts, the LLM judge selected human-implying explanations in over 83% of cases. This lexical-focus analysis is one possible reason for the class asymmetry observed in downstream performance. Human participants evaluating texts with DPO-RLAIF explanations achieved an overall accuracy of just 51.9% (GLMM-estimated accuracy: 51.0%, 95% CI: [47.5%, 54.5%]), and their detection accuracy on AI texts was 45.7%.

# A LIMITATION 

While the goal of HD-RL is to improve human decision accuracy, our implementation relies on a logarithmic surrogate reward. Specifically, rather than directly optimizing the continuous expected accuracy qϕ(x, e, y ) = Sϕ(a = y | x, e ) predicted by the simulator, we optimize the transformed surrogate 1 + log( qϕ(x, e, y )) . This logarithmic transformation preserves the rank ordering of can-didate explanations for a fixed instance prior to clipping, it inherently penalizes low-qϕ explanations more heavily than a linear reward would. Future work should compare this surrogate against the direct optimization of predicted expected utility.

# D DESIGN AND JUSTIFICATION OF THE EXPLANATION FORMAT 

In this project, our objective is to provide useful diagnostic explanations without inducing overre-liance or fatigue. Our explanations format looks like: • Look for [specific linguistic or semantic feature]... • Look for [structural, rhetorical, or thematic pattern]... This specific format was selected for four primary reasons. (1) Reading complex explanations intro-duces cognitive fatigue, often causing users to ignore the text entirely. Our format minimizes reading time, presenting the user with straightforward criteria. (2) A common failure mode in human-AI col-laboration is when users focus more on the explanation rather than the source text. Our explanation forces the human to redirect their attention back to the text to verify if the suggested pattern actually exists. (3) Our explanation never reveals a decision to the user (e.g., it never says, “This text is AI-generated because...”). Stating a decision introduces severe anchoring bias, where humans un-consciously align their belief with the AI’s stated belief. Therefore, we let the human control the final decision, preserving ultimate human agency. (4) Our format is highly controllable. LLMs can easily follow this syntactic structure. Furthermore, we make downstream automated processes, such as BERTScore filtering, much more accurate and computationally stable. For specific examples of texts and explanations, refer to Appendix H.
