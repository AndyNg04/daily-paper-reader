Abstract 

Training long-horizon language model agents requires assigning credit to intermediate decisions from sparse terminal feedback. Within a finite group of sibling rollouts, useful action comparisons depend on their context, evidence for both alternatives, and how that evidence enters learning. We propose 

Matched-Context Group Policy Optimization (MCGPO) , a procedure for constructing weighted action preferences from already collected trajectories. MCGPO defines comparison contexts with pre-action state summaries and observed rubric progress, annotated by task rules. Two-action support selects among ordered exact and coarse matching rules. Fixed folds separate candidate decisions from the terminal outcomes used to orient their preferences, and support-dependent evidence weights scale an auxiliary pairwise objective alongside shared GraphGPO task learning. MCGPO reuses rollout data and each response’s original history, preserving critic-free training. Across six environment–backbone settings with three training seeds each, MCGPO improves mean success over matched GraphGPO: by 0.47–1.46 percentage points on ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B, and by 3.96 and 5.16 points on WebShop with Qwen3-4B and 8B. The ALFWorld gains build on baseline success above 92%, where remaining headroom is limited. Integrated success improves in three of four Qwen2.5 settings. Three-seed factorial controls support opposite-fold evidence and evidence weighting; joint state–rubric matching achieves the highest mean in all four representation-control settings.

1 Introduction 

Training large language model agents requires credit assignment : sparse terminal rewards must guide the intermediate actions that shape task outcomes (Zhou et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). In ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) and WebShop (Yao et al., 2022), success depends on a sequence of interdependent decisions. Group-relative reinforcement learning offers critic-free updates by comparing outcomes within a group of sampled responses or trajectories (Shao et al., 2024; Ahmadian et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). For multi-turn agents, GiGPO localizes these comparisons at recurring anchor states (Feng et al., 2025), while trajectory-graph methods extract step credit from collected transitions (Li et al., 2026b; Cheng et al., 2026). However, grouping by repeated states or transitions can compare decisions with different prior task progress, obscuring what their outcome contrast represents. This mismatch arises when the same state summary describes different remaining decision problems .A page, location, or inventory summary may omit progress established before the current choice. Consequently, an action taken after more prerequisites have been met can inherit a higher success rate, while a useful action taken earlier can receive weaker relative feedback. Their outcome contrast can mix prior progress with the contribution of the current action. A captured ALFWorld task illustrates this representational issue: two empty-handed visits to the same countertop share a coarse state key, although one follows acquiring and relocating a target object (Appendix D). Adding context can separate such decisions, but stricter conditions also reduce the repeated evidence available for comparing different actions. Recent work enriches comparison context to address this tension. HGPO combines groups at multiple history depths, and BiPACE uses policy representations and action-conditioned peer baselines (He et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Task structure offers another source of context: explicit criteria guide reward aggregation (Gunjal et al., 2025; Lv et al., 2026), policy stages and memory (Li et al., 2026a), and milestone-level credit (Wang et al., 2026d; Qian et al., 2026). However, an explicit preference between different actions also needs evidence for both alternatives. A richer context can leave one action poorly supported, so a larger outcome gap may reflect only a few trajectories. The remaining challenge is to select comparisons and weight their influence using the evidence available within a finite rollout group. This leads to our research question:  

> RESEARCH QUESTION

How can a finite group of existing rollouts supply supported local action preferences for policy learning? 

We introduce MCGPO (Matched-Context Group Policy Optimization) to construct auxiliary action preferences from collected sibling rollouts. The core idea is to select a comparison context supported by both actions before forming a weighted preference. Match groups pre-action decisions by state summaries and observed rubric progress, with a rule-derived frontier annotating progress. It tries ordered exact and coarse state rules, selecting the first with evidence for both actions. Label uses terminal outcomes from the opposite fixed fold to orient pairs and assign support-discounted evidence weights. Learn optimizes a weighted pairwise objective alongside the shared GraphGPO task objective. The labels express observational outcome contrasts under the rollout policy; their weights control each pair’s optimization contribution. MCGPO reuses rollout data and preserves critic-free training and each response’s original history. We evaluate MCGPO on ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B, using three training runs per setting. Mean success improves over matched GraphGPO in all four settings by 0.47–1.46 percentage points, including ALFWorld baselines already above 92% success. A three-seed WebShop extension yields gains of 3.96 and 5.16 points with Qwen3-4B and 8B, respectively. Integrated success over the full training horizon improves in three Qwen2.5 settings, with positive paired differences in each of their three seeds. Three-seed factorial controls support opposite-fold evidence and evidence weighting. Across all four representation-control settings, joint state–rubric conditioning raises three-seed mean success over both State-only and Rubric-only matching. These results demonstrate performance gains across the tested backbones and support joint state–rubric conditioning as an effective comparison design. 2Preprint. Under review. Our contributions are threefold: • Supported local preference construction. We combine explicit comparison contexts, two-action support checks, and opposite-fold terminal evidence within an existing rollout group. • Evidence-weighted policy learning. We connect these observational preferences to an auxiliary pairwise objective while retaining the shared task objective and original response histories. • Evaluation of the construction and its scope. Matched comparisons, supervision controls, and representation diagnostics assess overall utility and setting-dependent component effects. 

2 Related Work 

Agent training and group-based credit. Agent RL learns from interaction outcomes (Zhou et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025), with GRPO, RLOO, and DAPO estimating within-group advantages (Shao et al., 2024; Ahmadian et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). GiGPO groups anchor states; HGPO combines historical-context depths (Feng et al., 2025; He et al., 2026). SALT matches recent interaction histories, while GraphGPO and RTMC aggregate credit through transition graphs and shared-state trees (Li et al., 2026b; Cheng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). In the same sparse-reward, long-horizon setting, G 2PO merges identical observations into a global state-transition graph and standardizes temporal-difference errors across edges to assign action credit (Wang et al., 2026c). AT-GRPO adapts tree-expansion depth to dialogue stage (Peng et al., 2026); STAPO selects steps using state-normalized entropy and combines trajectory-aware rewards with trajectory-independent penalties (Qi et al., 2026). Other designs use peer baselines, progress signals, or hierarchical updates (Wang et al., 2026a; Fan & Liu, 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026). ECPO calibrates anchor-state credit using action-return shrinkage and reliability gates (Li et al., 2026c). MCGPO instead constructs action-pair supervision: pre-action state and rubric progress define comparability, two-action support selects the matching resolution, and opposite-fold outcomes label an auxiliary pairwise objective. 

Task-structured supervision. Task structure supports rubric rewards, exploration, and finite-state reward representations (Gunjal et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Toro Icarte et al., 2022). RubricEM organizes stages and memory; GEAR models criterion dependencies (Li et al., 2026a; Lv et al., 2026). SRaR, TRCA, and DRACO provide rubric-based step feedback (Xie et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Gandhi et al., 2026). BEACON combines milestone groups with reward shaping (Wang et al., 2026d); MileGPO infers milestones and traps from rollout graphs (Qian et al., 2026). MCGPO uses task-specific progress extractors to condition comparisons, with terminal outcomes supplying preference labels. SALT and MileGPO derive their comparison structure from histories and observed graphs. 

Process, preference, and value supervision. Process supervision extends terminal feedback (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024). DPO, REBEL, SimPO, and Contrastive Preference Learning optimize response or sequential-policy comparisons (Rafailov et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2024; Hejna et al., 2024); Step-DPO uses step preferences (Lai et al., 2024). Agent Q, VinePPO, OmegaPRM, and RTPO obtain intermediate evidence through search or additional continuations (Putta et al., 2024; Kazemnejad et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026d). POISE and SAPO learn value baselines or joint policy–value models (Choi et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2026). MCGPO reuses collected sibling rollouts, separating candidate decisions from label contributors with fixed folds. Appendix Table 4 compares these designs.

4 Experiments 

We organize the evaluation around four questions: whether MCGPO improves task completion and learning progress (RQ1), how comparison conditions, outcome evidence, and preference learning contribute (RQ2), how matched contexts produce weighted supervision on WebShop (RQ3), and whether the WebShop gains extend to Qwen3 backbones (RQ4). 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

4.1.1 Benchmarks and Models 

We evaluate household interaction in ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) and product search and purchase in WebShop (Yao et al., 2022). The main experiments use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024), with training seeds 0, 1, and 2. GraphGPO supplies the shared task-credit objective and matched baseline, measuring the effect of the complete rubric-conditioned preference branch. WebShop rubric extraction uses structured training information from the goal scorer, including attribute, option, and price matches and a pre-purchase preview score (Appendix A.1). Controls include GraphGPO with rubric-event shaping. For Qwen3-4B/8B, 

GraphGPO + shaping uses the same scorer information as MCGPO through training rewards, with three seeds per backbone. 

4.1.2 Implementation Details 

Within each backbone, MCGPO and GraphGPO use the same rollout, optimizer, and environment settings. Following the agent protocol of Feng et al. (2025), the Qwen2.5 policy conditions on the two most recent interaction steps and generates reasoning inside <think> tags followed by an action inside <action> tags. Each response is limited to 512 generated tokens. The environment horizons are 50 turns for ALFWorld and 15 for WebShop. Each policy update uses 16 task groups with eight sibling rollouts per group. We train for 250 policy updates with rollout temperature 1.0, a constant learning rate of 10 −6, weight decay 0.01, and KL coefficient 0.01. The 1.5B and 7B runs use two and four GPUs, respectively, with tensor-parallel sizes of two and four. MCGPO uses the fixed preference-construction settings in Table 6. The Qwen3 extension uses action-only responses with thinking disabled. Resource settings, environment prompts, and an illustrative rollout appear in Appendices B, F, and G, respectively. 5Preprint. 4.3 RQ2: Which Parts of the Supervision Design Matter? 

We test three design choices: task conditions for matching, the outcome evidence used to orient preferences, and the objective that transfers those preferences to the policy. Table 2(a) compares Full with four Qwen2.5-1.5B variants, using one training run per variant in each environment. 

Comparison conditions and outcome evidence. w/ Shuffled Rubric permutes pre-action (C, F )

labels among same-task decision rows, disrupting their alignment with actual progress. Success falls by 2.34 points on WebShop and 0.63 on ALFWorld. The Candidate-progress labels variant uses the candidate trajectories’ progress evidence in place of opposite-fold terminal outcomes to label comparisons, reducing success by 1.41 and 3.13 points. This joint replacement changes evidence source, label content, and available support. Its result favors the complete terminal-outcome preference design over the candidate-progress alternative. 

How the supervision enters training. w/o Pairwise Loss sets λpair = 0 while retaining preference diagnostics; its success drops by 1.41 and 0.78 points. GraphGPO + Rubric Shaping adds positive-progress rewards and regression penalties to the task rewards, retaining GraphGPO credit assignment with the pairwise loss disabled. This event-shaping baseline scores 8.28 and 2.66 points below Full. It compares two uses of the rubric channels under the stated shaping rule and coefficient. The three-seed Qwen3-4B/WebShop factorial controls in Table 2(b) show gains of 2.34–2.40 points from opposite-fold evidence and 1.61–1.67 points from evidence weighting, with all four contrasts positive in every seed. 5 Conclusion 

MCGPO constructs action preferences by matching state and rubric progress within existing rollout groups. Opposite-fold terminal outcomes supply preference directions, and an evidence-weighted pairwise loss complements the GraphGPO task objective. Across six environment–backbone settings, MCGPO improves three-seed mean success over matched GraphGPO, including ALFWorld baselines above 92% and Qwen3 WebShop gains of 3.96 and 5.16 points. Representation and factorial controls support joint context matching, opposite-fold evidence, and evidence weighting; scorer-matched comparisons favor MCGPO over reward shaping. Together, these results support extracting useful action-level supervision from existing rollout groups.

E Limitations 

MCGPO is evaluated on ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen backbones. Transfer to other domains requires suitable state summaries, rubric channels, and task rules; WebShop also uses structured goal-scorer information during training. Preferences are inferred from finite groups of observational rollouts: coarse matching can retain outcome-relevant differences, and terminal success depends on subsequent decisions as well as the matched action. Evidence weighting moderates the influence of 25 Preprint. Under review. sparse comparisons, while direct validation of local action preferences remains an important direction. Our main comparisons use three training seeds and repeated evaluation on a fixed validation set, so small gains retain statistical uncertainty. Evaluation with broader model families, more seeds, and independent test tasks would further assess generalization and statistical precision.
