# CALM: Decentralized Control of Cooperative Aerial Manipulation with Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 

Jiaxi Huo, Petre Ricioppo, Fausto Francesco Lizzio ∗

Abstract —Cooperative aerial manipulation is difficult under wind, payload motion, and noisy goal broadcasts because mul-tiple Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) are coupled through a shared payload. We present CALM (Cooperative Aerial Learned Manipulation), a decentralized controller for aerial manipulators carrying a basket while tracking a moving ground robot. CALM combines a parameter-shared Multi-Agent Re-inforcement Learning (MARL) policy, trained using Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO), with goal es-timation, per-UAV Extended State Observers (ESOs), basket-mass estimation, and anticipatory formation-yaw feedforward. The anticipatory formation-yaw feedforward uses estimated goal motion to rotate the formation before position error develops, reducing basket-yaw lag without adding net commanded force. A disturbance-conditioned critic supports centralized training while execution remains decentralized. Across held-out trajectories, CALM achieves lower mean position and orientation errors than all evaluated baselines, including the matched arm baseline (7.68 versus 10.95 cm and 3.3 versus 4.7 ◦), the cable-suspended realization, and two model-based controllers. Under unseen full-information-path degradation, CALM remains statistically comparable or superior to the arm baseline. Videos are available at https://petreric.github.io/CALM/. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Transporting a shared payload with multiple Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) is challenging because payload dy-namics and interaction forces couple UAV motions. These issues become even more prominent in outdoor agricultural environments, where the wind acts as a persistent disturbance, and variations in the payload mass distribution can cause the Center of Mass (CoM) to shift over time. Model-based cooperative transport controllers directly allo-cate payload wrenches and enforce coupling constraints, often with stability guarantees [1], [2]. However, they remain sen-sitive to model mismatch, compliance, and payload variation. Learning-based control complements them by training across plant and disturbance distributions, as in recent work combining Reinforcement Learning (RL) with model-reference adaptation [3], robust model predictive control [4], or control-barrier-constrained Multi-Agent RL (MARL) [5]. De-centralized MARL for cooperative transport has been explored solely on cable-suspended payloads [6], [7], whose unilateral interface leaves payload orientation weakly actuated.

Thus, in this work, we consider aerial manipulators, defined as UAVs equipped with an articulated arm that is rigidly coupled to the shared payload. This configuration provides direct control authority over the payload pose, but also intro-duces additional sources of complexity, including structural compliance, actuator saturation, and redundant interaction-force distribution. We consider an air-ground setting in which three aerial manipulators carry a collection basket, a payload whose mass varies with its contents, across terrain with limited ground accessibility, while a ground robot performs the field task. The aerial team tracks the robot from noisy goal broadcasts rather than a preplanned trajectory, exploiting its differential-drive motion for local coordinated-turn prediction. Three aerial manipulators provide the minimum non-collinear formation for planar-force and yaw-moment allocation, while allowing asymmetric load sharing under a moving basket CoM. We therefore present CALM (Cooperative Aerial Learned Manip-ulation), a MARL-based decentralized controller. Our contributions are as follows:

We formulate a decentralized cooperative aerial-manipulation task in which aerial manipulators carry a shared basket and track a ground robot from noisy goal broadcasts alone.

R ELATED WORK 

Classical work formulates cooperative aerial transport as grasp-map wrench allocation [1] or geometric control on the Special Euclidean group SE (3) [2], later extended to model uncertainty [8] and whole-body optimization [9]. RotorTM [10] cooperatively controls rigidly attached payloads. To reduce model dependence, data-driven aerial control learns residual dynamics for model-based prediction [11], [12] or maps observations directly to commands within adaptive or robust control [13]. Examples include Deep Adaptive Tra-jectory Tracking with reference feedforward and disturbance estimation [14], learned whole-body control for underactuated aerial manipulators [15], [16], and Gaussian-process compen-sation for leader-follower cooperative transport [17]. Decentralized MARL treats all UAVs as peers. Central-ized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) trains each agent’s policy, called the actor, with a critic that sees global information [18]. Privileged critics that see training-only information [19], distributional critics [20], and domain randomization [21] have been studied individually. The work of [6] shows communication-free full-pose control of a cable-suspended payload using shared local policies. CrazyMARL [7] extends it to direct motor commands and slack-taut cable transitions. However, both [6], [7] retain passive cable inter-faces that transmit tension only, whereas rigid arms transmit bidirectional forces and moments [22], [23]. Moreover, both provide the payload goal pose directly to the UAVs. CALM instead combines explicit goal, mass, and distur-bance estimates with a shared decentralized policy coordinat-ing UAVs and arms without centralized online optimization. III. B. Control objective 

The ground robot generates online a goal gt = ( pgt , ψ gt ) for the position and the yaw of the basket. The desired altitude is fixed a priori , while the desired pitch and roll angles are zero. However, wind, CoM torque, and unequal arm loads may tilt the basket, so the full orientation is tracked. Each UAV receives gt only through a broadcast with additive Gaussian position and yaw noise of standard deviations σp and σψ , and computes its commands on board. Then, the control objective is the decentralized tracking of the basket pose under the considered disturbances. Each arm tip tracks its attachment point on a basket at gt, and each UAV chooses its own position. IV. CALM C ONTROL AND LEARNING ARCHITECTURE

Since the equivalent wind speed matches the total applied force to the system drag, the basket receives a smaller force share than under physical wind. Arm-platform trials are paired under matched resets and disturbances, whereas the cable code of [6], [27] yields distinct episodes per seed. The 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) use 20 000 percentile-bootstrap resamples of paired differences.

B. Differential-drive ground robot tracking 

CALM attains the lowest position and orientation errors on every trajectory family in Table II, and paired comparisons favor it over Dec-MARL (arm) in all 6 families. The margin is smallest on the boustrophedon, whose long straight phases suit both arm controllers, and largest on the circle, perimeter, and contour, where sustained turns and U-turns dominate (Fig. 3). Altitude error increases the 3-D position metric of Dec-MARL (cable) by 6–9 cm relative to the planar trace, and its orientation error on the circle reaches 123 .2◦ versus 2.4◦–

4.4◦ for CALM. The model-based controllers remain near 53 and 74 cm in position, with orientation errors of 75 ◦ and 92 ◦, respectively. Their low across-family SDs of 1.9 and 1.5 cm indicate that they fail consistently. The anticipatory feedforward alone reduces the basket orientation error in the training distribution by 10–16% at the median and 8–18% at p90, mainly during turns, with no consistent position effect. 

C. Wind and moving-CoM sweeps 

In Fig. 4(a) and Fig. 5(a,b), both arm controllers degrade with wind, and CALM’s margin over Dec-MARL (arm) widens. CALM remains lower at every level tested, with every 95% CI excluding zero. At an equivalent wind speed of 10.0 m/s, Dec-MARL (cable) is less accurate than CALM, with a median UAV tilt of 47 ◦ and uncontrolled basket orientation. From 0.05 to 0.20 m CoM deviation (Fig. 5(c)), CALM’s position error rises moderately, while Dec-MARL (cable) degrades steeply in position and remains at least 11 × worse in orientation. At 0.20 m, Dec-MARL (arm) is marginally better than CALM, with a paired difference of -0.08 cm (CI [-0.10, -0.06]). Clipping the CoM offset at ¯c places both arm controllers in the same torque-saturation regime. As a result, CALM’s largest margins arise on turn-rich trajectories and in strong wind, and both controllers hold basket orientation. D. Arm joint-group ablation 

All actuated joints move under nominal disturbance with mean torques close to their limits, and at 0.20-m CoM devi-ation the telescoping brackets mostly sit at their travel limits. Table III reports the effect of unpowering each joint group by zeroing its position-control stiffness over n = 64 paired trials. Unpowering the full arm raises position and orientation errors the most, mainly through wrist roll and pitch, and unpowering only the shoulder and elbow still degrades both. Wrist yaw matters less despite frequent saturation, as formation rotation provides the basket yaw (Sec. IV-B). The elbow is active, but unpowering it alone has no significant effect, as the attachment height follows the desired altitude fixed a priori (Sec. III-B).
