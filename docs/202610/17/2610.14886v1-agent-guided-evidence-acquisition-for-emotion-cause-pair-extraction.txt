## Abstract 

Emotion-Cause Pair Extraction (ECPE) requires identifying the causes associated with emotions expressed in a conversation. Accu-rate extraction can depend on evidence that is not initially available, making it important to decide when further information is worth acquiring. We study agent-guided evidence acquisition for ECPE, where a student agent selectively accesses expert judgments for borderline relations on both sides of its validation-selected F1 de-cision boundary and revises only queried predictions. Through controlled comparisons under different information-access condi-tions, we examine how delegation granularity, query selection, and response integration affect prediction quality. At a nominal 10% query rate, selective fusion improves mean pair F1 by 2.64–3.88 points over the student in our main experiments, reaching 65.14 on ECF and 49.74 on MEC4 across model families. Our analysis suggests that greater access does not always translate into better cause identification. These findings motivate acquisition strategies that consider both the relevance of newly available evidence and its effect on prediction revision, providing an empirical basis for more effective and efficient evidence acquisition in ECPE. Code will be released upon acceptance.

## 1 Introduction 

Emotion–cause pair extraction (ECPE) identifies utterance pairs linking emotions to their causes in conversations [28, 32, 34]. These relations inform empathetic responses [9] and connect reactions to earlier events, multiple causes, or later explanations [25]. This study considers a student model with limited expert access. Assistance can address a specific relation, all candidate causes of a target, or an entire dialogue. Grouping requests by target creates an opportunity cost (Fig-ure 1). An answer spent on a confidently judged relation is unavail-able for an uncertain relation elsewhere in the dialogue. Delegation by relation lets the student distribute its allowance across targets, but an expert can still repeat a student error or overturn a correct judgment. Choosing when to seek help must therefore account for its likely benefit [2]. We compare delegation units and query selec-tion while holding the student, expert, response rule, and answer budget fixed. Learning to defer formalizes decision risk given an expert’s ca-pabilities [21, 22]. Language model cascades and learned routers allocate calls by predicted quality and cost [1, 3, 23]. In ECPE, a tar-get can have several candidate causes with different levels of uncer-tainty. Pair F1 may also favor a threshold different from probability 0.5 [18]. ## 2 Related Work 

Emotion–cause relation extraction. ECPE jointly identifies emo-tion and cause utterances [32], with conversational settings adding speaker interactions and multimodal evidence [29]. MECPE-2steps detects these utterances before filtering candidate pairs [28]. HiLo combines holistic interaction with label constraints [15], while M3F introduces Chinese MEC 4 with a memory-inspired framework [30]. M3HG models emotion and cause contexts with heterogeneous graphs [16], and SCALE separates their semantics before align-ment [20]. BridgeProt-SFT generates dialogue-level pair sets and locally verifies causes for each proposed emotion turn, using a gen-erative objective rather than thresholded pair classification [33]. ECFlow combines appraisal-guided generation with graph rewards for triplet consistency [17]. HCPRA organizes agents through hi-erarchical cognition, perception, and reasoning [27], while DEC-Debate resolves model disagreements over emotions and pairs [31]. 

Pair judgement and verification. ECPE-2D learns joint representa-tions for pair classification [4], while PRG-MoE uses relation-guided classifiers for conversational pairs [12]. GMEC supplements gener-ative extraction with GPT-3.5 pair verification [13]. Feng et al. [6] compare generation, target selection, and pair judgement, showing that explicit pair queries recover relations omitted by generation. Their auxiliary retriever revisits the highest-ranked rejected candi-date for each target. These studies motivate the pairwise foundation used here. The present study examines how to allocate limited ex-pert access over these decisions. 

Delegation and model cascades. Learning to defer accounts for expert capability [22], including consistency guarantees for rout-ing to multiple experts [21]. AutoMix relies on self-verification [1], RouteLLM learns from preference data [23], and GraphRouter mod-els task–query–model interactions [5]. Unified routing and cascad-ing estimate quality and cost [3]. Conformal Arbitrage controls risk across competing objectives [24], and SafeRoute selectively invokes a larger safety guard [14]. Allocation can also operate be-low the level of a whole answer. CITER routes individual generated tokens [38], while selective depth reading allocates sentiment com-putation per aspect [35]. The unit studied here is an independently scored emotion–cause relation within a dialogue. 

Agent decisions and external assistance. Router-R1 learns when to invoke models over multiple rounds and how to aggregate their responses [37]. RouterHGC selects collaboration modes, roles, and model configurations [36]. In human–AI moderation, performance predictors use model outputs to decide when to escalate [2]. Ques-tion answering with tools must also account for uncertainty in the returned information [19]. We likewise separate decisions about requesting assistance from decisions about integrating the response. 

Calibration and task adaptation. Calibration relates confidence to observed outcomes [8], while F1 maximization determines a threshold for the task [18]. Models receive low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [11]. A secondary knowledge distillation (KD) condition [10] tests consultation after additional training with the same expert. 

## 3 Selective Expert Consultation 

The evidence acquired here is an expert’s judgment about a relation in the given dialogue. The student agent first predicts the relations and then selects where to request assistance. Its state contains the relation graph, student scores, and query allowance. The expert receives the full dialogue and answers only the selected relation questions. Those answers can revise the queried predictions, as shown in Figure 2. 

## 3.1 Relations as the Unit of Delegation 

Let 𝐷 = (𝑢 1, . . . , 𝑢 𝑛 ) contain utterance text, speaker identities, and turn indices. A relation 𝑟 = (𝑖, 𝑗 ) states that 𝑢 𝑗 triggers or explains a non-neutral emotion in 𝑢 𝑖 . The decision interface draws on pair classification [4, 12] and LLM verification [6, 13]. Every utterance is considered as a target, with candidate causes 

C( 𝐷 ) = {( 𝑖, 𝑗 ) : max (1, 𝑖 − 5) ≤ 𝑗 ≤ min (𝑛, 𝑖 + 2)} . (1) This window includes five preceding utterances, the target itself, and two following utterances ( −5 ≤ 𝑗 − 𝑖 ≤ 2). It restricts the candidate pairs. Both models still receive the full dialogue and the indexed target and candidate for every question, without gold emotion labels. Self-causes, later explanations, multiple causes, and targets with no cause are all allowed. The student scores every relation in C( 𝐷 ). Delegation by target or dialogue requests all candidate answers in that group, whereas delegation by relation can distribute queries across targets. All three use the same pairwise expert interface. Evaluation retains every gold relation, counting causes outside the window as false negatives.

## 4.5 Uncertainty and Robustness 

Unless noted otherwise, tables report means and sample standard deviations (SD) across three student seeds. Paired 95% bootstrap intervals use 2,000 dialogue resamples, retaining all predictions and full gold counts. Group comparisons average differences across seeds within the same resample. The intervals measure variation across dialogues for the trained models and fitted policies. SD mea-sures variation across training seeds. Comparisons across families exchange the expert while preserv-ing student queries. Calibration sensitivity uses 200 resamples of validation dialogues per student, refitting maps, thresholds, and query bands. Identity checks count unqueried changes and repro-duce the student at zero queries or with the student itself as expert (Appendix M). Evaluation of multiple causes measures exact re-covery of the full annotated cause set, including gold outside the window.

## 5.2 Query Center and Sides Matter 

F1 thresholds below probability 0 .5 shift the relevant uncertainty region (Table 1(b)). With dialogue query counts fixed, boundary ranking outperforms entropy at nominal 10% access (Table 4(a)). Se-lection benefits from locating decisions near the threshold actually used for extraction. Boundary is competitive with Learned, whose extra features offer no consistent allocation advantage. Paired intervals favor Boundary in three settings and include zero for Qwen/MEC 4. Its mean advantage at 10% also holds under global allocation and direct replacement (Appendix E). The gain varies with the allowance, with Qwen/ECF nearly tied at 5%. Both sides of the boundary receive substantial access (Table 1(b)). Bilateral selection outperforms all restricted selectors at matched actual counts (Table 4(b)), allowing expert help to address missed causes and false explanations together. 

## 5.3 Grouping Queries Has an Opportunity Cost 

Delegating a whole group ties a useful query to other relations the student might not choose individually. Under the common pairwise expert interface, relation selection yields higher F1 than target or dialogue selection (Table 5). Its advantage persists when its query count is reduced to the exact number used by each group policy (Appendix D.1). Grouping therefore affects the value of the queried set even after accounting for unused budget. Relation selection also improves exact recovery of multi-cause sets (Table 5(b) and Appendix D.2), extending the benefit of individ-ual query allocation to complete sets despite independent expert judgments.

## 5.5 Revision Value by Location and Rule

Consultation remains useful after further ordinary SFT. Table 8(a) covers all four dataset–model settings at seed 42, retaining the original expert from the first pass. ## 6 Discussion 

The results motivate aligning acquisition with the deployed decision threshold and evaluating repairs together with damage. Student uncertainty guides selection, while the response rule determines which expert answers change the output. Overall expert F1 alone captures neither interaction. These considerations connect query allocation to its effect on the final task decisions. The granularity comparison holds expert answers fixed through a common pairwise interface. Mandatory groups then spend part of the allowance on relations that individual selection would skip. A joint cause-set interface could change both answer quality and group cost. Runtime also reflects dialogue coverage, batching, prefix reuse, and model residency. Delegation should therefore be eval-uated against the available response interface and its execution costs. 

Limitations. The current study uses textual transcripts, leaving acoustic and visual evidence outside the consultation process. Incor-porating audio and video would require deciding which modality to inspect as well as which relation to query. Experiments restrict candidate offsets to [− 5, 2] while retaining the full dialogue as context. Relations outside this window cannot be recovered and remain false negatives. Performance and query allocation over the full 𝑛 2 candidate space remain untested. Future work will examine multimodal consultation and adaptive candidate expansion, with acquisition costs measured for the additional inputs. 

## 7 Conclusion 

Selective consultation improves ECPE by directing limited expert assistance to borderline relations. Boundary selection is competitive with learned routing across response rules and training conditions. With a common pairwise expert, relation selection outperforms target and dialogue grouping, while bilateral queries address both error directions. Revision analysis links these gains to query loca-tion and response integration.

## A.3 Response rules and statistical reporting 

F denotes fusion with equal logit weights at the unchanged student threshold. R denotes direct replacement at the expert threshold. Both act only on queried relations. Random F and Random R average 20 random draws with exactly the selective policy’s query count in each dialogue. A selector sees the student’s scores before querying and receives expert scores only at the selected positions. Group summaries are arithmetic means and sample standard deviations (SD) over three students. Random policy results are first averaged within each student. Paired intervals use 2,000 bootstrap resamples of dialogues, retaining every candidate decision and the complete gold count. For a comparison with random consultation, each resample averages F1 over the same 20 random draws before subtraction. These intervals describe dialogue variation conditional on the trained models and frozen calibration. The standard devia-tions describe variation across student training seeds.

## D Granularity and Complete Cause Sets D.1 The opportunity cost of grouped answers 

Target actions request all candidate answers for one target. Dialogue actions request all candidates in a conversation. Every granularity uses the same pairwise expert interface. Group priority is either minimum boundary distance or mean 𝜎 (−| 𝑧 𝑠 − 𝜏 𝑠 |) , selected on validation for each budget. A group is admitted only if it fits the remaining global allowance. Oversized groups are skipped, leaving later groups eligible. Relation selection retains its advantage when matched to the exact number of answers used by each group policy (Table S11). All paired intervals are positive. Requiring a whole group to reach a useful relation lowers the quality of the selected set even at the same actual access.

## E.2 Learning objectives and fitting 

The router uses histogram gradient boosting with squared-error loss, at most 255 bins per feature, 120 iterations, at most seven leaves per tree, minimum leaf size 40, learning rate 0.05, 𝐿 2 regular-ization 1, random seed 3417, and no early stopping. Five GroupK-Fold partitions separate dialogues. Out-of-fold validation F1 selects between two training targets under the relevant response rule, ac-cess constraint, and budget. A tie selects count improvement. The final model is refitted on all validation dialogues. Student and ex-pert score calibrations remain the common frozen validation maps throughout. ## F Query Sides and Composition Controls F.1 Why retain both sides of the boundary? 

Negative-only (rejected) and positive-only (accepted) policies rank eligible relations by distance to the student threshold. The restricted policy for rejected candidates retains at most the nearest one for each target. If a dialogue has fewer eligible relations than its al-lowance, both the restricted selector and its bilateral comparator use that smaller count. At the primary 10% allowance, every paired interval favors query-ing both sides of the threshold (Table S19). Restricting queries to rejected predictions leaves false positives untouched. Restricting them to accepted predictions leaves missed causes unrecovered. Limiting queries to one rejected candidate per target also loses in all four settings at the same actual answer count. 

## F.2 Smaller and larger allowances 

Tables S20 and S21 examine this pattern at other budgets. At 5%, bilateral selection has a higher mean in every comparison. Its small-est difference is against positive-only Qwen/ECF, at 0.28 F1 with interval [− 0.15 , 0.74 ]. At 20%, every interval favors bilateral selec-tion. Access to both repair directions becomes more valuable as the allowance grows.

## I Location and Specificity of Expert Revisions I.1 Net value across boundary-distance bands 

Table S28 reports repairs and damage per 100 queried relations in distance bins defined on validation. A repair corrects a student error. Damage turns a correct student decision into an error. Their difference gives net correct revisions per 100 queries. The table pools correction counts across three students and reports the mean bin size. Near the boundary, answers often change predictions and repairs outnumber damage (Table S28). Farther away, fusion usually leaves the prediction unchanged because a larger adjustment is required to cross the student’s threshold. The repair counts also distinguish precision and recall effects. At nominal 10%, Qwen/ECF fusion adds 84.7 true positives and removes 177.7 false positives on average, while losing 37.0 true positives and introducing 99.0 false positives. Net correct revisions count these changes equally. Pair F1 weights their effect through the existing true positive, false positive, and gold counts. Replacement changes more confident predictions farther from the boundary, including correct student decisions. For Qwen/MEC 4,the 10–20% bin has 3.30 repairs and 5.16 damaging revisions per 100 queries. This negative balance explains why widening access can reduce the benefit of replacement. The F1 effect also depends on whether each revision changes a true positive or false positive. 

## I.2 Full fusion outside the query band 

For all six MEC 4 students, 20% selective fusion and full fusion at the student threshold produce identical prediction sets (Table S29). The plateau reflects unchanged predictions outside the band, not offsetting repairs and damage. On ECF, the few differences outside the band include both repairs and damage. Full access recovers some true positives and loses others, while also removing and adding false positives. The small net F1 change combines those opposing revisions. Replacement can affect more decisions because it does not average the expert score with the student score. 

## I.3 Linking queries to expert answers 

The control in Table S30 keeps each dialogue’s selected positions fixed and permutes the returned expert scores among them. The score multiset and query count remain unchanged. Results are first averaged over 50 permutations per student, then summarized as the mean and sample SD across the three student seeds in each setting. Permuting responses lowers F1 below the student in every group. Useful query positions cannot compensate for answers assigned to the wrong relations. Matched random selection and response permutation test complementary requirements. The former varies where help is requested, while the latter varies whether the response addresses that request.
