Abstract 

AI agents depend on repeated decisions to ac-complish tasks, with large language models (LLMs) commonly serving as their core de-cision makers. The recent emergence of Jev offers an opportunity to make these decisions faster and at lower cost. However, it remains unclear whether such local savings can be trans-lated into efficient and reliable task comple-tion. In this paper, we investigate this effi-ciency transfer across five agents in two repre-sentative application settings, embodied tasks in ALFWorld and mobile app use in Android-World. Across 4,500 task executions, we jointly evaluate task effectiveness, decision-level and task-level resource use, and execution behavior. Experimental results show that Jev integration substantially reduces decision cost and latency across all five agents, with task-level gains vary-ing by workflow. Task success remains close to baseline levels on ALFWorld and declines more substantially in two mobile agents. Aux-iliary reasoning and additional interactions ab-sorb decision savings, while failed recovery and premature completion limit successful delivery. Mean cost per successful outcome increases in one agent even as spending per attempt falls. These findings highlight the importance of pre-serving progress, supporting recovery, and ver-ifying completion when integrating efficient decision models into agent workflows. Their practical value should be assessed through suc-cessful task delivery, recovery behavior, and resources per delivered outcome. 

1 Introduction 

AI agents pursue goals through interaction with their environments, with LLMs supporting this process in different scenarios (Yang et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Progress toward a goal depends on repeatedly interpreting observations and deciding what to do next (Yao et al., 2023). Some decisions require open-ended code or text generation, while others involve se-lecting among available tools, interface elements, or admissible commands (Wang et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024; Shridhar et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2025). These bounded choices are repeated throughout a task, and make both action-selection quality and resource efficiency important to overall agent per-formance (Kapoor et al., 2025). The resources spent on these decisions should reflect their reasoning demands. On S1-Bench, Zhang et al. (2026b) find that large reasoning mod-els can overthink simple questions while still mak-ing errors, showing that extended reasoning does not consistently yield accurate, economical judg-ments. This finding motivates examining models designed for focused decisions. Jev, recently re-leased by TypeSafe AI as a System One Model ,provides instruction-defined judgments through typed outputs, including probabilities over supplied choices (Almeida, 2026; TypeSafe AI, 2026b). The company positions it as a fast, inexpensive decision model (Almeida, 2026). Its choice interface offers an opportunity to reduce the cost and latency of re-peated action selection in AI agents. Whether these local savings translate into efficient and reliable task completion remains unclear. Answering this question requires following de-cisions through complete agent workflows. Ac-tions shape subsequent observations and choices, so each decision affects the conditions for the next (Ross et al., 2011). An executable action can move an agent away from its goal, requiring fur-ther reasoning or recovery whose cost may offset local savings. Reflection extends these dependen-cies across attempts (Shinn et al., 2023), and can-didate construction shapes the choices available to the decision model (Yang et al., 2024). Task efficiency therefore depends on auxiliary reason-ing, interaction overhead, and the sequence of ac-tions performed. Failed attempts also consume re-sources without delivering the requested outcome. arXiv:2610.13092v1 [cs.AI] 9 Oct 2026 Evaluating agent performance requires connecting decision-level savings to task success, overall re-source use, and resources per successful outcome, supported by repeated evaluation and execution analysis (Kapoor et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025). To provide this evidence, we conduct a compar-ative empirical study of AI agents with Jev-based action selection. The study covers five agent fami-lies in two representative application settings, em-bodied tasks in ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) and mobile app use in AndroidWorld (Rawles et al., 2025). Within each family, we compare the original configuration with a Jev-integrated variant, using text-only observations to match Jev’s supported inputs (TypeSafe AI, 2026a). Each configuration runs three times on the same tasks, yielding 4,500 task executions. We jointly evaluate task effec-tiveness, decision-level and task-level resource use, and execution behavior. Paired task-level analyses estimate uncertainty in the observed differences, while systematic trace coding characterizes fail-ure and recovery patterns. Comparisons with the original configurations assess integration effects. Our results show that Jev substantially reduces decision cost and latency across all five agents, with task-level gains varying by workflow. Task success remains close to baseline levels on ALF-World and declines more substantially in two mo-bile agents. Mean task costs fall across all agents, while auxiliary reasoning and additional interac-tions limit overall speed gains. Failed recovery and premature completion further constrain suc-cessful delivery. Lower spending per attempt can therefore coexist with higher cost per successful outcome. These findings highlight the importance of preserving progress, supporting recovery, and verifying completion when integrating efficient de-cision models into agent workflows. Their practical value should be assessed through successful task delivery and the resources it requires. In summary, we make the following contributions: • We provide an empirical study of AI agents with Jev-based action selection across five agent families and two benchmarks, covering 4,500 task executions. • We characterize how decision-level savings translate into task cost, latency, and resources per successful outcome across workflows. • We systematically annotate execution traces and analyze recovery outcomes to identify fail-ure and recovery patterns. The experimental code is available at https:// anonymous.4open.science/r/jev-emp-5BF4/ .

2 Background 

Jev. TypeSafe AI introduced Jev in September 2026 as a System One Model for fast, structured decisions in software automation (Almeida, 2026). Its API maps a shared state and instruction-defined questions to typed choices, scores, or binary prob-abilities. Questions are evaluated independently, and choice questions return probabilities over sup-plied candidates (TypeSafe AI, 2026b). Entailment-based classifiers and GLiClass support labels sup-plied at inference time (Yin et al., 2019; Stepanov et al., 2025). Jev’s choice interface accepts candi-date descriptions and instructions that define the decision to make. An agent can supply its goal, observations, and available actions as context for each selection. This connects the typed interface to repeated decisions within a workflow. Early Jev studies examine probabilistic coding of crash nar-ratives (Rafe and Das, 2026) and adoption across public software projects (Ling et al., 2026). We examine how its structured decisions interact with reasoning, environment feedback, and repeated ac-tion selection during task execution. 

3 Related Work 

Agent Action Selection. LLM agents repeatedly map goals, observations, and interaction history to actions through reasoning and environment inter-action (Yao et al., 2023). Their interfaces expose commands, UI operations, and tools across em-bodied, mobile, and software tasks (Wang et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). Tool use requires deciding which APIs to invoke, when to call them, and which arguments to supply (Schick et al., 2023). A finite set of action types can still re-quire open-ended arguments, so bounded selection and generation coexist within one agent. The sur-rounding workflow determines which candidates are available and how the selected action is exe-cuted. Feedback, reflection, and memory condition later decisions on earlier outcomes (Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026). We focus on the bounded-selection component, exam-ining how a specialized decision model interacts with the workflow’s reasoning, environment inter-action, and recovery mechanisms. 

Efficient Decision Models and Routing. Rout-ing and cascades assign requests across models using difficulty, preferences, or confidence to bal-ance cost and response quality (Ding et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025). Agent systems extend model allocation to collaboration modes and roles through MasRouter (Yue et al., 2025). EvoRoute uses experience and environment feed-back for step-level backbone selection, evaluat-ing performance, cost, and latency (Zhang et al., 2026a). These methods optimize which model a system invokes. We study specialized bounded action selection across five agent families, char-acterizing when decision-level savings transfer to task-level efficiency considering auxiliary reason-ing, interaction length, recovery, and completion. 

Agent Evaluation and Sequential Reliabil-ity. Benchmarks assess task outcomes across embodied, web, mobile, desktop, and software-engineering environments (Shridhar et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024b; Rawles et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2024; Jimenez et al., 2024). ALFWorld and An-droidWorld provide the outcomes for our house-hold and mobile evaluations. Resource-aware eval-uation considers accuracy and expenditure, while repeated trials assess consistency (Kapoor et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025). Sequential errors can com-pound as earlier decisions alter the states encoun-tered later (Ross et al., 2011). Feedback, reflection, and tree search support correction across attempts or alternative action paths (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024a). Failure analysis identifies difficul-ties in long-term reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following (Liu et al., 2024). Trajectory diagnosis evaluates backtracking, observation read-ing, and self-verification in web agents (Ou et al., 2025). We connect decision-level resources to total expenditure and resources per delivered success, in-cluding failed attempts, and characterize recovery and completion errors through trace coding. 

4 Empirical Study Design 

Selecting among finite candidates matches Jev’s stated focus on bounded judgments (TypeSafe AI, 2026b). We therefore assess the benefits and limita-tions of Jev integration through task effectiveness, execution efficiency, inference cost, and consis-tency across runs, examining how decision-level savings translate into successful task delivery. 

4.1 Research Questions RQ1: Effectiveness. How does integrating Jev into LLM-based agents affect task success relative to their original configurations? 

RQ2: Efficiency Transfer. When do decision-level efficiency gains transfer to the overall task-level efficiency? 

RQ3: Failure and Recovery. What failure pat-terns and recovery behaviors characterize agents with Jev-based action selection? 

4.2 Experimental Setup Benchmarks. We study action selection in two complementary interaction environments. Android-World (Rawles et al., 2025) covers mobile appli-cation tasks, where agents act on changing UI el-ements and application states. We use text-based UI observations and one fixed parameter instance for each of its 116 task types. ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) covers household tasks through tex-tual observations and admissible commands. We use its text environment and all 134 tasks in the 

valid_unseen split. Together, these settings cover UI interaction and command-based control with language-based observations. 

Agent Configurations. Since Jev accepts only text input currently, we select text-only agent con-figurations to match input modalities across vari-ants. AndroidWorld in-cludes T3A (Rawles et al., 2025), AutoDroid (Wen et al., 2024), and Droidrun’s FastAgent (Droidrun, 2026), covering UI action generation, candidate-event selection, and tool invocation. 4.3 Evaluation and Metrics Repeated Runs and Aggregation. To capture consistency and variability in agent execution, we evaluate each agent configuration three times on the same tasks, resetting environment state and agent memory between runs. We retain all task– run records and report the mean and sample stan-dard deviation of each metric across runs. Paired analyses preserve task correspondence. We report 

J minus control differences with 95% task-cluster bootstrap intervals, resampling tasks and retaining their three runs together in 20,000 replicates. See Appendix A.2 for details. 

Effectiveness. Our primary metric is task suc-cess rate, the proportion of tasks completed success-fully according to the benchmark’s official evalua-tor. For AndroidWorld, we additionally report the official task score to capture partial completion. 

Efficiency and Cost. We measure token usage, inference cost, and elapsed time at both task and decision levels. Task-level token usage and cost include all model calls, including auxiliary gener-ation and reflection, while decision-level metrics cover only action-selection calls. Inference costs are calculated using fixed model-specific input and output token prices. We report each metric over all tasks and over successful tasks only, with the latter including only decisions from successful ex-ecutions. Task-level means divide the correspond-ing totals by the number of tasks, and decision-level means divide them by the number of action-selection calls within each scope. We also report cost and time per delivered success by dividing the corresponding totals across all attempts, including failures, by the number of successful executions. 

5 Results and Analysis 

5.1 RQ1: Effectiveness An uneven effectiveness gap. On ALFWorld, 

ReAct-J and Reflexion-J achieve 96.02% and 98.01% success, trailing their original configura-tions by 1.49 and 1.74 percentage points (pp), re-spectively (Figure 1). With the shared candidate interface in C, these gaps narrow to 1.00 and 0.00 pp. On AndroidWorld, Jev trails T3A, AutoDroid, and FastAgent by 13.22, 2.30, and 18.10 pp, respec-tively, with both AutoDroid variants below 20% success. Comparisons with original configurations capture system-level integration effects, including an interface change on ALFWorld. AndroidWorld agents already use predefined action lists. Jev thus retains nearly all success under ALFWorld’s shared interface but incurs larger losses in T3A and FastA-gent. The 95% task-cluster bootstrap intervals for J–B success differences are entirely negative for T3A and FastAgent and include zero for the other agents and both ALFWorld J–C comparisons (Ta-ble 10). Appendix B reports supplementary results.

Errors become failures through termination or failed recovery. Trajectories connect these task differences to goal interpretation and correction. In the Wi-Fi task, Jev selects an ambiguously labeled switch and declares completion with the goal un-met. This terminal judgment ends the opportunity for correction. In ALFWorld, selecting a hand-towel for a cloth leads the workflow to continue reasoning on the wrong object. The initial error is amplified as the agent repeatedly preserves the mis-taken premise. These cases show how a decisive stopping error can end a short task, while an earlier selection error can develop into sustained failure through unsuccessful correction. Reflexion demon-strates recovery’s contribution at the task level, as Jev’s first-trial success advantage becomes a final deficit after further trials (Table 4). Its selection and auxiliary LLM reflection jointly determine this outcome. Effective decision making consequently requires both selecting a goal-appropriate action and revising previous choices when feedback re-veals a mismatch. 

Jev suits clearly specified decision roles. Jev is most promising when the decision input makes the goal, current state, and action prerequisites ex-plicit, so selecting an action maps an understood state to a suitable next operation. Promising appli-cations include workflow routing, API or tool selec-tion, and routine software operations with identifi-able targets and observable completion conditions. Workflows involving ambiguous goals, incomplete observations, or repeated sub-goal revision place greater demands on interpretation and recovery, the capabilities implicated in the observed mobile deficits. The effectiveness trade-off is strong execu-tion within well-specified states versus adaptability when the agent must revise its understanding of the task. The strongest candidates for Jev have explicit conditions for choosing, checking, and completing an action.

Tokens per decision increase by 53.5% –134.9%, as selection consumes observations, choices, and history. Jev’s lower input price and zero output charge offset this increase.

6 Discussion 

Model development. Our findings point toward decision models trained for the consequences of sequential choices. Jev’s fast selection coexists with errors in target interpretation, correction, and termination, and these errors can consume its re-source savings through prolonged execution. For Jev and Jev-like models, a promising direction is to retain concise numerical outputs while strength-ening sensitivity to goals, action prerequisites, and contradictory feedback. Training on contrasting successful and failed trajectories could target deci-sions that restore progress or correctly end a task. Another useful objective is calibrated confidence in whether any available action advances the goal, giving the model a basis for requesting more in-formation or reconsidering its choices. Progress should be assessed through successful task delivery and the resources it requires, alongside decision accuracy and response time. 

Agentic system design. The engineering impli-cation is to treat decision making as an explicit system component with observable inputs, out-comes, and costs. Candidate interfaces should ex-pose target identity, prerequisites, and expected state changes, with feedback indicating whether the last action advanced the goal. Checks should focus on completion judgments and repeated lack of progress, the points where our traces show fail-ures becoming terminal or persistent. Additional observation or reasoning can then be directed to unresolved decisions. This allocation matters be-cause auxiliary calls and interaction overhead can absorb the savings from faster selection. Deploy-ment decisions should jointly consider the original runtime bottleneck, changes in interaction length, and expenditure per successful outcome. Jev and Jev-like models are consequently most promising in systems that make routine choices inexpensive while concentrating verification and recovery on decisions that threaten completion. 

7 Conclusion 

We evaluate AI agents with Jev-based action se-lection to examine whether faster, cheaper deci-sions translate into efficient and reliable task com-pletion. Across five agents over two scenarios, decision savings yield workflow-dependent task gains. Success remains close to baseline on ALF-World and falls more substantially in two mobile agents. Auxiliary reasoning and additional inter-actions absorb savings, while failed recovery and premature completion limit delivery. Mean cost per successful outcome rises in one agent despite lower spending per attempt. Agent workflows should pre-serve progress, support recovery, and verify com-pletion. Efficient decision models should be as-sessed through successful delivery, recovery behav-ior, and resources per delivered outcome. Limitations 

Evaluation scope. This study examines Jev in-tegration in selected agent families across mo-bile application and household-task environments. Both settings use language-based observations and bounded action interfaces. Transfer to visually grounded agents, open-ended tool creation, and longer workflows requires further evaluation. The three repetitions characterize execution variability on fixed task instances with a shared seed. They do not measure variation across newly sampled environments or task distributions. 

System-level comparisons. Jev operates within an agent workflow whose candidate construction, auxiliary generation, and feedback mechanisms jointly influence performance. Our comparisons therefore concern the evaluated integrations. The ALFWorld LLM-Choice control helps distinguish decision-model effects under a shared candidate interface, but this control does not cover every integration. Within-family budget matching sup-ports comparisons between variants. Comparisons across families also reflect differences in interac-tion budgets and workflow design. The findings should consequently be interpreted in the context of these configurations, without attributing every observed difference to Jev alone. 

Measurement conditions. Latency and cost depend on the evaluated model versions, service conditions, and experimental prices. Hosted infer-ence introduces variation beyond the agent’s con-trol, while future model updates or pricing changes may alter the observed trade-offs. Our account-ing includes auxiliary model use and failed tasks, but retry adjustments and imputed usage introduce measurement uncertainty where applicable. The reported costs represent estimates under the stated accounting rules, and timing comparisons retain the measurement boundaries of each benchmark.

for AndroidWorld. Every sampled task contributes all three runs of both configurations. We compute J minus control differences in success rate, task cost, and task time for 20,000 replicates. C Trace Analysis and Case Evidence 

C.1 Coding Protocol and Behavioral Patterns 

The unit of annotation is one execution. The selected set contains all 43 family–task pairs in the persistent-loss, persistent-gain, and mixed-outcome groups defined in RQ3. Each contributes three baseline and three Jev runs, giving 258 execu-tions. Selection uses official outcomes, so category frequencies describe these groups. Two authors independently applied the same codebook to the goals, outcomes, and action records without ac-cess to each other’s annotations. A third author resolved disagreements using actions, observations, and final UI states. The annotators consulted the original records when the extracted records were insufficient. Agreement was calculated from the two independent annotations before adjudication. The independent annotations contained 51 label-set disagreements and one coverage disagreement. 

Behavior definitions. Target identification marks a wrong object, entity, or UI target. Pre-requisite violation marks an attempted action or search with an unmet state or inventory condition. Failed recovery marks repeated unsuccessful oper-ations or persistence in a contradicted premise after feedback. Premature completion marks an explicit completion claim while a goal condition remains unmet. Ineffective search marks repeated unpro-ductive search, navigation, or strategy changes. Ex-ecution granularity marks an observed error from batched operations or stale action references. Out-put or state mismatch marks an explicit discrep-ancy between the delivered answer or state and the requested result or evaluator criterion. A failed outcome alone provides no behavior label. An ex-ecution can receive several labels, and successful executions can contain corrected errors.

Feedback and completion checks. Across these cases, reliability hinges on observations changing consequential choices. Object mis-matches require revised targets, changing forms require updated action references, and stalled progress calls for another strategy.
