Abstract. Generative Social Simulation (GSS) leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to create so-phisticated personas that define agents with demographics, emotions, and political opinions. While prior work shows that personas compress outputs into stereotypical caricatures, there is no comprehensive study of how these caricatures evolve into collective artifacts when they interact. Using a Twitter-like GSS platform with personas derived from the American National Elections Study (ANES), we ablate three layers of partisan identity: affective polarization markers, party identification, and vote choice, while retaining demographics, ideological positioning and important issues. We find that collective out-comes depend heavily on how survey responses are translated into personas. Removing a few explicitly partisan statements moves the network from near-complete segregation to cross-party ties approaching those of real Twitter networks. We argue that matching one outcome is not evidence for behavioral validity and point to salience as a possible explanation. Surveys ask for a position on every question regardless of its importance to the respondent, and persona construction turns each answer into a direct behavioral instruction. We conclude with a checklist for more comprehensive evaluation of personas in future works. 

Keywords: Generative Social Simulation · Personas · Mechanism Ablation · Polarization. 

## 1 Introduction 

Generative social simulation (GSS) uses large language models to simulate social behavior among artificial agents. A common application simulates synthetic social media platforms with agents that consume news, share content and form connections [15,44,41,23,33,51]. In these simulations, a common approach is to define agents through a textual persona : a description, often derived from survey data, that specifies the agent’s demographics, beliefs, political leanings, and interests [3,14,8,23,34]. Personas take the place of parameters in classical agent-based models, which have long been criticized for their simplicity. Instead of a homophily parameter, a GSS agent receives an instruction, say, "You strongly prefer to connect with like-minded others." Personas are meant to introduce more nuanced and culturally relevant instructions. However, because agent behavior is influenced by the patterns in the text Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on [32], personas can inherit biases present in the data. There is plenty of evidence that personas poorly represent the people they portray [25]. They produce more stereotypical and marked language than real people [9], caricature the groups they are asked to portray [10,32], are biased toward American cultural norms [7], give very homogeneous output [2,48,27], lack the emotional diversity of humans [35], and are more partisan than the humans they represent [5,22]. These works compare individual persona answers against human ones [27]. How collective-level outcomes emerge when personas interact remains less understood. arXiv:2610.16498v1 [cs.SI] 12 Oct 2026 2 D.Michailidis, P. Törnberg, F.P. Santos 

Recent studies report that simulated social networks trend toward polarization, segregation, and hierarchy [23,33,39]. Do these results reflect real social media platforms? Real-world evidence shows that pro-social behavior constantly emerges in online social networks [12]. If humans find common ground, why do their digital equivalents fail? We study this question using political polarization as a case study. We argue that collective outcomes in GSS can be artifacts of how attributes are translated into personas, and show that even small changes in personas can alter those outcomes. Surveys ask respondents to take a position on every topic on a question-naire, whether or not it matters to them, and record where they stand, but not necessarily how much they care [45]. Converting these responses into personas creates a flattened representation of a person [46]: each answer becomes an always-active description, equally weighted with every other aspect of their personality. One possible explanation of our results is salience : given the tendency of LLMs to output text consistent with the input [32], every political statement in the persona acts as an instruction. Even statements such as "You never talk about politics" can create an "ironic rebound" effect and activate the topic [28]. We investigate how these individual-level distortions affect collective behaviors in artificial intelligence systems [40], particularly the emergence of polarization. We ask three research questions. RQ1 : do personas amplify the political attitudes of the respondents they represent, even without any social interaction between them? We use a standard affective-polarization questionnaire under increasingly ablated persona conditions and compare their responses with the original American National Election Studies survey (ANES) responses. RQ2 : do persona-level distortions translate into collective network artifacts? We perform controlled simulation ablations, removing partisan information from both public biographies and internal personas while holding the platform fixed. RQ3 : does enriching personas with non-political statements reduce the effect of their partisan identity? We introduce non-political preferences and measure whether political polarization changes. Together, we provide a comprehensive in-dividual and collective-level ablation of political personas in generative social simulations, and show how persona-level distortions become collective-level artifacts in the simulation. Our experiments show that LLM agents are very sensitive to their assigned identities, and that this sensi-tivity becomes a collective artifact at the network level. Full personas lead to extremely polarized networks, while ablated agents form cross-party ties more often, approaching those observed in empirical studies [20,18] and engage with less polarizing content, even without being stripped of all political orientation. Question-naire answers show that agents are polarized before any interaction, and our simulations show that persona specification is sufficient to produce large differences in collective outcomes within the same platform. In sim-ulations, ablating public biographies has little impact on collective outcomes, while ablating internal personas has significant impact, suggesting that internal personas are more consequential than how agents perceive each other. No ablation condition matches all empirical benchmarks, meaning that a single outcome is not enough evidence for behavioral validity. Finally, we show that enriching personas with non-political state-ments does not reduce polarization. We conclude with a concrete checklist for persona construction, ablation, and reporting in GSS. 

## 2 Related Work 

For comprehensive surveys of LLM-based agents in social simulation, we refer the reader to recent reviews [47,30,4]. Here, we focus specifically on simulated social media interactions. From Caricature to Collective Artifact 3

2.1 Generative Social Media Simulation 

Park et al. introduced the concept of LLM agents with memory, planning, and social reasoning, showing that they produce believable behavior in a sandbox environment [37]. Ghaffarzadegan et al. formalized generative agent-based modeling (GABM) as a methodological framework [17]. Several systems have since been proposed to model platform-level phenomena. Gao et al.’s S3 models users as LLM agents that read messages, update their emotional state, and reshare or create content, reproducing information, emotion, and attitude propagation [15]. Zhang et al. extend it with group agents [51]. Yang et al. built OASIS, a large-scale Twitter and Reddit simulator capable of scaling to millions of agents, demonstrating information propagation and opinion herding dynamics [49]. Other work combines LLMs with opinion dynamics models to study echo chamber formation [19], or simulates Twitter-like platforms with personas derived from real posts, replicating empirical phenomena such as the friendship paradox [14,31,34]. A closely related line of work examines generative social simulation (GSS) specifically, in which LLM agents operate in synthetic social media platforms and interact through posting, re-posting, and following. Tornberg et al. proposed one of the earliest such frameworks, simulating a Twitter-like environment where persona-driven agents consume and share news [44]. Subsequent work has extended this approach by in-corporating different algorithmic feed designs [23,39] and by studying whether the resulting networks trend toward polarization, segregation, and hierarchical structures [33]. Our experiments build directly on this GSS framework [23]. Beyond platform-level simulations, research has shown that multi-agent LLM systems can reproduce properties of human social networks, such as preferential attachment, triadic closure, and small-world effects [36]. LLMs also show stronger homophily than observed in the real world [8]. While these systems vary in memory, reasoning, or representation, they share a common reliance on textual personas to drive agent behavior. We instead examine how sensitive simulation outcomes are to persona specifications. 

2.2 Evaluating Personas for social simulation 

Individual-level biases of persona-enhanced LLM agents are well documented (see Introduction). We ask how persona design affects collective outcomes in artificial systems, an endeavor closely aligned with understanding dynamics of machine behaviour [40]. Recent works examine this at the individual level. Gu et al. find that LLM-generated social media posts have less linguistic diversity than real-world posts [19]. Cheng et al. introduced CoMPosT, one of the first frameworks to identify and evaluate the caricature problem in LLM simulations with personas [10]. We extend this work by demonstrating empirically what happens when such caricature personas are embedded in dynamic social network simulations. At the network level, Chang et al. prompt LLMs to generate social networks in a single pass based on personas sampled from the US census, finding that LLMs substantially overestimate political homophily relative to empirical levels [8]. Their ablation study further reveals that political affiliation is the strongest predictor of homophily in generated networks, outweighing gender, age, race, and religion. Our setting differs in that we do not examine one-shot network generation, but embed personas in dynamic social simulations and show that internal persona instructions, and not just demographic labels, are the primary driver of polarized network outcomes. Bisbee et al. also use the ANES survey to generate personas and prompt them to answer political feeling thermometer questions [5]. They find that, although average responses resemble human ones, subgroup-level analysis reveals higher partisanship in synthetic responses than in real data. We perform a more fine-grained ablation of the political components of the persona to identify the specific mechanisms that produce hyper-partisan synthetic users. 4 D.Michailidis, P. Törnberg, F.P. Santos 

At the methodological level, Li and Tao argue that a fundamental mismatch exists between what agentic simulations optimize for and the demands of social simulation, particularly behavioral validity that is robust to implementation details [26]. They warn that personas are vulnerable to stereotyping and prompt sensitivity and call for mechanism ablations to test whether outcomes reflect emergent dynamics or artifacts of design choices. These concerns are systematized by Touzel et al., who identify common evaluation fallacies in LLM social simulations and argue for shared benchmarks and reproducible evaluation protocols [43]. Ye et al. even demonstrate that small changes in writing style cascade into massive differences in macro-level interactions [50]. Salience has recently been investigated in generative agents, with researchers pointing to additional political context in personas as increasing ideological extremism [32]. Their context, however, comes from users’ previous posts on X rather than from surveys that are more representative of the general population. Our method also pinpoints which information increases polarization. 

## 3 Personas in Generative Social Simulation 

Personas are the core of agents in GSS. A scalar opinion parameter in the range [−1, 1] in traditional ABMs translates to a qualitative statement such as "You strongly believe in X." Scalar values, however, carry both magnitude and direction. A modeler defines how much weight will scalar parameters have on the agent’s update rules. In LLM-based agents, the model infers the relative weight of each statement in the prompt, which may give partisan statements more influence. Personas are built from survey data, like ANES [1], a four-yearly survey of demographics, political par-ticipation, party affiliation, voting behavior, and policy attitudes. By converting answers into statements, an agent takes up a very specific persona. Figure 1 shows an example. Surveys do not measure importance to the respondent. Once converted to persona statements, all responsed become always active and equally im-portant. This motivates our ablation study: rather than changing how much information a persona contains [25], we change the attributes that are stated, while holding the underlying infrastructure fixed. 

## 4 Methodology 

4.1 Persona Construction 

We generate personas by randomly sampling respondents from the 2020 ANES [1] (Details in Appendix 1.2). We extract demographics (gender, age, marital status, income, education, race, sexual orientation, religion, state), political engagement, ideology, most important problems, thermometer answers for political parties, organizations and movements (details in Appendix Table 3), party registration, voting behavior, interests, TV programs. Love/hate list statements are appended on the condition: if the thermometer answer is ≤ 10 , then it goes on the hate list, and if the thermometer answer is ≥ 90 , then it goes on the love list, in accordance with previous works [44,23]. We also generate a public social media biography by prompting the LLM-model with the full persona. Each agent, therefore, has two texts: the internal persona, which is included in the system prompt of every LLM call, is visible only to that agent and determines which posts to repost, headlines to share, and profiles to follow, and the public biography (max 140 characters), which is what other agents see about the author of a post when reposting it, before deciding whether to follow them (details and prompts in Appendix 1.4).

4.3 Simulation Platform 

We use the GSS framework of [23], to create a Twitter-like platform. At each timestep, an agent reads a feed of ten posts, through two strategies: chronological : ten most recent posts, and boost out partisan : five 6 D.Michailidis, P. Törnberg, F.P. Santos 

from other agents they follow and five selected by an algorithm, which prioritizes content from agents of the opposing political party [23]. The agent then chooses to repost one of the ten visible posts, or share a news headline from a curated database with an original comment, or take no action. If they repost, they are provided with the author’s public biography and follower count and decide whether to follow them. These iterative interactions produce an evolving network that we can evaluate across several metrics. We provide the full pseudocode of the simulation and the prompts in Appendix Algorithm 1, and more details on the parameters in Appendix Table 1. The feed algorithm, action space, news database, network initialization, and scheduling remain fixed. Only the textual content of the agent persona changes. This allows us to attribute differences in collective outcomes to persona specification rather than to the simulation infrastructure. 

4.4 Evaluation 

Before the simulation, each persona answers a standard three-part polarization questionnaire [21]. First, a feeling thermometer toward their own and the opposite party [1]. Second, a series of specific trait questions, asking whether their own and the opposite party members are intelligent, honest, generous, hypocritical, selfish or mean [24,16], and third, behavioral questions that assess whether each agent would follow others of the same/opposing party, or someone who loves or hates their or the opposing party’s leader. Answers are stored and analyzed together with the original ANES responses. Full details on the prompts in Appendix 1.5. Each question is asked in a separate API call, without shared history, with the full persona re-entered every time. Trait questions also allow a "don’t know" response, so that the agents do not resort to a binary answer every time. Agents reply via an output schema, so responses are standardized. Following the static evaluation approach [26], we assess the persona behavior through constrained tasks with measurable targets. At the collective level, the mechanism ablation addresses an important current credibility gap in generative social simulation: the lack of proper analysis on whether targeted edits lead to robust outcomes [26,43,50]. 

4.5 Comparison with Empirical Networks 

We compare simulation outcomes with two observational studies of real political networks on Twitter [20,18]. Such datasets are rare because users’ political orientation is hard to infer from their profiles. Halberstam and Knight study a network of 2.2 million political tweets during the 2012 US elections, classifying users as liberal or conservative based on how many politicians of each party they follow. They observe high homophily: only 33% of liberal users follow conservatives, while 20% of conservatives follow liberal users [20]. Gopal et al. study a population of 4000 US legislators on Twitter and find that 21 .5% of connections in total are cross-party (reporting only the overall rate). We note a few caveats about this comparison. First, neither network represents ordinary users: one consists of users who follow politicians and are therefore more likely to be strong partisans [20], the other of elected officials, whose incentives differ from everyday users [18]. Second, both datasets were collected during specific political periods (the 2012 elections and the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, respectively), during which the partisan environments were different from the current or future ones. We use them as external benchmarks, not as a validation or a means to identify a correct persona configuration.

## 5 Results 

The results below use OpenAI’s gpt-4o-mini model and the boost out partisan timeline algorithm. Our results remain consistent across LLM models and timeline strategies — we fully report them in the Appendix Fig. 3. 

5.1 RQ1: Persona ablations alter individual political responses before interaction 

Our goal is to observe if partisan markers are redundant for polarization, or if their removal eliminates the political information needed to reproduce it. 

Full personas amplify political polarization, while ablated ones dampen it Figure 2 (top) shows the thermometer gap under each ablation, together with the original survey values when available. Under the full persona, agents show exaggerated polarization when asked both about how they feel about the parties and the candidates. Removing the affective polarization (AP) marker alone suffices to bring the agents to the ANES value. Further ablations move the agents below the observed benchmark, although not fully into neutrality. 

Trait gaps are reduced under further ablations Figure 2 (bottom) shows the gaps in positive and negative trait attribution between in- and out-group agents (ANES has no comparable questions). Under the full persona, agents attribute every positive trait to their own party and every negative trait to the opposing one. All Democratic agents describe Republicans as hypocritical, selfish and mean, and vice versa. While AP affected the thermometer gaps, it has no effect on personality traits. The gaps shift only once party identification (PID) is removed. Each persona layer affects the measure that corresponds to it: statements derived from thermometer items affect thermometer rating, while group-membership statements affect the agent’s judgment of in- and out-group members. 

Party identification drives follow gaps Figure 3 shows that under the full persona the co-partisan preference is maximized and symmetric across parties. Here, too, removing the AP marker does not affect the gaps — the decline begins only when PID is removed. The same pattern appears in the trait results, although the love/hate list might be expected to matter more in decisions about other groups. Additionally, we see an asymmetry introduced during the ablations. After removing PID and voting behavior (VB), Republican agents remain more reluctant to follow opponents than Democratic ones, with the gap between the two parties widening as more persona statements are removed. Under full ablation, 46 .0% of Democratic and 22 .4% of Republican agents report that they would follow a member of the opposing party, against the 33% and 20% empirically observed by Halberstam and Knight [20], while under the full persona both rates are much lower. While some observational studies have found some evidence of this asymmetry, [20,6], recent experimental work has shown that co-partisan preferences in link formation are symmetric across parties [29]. This finding is consistent with the broader finding that LLMs overestimate political homophily [14,8] and exaggerate affective polarization [5], and with the recent evidence that right-leaning users are more likely to be caricatured by LLMs than left-leaning ones [32]. Further ablations reduce extreme partisan behavior, but also reduce the gap below the empirical bench-marks. No single condition matches all three benchmarks, meaning that individual responses are sensitive to the specific political statements included in the persona prompt.

## 6 A Checklist for Persona Reporting in Generative Social Simulation 

GSS’s credibility heavily depends on the design of the persona. We suggest the following framework to implement in practice. 1. Publish the persona prompt verbatim, including the mapping from source data. Report not only the template or a vague algorithm, but the thresholds and rules that generated each statement. 2. Separate internal personas from information other agents see, and report both. These are distinct artifacts with distinct functions. One specifies an agent’s policy, the other specifies what other agents observe about the agent. They are not interchangeable: in our case, ablating the public biography does not change collective outcomes, while ablating the persona does significantly. 3. State which attributes are behavioral instructions. Personas from surveys assign attributes equal weight. Authors should report whether that is intended, and whether any attribute is meant to be latent. 4. Perform mechanism ablations on personas. Report both the marginal and cumulative effects of each component, with statistical tests across seeds. An ablation should be treated as a control condition. 5. Test alternative persona formulations. Vary the persona’s representation, while holding the data and environment settings fixed, and report whether the conclusion remains. Ye et al. show that personas stated as prose versus a bullet list can change cooperation rates by up to 76 percentage points [50]. 6. Benchmark against several empirical findings. When available, compare against multiple empirical observations and report which observation the simulation matches and which it does not. Matching only one outcome is not enough for behavioral validity. 14 D.Michailidis, P. Törnberg, F.P. Santos 

7. Report model sensitivity across multiple models. Report both the sign and the magnitude of the effect, to test whether the effect is robust across multiple different models and not just an artifact of a specific family. 8. Vary the component you want to draw conclusions about. A claim about platform architecture needs the architecture varied and the personas held fixed. In our experiments the architecture was fixed and the personas alone drove the conclusions. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We examined how survey-driven personas shape polarization in generative social simulation at the individual and collective level. Explicit partisan markers amplify specific dimensions of stated political behavior. In controlled simulation ablations, we found that removing polarizing statements from the persona reduces collective polarization, while removing them only from public biographies has little effect. Adding non-political statements does not reduce the influence of partisan identity on collective behavior. Our results suggest that collective outcomes in GSS can be artifacts of how survey attributes are translated into personas and gain disproportionate influence in the agent’s behavior. We point to salience as one possible explanation. Survey responses do not report how much respondents care about issues. Persona statements, on the other hand, treat every response as a behavioral instruction. Our analysis shows the limitations of surveys as persona construction sources. Personas built from interviews, where people only voice what matters to them, may reduce the problem [38]. We provide direct empirical support for recent concerns about the epistemic reliability of LLM-based social simulation [26,43,50], and suggest concrete steps to detect this sensitivity in future studies. We do not suggest that personas are the only source of distortion in GSS, or that ablating them is sufficient for valid collective dynamics. Distortions may also arise from the way interactions and communications are performed in the simulation, or even from the LLM itself. We did not explore the interaction of these mech-anisms, so they may introduce further limitations of GSS. For example, Ye et al. find that recommendation feed size can strengthen echo-chamber outcomes while the memory window does not [50]. Generative social simulation could be a promising tool for complementing human-subjects research [2]. Applying such a tool, however, requires understanding how decisions to construct agents affect both in-dividual behavior and emerging collective dynamics. Future studies should follow rigorous frameworks, in which persona construction, interaction and communication protocols are subjected to sensitivity analysis and benchmarking.
