Affective personal informatics (PI) involves capturing, tracking, analyzing, and reflecting on personal data to support emotional self-awareness and mental well-being. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) are reshaping affective PI, yet a comprehensive survey of LLM-based affective PI systems is lacking. To bridge this gap, we systematically analyzed 45 papers published between January 2020 and June 2026 across four major computing and health databases, examining their macro trends, system taxonomies, and research methodologies. Our synthesis reveals an overreliance on multi-stage, text-centric pipelines, a predominance of active, screen-bound interactions, and a severe demographic skew toward young, university-campus-based cohorts in empirical evaluations. Based on these findings, we critically reflect on prevailing architectural and methodological shortcomings and outline actionable future directions for building end-to-end multimodal, low-burden, and inclusive affective PI systems. 1 Introduction 

Affective personal informatics (PI) has emerged at the intersection of affective computing and personal informatics [23], dedicated to helping individuals capture, track, analyze, and reflect on affective states ( e.g. , emotion, mood, and stress) to foster emotional self-awareness and promote mental well-being. Early work in affective PI functioned primarily as recording tools, capturing physiological signals and self-reported moods, and visualizing these metrics to facilitate self-monitoring and emotional reflection [66, 91]. Recently, the paradigm in affective PI has progressively transitioned from isolated tracking toward diverse interaction forms [117], contextualized emotional reflection [53], and personalized intervention [46]. Together, these developments position affective PI not only as a means of tracking affective states, but as a means of supporting reflection and action in everyday emotional self-care. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) [9, 27, 33] have significantly influenced research across many different domains, including human-computer interaction (HCI) and affective computing. Affective PI is no exception, with researchers increasingly exploring how LLMs can extend existing systems and enable new forms of interaction and support. This is because LLMs can interpret language in context and generate conversational responses, allowing systems to make sense of fragmented, subjective, and unstructured self-reports that are difficult to process with traditional rule-based methods or classifiers [20, 107]. These capabilities create opportunities for affective PI systems to support more flexible, contextualized, and personalized forms of emotional reflection and support. Despite growing interest, there is no comprehensive review of existing literature on LLM-based affective PI tools. Synthesizing this emerging literature is important for understanding how LLMs are used in affective PI, identifying structural limitations in current approaches, and informing future research and design. Existing surveys at the inter-section of AI and mental well-being have primarily focused on emotion-related computational tasks [87, 110], the capacity of LLMs to demonstrate empathy [89], or the evaluation of LLMs from clinical and psychiatric diagnostic perspectives [28, 102]. However, none provide a dedicated synthesis of how LLMs are designed and integrated into affective PI tools. To address this gap, we conducted a scoping review of 45 papers published between January 2020 and June 2026 across four major computing and health databases, examining research on LLM-based affective PI tools. Specifically, our review is guided by three research questions (RQs): 

- RQ1 (macro trends): What are the temporal, geographical, disciplinary, and model adoption trends of LLMs in affective PI? 

- RQ2 (system taxonomies): How are current LLM-based affective PI tools designed in terms of multimodal data input, processing architecture, and interaction approach? 

- RQ3 (research methodologies): What design and empirical research methods are employed across the research lifecycle of LLM-based affective PI systems, from requirements gathering to system evaluation? Our synthesis of the 45 reviewed articles reveals three recurring limitations across current tools. First, existing tools rely heavily on multi-stage, text-centric pipelines, where non-textual data ( e.g. , physiological and behavioral signals) are converted into intermediate textual proxies, risking information loss and error propagation. Second, most tools rely on active, screen-bound interactions, such as text-based journaling and multi-turn conversations, which can impose cognitive and interaction burdens on users. Third, empirical evaluations are skewed toward young, educated, university-affiliated cohorts, limiting the generalizability of their findings. Based on these findings, we discuss current limitations The Landscape of Large Language Model-based Affective Personal Informatics 3and outline actionable future directions for developing end-to-end multimodal, low-burden, and demographically inclusive affective PI tools. In summary, this work makes three primary contributions: (1) We provide a scoping review of the macro trends, system taxonomies, and research methodologies across 45 articles of LLM-based affective PI tools; (2) We identify three recurring limitations in current tools: reliance on multi-stage text-centric pipelines, active and screen-bound interactions, and empirical evaluations skewed toward young, university-affiliated populations. (3) We translate these findings into concrete research opportunities by providing actionable design suggestions toward end-to-end multimodal modeling, low-burden interaction, and inclusive design. 

2 Background and Related Work 2.1 Affective PI 

At the intersection of affective computing and PI [23], affective PI focuses on helping individuals to systematically track, interpret, and reflect upon their inner psychological experiences, such as emotions, stress, and mood, to foster emotional self-awareness and sustained psychological well-being. Early explorations in this domain concentrated primarily on using personal data ( e.g. , physiological and visual cues) to support retrospective reflection. For example, Affective Diary [91] paired physiological signals with mobile logs and translated them into expressive visual avatars for emotional reflection. Similarly, AffectAura [66] integrated multimodal behavioral cues and desktop contexts into an interactive timeline for retrospective reflection. Currently, the evolution of affective PI research exhibits three prominent trends. First, interaction paradigms have become increasingly diverse, expanding beyond traditional mobile applications to include conversational [68], tangible [117], and peripheral [88] interactions. Specifically, Morris et al. [68] proposed an empathic conversational agent to support mental health reflection; Zhou et al. [117] designed a squeezable physical artifact for workplace emotion regulation; and MoodLight [88] dynamically adjusted ambient lighting to convey biosensor-derived arousal states. Second, because of the significance of contextual data in affective interpretation [80], contextualization has emerged as another key trend in affective PI. For instance, Li et al. [53] contextualized affective metrics using daily situational data ( e.g. , calendar events, locations, and social activities), while MoodScope [58] inferred users’ mood states directly from background smartphone usage contexts. Third, researchers have increasingly prioritized personalization within affective PI systems: Sarker et al. [86] modeled individuals’ dynamic receptivity to stress interventions to personalize the timing of delivery, while Kocielnik et al. [46] tailored conversational prompts and dialogue pacing to an individual’s unique interaction habits. In terms of development, affective PI has utilized a variety of technologies, such as mobile system development [91], passive sensing [100], virtual reality (VR) [97], classical machine learning (ML) algorithms [85], and rule-based dialogue systems [24]. However, these conventional technologies present critical limitations: classical classifiers produce coarse, uninterpretable labels, while rule-based dialogue systems are constrained by predefined interaction rules and lack the flexibility to handle nuanced human feelings. In contrast, the emergence of LLMs [9, 33] marks a significant shift. LLMs offer new capabilities by interpreting and integrating different forms of emotional data and generating personalized reflections and responses [104, 109]. These capabilities have led researchers to increasingly explore LLMs in affective PI systems. 4 Liu et al. 

2.2 LLMs in Affective Computing 

Affective computing aims to develop digital systems capable of recognizing, processing, understanding, interpreting, and simulating human affect [80]. Leveraging their advanced reasoning and language generation capabilities, LLMs [9, 27, 33] have been deployed across a diverse range of affective computing domains. The first category is affective algorithms ,such as emotion recognition [21, 55], emotion reasoning [15, 57], and emotion-cause pair extraction [103]. The second category focuses on computational empathy [18]. A growing body of research suggests that LLMs can, in specific scenarios, match or even surpass human levels of perceived empathy. For instance, Wang et al. [101] developed a text-based emotional understanding benchmark and found that GPT-4 [2] outperformed 89% of human participants. Similarly, across two empirical studies, Lee et al. [51] demonstrated that LLMs could generate peer-support responses perceived as more empathic than those written by humans. Conversely, other work highlights critical limitations, cautioning that this apparent empathy may be superficial; for example, Cuadra et al. [17] showed that LLMs exhibit a significantly poorer grasp of nuanced user experiences than human respondents. Overall, while LLMs display substantial empathetic competence in controlled textual settings, their actual depth of empathy varies considerably across task demands. Clinical applications are the third category of LLM applications in affective computing. For example, Chen et al. [14] evaluated LLMs in psychiatric interview simulations, showing their ability to assess multi-dimensional clinical symptom profiles. In a different project, Yang et al. [105] presented MentalLLaMA, and employed instruction-tuned LLMs to perform standardized mental health screening while generating clinical interpretations for their assessments. Similarly, Englhardt et al. [22] used LLMs to derive clinical insights from multimodal behavioral data. Distinct from work on affective algorithms , the empathy of conversational agents , or clinical diagnostics , affective PI 

shifts the focus toward supporting everyday, longitudinal emotional well-being for individuals [104, 109]. Instead of formal clinical evaluation [14] or emotion recognition [21], LLM-based affective PI focuses on unobtrusively supporting ongoing self-awareness [107], daily reflective sensemaking [10], and contextual emotional regulation [54]. Despite rapid progress, existing literature has yet to provide a focused synthesis of how LLMs are used in affective PI tools. We address this gap in this paper through a scoping review of LLM-based affective PI. 

3 Methodology 

We constructed the corpus by following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [75], as shown in Fig. 2. We utilized the software Covidence 1 to manage the review papers. 

3.1 Identification 

To comprehensively search the literature on LLM-based tools for affective PI, we conducted a literature search across three major computer science (CS) databases (ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, and Scopus) and one major healthcare database (PubMed). We designed the search string by combining LLM-related keywords ( e.g. , Large Language Model and LLM) and affective PI-related keywords ( e.g. , emotion regulation [98], emotion reflection [52], and emotion self-awareness [11]). Our search timeframe was from January 2020 to June 2026. We set January 2020 as the start date because LLMs emerged following the GPT-2 breakthrough in 2019 [83]. Our preliminary search confirmed that no prior work applied LLMs to affective PI before 2020. We concluded the search at the end of the first half of 2026 (June 2026) to capture a complete semi-annual trend.

3.2 Screening and Eligibility 

To ensure methodological rigor and minimize selection bias, three independent reviewers conducted the screening process using the Covidence platform across two screening phases. In the initial title and abstract screening phase, two reviewers independently screened all identified records against the predefined eligibility criteria. Any conflicting decisions between the two reviewers were resolved through discussions arbitrated by a third reviewer, the first author. To prevent the inadvertent omission of relevant literature, the first author also conducted a secondary audit of all articles excluded during this initial round. Subsequently, all candidate papers advancing to the second phase underwent 6 Liu et al. full-text retrieval and independent evaluation by all three reviewers. Disagreements regarding final study eligibility were resolved through collective discussions until a unanimous consensus was reached. Prior to screening, all reviewers collaboratively discussed and finalized a strict set of inclusion and exclusion criteria. If papers met ALL of the following inclusion criteria (IC), they were included: (1) IC1 : Empirical papers that proposed and developed new interactive tools or evaluated existing interactive tools. (2) IC2 : LLMs worked as the core component of the tool’s functionality. (3) IC3 : Conducted empirical user studies to evaluate the interactive tools. (4) IC4 : The interactive tools were designed for affective PI, which collected users’ personal information to help track, analyze, reflect, or regulate personal affective states, e.g. , emotion, mood, and stress. Conversely, if papers met ANY of the following exclusion criteria (EC), they were excluded. (1) EC1 : Non-empirical papers ( e.g. , research proposals, prospective papers, or literature reviews) that neither proposed nor developed new interactive tools nor evaluated the existing interactive tools. (2) EC2 : Utilized LLMs only for non-core functions of the interaction tools, e.g. , data generation or method evaluation. (3) EC3 : Did not conduct empirical user studies to evaluate the interactive tools. (4) EC4 : The interactive tools were not for affective PI. As illustrated in Fig. 2, we started by conducting a title/abstract screening on the 1907 papers and retained 93 papers for full-text assessment. During the full-text assessing stage, we excluded 48 papers in total (13 for EC1, 1 for EC2, 14 for EC3, and 20 for EC4). As a result, the final corpus contained 45 papers. 

3.3 Data Analysis 

To systematise the data extraction and analysis process for reviewing the selected literature, we developed a compre-hensive extraction protocol. Our protocol captured three key dimensions across each selected paper to answer our three RQs: (1) macro trend, (2) system taxonomies, and (3) research methodologies. The specific contents in the three dimensions were as follows: 

• Macro trends: publication year, the utilised LLM, geographical distribution, publication venue, and discipline domain. 

• System taxonomies: system input modalities, data representations directly processed by LLMs, the tool categories, core functionalities, and the interaction paradigms. 

• Research methodologies: need-finding and requirement elicitation methods, designing methodologies, evalu-ating methods, study designs, and the participant demographics ( i.e. , sample size, gender ratio, age distribution, and mental health state). To ensure consistency and reliability, we first independently extracted data from each included paper, followed by detailed reviews and discussions to resolve any discrepancies and achieve a robust consensus. We then adopted an explanatory and synthesis-driven approach to analysis: we systematically categorized and cross-compared the extracted variables to map out current technological architectures, theoretical foundations, and evaluation paradigms in LLM-driven affective PI. 4 Results 4.1 Temporal Trend and the Adoption of LLMs 

4.1.1 Temporal Trend in the Context of LLM Advancements. The publication timeline closely mirrored the technological evolution of LLMs, revealing a clear trajectory from early exploration (2020–2024) to a recent exponential surge (2025–June 2026), as illustrated in Fig. 3. Before 2022, LLMs, i.e. , GPT-2 [83] (2019) and GPT-3 [9] (2020), were still in the early exploratory stage. There was only one paper that applied LLMs to affective PI during 2020-2022, i.e. , Mirror Ritual [84] (2020). In 2022, the foundation LLM reached its milestone with the release of GPT-3.5. OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 greatly promoted the public adoption and the development of LLMs. As shown in the bottom timeline of Fig. 3, various companies began introducing their own LLM families starting in 2023, e.g. , Meta’s Llama family, Google’s Gemini and Gemma families and Anthropic’s Claude family. In 2024, the emergence of multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), e.g. , GPT-4o [33], LLaMA 3 [27], and Gemini 1.5 [92], became another milestone in the development of LLMs. MLLMs enabled fine-grained reasoning over multimodal data, which presented more opportunities for processing complex personal affective data. Compared to the development of foundational LLMs, the application of LLMs in the research of affective PI had lagged behind. There existed one reviewed paper published in 2023 [72] and five papers published in 2024 [37, 61, 70, 82, 109]. The research in LLMs’ application in affective PI culminated in a significant boom in 2025 ( 𝑛 = 25), which accounted for over half (55.6%) of the entire reviewed corpus. The upward trajectory remained remarkably robust into 2026, with 13 papers recorded in just the first half of the year (January–June 2026), 2026. This sharp upward trend stressed how continuous breakthroughs in foundation LLM architectures dynamically drove and accelerated methodological innovation across affective computing and personal informatics, reinforcing the need for a review such as ours to map this emerging field. 8 Liu et al.

4.4 Research Methodologies 

4.4.1 Identifying Users’ Needs and Requirements. Regarding the approach to user need identification prior to system design, the reviewed studies fall into three distinct paradigms: evaluation of existing tools (6 .7%, 𝑛 = 3) [44, 49, 64], empirical formative research (42 .2%, 𝑛 = 19), and non-empirical derivation (51 .1%, 𝑛 = 23). Overall, 42 .2% of the studies ( 𝑛 = 19, deduplicated) conducted empirical formative research . Among these empirical methodologies, user interviews were the most prevalent (17 .8%, 𝑛 = 8). For example, MindScape [70] conducted interviews with five undergraduate students to uncover their journaling habits and needs. Following interviews, quantitative surveys were the second most common approach (8 .9%, 𝑛 = 4), utilized to characterize user preferences and baseline symptoms at scale. Additionally, three articles employed autobiographical design (6 .7%, 𝑛 = 3), in which authors leveraged first-person lived inquiries. Other formative research methods included expert consultations with clinical practitioners (6 .7%, 𝑛 = 3), focus groups (4 .4%, 𝑛 = 2), as well as technology probes, experience sampling, and co-design workshops (2 .2%, 𝑛 = 1 each). Notably, four studies [54, 59–61] adopted multi-method empirical triangulations to comprehensively scope user requirements. Specifically, HealBubble [54] and MoodMonster [61] conducted two-phase formative studies combining online surveys and interviews to uncover user needs; RageSense [59] engaged 24 mobile gamers through a combination of focus group discussions and semi-structured interviews to capture preferred real-time intervention strategies; and TherAIssist [60] conducted both one-to-one interviews and focus groups with five professional art therapists to understand the design requirements for art therapy homework. Conversely, 51 .1% of the corpus ( 𝑛 = 23) relied on non-empirical formative research , in which system requirements were derived directly from prior literature, psychological theories, or problem-driven theoretical rationales without pre-deployment user studies.

5 Discussion 5.1 Critical Reflection 

I. Information Loss and Error Accumulation in Text-Centric Processing. A significant limitation across current LLM-based affective PI systems is their overwhelming reliance on text-centric processing. As synthesized in Tab. 4 and Sec. 4.3.1, pure textual input constitutes the clear majority of reviewed implementations (60 .0%, 𝑛 = 27). More crucially, most of the remaining 18 studies (40 .0%) that incorporated non-textual modalities ( e.g. , visual, speech, physiological, and behavioral data) did not employ LLMs to directly process raw sensory representations. Instead, they relied on multi-stage preprocessing pipelines that convert raw non-textual data into intermediate textual summaries, feeding only these textual proxies into downstream LLMs. This architectural paradigm inevitably leads to substantial loss of multimodal information and cascading error accumulation. 20 Liu et al. Summarizing non-textual modalities into textual descriptions frequently results in the loss of original, fine-grained affective information [29, 40]. For instance, in speech-based interactions, standard ASR transcriptions strip away rich paralinguistic cues ( e.g. , pitch inflection, vocal tremors, and pauses) that often carry far more critical emotional information than semantic words alone [40]. Similarly, continuous physiological signals ( e.g. , HRV) contain abundant temporal dynamics [29]; condensing them into static text prompts with discrete affective labels erases these continuous temporal trajectories. As a result, LLMs are unable to align sudden physiological shifts with specific contextual triggers, which compromises the accuracy of reflective causal explanations [22]. Furthermore, such multi-stage pipelines are inherently susceptible to cumulatively cascading computational errors. For example, in MindScape [70], geospatial clustering and activity recognition APIs aggregate raw behavioral data into textual summaries. However, imprecise clustering thresholds or recognition errors can cause inaccurate behavioral inferences to cascade directly into the prompt, misleading the LLM. Consequently, the LLM may generate inaccurate causal reasoning and subsequently issue unhelpful or incorrect recommendations, ultimately confusing users. Similarly, AI-HEAL [82] converts raw EEG biofeedback into discrete affective state estimates, where coarse-grained classification rules can mislabel a user’s emotional state, causing the downstream LLM to base its entire cognitive reappraisal dialogue on an erroneous premise. At a broader scale, these technical bottlenecks propagate into broader application failures, preventing affective PI tools from delivering reliable, sustained psychological support in users’ everyday lives. To mitigate these limitations, future research must shift away from inadequate and fragile textual proxies toward native multimodal large language models (MLLMs) capable of jointly modeling textual and non-textual signals end-to-end, which we further discuss in Sec. 5.2, Part I. 

II. Over-Reliance on Active Screen-Based Interactions. A prominent limitation across the reviewed articles is their pervasive reliance on active, screen-bound interactions, which places a significant interaction and cognitive burden on users [23, 62]. As illustrated in Table 5 and Sec. 4.3.2, the vast majority of systems are instantiated as affective support & intervention systems (31 .1%, 𝑛 = 14), journaling support (28 .9%, 𝑛 = 13), and chatbots (20 .0%, 𝑛 = 9) deployed on smartphones or web interfaces. Across these tools, interaction paradigms remain overwhelmingly active and text-centric. To illustrate, users need to explicitly formulate verbose reflections in TrueBalance [112], type out introspective narratives in Augmentiary [34], or repeatedly engage with multi-turn Socratic questioning sequences in Socrates 2.0 [31]. This reliance on demanding active engagement creates a critical usability paradox: individuals undergoing acute emotional distress, burnout, or cognitive overload often experience severe executive function depletion [6], which greatly inhibits their capacity to perform complex text-based interactions or process lengthy AI feedback [23]. Forcing users to input complex text or read dense information on screens during vulnerable moments introduces secondary cognitive fatigue [42]. Consequently, affective support tools risk becoming a digital burden that produces the opposite of the intended therapeutic effect. In contrast, systems exploring physical embodiment and spatial immersion remain markedly under-explored, with embodied AI (11 .9%, 𝑛 = 5) and VR environments (7 .1%, 𝑛 = 3) collectively representing only 19 .0% ( 𝑛 = 8) of the corpus. Nonetheless, they demonstrate diverse possibilities to reduce active operational demands and screen reliance, such as implicitly collecting users’ facial cues through smart mirrors (Mirror Ritual [84]), providing supportive physical co-presence guided by biofeedback signals (QTrobot [49]), and dynamically synthesizing relaxing 3D safe places via voice commands (Refugio [67]). The Landscape of Large Language Model-based Affective Personal Informatics 21 Therefore, transitioning from demanding active, screen-bound affective support toward low-burden, implicit, and physically or spatially grounded interactions is critical to reducing cognitive load for vulnerable users. We provide future solutions in Sec. 5.2, Part II. 

III. Skewed Participant Demographics and Campus Bubbles. A critical empirical gap across the reviewed literature lies in the pronounced demographic homogeneity and sampling skew in user evaluations. As summarized in Table 9, evaluated cohorts were disproportionately concentrated among young adults aged 18–25 (37 .8%, 𝑛 = 17) and young-to-middle-aged cohorts aged 18–40 (35 .6%, 𝑛 = 16), which together account for 94 .3% of studies with reported age data (73 .4% of the entire corpus), while emotionally vulnerable demographics across broader life stages—such as adolescents (< 18 years, 2 .2%, 𝑛 = 1) and older adults ( > 60 years, 2 .2%, 𝑛 = 1)—remain substantially under-represented. Furthermore, participant recruitment was overwhelmingly confined to academic campus environments ( e.g. , university students, staff, and faculty). This prevalent ’campus bubble’ of participants in evaluations likely introduces severe bias into the evaluation results of existing systems. Specifically, university populations and young cohorts generally exhibit higher digital literacy, higher cognitive flexibility, and more frequent exposure to LLMs, enabling them to navigate product usage obstacles, prompt formulation barriers, and LLM hallucinations [16, 108]. Consequently, positive feasibility and effectiveness assessed within these digitally literate populations do not guarantee validity when translated to broader sociodemographic groups. Diverse occupational populations ( e.g. , blue-collar workers) and older adults often encounter steeper digital readiness gaps [96], age-related perceptual declines [19], and distinct psychological coping mechanisms [12, 50], which cannot be adequately captured by evaluations on campus cohorts. Therefore, robust validation across heterogeneous demographics remains an open challenge, which we explain in Sec. 5.2, Part III. 

5.2 Future Directions 

I. From Textual Proxies to End-to-End Multimodal Modeling. To resolve the information loss and cascading errors inherent in multi-stage text-centric pipelines (as shown in Sec. 5.1, Part I), future affective PI systems must transition toward MLLMs that can natively process diverse modalities, e.g. , visual, audio, and time-series data. For acoustic and visual streams, instead of relying on discrete ASR transcriptions ( e.g. , Zisquit et al. [119] and Honda et al. [32]) or isolated VLMs ( e.g. , MindFeed [107] and MemoryLib [118]), researchers can leverage emerging audio- and vision-enabled MLLMs, e.g. , GPT-4o [33], Gemini 1.5 Pro [92]. Processing continuous acoustic and visual cues alongside textual content end-to-end preserves rich affective nuances that are otherwise stripped away by intermediate textual proxies. Time-series signals, i.e. , physiological and behavioral data, represent another critical input for affective systems owing to their objectivity, passivity, and high temporal resolution. However, general-purpose MLLMs remain ill-equipped to process continuous sensor time-series, such as raw EEG, electrocardiogram (ECG), and inertial measurement unit (IMU) streams. Rather than collapsing these non-stationary signals into coarse-grained textual descriptions as seen in existing tools ( e.g. , QTrobot [49] and AI-HEAL [82]), future research can draw inspiration from emerging domain-specific sensor-language models. Recent advances demonstrate that continuous biosignals can be modeled natively at scale: for instance, SleepFM [93] and Abbaspourazad et al. [1] perform self-supervised pre-training on large-scale multimodal physiological datasets, while NeuroLM [39] and ECG-Chat [114] employ cross-modal projectors to bridge raw biosignals directly with LLM latent spaces for conversational clinical reasoning. Similarly, sensor-language architectures such as SensorLM [113] and SensorLLM [56] map temporal sensor patches into unified semantic token spaces. Adapting 22 Liu et al. these cross-modal alignment mechanisms to affective PI would enable LLMs to ingest continuous sensor dynamics as soft tokens alongside conversational dialogue, mitigating the information loss and cascading errors inherent in coarse textual proxies. We also note that while upstream MLLMs demand continued technical innovation, affective interactive systems research simultaneously lacks sufficient engagement with cutting-edge, domain-specific sensor foundation models. Moving toward native end-to-end multimodal modeling requires not only paradigm shifts in affective interaction design and breakthroughs in upstream model architectures, but also active synergy and mutual reinforcement between both fields. For example, AI researchers can design end-to-end LLM architectures to directly process raw wearable data, while HCI designers can employ these architectures in affective PI applications and contribute subjective user reflections to align these architectures with real-world emotional contexts. 

II. Towards Low-Burden Affective Interactions. As identified in our critical reflection (Sec. 5.1, Part II), the prevailing tools’ reliance on demanding, active, screen-bound interactions creates a severe usability paradox by overloading (vulnerable) users during acute emotional distress. To address this limitation, it is necessary to reduce users’ workload and cognitive load when using the systems. However, a certain degree of active user operation is indispensable in affective support systems, as emotional understanding requires users to actively externalize subjective experiences and give feedback to AI-generated analyses [73]. Therefore, blindly pursuing fully passive interaction is not feasible. Instead, future research needs to find a balance between achieving low-burden interaction and ensuring the effectiveness and usability of tools. To build this low-burden yet effective paradigm, researchers can draw upon established interaction design principles. We suggest three feasible paths: (1) micro-interaction [23], (2) peripheral interaction [8], and (3) adaptive interaction [25]. First, future research can adopt micro-interaction, which involves lightweight and single-action engagements allowing users to complete a targeted action in just a few seconds [23]. For example, compared to multi-step textual workflows, lightweight one-tap mood check-ins or single-action visual anchors substantially lower manual input workload [36]. Second, peripheral interaction [8], which enables users to interact with systems at the periphery of their attention without interrupting primary activities, also offers a promising approach to lightweight affective support. Specifically, moving beyond rigid screens toward tangible objects and environments-such as soft lighting transitions [88], or gras-pable physical artifacts [117]-enables emotional offloading without requiring focal visual attention. Third, researchers could consider systems that adaptively adjust to users’ fatigue levels and emotional states. To illustrate, the widely used conversational agents in the reviewed papers can be designed to dynamically regulate their dialogue pacing and verbosity: delivering concise text during periods of acute depletion and gradually expand into open-ended, reflective dialogues only as users’ cognitive capacity recovers. 

III. Evaluating Across Heterogeneous and Vulnerable Demographics. As shown in our critical reflection (Sec. 5.1, Part III), the reliance on campus-based sampling introduces severe bias and leads to a failure in establishing ecological validity across real-world populations. To overcome this limitation, future research must proactively expand participant recruitment beyond university bubbles to include underrepresented and emotionally vulnerable populations across diverse life stages. This also requires researchers to reconsider interface complexities, prompt formulation barriers, and system misunderstandings in the design of systems for less tech-literate or non-tech-literate populations. Furthermore, empirical evaluations must account for heterogeneous socioeconomic backgrounds [69], occupational strains [65], and differing psychological coping mechanisms [13]. Rather than relying on short-term laboratory trials The Landscape of Large Language Model-based Affective Personal Informatics 23 with healthy participants, future investigations should conduct longitudinal in-the-wild studies [45] involving indi-viduals exposed to diverse real-world stressors, including blue-collar workers [38] and subclinical populations [7]. Such inclusive evaluations will enable researchers to identify demographic-specific failure modes—such as clinical misinterpretations [106], cognitive friction from unadapted interaction pacing [26], or cultural misalignments in LLM advice—thereby [5] thereby ensuring that affective PI tools deliver genuinely safe, robust, and equitable psychological support. 

5.3 Limitations 

Our scoping review exhibits several methodological limitations that warrant consideration. First, our search protocol was bounded by peer-reviewed English literature published between January 2020 and June 2026 across four established digital libraries. Consequently, commercial, closed-source consumer applications, emerging preprints, and non-English systems were excluded, which may omit certain industrial advances in generative affective tools. Second, given the rapid release cycles of LLMs, our analysis captures a specific evolutionary window; the model capabilities and architectural paradigms documented here will naturally continue to shift as native multimodal models mature. Finally, our synthesis relied primarily on authors’ self-reported system implementations and empirical findings. Due to the scarcity of open-source artifacts, public benchmarking datasets, and standardized validation protocols across the reviewed corpus, we could not independently audit technical claims or benchmark quantitative performances across systems. 

6 Conclusion 

In this work, we presented a comprehensive scoping review of 45 research articles to systematically chart the emerging landscape of LLM-based affective PI tools. By mapping macro temporal and model adoption trajectories, establishing a multi-layered system taxonomies, and dissecting research methodologies across the system lifecycle, our results indicate that current LLM-based affective PI tools still encounter design and methodological challenges in pipeline construction, interaction modality, and participant recruitment. To address these issues, we provide actionable recommendations for future research to develop more robust, low-burden, and inclusive systems within the field.
