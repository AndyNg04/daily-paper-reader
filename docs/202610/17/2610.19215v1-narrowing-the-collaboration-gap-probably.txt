Abstract 

Large language models are increasingly deployed as teams of agents that hold different private information about a shared task. How should such agents concisely communicate in a way that promotes aggregation of decision-relevant information? Communicating only proposed actions can discard decision-relevant uncertainty: two agents may prefer the same action for different reasons. We study an alternative interface in which agents communicate calibrated beliefs over actions. We first analyze a simple synthetic setting that captures the structure of multi-round reasoning under partial information. In this setting, we show theoretically and empirically that communicating unbiased probabilities can be strictly more powerful than communicating actions. We then test the same principle in a collaborative maze-solving task with trained transformers and pretrained LLM agents. Our results demonstrate that probability communication outperforms action communication when the exchanged predictions preserve calibrated uncertainty. When communicated probabilities are biased, we show that the benefits of probability communication can disappear. We give a post-hoc conversational calibration intervention that consistently improves decision-making by correcting these biases both in a toy transformer setting and when pretrained LLMs use the corrections in context. These results suggest that probabilistic communication is useful for multi-agent collaboration not merely because it transmits more information, but because calibrated uncertainty gives other agents a reliable object to update on. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used within multi-agent systems where agents may share a task but hold different private information. For example, physical robots may see different sensor data, and coding agents may inspect different files when the relevant repository is too large for any single context window. To succeed in such settings, agents must communicate to combine their private information to make better decisions than any single agent could alone. In practice, the same constraints that motivate multi-agent design often make full information sharing infeasible. Communication may be limited by bandwidth, memory, context length, or by the fact that different agents’ observations are not mutually legible across modalities. And even if one agent could transmit its full observation, the receiving agent may not be able to process it into a correct answer [Davidson et al., 2025]. More generally, recent work has shown that LLMs often struggle in these collaborative settings, exhibiting failures in information sharing [Cemri et al., 2025], belief updating [Li et al., 2023, Wilie et al., 2024], and joint decision-making under partial information [Davidson et al., 2025, Eisenstein et al., 2026]. What should agents communicate in order to aggregate distributed private information? 

A natural low-bandwidth alternative to communicating their hidden information is for agents to exchange only their proposed actions. However, communicating actions can omit decision-relevant uncertainty. A promising direction comes from the agreement literature, which shows that Bayesian reasoners with different observations can exchange beliefs to converge towards predictions that aggregate their information [Frongillo et al., 2023]. More recent work weakens Bayesian rationality to calibration-style conditions, including 

conversation calibration , and studies when agreement would lead to optimal actions given the agents’ combined observations [Collina et al., 2025, 2026, Kearns et al., 2026]. This perspective suggests that reliable multi-agent LLM collaboration may require mechanisms that go beyond ad hoc message exchange: agents must be able to recognize their own uncertainty and interpret others’ uncertainty. 

# 1.1 Our contributions 

Our hypothesis is that, under partial information, agents collaborate more effectively when they communicate calibrated beliefs rather than only their preferred decisions. We investigate this hypothesis theoretically in a stylized theoretical model and empirically on the collaborative maze task of Davidson et al. [2025]. We study both transformers trained for the task and large-scale, pretrained LLM agents. Across these settings, we compare two low-bandwidth communication protocols: 1. Action-only communication: In each round, the speaker sends a single action that it currently believes is most likely correct. For transformers this is the action with highest softmax probability; for LLM agents, it is the action selected in the model’s response. 22. Probability communication: In each round, the speaker sends a distribution over actions. For transformers this is the softmax vector. For LLM agents, we consider verbalized probabilities , where the model reports a probability vector in text, and sampled probabilities , where the message is the empirical distribution over independent response rollouts. Our results are threefold. First, in a stylized chained-parity model of multi-round collaboration, we show a theoretical separation between communicating probabilities and communicating actions. Exact probability messages aggregate one new piece of information per round, ultimately leading to full information aggregation. Meanwhile, action-only messages can reveal only the first agent’s information and then stall. Second, we test the same principle in controlled transformer experiments on collaborative maze solving (see Figure 1). We see that probability communication improves over action communication when the learned messages preserve calibrated uncertainty. When probability messages are biased , this advantage can disappear. To address this, we introduce a post-hoc calibration algorithm that corrects biased reports and recovers much of the performance lost when raw probability reports are distorted. Third, we evaluate the same communication protocols using pretrained LLM agents on the maze task. We find that simply asking models to verbalize probabilities is not enough: raw verbalized probabilities are often no better than action messages. We show that across the tested models, applying post-hoc calibration as an intervention consistently improves performance, indicating that probabilistic messages become substantially more useful in-context when corrected into forecasts that other agents can reliably update on. Beyond the agreement literature discussed above, our work also draws on research on calibrated forecasting, uncertainty elicitation in language models, sampling-based uncertainty, multi-agent LLM debate and collabo-ration, and learned communication in multi-agent reinforcement learning. We defer an extended discussion of these connections to Appendix A. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

Formally, we study a setting with two agents, Alice and Bob, who collaborate to find a correct action to take. Each data point consists of a feature x ∈ X and its associated correct action a∗ ∈ A , drawn from a joint distribution over X × A . Neither agent observes the full feature: Alice observes xA ∈ X A and Bob observes 

xB ∈ X B , with x = ( xA, x B ) ∈ X A × X B . The correct action depends on the full feature x, so neither agent can reliably identify a∗ from their own observations alone. In the special case where a∗ is deterministically determined by x, the agents’ combined information is sufficient to identify a⋆.

Communication protocol. The agents communicate for a fixed number of rounds K before selecting an action. Alice speaks on odd rounds and Bob on even rounds; we write σ(k) ∈ { A, B } for the round-k speaker. We focus on the bandwidth-limited regime, in which each round’s message is much smaller than the speaker’s private observation. We consider two types of communication protocols. In the action-communication protocol, the round-k

message is a single proposed action ˆak ∈ A . In the probability-communication protocol, the round-k message is a distribution over actions ˆpk ∈ ∆( A). After round K, the agents execute the action chosen by the final speaker: the final action message ˆaK under action communication, and arg max a ˆpK (a) under probability communication. 

Calibration. Calibration formalizes when a probability message can be interpreted as meaningful uncertainty. Intuitively, a probabilistic message is calibrated if its probabilities agree with long-run frequencies. For example, among all instances in which an agent assigns probability 0.7 to an action a, that action should be correct approximately 70% of the time. 

Definition 2.1 (Calibration) . Let ˆp be a ∆( A)-valued random variable, where the randomness is over the draw of the instance and any randomness in the agents’ procedure. We say ˆp is calibrated if, for every action 

a ∈ A , Pr( a⋆ = a | ˆp) = ˆ p(a) almost surely. 3Calibration is a marginal property: it says that an agent’s stated uncertainty is correct in aggregate over all instances in which the agent sends the same distribution. With multiple agents, we may also ask if one agent’s forecast remains calibrated after accounting for another communicated forecast. This is the notion of cross-calibration. Intuitively, Alice’s forecast is cross-calibrated with Bob’s forecast if, among all instances where Alice sends a particular distribution, subdividing those instances according to Bob’s distribution does not change the correctness of Alice’s probabilities. 

Definition 2.2 (Cross calibration) . Let ˆp and ˆq be ∆( A)-valued random variables. We say ˆp is cross-calibrated with respect to ˆq if, for every action a ∈ A , Pr( a⋆ = a | ˆp, ˆq) = ˆ p(a) almost surely. Calibration requires an agent’s probabilities to be correct in aggregate, while cross-calibration requires them to remain correct even after conditioning on another communicated forecast. This definition conditions only on the communicated probabilities, not on the other agent’s private observation. Conversation calibration instantiates cross calibration in a communication protocol, requiring forecasts in every round to be cross-calibrated with respect to forecasts of the previous round. 

Definition 2.3 (Conversation calibration) . Following Collina et al. [2025], in a probability-communication protocol, the conversation is calibrated through round K if the initial forecast ˆp1 is calibrated and, for every round 2 ≤ k ≤ K, the round-k forecast is cross-calibrated with respect to the previous round’s forecast: 

Pr( a⋆ = a | ˆpk, ˆpk−1) = ˆ pk(a) for all a ∈ A almost surely. 

# 3 When do probabilities outperform actions? 

This section establishes when probability communication can be more useful than exchanging only actions. The gap appears when the best action is too coarse to reveal which evidence state produced it, which we demonstrate via a multi-round separation using a chained parities task. A warm-up example for one-round, where two posterior states induce the same best action but different probabilities, is included in Appendix B. The chained-parity task is intended as a stylized abstraction of general multi-round reasoning problems. In particular, many reasoning problems can be represented as a computation over a chain or graph of interdependent states: each state represents an intermediate reasoning step that incorporates new evidence towards solving the problem. This structure appears, for instance, in multi-hop or inductive reasoning, where intermediate facts are composed along a graph [Yang et al., 2018, Trivedi et al., 2022, Sinha et al., 2019]; and in proof-style reasoning, where intermediate claims support later ones [Tafjord et al., 2021, Dalvi et al., 2021]. In the chained-parity task, each “prefix parity" represents an intermediate state, and the next bit in a bitstring is the evidence needed to advance the chain (Figure 2). We are interested in such problems in collaborative settings, where no single agent observes all information needed to perform the reasoning task, and each agent refines its prediction by using information supplied by the other. We will show that probability messages can preserve a sufficient summary of the accumulated state, allowing each round to incorporate one new piece of private information, whereas action messages can collapse intermediate states and thereby prevent later information from being aggregated.

# 4 When do probability reports need calibration? 

Theorem 3.2 assumes the round-k speaker transmits its Bayes posterior pk exactly. In practice, reported probabilities may be distorted. # 5 Maze experiments with learned and pretrained agents 

We next move beyond chained parities to test these communication principles on a more natural decision problem: collaborative maze-solving. The task, proposed by Davidson et al. [2025] to test collaborative capabilities of language models, preserves the same core structure: agents have partial information and must aggregate information over multiple rounds before choosing an action. However, the underlying object of uncertainty is now much more complex: agents must form and update beliefs about the correct next move by reasoning over partially observed paths. This setting therefore lets us test whether the lessons from the chained parities example extend to richer collaborative problems. 

Maze task. The maze-solving task consists of a dataset of N × N mazes, each with a fixed start state, fixed end state, and cells denoting either a path or a wall, see Figure 1. Mazes are sampled according to a wall-density parameter, which controls the expected fraction of cells that are walls. In our collaborative setup, each agent observes only a partial map of the maze with half of the cells hidden. Together, the two agents’ maps recover the full maze. Our experimental setup follows the primary setting studied by Davidson et al. 8[2025]. We use N = 6 , a wall density of 30%. The start state is in the top left corner and the end state in the bottom right corner, which creates a directional bias where some actions are more likely than others. Unlike Davidson et al. [2025], we restrict attention to mazes with a single correct solution for ease of evaluation. An example maze (and the corresponding two views) are presented in Figure 11. 

# 5.1 Controlled transformer experiments 

We first study the problem in a controlled setup, where, as in Section 3, we train a sequence of transformers that communicate across rounds. 

Transformer setup. We train small transformers on a supervised next-move prediction task. Each example is represented as a partial-view maze string with the @ symbol denoting the current position, along with any previously communicated message. The target label is the next move along the solution path. Full experimental details are in Appendix E. We report maze success rate, defined as the fraction of mazes for which the model predicts every move in the solution path correctly. 

(R1) Probability messages improve controlled maze-solving. Figure 4 shows that trained models under probability communication indeed outperform trained models under action communication. Moreover, it shows that over four rounds of communication, maze success rate improves consistently when agents communicate probabilities; this is not the case for action communication. In Appendix F we give examples of mazes where communicating probabilities over multiple rounds converge to the correct answer. 

(R2) Calibration repairs biased transformer messages. Next, we study the two biases that conversation calibration is designed to mitigate when agents communicate probabilities: global miscalibration and sycophantic anchoring. Rather than communicating the transformer probabilities directly, we first distort them according to Equation 1, and then optionally apply a post-hoc debiasing protocol. The debiasing model is a single-layer network trained with cross-entropy loss against the true label. We compare self-calibration, which uses only the current transformer’s probabilities, with conversation calibration, which uses both the current and previous round’s probabilities. The unbiased probability baseline improves monotonically over four rounds; see Figure 5. In contrast, global miscalibration alone and global miscalibration combined with anchoring cause performance to collapse and deteriorate over rounds. Self-calibration recovers a substantial fraction of the lost success under global miscalibration, but its imperfect corrections still accumulate through the conversation. When both biases are present, conversation calibration recovers nearly all of the baseline performance. Thus, cross-calibrating against the previous round’s probabilities can recover approximately unbiased messages and substantially improve downstream maze success. 

# 5.2 Pretrained LLM experiments 

We now test if the same principle holds for large-scale, pretrained language models on the maze task.

We allow models to generate reasoning (up to 2048 tokens). We consider three communication protocols: actions, verbalized probabilities, or sampled probabilities. In the action exchange protocol, each agent communicates the action chosen in its response. In the verbalized probability exchange protocol, each agent communicates the probability vector reported in its response. In the sampled probability exchange protocol, we sample M independent responses from the model, and the message communicated is the empirical distribution over the answers. Applying Algorithm 1 to verbalized probabilities consistently improves performance across all models. We note that final performance is far below what can be achieved by training a next-move predictor on the maze data, as shown in the previous section. This gap can be attributed to three factors. First, the LLMs were not explicitly trained on mazes like ours, and so their prior knowledge this task is limited. Second, the calibration procedure does not help the model re-interpret information: it is trained only on the communicated probabilities and therefore does not introduce new knowledge specific to each maze that is absent from what is communicated. If the verbalized probabilities carry little information, calibration cannot alleviate that. Third, the LLM must interpret probabilities in-context, and it may not reason reliably about uncertainty information. Whereas prior work has highlighted the benefits of calibration in a single-agent setting [Zhao et al., 2021], our results point to the need for improved uncertainty calibration in multi-agent contexts. 

(R4) What does the post-hoc calibrator learn? Next, to better understand where the calibrator’s gains come from, we investigate what the post-hoc calibration procedure learns, and in particular whether it learns anything beyond the marginal action frequencies. Because our mazes start from the top left, the correct action is down or right 97% of the time (with roughly equal frequencies on down and right), while left or up only 3% of the time. Since the calibrator is trained on the distribution of correct actions, it could exploit 11 Figure 8: (R5) Our post-hoc calibration procedure matches or outperforms sampled probabilities constructed from M = 10 sampled responses. this imbalance by simply learning a correction that suppresses predictions on the rare actions. We test this hypothesis with a naive rare-action correction baseline. Whenever the model’s verbalized probability vector assigns the highest probability to up or left, we replace it with the vector assigning probability 0.5 to each of down and right; otherwise, we leave the prediction unchanged. Note that this baseline uses only the marginal action frequencies and does not depend on the models’ reported probabilities beyond the argmax. Figure 7 shows the results. For GPT-5.4-nano, the naive baseline performs similarly to the calibrator, indicating that much of the calibrator’s gains can be explained by correcting the mode bias. For GPT-5, the calibrator slightly outperforms the naive baseline. This suggests that the calibration procedure learns corrections that cannot simply be reduced to suppressing rare actions and that LLMs can sometimes leverage nuanced probabilities to improve downstream performance. Further work on analyzing and improving how LLMs output and interpret calibrated probabilities offers the opportunity to unlock the full benefits of conversation calibration. 

(R5) Sampled probabilities only partially elicit model uncertainty. Having established that verbalized probabilities are unreliable, we next compare against a stronger elicitation baseline: empirical probabilities from multiple sampled rollouts. This protocol estimates the model’s action distribution directly from repeated responses, but is more expensive than a single verbalized probability report. We compare sampled against verbalized-probability exchange as well as post-hoc calibration. Figure 8 shows that on GPT-4.1-mini, sampled probability exchange improves over verbalized probability exchange and is within variance of our post-hoc calibration approach. On GPT-5.4-nano, sampled probability exchange performs similarly to verbalized probability exchange. Here, post-hoc calibration consistently achieves higher mean success rates. These results suggest that this simple post-hoc procedure can compete with a sampling-based uncertainty estimate at a fraction of the cost, since it only requires one rollout. 

(R6) Calibration explains when sampled probabilities help. GPT-4.1-mini behaves as expected: it performs poorly with verbalized probabilities but improves with sampled probabilities. For GPT-5.4-nano, however, sampled probabilities are on par with verbalized probabilities. This might be surprising at first glance, since we would expect the sampled probability measure to perform better according to our hypothesis. To understand why this might happen, we dig deeper into the distribution of both the verbalized and sampled probabilities in Figure 9. We plot a histogram of maximum probability values under the verbalized and sampled probability exchange protocols, together with the accuracy of the corresponding argmax action. For GPT-4.1-mini, the verbalized and sampled top-choice confidences are similarly miscalibrated across most bins in both rounds 1 and 2. However, in round 2, sampled probabilities more often assign a maximum probability of 1, and these predictions are reasonably well-calibrated. By contrast, verbalized probabilities continue to be overconfident. As a result, sampled probabilities achieve higher accuracy in round 2. For 12 Figure 9: (R6) Histogram of maximum assigned probabilities and corresponding argmax-action accuracy under verbalized and sampled probability exchange. For GPT-4.1-mini, sampled probabilities are better calibrated than verbalized probabilities and lead to stronger downstream performance. For GPT-5.4-nano, sampled probabilities are more overconfident, while verbalized probabilities are comparatively better calibrated. 

Figure 10: (R7) Communicating more information does not always help. Solely communicating probabilities produced by our post-hoc calibration procedure leads to higher success than communicating the full response. GPT-5.4-nano, we observe a similar pattern in the 1.0 probability bin, but sampled probabilities do not outperform verbalized probabilities overall. This can be attributed to the fact that, outside the 1.0 probability bin, the sampled probabilities are highly overconfident. This contrast further highlights the close relationship between calibration and downstream performance. The behavior of both models is consistent with broader trends in LLMs. As model capability improves, LLMs appear to become better at expressing uncertainty through verbalized probabilities [Xiong et al., 2024], which may help explain GPT-5.4-nano’s improved verbal calibration. One possible explanation is that GPT-5.4-nano’s sampled rollouts reflect stronger commitment due to more alignment training such as RLHF. This is consistent with prior work linking RLHF-style alignment to sharpened output distributions and increased overconfidence [Leng et al., 2025]. 

(R7) Communicating more information does not necessarily improve collaboration. Finally, using GPT-4.1-mini and GPT-5.4-nano, we compare against an unconstrained communication baseline, in which each agent receives the previous agent’s full response, including any generated reasoning. This is the setup studied by Davidson et al. [2025]. For GPT-4.1-mini, we use the same prompt as in our other experiments, which asks the model for its uncertainty; therefore the communicated full response includes both the model’s reasoning and verbalized probabilities. For GPT-5.4-nano, we slightly modify the prompt to also explicitly have the model tell us what to communicate to the other agent, as otherwise it’s reasoning is hidden inside the API. We provide the modified prompt in Appendix E. The results are found in Figure 10. For both models, we see that unrestricted communication performs poorly as its success rate across all rounds 13 is close to zero. This failure is unlikely to reflect a basic inability to parse or reason about any maze structure the communicated message may contain, since GPT-5.4-nano solves more than 85% of the mazes when given the full maze directly (Figure 6). Rather, this experiment demonstrates that simply providing the next agent with more information does not necessarily improve collaboration, and probability exchange can be more effective between agents than unconstrained natural-language communication, especially when long or noisy contexts make full-response communication difficult to use.

# A Additional Related Work 

Calibration, scoring rules, and agreement-based aggregation. Our use of probabilistic messages is closely related to the classical literature on calibrated forecasting. Dawid [1982] formalized the idea that a forecaster’s stated probabilities should match empirical frequencies, while Foster and Vohra [1998] showed that calibrated forecasting can be achieved constructively in repeated prediction. The theoretical backbone of our work also connects to agreement and information aggregation. Classical agreement results show that Bayesian agents with common priors cannot sustain common-knowledge disagreement [Aumann, 1976, Aumann and Hart, 2006], and that repeated exchange of beliefs can converge to agreement [Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis, 1982, Aaronson, 2005]. Recent work replaces ideal Bayesian assumptions with tractable calibration-style conditions [Collina et al., 2025, 2026, Kearns et al., 2026]. Frongillo et al. [2023] show that agreement need not imply accuracy without additional signal-structure assumptions. Our contribution is complementary: we study how these ideas behave in LLM-agent collaboration, and empirically compare action messages, raw probabilistic messages, sampled probabilities, and post-hoc conversation-calibrated probabilities. 

Uncertainty and calibration in language models. A large body of work shows that neural-network and language-model probabilities are not automatically calibrated. Guo et al. [2017] showed that modern neural networks can be substantially miscalibrated and that simple post-hoc temperature scaling can improve calibration. Desai and Durrett [2020] studied calibration in pretrained transformers such as BERT and RoBERTa, while Jiang et al. [2021] evaluated when generative language models’ confidence estimates align with correctness. Zhao et al. [2021] showed that few-shot language-model predictions can be biased by prompt artifacts and proposed contextual calibration to mitigate these effects. Recent work studies how to elicit uncertainty from large language models more directly. Kadavath et al. [2022] evaluate whether language models can predict their own correctness, finding that self-evaluation can be informative but is format- and scale-dependent. Lin et al. [2022] train models to express uncertainty in words, and Mielke et al. [2022] study whether conversational agents’ linguistic expressions of confidence are calibrated. Tian et al. [2023] compare confidence-elicitation strategies for RLHF-trained models, while Xiong et al. [2024] provide a broad empirical evaluation of verbalized, token-probability, and sampling-based confidence estimates. Kapoor et al. [2024] show that verbalized uncertainty in LLMs remains unreliable under prompting alone. These papers motivate our distinction between verbalized probabilities and probabilities that are calibrated for use by another agent in a conversation. In addition, work on sycophancy [Perez et al., 2023, Sharma et al., 2024] motivates our biased-report model, where an agent’s stated probability may be pulled toward a previous speaker’s report rather than reflecting only its private evidence. 

Sampling-based uncertainty and reasoning. Sampling multiple model outputs is another common way to estimate uncertainty. Chain-of-thought prompting [Wei et al., 2022] and self-consistency decoding [Wang 18 et al., 2023] use multiple reasoning traces to improve single-agent reasoning by marginalizing over sampled answers. SelfCheckGPT [Manakul et al., 2023] uses consistency across sampled generations as a black-box signal for hallucination detection. Kuhn et al. [2023] and Farquhar et al. [2024] argue that uncertainty in language generation should often be measured over semantic equivalence classes rather than surface forms. Our sampled-probability protocol uses a related sampling signal, but for a different purpose: the empirical distribution over actions is communicated to another agent with complementary private information, rather than being used only to choose a single model’s final answer. 

Multi-agent LLM collaboration. Our maze experiments build on the collaborative maze-solving bench-mark of Davidson et al. [2025], which demonstrates a “collaboration gap” between single-agent and multi-agent performance under partial observability. Eisenstein et al. [2026] study stylistically similar tasks to evalu-ate multi-agent LLM collaboration in partially observable settings. Other work studies multi-agent LLM interaction as a general reasoning or evaluation scaffold. Du et al. [2024] use multi-agent debate to improve factuality and reasoning, Chen et al. [2024] propose a round-table consensus procedure among diverse LLMs, and Chan et al. [2024] use multi-agent debate for LLM-based text evaluation. Guo et al. [2024] survey the broader literature on LLM-based multi-agent systems. Unlike these works, our agents have different private observations, and our main question is not whether discussion helps in aggregate, but which low-bandwidth message format preserves decision-relevant information. 

Learned communication in multi-agent reinforcement learning. There is also a large literature on learning communication protocols in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning. Foerster et al. [2016] introduce RIAL and DIAL, showing that agents can learn discrete or differentiable communication protocols in partially observable cooperative tasks. Sukhbaatar et al. [2016] propose CommNet, where agents learn continuous communication alongside their policies. Das et al. [2019] introduce TarMAC, which learns targeted communication by deciding both what to communicate and whom to address. Our work differs from this line by focusing on an interpretable communication channel—beliefs over actions—and by identifying conversation calibration as the condition under which such messages can be reliably used by another agent.

# E.2 Transformer experiments 

For both tasks we use a small, character-level GPT based on the NanoGPT implementation [Karpathy, 2022]: 

4 transformer blocks, 6 attention heads, embedding dimension 240 , and a causal mask. Each agent maintains its own per-task vocabulary derived from the raw character set of its inputs, so all sequences are tokenized at the character level. The context length is set to prefix_size + 1 , where the prefix is the serialized example up to (but not including) the answer; any annotations appended by a collaborator (see below) extend the 25 prefix accordingly. We treat each task as next-token prediction with the loss measured only at the answer position(s). 

Per-agent inputs. For chained parity, agent 0 ("Bob") sees the odd-indexed bits and agent 1 ("Alice") sees the even-indexed bits; the unobserved positions are replaced with a mask token, and the label bit is appended after an = delimiter. For mazes, each agent’s input is the flattened 6 × 6 grid restricted to its assigned half of the cells, with the current position marked by @, followed by = and the next move from 

{d, r, u, l }. A single maze with path length ℓ contributes ℓ training examples, each predicting the next move from the current position. 

Conversation loop. We instantiate our protocol as iterative training across rounds: in round r, agent 

r mod K (with K the number of agents) trains a fresh transformer from scratch on its currently annotated dataset. After a fixed number of optimizer steps, the trained agent runs over its inputs and emits a message for each example, which is then appended to the prefix of the next agent’s inputs in round r+1 . Because each round trains a freshly initialized model, any improvement in joint performance must come through the appended messages rather than transferred weights. 

Optimization. Each agent is trained from scratch with AdamW ( β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .99 , weight decay 

0.1, gradient clip 1.0) in bfloat16 mixed precision on a single GPU, with batch size 1024 and no gradient accumulation. The learning rate uses linear warmup over 100 steps to a peak of 10 −4, followed by cosine decay to 10 −5 over the remainder of training. We run 2000 iterations per round on chained parity ( 5 rounds) and 50 ,000 iterations per round on mazes ( 4 rounds); in both cases we keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss within each round. 

# E.3 LLM experiments 

The LLM experiments follow the same per-round procedure as our transformer experiments, with each agent now replaced by a pretrained LLM queried through the API rather than a model trained from scratch. Unless otherwise noted we use temperature 1.0, run 4 rounds of conversation per maze, and evaluate on N = 30 

mazes, with 10 rollouts each, drawn from the held-out test split. At each round, the active agent receives a prompt containing (i) a legend-annotated rendering of its own masked 6 × 6 maze, (ii) the sequence of moves taken so far (from which the current position can be inferred), and (iii) the other agent’s most recent response from the previous round. We consider three communication settings, which differ only in what part of the partner’s response is appended to the next prompt: the full free-form message, a single move token in 

{d, r, u, l }, or a probability vector over the four moves. Probabilities can either be elicited directly from the model in the response, post-calibrated from the verbalized answer, or obtained from rollouts. The agent is to produce a single move after the delimiter ~, and – in the verbalized setting – a probability vector after the delimiter %. The joint trajectory advances by one step after each conversation of K rounds; a single incorrect move terminates the maze, and we report the resulting per-maze success rate.

# G Limitations 

Our work studies one specific axis of multi-agent LLM collaboration—the communication protocol when information is partitioned between two agents—and we make no claim to have addressed the full complexity of this space, including issues of task decomposition, trust between agents, and prompt sensitivity. On the theory side, while we establish a clean separation between action and probability communication in the chained parity model, a complete characterization of when and why this gap arises in general remains open. The maze task, though a useful controlled testbed, is stylized relative to real collaborative tasks. Finally, our setting is restricted to two agents; extending the framework to networks of more than two agents is a natural direction for future work. 

# H Compute Resources 

Our experiments were conducted partially on a local desktop computer and on a compute cluster with various types of graphical processing units (GPUs), including Nvidia RTX3090 and Nvidia A6000 GPUs. Training a single parity experiment can be done on a household-grade GPU in less than one hour time. The training of the maze transformers took approximately 6-12 hours per run depending on the GPU. Our experiments thus amount to approximately 300 − 400 GPU hours. This is an estimate for the experiments included in the paper and does not reflect potential developing and debugging runtime. All LLM experiments were conducted directly via OpenAI’s public API and did not require local computational resources beyond a standard laptop. 30
