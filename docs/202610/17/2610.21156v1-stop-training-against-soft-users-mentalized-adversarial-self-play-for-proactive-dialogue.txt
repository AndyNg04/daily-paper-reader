Abstract 

Proactive dialogue agents must guide multi-turn interactions toward task goals while adapt-ing to users whose intentions and attitudes evolve. Existing planners improve system side action selection through search, learned poli-cies, or user aware planning, but are often trained and evaluated against fixed or overly compliant simulators; as a result, high simu-lated success can reflect soft user bias rather than robust dialogue ability. We introduce MASP, a Mentalized Adversarial Self Play 

framework that treats proactive dialogue as a partially observable game between a system and a rationally resisting user. MASP repre-sents the user’s evolving mental state with a BDI based latent state, separates reward side teacher mentalization from policy side student mentalization, and alternates self play updates for the system and user policies. This turns robustness to rational resistance into a training objective rather than only an evaluation condi-tion. Across diverse proactive dialogue bench-marks, compared with the widely used SFT and Planner-only approaches, MASP substan-tially improves success rate (SR) and rationality constrained success (RCSR), reduces average turns (AT), and performs better against hard users who make the task goal more difficult to achieve. 

1 Introduction 

Proactive dialogue requires more than a fluent next response. In tasks such as persuasion, emotional support, empathy, and negotiation, an agent must guide a multi-turn interaction toward a goal while anticipating how each utterance may change the user’s beliefs, desires, and decisions. Although large language models (LLMs) are strong reac-tive conversationalists, proactive dialogue remains challenging because next-token optimization alone does not provide long-range strategic adaptation (He et al., 2024a,b). Recent work has studied var-ious planning and simulation approaches to this challenge (Wang et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). Most progress, however, remains centered on planners. Prior methods improve system-side ac-tion selection through trainable planners, search, and user world modeling (Deng et al., 2023; He et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2024b; He et al., 2025, 2024b; Rakib et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025; Dao and Liao, 2025; Dong et al., 2025). Despite their different mechanisms, they largely preserve the same training asymmetry: the system is optimized as the sole strategic learner, whereas the user is fixed, externally specified, or only weakly mod-eled. This asymmetry can lead to misleading progress. A policy trained against a fixed or overly permis-sive simulator may achieve high simulated success while learning brittle behavior that fails once the user is less compliant (Kreyssig et al., 2018; Keizer et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a). Recent LLM-based work moves beyond prompted role-play to-ward dedicated user LMs, profile-conditioned sim-ulators, and iterative adversarial refinement (David-son et al., 2023; Sekuli’c et al., 2024; Naous et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). These works make the soft user problem explicit, but they mainly strengthen the simulator or evaluation environment; they do not directly answer how a proactive policy should be trained so that its gains survive stronger, more strategic, and partially observed users. A natural way to address this issue is to model the user’s evolving internal state. Recent studies have examined intention modeling, user state track-ing, and theory of mind for persuasion and emo-tional support (Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Park et al., 2026), yet such information remains an auxiliary feature or simulator-side component rather than the basis of the training interaction. We propose MASP ( Mentalized Adversarial Self arXiv:2610.21156v1 [cs.CL] 15 Oct 2026 Play) 1, a training paradigm that instead formulates proactive dialogue as a partially observable game between two agents over a shared latent mental state space. Each turn is governed by a hidden user state represented by free-form belief , desire ,and intention (BDI) together with compact affec-tive variables. The user policy conditions on this hidden state, while the system observes only an esti-mate inferred from dialogue history; consequently, the system is trained not only to choose useful ut-terances, but also to infer who it is talking to and how that person changes over time. The key design of MASP is to make mental-ization part of the optimization itself. A frozen teacher mentalizer defines the latent state used by reward computation, whereas the system observes only a student estimate, reducing the risk that the policy directly exploits an adaptive reward state es-timator. On top of this latent state, MASP defines dense progress toward a task-specific target mind state, measured relative to the user’s own starting state. The system is rewarded for both moving the user toward the target and accurately inferring the current state, while the user is rewarded for rational resistance rather than unconditional rejec-tion. This formulation differs from planner-focused approaches by coupling opponent modeling, long-horizon dialogue control, and simulator robustness within a single training game; it also differs from work that only builds simulators because the user model is not merely an evaluation tool, but an adap-tive training opponent. We evaluate MASP on proactive dialogue bench-marks spanning persuasion, emotional support, em-pathy, and negotiation, with a focus on rationality-constrained success under difficult user simula-tors. Our contributions are threefold: (1) to our knowledge, MASP is the first multi-turn dialogue training framework to explicitly model BDI mental states and let those states evolve during training; (2) we are the first to apply self-play to a multi-turn di-alogue training framework, proposing a mentalized self-play method that jointly trains the system and user and is designed to avoid the soft user problem in practical applications; and (3) to address the fact that widely used evaluation rules do not capture model performance against difficult users in realis-tic settings, we evaluate models under difficult user simulators and introduce rationality-constrained

2 Related Work 

Proactive dialogue planning has progressed from search at test time to trainable planners and policy learning with user information. GDP-Zero uses Monte Carlo Tree Search over LLM simulated con-tinuations (Yu et al., 2023), while PPDPP trains a planner module with supervised fine tuning and self play reinforcement learning (Deng et al., 2023). Later methods improve planning through dual pro-cess deliberation, diversified user simulation, la-tent policy learning, intrinsic user world modeling, and online selection based on value estimates over frozen LLM proposals (He et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2024b; He et al., 2024b, 2025; Rakib et al., 2025). These methods strengthen system action selection, but still largely optimize a system plan-ner against fixed, prompted, or otherwise externally specified users. User simulation is central to dialogue training and evaluation, but simulator realism remains a bottleneck (Shi et al., 2019; Davidson et al., 2023; Sekulic et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b). Prior work shows that simulator choice affects policy quality and transferability, and that offline policy learning can overfit to simulator ar-tifacts (Kreyssig et al., 2018; Keizer et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a). Recent LLM based studies make this issue sharper: Flipping the Dialogue 

trains dedicated User LMs from converted human assistant conversations (Naous et al., 2025), and DIAL improves simulator realism through iterative adversarial refinement (Zhu et al., 2025). These works improve simulators or evaluation environ-ments, but do not directly turn user resistance into a coupled training objective for the system policy. MASP also connects to adversarial and multia-gent training. SMAC-Hard shows that narrow op-ponent distributions can induce brittle multiagent policies (Deng et al., 2024), and GAIA formulates noncollaborative dialogue planning as a two player zero sum game with self play and Chain-of-Mind reasoning (Wang et al., 2025a). MASP shares the concern that soft or narrow user models produce brittle policies, but differs by modeling proactive dialogue as a partially observable game over a la-tent mental state geometry: the system sees only a student estimate of the user’s hidden state, while the user is trained to resist rationally. 3 Method 

As shown in Figure 1, MASP is organized as a three-phase training pipeline. Phase 0 trains BDI mentalizers from silver annotations; Phase 1 uses behavior cloning to obtain warm-start and reference policies for the system and user; Phase 2 alternates GRPO updates for the system and user policies with frozen reward-side modules.

3.2 Latent Mental State and Mentalization 

A central question is how to represent the user’s in-ternal state. A pure dialogue embedding is flexible but opaque, making it difficult to specify mean-ingful target states; task-specific slot schemas are interpretable but transfer poorly across domains. BDI provides a useful middle ground: beliefs, de-sires, and intentions are semantically interpretable enough to anchor target state design, yet general enough to apply across persuasion, support, empa-thy, and negotiation. We therefore adopt a BDI-based hybrid representation: 

mt = ( Bt, D t, I t, ρ t, c t, v t), (6) where Bt, Dt, and It are free form natural language descriptions of the user’s belief , desire , and inten-tion , respectively; ρt ∈ [0 , 1] denotes receptivity, 

ct ∈ [0 , 1] denotes commitment or confidence, and 

vt ∈ [−1, 1] denotes valence. The textual BDI com-ponent captures what the user currently believes, wants, and plans to do, while the three scalar vari-ables provide a compact affective geometry that remains stable across tasks. We encode the three textual BDI components separately with a frozen text encoder E(·). In our implementation, E is the corresponding frozen Qwen3 backbone used as a sentence encoder: we mean-pool its final hidden states and ℓ2 normalize the pooled vector. 

eB = E(Bt), eD = E(Dt), eI = E(It),

(7) where each component embedding is normalized to unit length. We then define the final vector state as 

zt = [ eB ; eD; eI ; √αρρt; √αcct; √αvvt]. (8) This preconditions the scalar dimensions by the same weights used in the latent metric, while keep-ing the representation deliberately dynamic: it de-scribes the user’s current state , not a static persona category. In persuasion, support, empathy, or ne-gotiation, what matters is not only who the user is, but what the user currently believes and how that state changes over the course of a dialogue. To obtain this latent state, MASP uses two men-talization modules. A teacher mentalizer Fω pre-dicts the latent state from dialogue history, optional profile, and task goal: 

zt = Fω(ht, p u, g ). (9) This teacher is pretrained on offline silver annota-tions and then frozen. Crucially, it is the only state estimator used on the reward side. The system pol-icy never observes zt directly. Instead, it receives a student estimate 

ˆzt = Mϕ(ht), (10) where Mϕ only conditions on the observable dia-logue history. This split between teacher and stu-dent is one of the key design choices of MASP. Because the reward depends on the frozen teacher state while the policy only sees a student estimate, the policy must learn to infer the user rather than directly exploit the reward model. In other words, mentalization is not just an auxiliary diagnostic module; it becomes part of the optimization prob-lem itself. The offline silver annotations also define a BDI cache. The cache stores dialogue-prefix records with silver BDI states, and also stores a session-level initial BDI state for each training dialogue. We use the term BDI prior for the non-parametric pool of these initial training states, together with any available profile text. This prior is used to sample initial user states in self play and to render continuous student estimates into text by nearest-neighbor retrieval in the encoded BDI space.

The system should both influence the user and un-derstand the user. We therefore define the system reward as

The inference term is especially important: it makes the system optimize not only for what to say , but also for how well it understands the person it is talking to .The user policy is trained adversarially, but not as an unconstrained adversary. A user that always rejects everything is not realistic, and a user that always agrees is equally uninformative. To prevent both collapse modes, we define 

rUt = −wprog ∆st + wrat qt, (16) where qt ∈ {− 1, 0, 1} is a fixed rationality judge signal indicating whether the user response is ratio-nal under the current mental state. The first term rewards resistance to unjustified state change, while the second rewards coherent and rational behavior. Intuitively, the user is not rewarded for rejecting the system blindly; it is rewarded for resisting when resistance is warranted and yielding when change is genuinely justified . This is what makes the user a useful training opponent rather than merely a harder simulator. The scalar rollout rewards in Equations 15 and 16 do not include KL penalties. Instead, both policies are regularized by a token level KL term inside the GRPO training objective against their su-pervised warm start reference policies, as described in Appendix B.4. This matches the implementation: rewards provide task, mentalization, and rationality feedback, while the trainer applies the KL term to the reference policy. 

3.5 Training 

Training follows the three stage pipeline in Algo-rithm 1. We first train teacher and student mentaliz-ers from silver BDI annotations, then use behavior cloning to initialize the system and user policies as warm start references, and finally run alternat-ing GRPO self play with frozen reward side mod-ules. Closed-loop evaluation. Each evaluation episode starts from a held-out initial context. At turn t, the system generates at, the user policy generates ut, and both are appended to the history 

ht+1 . The rationality judge then reads the full accumulated dialogue rather than an isolated response. This loop continues until success or the eight-exchange limit. Appendix C.2 provides the complete protocol and a concrete rollout.

Evaluation Metrics. For each method, we evalu-ate under soft and hard user conditions, where soft 

denotes the easier simulator for the system under the dataset’s task objective and hard denotes the more difficult one. For P4G, ESConv, and Empa-theticDialogues, these correspond to the conven-tional imitation-style and learned adversarial users, respectively; CraigslistBargain reverses this con-vention because the system is the buyer and the Phase 1 BC seller is harder for buyer-side utility (see Appendix C.7). We report SR@8 , the fraction of dialogues that reach the task-specific success cri-terion within T = 8 turns; AT , the average turns to success with failures counted as T ; and RCSR@8 ,success under the rationality constraint defined above. Thus, SR and AT measure task effectiveness and efficiency, while RCSR tests whether success reflects rational user behavior rather than degener-ate or overly compliant interactions. 

Implementation. We compare MASP with two controlled baselines under matched backbones, user settings, turn budgets, and success judges within each dataset. SFT uses the supervised di-alogue policy without self play. Planner-only 

uses the same system side warmup and adaptive planning scaffold as MASP, but does not train the adversarial user policy through mentalized self play. MASP is our full method, which further trains the system and user policies with alternat-ing GRPO style self play. Unless otherwise spec-ified, we report results for both Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-0.6B backbones. Main Results. Table 1 shows that MASP con-sistently improves over both SFT and Planner-only on the completed benchmarks. On P4G, MASP gives the best SR, AT, and RCSR under both soft and hard users for both backbones, indicat-ing that mentalized self play improves not only task completion but also rationality constrained success. On ESConv, MASP reaches near-ceiling SR, but this reflects the prior success conven-tion where somewhat_better counts as success-ful rather than requiring full resolution. We there-fore treat ESConv mainly as transfer and efficiency evidence and avoid overinterpreting saturated SR. On CraigslistBargain, MASP again leads on SR and AT for both backbones, including the buyer-side hard setting where the Phase 1 BC seller is less favorable to the buyer. The main exception is Qwen3-0.6B soft RCSR, where SFT slightly exceeds MASP; this is a small speed-rationality tradeoff in the most permissive seller setting, not a failure of the overall negotiation behavior. The very low Planner-only 0.6B soft SR is not a table bug: in the buyer role, the fixed planner scaffold can still miss closure against a seller whose concessions do not match its hand-written closing strategy, so the learned buyer policy is needed to exploit the phase 2 seller distribution. On EmpatheticDialogues, ab-solute SR is lower because success requires a strict resolved outcome, but MASP still yields the best results for soft and hard users across both back-bones. A P4G horizontal comparison with prior systems, reported in Appendix A.1, shows the same trend in absolute comparison.

4.3 Effectiveness Across Dialogue Turns 

The P4G results show that MASP reaches stronger final performance within the full eight turn budget. We next examine when this advantage appears un-der the rationality-constrained metric, focusing on the hard user setting where resistance is strongest. On the same Qwen3-4B P4G test split, we compute RCSR@t for intermediate turn budgets and retain SR@8 as endpoint task-success context. Table 3 shows that the rationality-constrained gains of MASP emerge well before the last turn un-der the hard user. MASP reaches 0.444 RCSR@3 and 0.485 RCSR@4, while Planner-only reaches 0.212 and 0.273, and SFT reaches only 0.071 and 0.141. Thus the final improvement is not mainly a late recovery effect, nor is it driven only by fast but poorly grounded successes. Instead, mentalized self play appears to help the system identify user state earlier and choose utterances that produce ra-tionally grounded progress with fewer turns against a difficult user. 4.4 Robustness Across User Simulators 

We further test whether the gains of MASP depend on a particular user simulator. On the P4G test split, each system is evaluated against an ensem-ble of three user simulators U. For a method m,we define WorstSR( m) = min u∈U SR( m, u ) and 

WorstRCSR( m) = min u∈U RCSR( m, u ). Ta-ble 4 reports both average and worst case perfor-mance across this ensemble. MASP is strongest under both average and worst-case views. This supports the central claim that men-talized adversarial self-play improves robustness rather than only fitting one simulator. 

5 Conclusion 

We presented MASP, a mentalized adversarial self play framework for proactive dialogue. Instead of training a system policy only against soft or fixed user simulators, MASP models dialogue as a par-tially observable game between two agents over a shared BDI based latent mental state. A frozen teacher mentalizer provides latent state estimates for reward computation, a student mentalizer sup-plies state estimates for the policy, and alternating GRPO self play trains the system to make task progress while training the user to resist in ratio-nal ways. Across completed proactive dialogue benchmarks, MASP improves success, efficiency, and rationality constrained success over SFT and Planner-only baselines. The P4G robustness analy-sis further shows that these gains are not confined to a single user simulator, supporting the value of treating user resistance as part of the training ob-jective rather than only as an evaluation condition. 

Limitations 

MASP has several limitations. First, training depends on silver mental state annotations, the teacher mentalizer, and the rationality judge, so bias or noise in these components can affect the learned policies. Second, the shared BDI style geometry is designed to transfer across domains, but may miss specialized user states in some tasks. Third, alternating self play with two policies and auxiliary mentalizers is more expensive than sin-gle policy planning. Finally, when a dataset does not provide explicit user profiles ( pu = ∅), the privileged information gap between the teacher and student mentalizers is reduced, so the split mainly regularizes reward side state estimation rather than providing strong profile conditioned hidden infor-mation.

B Additional Method Details 

B.1 Training Details 

Training proceeds in three stages. First, we ob-tain silver BDI annotations for dialogue prefixes and use them to train a teacher mentalizer and a student mentalizer. The teacher is frozen after pre-training and defines the state used by reward com-putation, while the student provides the estimate used by the system policy. Second, we warm start both agents with supervised learning on human dia-logues, yielding system and user reference policies that initialize self play and serve as KL anchors. We denote the resulting system and user policy adapters as pi_S and pi_U , corresponding to πθ

and πψ, respectively. During alternating adversarial self play, each outer iteration first freezes the user and updates the system, then freezes the system and updates the user. The reported experiments use a GRPO style update without a value function: rollout steps are partitioned into small groups, returns are nor-malized within each group to form relative advan-tages, and each LoRA policy is updated with a clipped ratio objective plus KL regularization to its supervised reference. After each self play cy-cle, the student mentalizer is refreshed on collected prefixes with targets from the frozen teacher. All quantities used for reward computation, including the teacher state, progress signal, and rationality signal, are treated as stop gradient signals during policy optimization. In implementation, MASP does not modify the causal LM backbone. The student estimate on the system side is rendered into a short BDI control block by retrieving the closest textual BDI record from the BDI prior, while the user side receives the current BDI state. Thus, mentalization enters policy learning through prompts for each role and parameter efficient LoRA updates. 

Silver BDI annotations. The silver BDI labels are produced by an offline LLM based Oracle Be-lief Updater (OBU), not by human annotators. For each dialogue prefix, the OBU receives the task description, role mapping, dialogue history, and task specific calibration hints, and is prompted to output exactly six fields: belief, desire, intention, receptivity, confidence, and valence. We use GPT-4o-2024-08-06 for this offline cache construction, with temperature 0.2 and a maximum of 320 tokens. The resulting cache provides silver supervision for Phase 0 mentalization and the initial states for the BDI prior; reward-side latent state estimates dur-ing self play are supplied by the frozen teacher mentalizer.

We do not modify the backbone architecture. In-stead, the latent state is rendered into a short text block and prepended to the prompt for each role. The system consumes the student estimate ˆzt

C.2 Common Closed Loop Evaluation Protocol 

All benchmarks are evaluated as closed loop roll-outs with an eight turn budget. At each system turn, the system policy generates a response conditioned on the dialogue history and the available mental state estimate. The user side is either the soft imi-tation style user policy or the hard adversarial user policy. After each exchange between assistant and user, a task specific success judge assigns the out-come label used to decide whether the task specific success criterion has been met. Unless otherwise noted, the success judge uses GPT-3.5 with three sampled judgments. We use the following common rationality judge prompt.            

> Common rationality judge
> Task context: [task description]
> User mental state: [current BDI state]
> Most recent exchange: Assistant: [assistant turn]
> User: [user turn]
> Question: Was it rational for a user with that internal mental state to respond this way? A rational response means that the user updates when the assistant gives a concrete and honest reason to update, or reasonably resists when such a reason is absent. Answer with exactly one label: RATIONAL or
> NOT_RATIONAL .

Each episode begins from a held-out initial con-text h0. At turn t, the system produces at from the complete current history and its estimated user state, the user simulator produces ut, and both utterances are appended to form ht+1 . The suc-cess judge then reads the complete accumulated dialogue through ht+1 . If the goal has not been reached, the next exchange continues from that same history, for at most eight system–user ex-changes. The judge is therefore a per-turn check-point over a growing trajectory rather than an eval-uator of an isolated response. C.4 Human–AI Agreement Audit 

Following the dialogue-level evaluation practice used in prior proactive dialogue work (Deng et al., 2023; Rakib et al., 2025), we conducted a supple-mentary human–AI agreement audit for the P4G success judge. We randomly sampled 100 P4G evaluation records from the union of the validation and test splits and asked five human annotators to label the final outcome, binary success, success or terminal turn, and turn-level task labels. The same records were evaluated by the automatic GPT-3.5 judge used in our closed-loop experiments. This audit is intended as a sanity check for the automatic judge rather than as a standalone human evaluation of policy quality. On the coarse endpoint labels, we observed no disagreements between human and GPT-3.5 annotations: final outcome and binary success both reached 100.0% exact agreement, cor-responding to Cohen’s κ = 1 .000 . To avoid rely-ing only on these endpoint labels, we also report finer-grained timing and turn-level agreement. For successful cases, the success-turn exact match was 97.4% with a mean absolute error of 0.039 turns; over all records, terminal-turn exact match was 98.0% with a mean absolute error of 0.030 turns. Turn-level task labels reached 95.7% agreement with Cohen’s κ = 0 .942 . These non-endpoint met-rics provide a more conservative view of judge reli-ability and show that the remaining disagreements mainly concern success timing or intermediate task states rather than the final donation decision.

C.7 CraigslistBargain Evaluation Protocol 

CraigslistBargain success means that the buyer and seller reach an agreement that satisfies the utility criterion for the buyer. The judge first determines whether a deal has been reached and extracts the final agreed price. If any sampled judgment indi-cates no deal, the outcome is treated as no_deal .Otherwise, the final deal price is converted to buyer normalized utility, (pdeal −pseller )/(pbuyer −pseller ),and the interaction is successful when this value meets the success threshold. In our CraigslistBar-gain evaluation, this threshold is fixed to 0.0, so SR@8 counts deals that close within eight turns and achieve nonnegative buyer utility. The main table therefore uses the same paper-level metrics as the other datasets: SR@8, AT, and RCSR@8. Deal completion without the buyer utility threshold is treated only as an auxiliary diagnostic outside the main metric set. For this dataset, the system role is the buyer and the user role is the seller. We there-fore define hard and soft by buyer-side difficulty rather than by the training-stage label: the Phase 1 BC seller is reported as the hard user because it is more difficult for the buyer-system to close favorable deals, while the Phase 2 self-play seller is reported as the soft user because it accepts buyer-favorable deals more often in our evaluation. This reversal is specific to buyer-system CraigslistBar-gain and does not change the soft/hard convention for the cooperative P4G, ESConv, or Empathetic-Dialogues tasks.
