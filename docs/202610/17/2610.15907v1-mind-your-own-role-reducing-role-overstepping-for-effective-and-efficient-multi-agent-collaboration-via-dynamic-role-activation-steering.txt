# ABSTRACT 

Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) have shown strong capability in solving complex tasks by assigning different agents specialized roles and corresponding subtasks. However, agents may overstep their assigned responsibilities and perform subtasks belonging to other roles, which can disrupt workflow, reduce system efficiency, and degrade task performance. To address this issue, we investigate role-related patterns in the model’s internal activation space and uncover two key observa-tions: (i) role instructions induce consistent directional shifts in activation space, and (ii) role-adherent and role-overstepping trajectories exhibit distinct activation patterns. Based on these observations, we propose Dynamic Role Activation Steer-ing (DRAS), a lightweight and effective inference-time activation intervention approach to reduce role overstepping in multi-agent collaboration. Specifically, DRAS constructs a role steering vector and a lightweight role-overstepping detector for each role. During LLM generation, the detector estimates the probability of role overstepping from the current activation state, and DRAS dynamically adjusts the steering strength accordingly to guide the agent toward its assigned respon-sibilities. Experimental results show that DRAS effectively reduces the number of role-overstepping trajectories by up to 86.6% compared with Vanilla MAS, thereby improving task quality score by 10.0%–11.1%, achieving a 1.50 ×–1.61 ×

task-completion speedup, and reducing token consumption by 26.8%–38.7%. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

In recent years, LLM-driven multi-agent systems have made significant progress in solving complex tasks (Ye et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024), particularly for highly collaborative applications like software development. By equipping agents with distinct role profiles and defining interaction protocols, these systems decompose the long-horizon task into coordinated subtasks, allowing different agents to handle different subtasks while jointly completing the overall task. Most well-known multi-agent systems (e.g., ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) and MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024) and AutoGen (Wu et al., 2023)) employ such structured collaboration to improve reliability and end-to-end task performance. However, recent studies (Kim et al., 2026; Cemri et al., 2026; Rath, 2026) have shown that in practice it does not always hold that each agent always follows its assigned role. Agents may exceed their predefined responsibilities and perform subtasks that belong to other roles, resulting in role overstepping (Zhou et al., 2026a). For example, an agent responsible for code review may go beyond providing review comments and directly modify the code. Unlike an isolated generation error, the content generated by role overstepping may propagate along the collaboration chain, and cause redundant execution, responsibility conflicts, and contamination of the shared context. Consequently, role overstepping may degrade the overall task performance and increase the task completion time (Kim et al., 2026; Rath, 2026). For example, even for Qwen-2.5-14B, the role-overstepping rate reaches 35.8%, and further increases to 55.9% for the 2.5B model. With ever growing interest of deploying multi-agent systems on resource-constrained mobile devices that typically run medium or small sized models (Fu et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026b), such a high role-overstepping rate and its consequent task performance degradation and low system efficiency become an essential problem. To address this issue, Wang et al. (2026) examine agent outputs after generation, using rule-based detectors or an additional LLM-as-a-Judge to determine whether a response violates the assigned role, and triggers regeneration when overstepping is detected. However, such post-hoc methods can only identify overstepping after a response has already been generated, wasting the computation spent on the overstepping response. The detection and regeneration process also introduces substantial computational cost and task completion time. Alternatively, Zhou et al. (2026a) leverage role-adherent trajectories to perform LoRA-based LLM fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2021) to improve role adherence. However, fine-tuning the LLM for each role incurs substantial training cost and hardware requirements, limiting its practicality and deployment flexibility. Therefore, how to promptly detect role overstepping and efficiently steer agent behavior remains an important challenge. Activation engineering has recently emerged as an effective approach to steering LLM behavior at inference time (Rimsky et al., 2024; Wehner et al., 2025; Valentino et al., 2026). Its key idea is to identify directions in the model’s activation space associated with specific behavioral attributes, such as honesty (Qiu et al., 2024), truthfulness (Li et al., 2023), and humor (Von R¨ utte et al., 2024), and to derive steering vectors from these directions. Instead of modifying model parameters, these methods directly intervene in internal activations using the extracted steering vectors, enabling cost-efficient behavioral control with adjustable steering strength. Inspired by this, we propose to study if it is possible to analyze and intervene in the model’s activation space during generation, so that we can promptly detect role-overstepping and efficiently fix it via activation steering. To answer this question, we conduct exploratory experiments and reveal two key observations. (i) Role instructions induce consistent directional shifts in activation space, and steering along these directions can be viewed as reinforcing the behavioral constraints specified by the role instructions. (ii) Role-adherent and role-overstepping behaviors exhibit distinct activation patterns. These observations indicate that the activation space contains both the directional information needed for role steering and the state information needed for role-overstepping detection. Based on those observations above, in this paper, we propose Dynamic Role Activation Steering (DRAS), an inference-time activation intervention approach for reducing role overstepping in multi-agent collaboration. For each role, we (i) compare the internal activations produced by the same subtask context with and without the corresponding role instruction and derive a role steering vector from their activation differences, and (ii) collect activations from role-adherent and role-overstepping trajectories to train a lightweight role-overstepping detector. During LLM generation, the detector estimates the probability of role overstepping from the current activation representation, which 2Preprint is then used to dynamically adjust the steering strength and promote role adherence. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DRAS reduces the number of role-overstepping trajectories by up to 86.6% compared with Vanilla MAS and consistently outperforms other methods. By reducing role overstepping, DRAS further improves task quality score by 10.0%–11.1%, achieves a 1.50 ×–1.61 ×

task-completion speedup, and reduces token consumption by 26.8%–38.7%. 

# 2 PRELIMINARIES 

2.1 ROLE OVERSTEPPING IN MULTI -A GENT COLLABORATION 

Unlike end-to-end evaluation of overall task performance in a multi-agent system, role overstepping concerns whether each agent properly executes its assigned role within the internal collaborative workflow. In role-based multi-agent systems, agent responsibilities are usually defined by natural-language role instructions in LLM prompts. Formally, consider a multi-agent system with a role set R = {r1, . . . , r K }. Each role ri is associated with a role instruction Iri , which specifies its responsibilities and behavioral constraints. Given a subtask context xi, the agent with role ri generates a behavior trajectory τi = LLM( xi, I ri ).

Definition: Role ri exhibits role overstepping if τi contains behaviors that fall within the responsibil-ities described in another role rj ’s role instruction Irj .For trajectory evaluation, existing studies (Wang et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026) commonly adopt LLM-as-a-Judge to determine whether each trajectory exhibits role overstepping according to the predefined role division. In addition, Zhou et al. (2026a) introduce a continuous metric based on semantic similarity in the embedding space. By comparing a trajectory with different role descriptions, the metric quantifies its alignment with the assigned role and its degree of shift toward other roles. 2.2 ACTIVATION ENGINEERING 

Activation Steering: Emerging research in activation engineering has uncovered that LLMs encode semantic concepts as linear subspaces within internal activations (Liu et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023; Arditi et al., 2024). This geometric interpretation enables targeted behavioral steering through linear interventions during inference. Rimsky et al. (2024) pioneered activation addition using steering vectors derived from contrastive activations for sentiment and topic control. Following the same principle, Konen et al. (2024) demonstrated that activation vectors can also steer linguistic style, while Zhang et al. (2024) further identified steerable activation directions associated with the truthfulness of model outputs. More recently, Liu et al. (2026); Zhang et al. (2025) extracted activation shifts associated with user personal information to guide personalized generation. In this work, we investigate whether role-specific behaviors exhibit identifiable directional representations in activation space that can be leveraged for role steering in multi-agent collaboration. 

Conditional Activation Steering: Applying a steering vector indiscriminately may interfere with model behaviors that do not require correction. For example, strengthening refusal to harmful queries can also suppress appropriate responses to harmless queries (Lee et al., 2025). Therefore, a fine-grained strategy is to condition the steering intervention on the model’s current behavioral state. Lee et al. (2025) uses activation differences between harmful and harmless prompts to construct a conditioning vector that determines whether steering should be activated. Wang et al. (2025); Zhang et al. (2024) use labeled activations from truthful and untruthful trajectories to train a lightweight linear probe, which detects the truthfulness state of the current generation and adjusts steering strength accordingly. These studies demonstrate that activation space not only contains directional representations for behavioral steering, but also encodes information that reflects the model’s current behavioral state.

For each role, alongside the original role instruction, we introduce a dynamic steering module consisting of a role steering vector and a role-overstepping detector. # 5 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) and LLM Backbones: We evaluate DRAS on two representative role-based multi-agent systems, ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) and MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024), both of which organize multiple LLM agents with predefined role responsibilities to collaboratively complete software-development tasks. For each system, we preserve its original workflow and role instructions. For the main experiments, we use two representative open-source LLMs, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), as the underlying backbone models for all agents. 

Datasets: We evaluate role overstepping and end-to-end task performance on the Software Re-quirement Description Dataset (SRDD), a curated collection of software requirements introduced in Qian et al. (2024). SRDD contains 1,200 software requirement tasks covering diverse software categories and task types. To avoid evaluation data leakage, we additionally use SWE-Dev (Du et al., 2025), another software-development task dataset, for the offline preparation of different methods, including collecting training data for LoRA FT method and constructing role steering vectors and role-overstepping detectors for DRAS. 

Evaluation Metrics: For role overstepping, we report Role-Overstepping Rate (RO Rate), Number of Role-Overstepping Trajectories (RO Traj. #), and Role Clarity Score (RC Score). Specifically, RO Rate is the percentage of tasks with at least one role-overstepping trajectory, and RO Traj. # counts the total number of role-overstepping trajectories. Following Zhou et al. (2026a), RC Score measures role adherence based on the semantic similarity between agent trajectories and their assigned role instruction, with higher values indicating stronger role alignment. Following Qian et al. (2024), task performance is evaluated using Completeness, Executability, and Consistency, and their average is reported as the overall Quality score. More details are provided in Appendix A.2. 

Methods for Comparison: We compare DRAS with the following methods. (i) Vanilla MAS uses the original multi-agent system with only the predefined role instructions and serves as the baseline. 

(ii) Few-shot augments the original role prompt with both role-adherent and role-overstepping examples to strengthen role constraints. (iii) Post-hoc (Wang et al., 2026) uses an LLM-based judge to detect role overstepping after each agent response and regenerates the response when overstepping is detected. (iv) LoRA FT (Zhou et al., 2026a) fine-tunes the backbone LLM on role-adherent trajectories using LoRA to improve role adherence. Detailed descriptions and implementation settings for each method are provided in Appendix A.3.

Ablation Study of Dynamic Steering. We further perform an ablation study on the dynamic steering mechanism of DRAS. Static Steering removes the detector-based control and applies the role steering vector with a fixed strength at every decoding step. As shown in Table 4, by avoiding missed interventions caused by detector errors, this strategy achieves slightly lower RO Rate and RO Traj. # than DRAS. However, this static intervention may disturb generation that is already role-adherent. We observe that Static Steering occasionally leads to meaningless or abnormal outputs in such trajectories. Consequently, although it achieves lower role-overstepping rates, both its RC Score and task Quality are noticeably lower than those of DRAS. We also consider Binary Gating, which adopts the same detector as DRAS but uses 0.5 as a threshold to convert the predicted probability into a binary signal that only determines whether steering is turned on or off. As shown in Table 4, DRAS consistently outperforms Binary Gating across both system-backbone settings in role-overstepping mitigation, task performance, and efficiency. This indicates that continuous adjustment of steering strength based on the estimated role-overstepping probability provides a more effective intervention strategy than a hard binary decision.

For both LLMs, effective correction of role overstepping is concentrated in a subset of middle-to-late layers (e.g., layers 16 - 20 in Qwen), while both shallow and very deep layers exhibit relatively weak steering effectiveness. This pattern may arise because role-related representations are not yet sufficiently developed in shallow layers, while deep layers’ representations may be less amenable to steering. Base Steering Strength Selection. We also evaluate the effectiveness of role steering under different base steering strengths α. As shown in Fig. 4c, increasing α does not consistently lead to better performance. When α is small, the intervention is too weak to effectively reinforce role-adherent behavior. As α increases, the RC Score improves substantially and reaches a favorable range (e.g., α

= 0.8 - 1.0 for the Llama model). However, further increasing α leads to performance degradation, indicating potential over-steering and showing that a larger α is not always better. In addition, our empirical results show that different roles exhibit similar effective α ranges within the same backbone. DRAS therefore uses a shared α setting for all roles under each backbone.

We first retain only the role-overstepping detector without applying steering during the generation of the original role-overstepping trajectory to observe its predictions. As shown in the upper example, the predicted role-overstepping probability begins to increase before the model explicitly generates content beyond the reviewer’s responsibility. This allows DRAS to perform activation steering before explicit role-overstepping content is gener-ated, rather than reacting only after role overstepping has occurred. When the role steering vector is applied, the predicted probability is used to dynamically adjust the steering strength. As shown in the lower example, relatively high role-overstepping probabilities appear toward the end of the trajectory, triggering stronger steering that redirects the generation toward the assigned role. 

# 7 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we presented Dynamic Role Activation Steering (DRAS), an inference-time activation intervention approach to effectively reduce role overstepping in multi-agent collaboration. Based on our observations of consistent role-induced activation shifts and distinct activation patterns between role-adherent and role-overstepping behaviors, DRAS jointly uses role steering vectors and lightweight role-overstepping detectors to dynamically control steering strength during generation. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DRAS reduces the number of role-overstepping trajectories by up to 86.6% compared with Vanilla MAS and consistently outperforms other SOTA methods. By reducing role overstepping, DRAS further improves task quality score by 10.0%–11.1%, achieves a 1.50 ×–1.61 × task-completion speedup, and reduces token consumption by 26.8%–38.7%. Moreover, 9Preprint DRAS has much lower offline preparation cost than LoRA-based fine-tuning, making it much easier to deploy in practice. In contrast to post-hoc methods that rely on detection and regeneration after response completion, DRAS performs timely detection and correction during the initial generation process, which enhances both task performance and system efficiency. These results collectively establish DRAS as a lightweight and effective approach for reducing role overstepping in multi-agent collaboration, thereby improving end-to-end task performance and efficiency. A.2 EVALUATION METRICS 

Role-Overstepping Evaluation. Following prior work (Ye et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025), we use GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) as an LLM-based judge to identify role-overstepping trajectories. For each trajectory, the judge is provided with the role definitions and responsibility boundaries of the multi-agent framework, the current agent role, and its generated trajectory. The judge evaluates the trajectory semantically and determines whether the current agent actually performs behaviors that belong to another role’s assigned responsibilities. Importantly, references to, evaluations of, or suggestions about another role’s work are not considered role overstepping unless the current agent directly performs that role’s responsibility. Role Clarity Score. Following Zhou et al. (2026a), we measure role adherence using the semantic similarity between each generated trajectory and its assigned role instruction. Specifically, we use the same backbone LLM to extract the final-layer hidden states of both inputs, apply mean pooling to obtain sequence-level representations, and compute their cosine similarity as the RC Score. The reported RC Score is averaged over trajectories from the selected target roles. A higher RC Score indicates stronger semantic alignment between the generated behavior and the assigned role responsibilities.
