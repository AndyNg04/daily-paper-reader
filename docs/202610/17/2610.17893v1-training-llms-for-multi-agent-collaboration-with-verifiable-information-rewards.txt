Abstract 

Effective collaboration among large language model (LLM) agents in multi-agent systems requires sharing relevant evidence, requesting missing information, and incorporating team-mates’ contributions into decisions. Training these behaviors is challenging because final task outcomes provide limited feedback on the value of individual messages. We introduce CoSIR (Collaboration via Surprisal-based In-formation Rewards), a post-training approach that uses a simple, verifiable environment to teach collaboration behaviors that generalize to more complex, unseen tasks. We propose a Bayesian evaluator that scores shared infor-mation by its reduction in team surprisal, de-fined as the sum of the negative log probabili-ties assigned to the correct option given each agent’s available evidence. Requests are scored by the potential reduction in the same quan-tity if the requested information were truthfully provided. Separate rewards encourage agents to use the received information in their private choices and calculations. We post-train Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Gemma-3-4B-it through self-play and evaluate transfer to seven unseen natural-language collaboration tasks. CoSIR achieves the highest aggregate score, while its performance in general tasks remains compet-itive. Behavioral analyses show increased dis-closure of private evidence and fewer judged failures to use teammates’ input.

We introduce CoSIR (Collaboration via Surprisal-based Information Rewards), an ap-proach that trains collaboration in simplified, verifiable environments while preserving the information dependencies underlying more com-plex tasks. The key is to retain the need to share relevant evidence, request missing information, and integrate teammates’ contributions, while making the value of these behaviors computable. By varying the available evidence and its distri-bution across agents, such environments provide diverse opportunities to learn these decisions with fine-grained, automatically computed feedback. The goal is to develop transferable collaboration behaviors in a controlled setting and evaluate whether they generalize to more complex, unseen natural-language tasks. To instantiate this approach, we construct a task in which agents jointly select the option with the highest total rating across several attributes. Each agent observes only part of a rating table, creating a need to share known ratings and request miss-ing information from teammates. Because reported and requested ratings correspond to specific table entries, we can identify the information exchanged, verify reported values, and track the evidence avail-able to each agent. We use a Bayesian evaluator to estimate the prob-ability that each option is optimal given an agent’s initial briefing and received reports. The complete table determines the true best option, but its hid-den ratings are not used to calculate these prob-abilities. We define team surprisal as the sum, across agents, of the negative log probabilities as-signed by the evaluator to the correct option. A shared rating is valued by the reduction in team surprisal it produces, while a request is valued by the potential reduction if the requested rating were truthfully provided. These quantities provide fine-grained training signals for sharing and requesting useful information, with numerical rewards com-puted from the table and information records rather than assigned by a learned reward judge. To encourage agents to use the information they receive, we complement communication rewards with supervision of private answers, including op-tion choices and calculated totals. The choice score uses the probability that the selected option is best given the agent’s available information, while the calculation score checks whether the agent cor-rectly combines its known ratings. We use these rewards to post-train Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), and Gemma-3-4B-it (Gemma Team, 2025) through self-play reinforcement learning. We test the transfer ability on seven unseen tasks: HiddenBench (Li et al., 2026), SILO-BENCH (Zhang et al., 2026), SplitQA, MathChat (Wu et al., 2023), BenchForm (Weng et al., 2025), Einstein Puzzles (Peng et al., 2025), and Collaboration Tax (Sun et al., 2026). We introduce SplitQA to test whether models trained to choose among options can combine facts held by different agents to pro-duce free-form answers. For each of the three mod-els, CoSIR improves all seven task scores over the starting checkpoint and achieves the highest sum of scores among the compared methods. We also find that behavioral analyses demonstrate in-creased sharing of private information and better 2utilization of teammates’ input, with general perfor-mance remaining close to initial checkpoints. Our contributions are threefold: • Scalable collaboration training. We construct a scalable training environment that preserves core collaboration behaviors with computable rewards. • Verifiable information rewards. We develop team-surprisal rewards for sharing and requesting information, alongside rewards for using it. • Cross-task generalization. We demonstrate transfer to seven unseen tasks across three LLM backbones while preserving general capabilities. 

2 Related Work 

LLM Multi-Agent Collaboration. Existing work on LLM multi-agent collaboration largely focuses on how agents interact at inference time. Theory-of-Mind methods help agents reason about their teammates’ beliefs (Li et al., 2023), while TalkHier organizes communication and answer re-finement through a hierarchical workflow (Wang et al., 2025a). These methods guide interactions among existing models. CoSIR trains the model itself, using rewards for sharing useful informa-tion, requesting missing ratings, and using received evidence. Our experiments compare this training approach with ToM and TalkHier under matched agent counts and public speaking-turn limits. 

Post-Training for LLM Collaboration. Recent studies train LLMs for multi-agent interaction. Peng et al. (2025) use supervised fine-tuning on planning trajectories to train information sharing and seeking, together with an environment-based verifier that guides actions at inference time. MA-PoRL rewards correct answers and discussion (Park et al., 2025). MAGRPO formulates collaboration as cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learn-ing (Liu et al., 2026b), while actor-critic methods train agents for decentralized execution (Liu et al., 2026a). CoSIR makes the information contributed by a statement or a potential reply an explicit re-ward signal. The rating-table environment lets us calculate this signal from the information available to each teammate. 

Transfer from Synthetic Multi-Agent Environ-ments. Synthetic environments provide scalable opportunities for LLMs to learn through interac-tion. SPIRAL uses self-play in zero-sum games to develop reasoning abilities that transfer beyond the training games (Liu et al., 2025). MARS ex-tends this approach to cooperative and competitive games, demonstrating transfer to unseen games and reasoning tasks (Yuan et al., 2025). We build on the idea of learning transferable capabilities from simple environments, but target collaboration it-self rather than general reasoning or game-playing strategies. In our training task, the facts agents share or request correspond to ratings in the table. This allows us to calculate how receiving a rating changes the probability of the correct option and use the result to train collaboration. 

3 Method 

We design CoSIR to train LLM agents to share, request, and use information through team discus-sions. Our aim is for these behaviors to transfer to unseen natural-language collaboration tasks. We build a simple rating-table environment that gener-ates tasks, gives each agent part of the information, and manages the discussion. Each task asks a team to choose the best option using ratings in a table. The environment scores the agents’ messages and private answers. We use these scores to update the model, then collect new discussions with the updated model.

3.3 Rewards for Communication and Answers 

We use reductions in team surprisal to evaluate in-formation sharing and requesting, and separately reward agents for using received information in their private answers. The information values de-fined below form the basis of our communication rewards, while Appendix D gives their complete computation, including reward allocation and ad-justments. 

Sharing information. We reward agents for shar-ing ratings that help their teammates identify the correct option. For a message containing rating statements M , we define its information value as 

Ishare (M ) = ∆Φ( M ). This value depends on 4what the listeners already know. For example, sup-pose every agent already knows that Option B’s safety rating is +1 . Repeating this rating does not change any listener’s probability of the correct op-tion, so it provides no information reward. Now suppose only Agent 1 knows that Option A’s safety rating is +1 . If receiving this rating increases the probability of the correct option from 

1/4 to 1/2 for both Agent 2 and Agent 3, each listener’s surprisal decreases by one bit. The total reduction is two bits before accounting for message delivery. A listener who already knows the rating gains no additional information. A message may contain multiple rating state-ments whose information values are not additive. We therefore evaluate the message as a whole and allocate its information value across individual statements. Appendix D provides the allocation details. However, reducing team surprisal alone is not sufficient to earn a positive reward. An agent could invent a rating that happens to increase the proba-bility of the correct option. We therefore require a statement to be both correct and supported by the speaker’s initial briefing or a received report. A correct guess without such evidence earns no posi-tive information reward, and faithfully repeating an incorrect report also earns no positive information reward. Fabricated statements receive an additional penalty. Conversely, even a correct rating may temporar-ily increase team surprisal. For example, the best option may have a poor rating on one attribute but strong ratings on others. Sharing the poor rating first could lower its probability while the other rat-ings remain unknown. We therefore avoid negative information rewards for statements supported by the speaker’s available information. Each rating statement also incurs a small communication cost to discourage unnecessary repetition. 

Requesting information. Unlike sharing a rat-ing, asking a question does not immediately pro-vide new information. We therefore evaluate a request by assuming that the requested rating is truthfully provided and measuring the resulting reduction in team surprisal, using the same mea-sure as for sharing. Let q be a teammate holding the requested cell c, and let xq,c denote a hypo-thetical reply containing its true rating. We de-fine the potential information value of the request as Irequest (q, c ) = [∆Φ( xq,c )] +, where [z]+ =max( z, 0) .Specifically, the request must concern a rating missing from the requester’s initial briefing but known to a teammate. The environment uses the true value from V to construct the hypothetical reply. We then calculate how receiving it would change the probability of the correct option for ev-ery agent other than the replying teammate, includ-ing the requester. The potential value is calculated when the question is asked, without waiting for an actual reply. For example, before Agent 1 shares Option A’s safety rating, Agent 2 could ask for it. If the truthful reply of +1 increases the probability of the correct option from 1/4 to 1/2 for both Agent 2 and Agent 3, the request has a potential information value of two bits. The final reward also accounts for previously received information, repeated requests, and whether the holder has another opportunity to speak. Appendix D provides these adjustments.

3.4 Learning from Team Discussions 

We use the sharing, requesting, and private-answer rewards to train LLMs through self-play, where the same model plays all agents in a team. At each speaking turn, we sample multiple candidate responses from the same briefing and conversation, allowing us to compare different responses under the same conditions. We score all candidates and randomly select one to continue the discussion, while the others also contribute to training. Private answers are sampled and scored separately. After collecting discussions, we update the model and repeat the process with the updated policy. A message may contain useful ratings, repeti-tions, and questions, so we assign rewards to the corresponding parts of the response rather than to the entire message. Statement rewards provide direct credit to their corresponding tokens, while re-quest and private-answer scores are converted into group-relative advantages. These signals guide up-dates to corresponding tokens through a variant of PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). Early in training, the model may rarely generate useful questions. When the evaluator identifies a request as the best available action, we add a few demonstrations expressing that request. These examples are scored alongside sampled responses and trained with a clipped imitation update, but never selected to continue the discussion. To help preserve general capabilities, we use a reference-model penalty and train on responses generated by the initial checkpoint for general prompts. Appendix F provides the sampling, token-level credit assignment, demonstration generation, and optimization details. 

4 Experiments 

We evaluate transfer to unseen collaboration tasks, changes in agents’ communication, and retention of general capabilities. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Models and baselines. We evaluate Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025), and Gemma-3-4B-it (Gemma Team, 2025). Base is the starting checkpoint, and CoSIR is the checkpoint after col-laboration training. Both use the same task prompts and evaluation settings. Outcome-RL uses the same self-play environment and model initialization as CoSIR, but learns from the final team outcome rather than behavior-specific information rewards. It retains the request demonstrations, while out-come feedback is assigned to the sampled discus-sion trajectory. This comparison tests the reward design within outcome-based multi-agent reinforce-ment learning (Liu et al., 2026b). We also evalu-ate Theory of Mind (ToM) (Li et al., 2023) and TalkHier (Wang et al., 2025a), which add reason-ing or revision steps at inference time. They use the same numbers of agents and speaking turns, with additional model calls described in Appendix H. 

Unseen collaboration tasks. We evaluate on seven tasks outside the training environment. Hid-denBench (Li et al., 2026) requires decisions from private facts, and SILO-BENCH (Zhang et al., 2026) requires computation over data divided among agents. SplitQA asks agents to connect facts from separate documents. MathChat (Wu et al., 2023) evaluates interactive mathematical problem solving, while BenchForm (Weng et al., 2025) eval-uates answers under peer influence. Einstein Puz-zles (Peng et al., 2025) and Collaboration Tax (Sun et al., 2026) evaluate further forms of reasoning and coordination involving distributed information or task responsibilities. Appendix H gives the pro-tocols, generation settings, and scoring rules. We construct SplitQA to test whether agents can use distributed evidence to generate an answer. In HiddenBench, private facts support or rule out the listed options. SplitQA instead uses HotpotQA questions (Yang et al., 2018) whose answers re-quire information from two supporting paragraphs. We assign those paragraphs to different agents and give distractors to the other two. Agents follow the HiddenBench discussion protocol and then pro-vide free-form answers. Appendix G describes the 70-question test set and its scoring.

4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models 

Table 1 shows that CoSIR achieves the highest SUM for all three models, exceeding the strongest alternative by 19.79, 22.95, and 24.86 points for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it, re-spectively. Each trained model also achieves a higher mean score than Base on all seven tasks, indicating that the gains are not driven by a single benchmark. Inference-time methods perform better on some individual tasks. ToM leads on SILO-BENCH with Qwen3-8B, while TalkHier leads on BenchForm with all three models. However, both methods reduce SUM relative to Base for Qwen3-4B and Gemma-3-4B-it, whereas CoSIR improves SUM across all three models. SplitQA further tests transfer beyond choosing among predefined options, requiring agents to com-bine facts from different supporting paragraphs to produce a free-form answer. CoSIR improves over Base by 2.50, 0.36, and 8.21 points for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it, respectively. The improvement is small for Qwen3-4B, but the re-sults suggest that the learned behaviors can also help agents combine evidence across documents. 

Comparison with Outcome-RL. Outcome-RL follows the same training procedure as CoSIR but rewards only the final team outcome. Although it improves SUM over Base for all three models, CoSIR exceeds it by 20.72, 22.95, and 24.86 points for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it, respectively. CoSIR scores higher on all seven tasks with Qwen3-8B and six tasks with each of the other models. It ties Outcome-RL on MathChat with Qwen3-4B, while Outcome-RL performs bet-ter on HiddenBench with Gemma-3-4B-it. Unlike Outcome-RL, which evaluates only the final team result, CoSIR rewards individual rat-ing statements and requests, as well as choices and calculations in private answers. Thus, re-sponses leading to the same final outcome can re-ceive different feedback based on how they share and use information. These results support the benefit of fine-grained information rewards over final-outcome feedback within our self-play train-ing framework. We next examine how agents’ com-munication changes after training.

5 Conclusion 

CoSIR trains LLMs to share, request, and use infor-mation with computable rewards in a rating-table environment. Across three backbones, the trained models achieve higher mean scores than Base on all seven unseen collaboration tasks and the high-est aggregate scores among the compared methods. Behavioral analyses and case studies show more sharing and use of teammates’ information, while general capabilities remain close to the starting checkpoints. 9Limitations 

Our evaluation covers three model backbones and seven collaboration tasks. The trained models improve over their starting checkpoints through-out this suite, but inference-time workflows and Outcome-RL remain stronger on some combina-tions of model and task. Training also does not re-move every communication failure: repeated steps and several other MAST failure categories remain frequent. The reward depends on a toy environment with known facts and a computable evaluator. Ex-tending the same scoring approach to open-ended tasks would require a way to assess information value when the complete state is unavailable.

B Communication Protocol and Parsing 

B.1 Discussion and request routing 

A message is one complete natural-language ut-terance. It may contain several statements, each claiming a rating for one cell, and at most one re-quest for a rating. Requests can name a teammate or address the group. Let T be the total number of discussion rounds. It is drawn uniformly from the selected family’s interval in Table 4. Agents see the current round and whose turn it is, but not T or the number of remaining rounds. The speaking order is fixed. A request identifies one cell and may name a recipient. Outside family D, a group-addressed request is routed to the next holder in speaking order. A request naming a teammate who cannot provide a useful answer can also be rerouted. Fam-ily D shows holders and retains the named target. Routing uses the environment’s allocation record, but does not insert the holder’s rating into the re-quester’s prompt. It is a training-time forwarding mechanism that uses information hidden from the agents. For each ordinary listener, the environment uses a deterministic hash of the game, turn, and listener to draw a message-delivery outcome. The miss probability is 0.3. All 16 candidate messages at one speaking state share this ordinary delivery mask. If a candidate has a routed request, its recipient receives the full message regardless of that mask. B.2 Text extraction and information checks 

The parser combines frozen initial Qwen3-8B with rules, using temperature zero, disabled thinking, and prompt caching. It is shared across all three training backbones. Rules first locate option names, attribute names, and rating words in clauses or tables. The model extracts statements, claimed sources, totals, and an optional request with its tar-get. Clear request phrases take precedence when identifying the intended action. Text checks remove conditional or negated claims, unknown-value placeholders, and ratings that do not match the corresponding clause. Asmall set of fixed sentence patterns recovers missed ratings. If model extraction is unusable, rule-extracted ratings that pass the checks remain avail-able. Empty messages yield no claims. These checks establish what the text says, not whether a rating is true. The environment then compares each extracted statement with the speaker’s briefing and actually received rating statements. A matching briefing value is an own statement. A matching received rating statement can be relayed with its original source and identifier. A rating with neither basis is unsupported, even if it equals the hidden table value. Checking truth against the table is a separate operation used in reward calculation.

F Self-Play Training Details 

F.1 Collection and candidate groups 

Each collection round contains 60 games played by the current shared policy. Sampling uses temper-ature 1.2, disabled thinking, and a limit of 1,024 generated tokens per message. Samples containing thinking markers are excluded. A speaking group has 16 policy candidates and up to two additional demonstrations. An intermediate or final private-answer group has eight policy candidates and up to two demonstrations. Demonstrations are separate from the policy sample counts. One of the 16 policy messages is selected uni-formly using the game seed and turn to continue discussion. Reward does not affect this selection. All retained candidates are scored against the same preceding records, so their differences concern the current action rather than different conversation histories. Private answers do not continue the di-alogue. Groups with no scoring differences are discarded.

F.3 Generated demonstrations 

When the evaluator ranks a request as the best avail-able action, demonstrations provide additional ex-amples of that action. Frozen initial Qwen3-8B 17 minimally rewrites the first usable policy response to express the selected action, with four attempts at temperature 1.0. Request templates provide two further candidates. The parser filters them, and at most two valid candidates with the lowest neg-ative log likelihood under the current policy are retained. Identical demonstrations within a group are deduplicated. Demonstrations are trained with the ordinary agent prompt, without rewrite instructions. They are never selected to advance the conversation. Their centered components use the policy group’s baseline without changing it. Action demonstration advantages are clipped to [−0.5, 0.5] . Statement and answer demonstration advantages do not share this cap. A demonstration was not sampled from the cur-rent policy under the ordinary prompt. For the training probability ratio defined below, its denomi-nator is evaluated under the model at the start of the round. The generating model’s token probabilities are not used. The resulting objective is a clipped, advantage-weighted imitation update without cor-rection for that generation distribution. It is not an unbiased on-policy PPO estimate. Demonstra-tion tokens receive no reference KL penalty. An entropy term, which encourages a broader token distribution, includes both policy and demonstra-tion tokens.

G SplitQA Construction and Evaluation 

Data selection. We construct a 70-question SplitQA test set from the distractor setting of Hot-potQA (Yang et al., 2018), with 63 bridge ques-tions and 7 comparison questions. Each source item contains two annotated supporting Wikipedia paragraphs and eight distractor paragraphs. Bridge questions link information through an intermediate entity, whereas comparison questions compare a property of two entities. We retain only bridge and comparison questions with two distinct supporting paragraphs. We also exclude any item whose gold answer appears verbatim in a distractor paragraph. This removes direct copies of the answer from the distractors. 

Evidence allocation. Agent 1 receives one sup-porting paragraph and Agent 2 receives the other. Agents 3 and 4 receive only distractor paragraphs. Thus, no agent receives both annotated supporting paragraphs, and exactly two agents hold the anno-tated evidence. To answer from that evidence, the team needs to identify which agents hold relevant facts and link or compare those facts across the two supporting paragraphs. The distractors add the need to separate relevant information from unre-lated material. 

Discussion and scoring. We retain the proto-col used in our HiddenBench evaluation (Li et al., 2026): four agents speak in a fixed order for five rounds, then each answers privately. Each round gives every agent one speaking turn. Final answers are free-form strings, so agents are not given a list of candidate answers. The team answer is the unique answer receiving the most votes among the four agents. If two or more answers tie for the highest vote count, the team produces no answer and receives an exact-match score of zero, even if one of the tied answers is correct. Otherwise, the team receives a score of one if the selected answer exactly matches the gold answer, and zero if it does not. Each of the 70 questions is run with four seeds. 

H Evaluation Settings and Score Aggregation 

Benchmark protocols. HiddenBench (Li et al., 2026) uses five rounds of discussion in a fixed speaking order, followed by private votes from all agents. Team accuracy is determined by majority voting. SILO-BENCH (Zhang et al., 2026) uses three discussion rounds followed by individual an-swers, and we report its task success rate. SplitQA uses five rounds followed by private answers, with team accuracy measured by exact match of the answer receiving the most votes. Tied votes are counted as incorrect, as detailed in Appendix G. 19 MathChat (Wu et al., 2023) pairs an LLM with a user proxy that executes tools and provides feed-back during mathematical problem solving. Bench-Form (Weng et al., 2025) requires a single answer rather than a new multi-round discussion. We aver-age answer accuracy across its five settings of peer influence. In the trust and doubt settings, the input includes five rounds of prior discussion history. For Collaboration Tax (Sun et al., 2026), we report the score achieved by the collaborating agents. Higher scores therefore indicate better performance, as they do for the other tasks in SUM. 

Generation settings. Table 7 lists the tempera-ture, output limit, and thinking setting for each task. The token limit is the maximum number of to-kens generated in one response. Thinking refers to the model’s thinking mode, where supported. Hid-denBench, SILO-BENCH, and SplitQA use top-

p = 0 .95 during discussion. Their final answers use temperature zero with thinking enabled. For SILO-BENCH, if an agent produces no final answer, we ask it to answer once more with think-ing disabled. In Einstein Puzzles (Peng et al., 2025), the sampling seed for each step is determined by the instance index and step index. 

Inference-time collaboration workflows. ToM (Li et al., 2023) and TalkHier (Wang et al., 2025a) follow the same benchmark protocols as the other methods, with matched numbers of agents, discus-sion rounds, and limits on speaking turns. However, they use additional model calls within each speak-ing turn. ToM makes two calls. The first generates a private belief-state description of at most 400 to-kens, which is retained in the agent’s context but not shared with teammates. The second produces the public message. TalkHier makes three calls. It first drafts a response, then obtains reviewer feed-back of at most 400 tokens, and finally revises the response using that feedback. These additional calls do not increase the number of public speaking turns, but they increase inference computation. 

Score aggregation. Table 1 reports means over the runs available for each task. All OOD bench-marks have four reported runs per method and model. We first average within each task, then add the seven means to obtain SUM. We compute the total before rounding the displayed entries to two decimal places. Consequently, adding the rounded table entries can differ slightly from the displayed total. Each task contributes equally to SUM. A one-point increase on any task adds one point to the total, regardless of that task’s number of examples or runs. The general-capability benchmarks and behavioral measurements are not included in SUM. The column ∆SUM subtracts the corresponding Base total, so it measures the change from the same starting model. Bold entries identify the highest score within a model and task, including ties. The comparison methods serve different pur-poses. ToM and TalkHier modify the inference-time workflow, whereas Outcome-RL uses CoSIR’s training procedure with the reward replaced by the final team outcome. Base measures perfor-mance before collaboration training. The Outcome-RL comparison therefore isolates the role of behavior-specific training rewards (Unlike CoSIR’s candidate-level information scoring, Outcome-RL assigns final-outcome feedback to the sampled trajectory. Both methods retain request demon-strations), while the inference-time methods test whether collaboration can instead be improved through additional reasoning and revision steps.
