Extended Reality (XR) headsets increasingly host familiar 2D user interfaces as windows floating in physical
environments. However, their usability depends on environmental conditions that designers cannot fully
control. Current design workflows often separate how a variant is designed from how it actually looks in
the target environment. We present AdapTest, an in-situ authoring and testing tool that lets designers
select interface elements, define when and how they change, compare the resulting variations, and test their
activation without coding. Based on prior research and a formative study with 12 interface designers, the system
targets three key environmental variables: distance, lighting, and occlusion. We evaluated AdapTest against a
desktop design and in-headset preview workflow in a within-subjects study with 12 designers. Compared with
the baseline, designers accounted for a wider range of environmental factors, addressed more overlapping

Introduction
Graphical user interfaces are moving off the screen and into physical space. Extended Reality (XR)
headsets now render familiar 2D interfaces—dashboards, control panels, exhibit guides—as windows
carried into immersive surroundings [14, 66], with deployments spanning museums, homes, and
workplaces [30, 49]. Unlike a phone or desktop screen, such an interface is at the mercy of its
environment: the viewer walks away and body text becomes illegible [22, 60]; the room dims and
low-contrast styling disappears against passthrough [15, 31]; furniture or passersby occlude the
panel [3, 40]; cluttered backdrops erode the readability of overlaid content [37, 54]. Most interface
design is fixed at authoring time, so designers have to anticipate these conditions as separate cases.
Current tools give visual designers partial control over how their interfaces adapt to the XR
environment and limited means to test that behavior in place. Automated systems decide the
adaptation at runtime—repositioning or anchoring windows to spatial context [11, 16, 40], matching
layouts to the surrounding scene [10], varying information density [42], projecting onto nearby
surfaces [21, 24], switching input techniques [34], or recoloring panels against their background
[53]. Because these decisions live in code and optimization objectives, designers cannot see or shape
what the interface becomes under each condition, and often cannot picture it in advance [8, 33].
Design practice keeps that control in the designer’s hands but loses the tie to condition: designers
hand-build each case as a duplicated static artboard (e.g., a dark-room, far-away, or occluded version
[1, 32, 33]) and preview the copies in headset prototyping tools such as ShapesXR [57]. These copies
let a designer inspect a mockup, yet they do not connect a condition to the change it should trigger,
and confirming that one behaves correctly in place would mean coding, rebuilding, and redeploying
to the headset—development work outside design practice [1, 48]. Designers are therefore left
either to accept automatic adaptation or approximate it by hand, without a way to author their
own condition-to-change mappings for the environmental conditions of a 2D interface in XR and
test them in the room.
To address this gap, we present AdapTest, an authoring tool that lets designers adapt the GUIs
they already create to the environments those interfaces will inhabit. After opening a design in
the headset, a designer places its screens around the room, examines them where they will be
used, and decides when the interface should change as spatial conditions shift. To author that
change, the designer selects an element, assigns an alternative style such as larger type, higher
contrast, or fewer details, and defines when that style should apply: when the viewer is far, the
room is dark, or the panel is blocked. Each rule’s outcome appears in a preview beside the default
design, so the designer compares how the interface should adapt across conditions. The designer
then tests the rules against the room’s actual conditions, observes each transition as it fires, and
refines the thresholds and styling accordingly. In a within-subjects study, twelve designers created

2
Related Works
AdapTest builds on three research areas: how adaptive behavior for XR interfaces is authored,
how designers compare design variants, and how XR designs are tested in place.
2.1
Authoring Adaptive Behavior for XR Interfaces
Research on XR adaptivity has made strong progress on automated adaptation, where the system
decides at runtime how the interface changes. AUIT [16] lets XR creators attach objectives, such as
visibility and reachability, to UI elements in Unity, and a solver adjusts the positions, rotations, and
scales at runtime. SemanticAdapt [10] retargets a world-anchored layout to a new room by matching
virtual to semantically related physical objects, and SituationAdapt [40] re-places elements after a
vision-language model rates how socially appropriate each surrounding area is. Further systems
select placement strategies by context [11], compute layouts against the physical scene [19], project
interfaces onto surrounding geometry [21, 24], and switch selection techniques as targets and
posture change [34]. Automated adaptation has also reached visual properties: correcting color and
contrast against the background [12, 45], repositioning and recoloring panels from sensed scene
context [53], adjusting level of detail with the viewer’s visual angle [62], and adapting rendering to
the sensed environment in web-based AR [17]. Purpose-built interfaces further demonstrate the
value of environmental conditions, such as a museum guide that swaps content based on visitor
distance [9]. Even when content creators pre-design the variants (e.g., [42]), where applications
carry designed level-of-detail versions and an optimizer selects among them by cognitive load, task,
and environment, the runtime still owns the mapping from context to variant. Optimization itself
has grown more open to users, who can choose among Pareto-optimal proposals [28] or steer the
objectives by spoken preference [41]. Across these systems, the condition–appearance mapping
lives in cost functions, objective priorities, and code, which makes it difficult for visual designers to
inspect what will happen under a given condition or to adjust it without programming [8, 33].
Rule-based authoring, in contrast, puts the mapping in the author’s hands, and XR tools have
primarily applied it to interaction events. Trigger-action programming has long enabled non-
programmers to state IF/THEN behavior [26]. In XR, XRSpotlight [18] presents the interactions of
a Unity scene as natural-language rules that novice developers can inspect and copy across scenes;
ProInterAR [65] lets creators stack blocks that react to grasps, gestures, and collisions between
AR contents; ProGesAR [64] lets designers place proxemic and gestural triggers that fire when a
person crosses a distance threshold or performs a gesture; and RealityCanvas [63] binds sketched

condition–variant mapping explicit, deterministic, and editable without code.
2.2
Comparing Design Variants
Seeing design alternatives side by side is an established aid to design judgment. Creating and
comparing prototypes in parallel yields better outcomes and more divergent exploration than
refining one at a time [13], and tools have operationalized this insight: Juxtapose [23] runs alternative
interface implementations side by side with runtime tuning, subjunctive interfaces let users set up
and view multiple scenarios in parallel [43], Scout [56] generates layout variants from high-level
constraints for rapid comparison, and DesignTrace [50] tracks alternatives as designers iterate
with a generative model. These tools compare alternatives that the designer freely explores; the
variants answer "which design do I prefer?" Adaptive XR interfaces pose a different comparison
question—"does my design hold up under every condition it will meet?"—which current practice can
answer only partially. Designers encode environmental cases as duplicated artboards or component
variants [32, 33], but the mapping from condition to variant remains only in file names and memory.
Responsive web design offers a designer-facing analogy: media queries bind layout variants to
viewport conditions, and design tools preview breakpoints side by side. The same side-by-side
preview for the environmental conditions of an XR space is the open question.
To answer this, AdapTest produces a condition-specific preview that wraps around the default
design, so a designer can inspect coverage across dark-to-bright, far-to-near, occluded-to-visible,
and combined conditions at a glance.
2.3
Testing XR Designs in Place
Validating an XR design still requires being in the deployment environment, which slows iteration.
Studies of XR practitioners report that creators cannot judge legibility, scale, or lighting from the
desktop [1, 33]. Site-specific work demands "frequent on-site visits and additional iterations" [48],
developers spent over a third of their time testing in situ and identified lighting as one condition that
desktop views cannot reproduce. In-situ authoring tools shorten this loop by moving creation into
the target environment: DART [44] moved AR authoring into the designer’s workflow, PintAR [20]
sketches interfaces directly in AR, Rapido [39] turns video captures into interactive AR prototypes,
ImaginateAR [38] assembles outdoor scenes in place with AI assistance, and commercial tools
such as ShapesXR [57] preview screen designs spatially in the headset; CAVE-AR [7] approaches
the same challenge from the opposite direction, simulating and debugging AR experiences from
within VR. Fast-Forward Reality [51] generates test cases for context-aware policies and plays as
immersive visualizations to verify in XR before deployment. In these tools—as in the live try-out
loops of the rule-based authoring tools above—testing is limited to the conditions the room offers
at that moment; a darkened room or a twenty-meter viewing distance is difficult to produce on
demand, and the tester has limited means to tell which authored state is currently active.
AdapTest’s builds on these testing practices: the designer dials environmental conditions with
manual controls or drives them from live headset sensing, and the system activates the currently
matched rule—turning situated testing from re-enacting circumstances into querying the rule set.

3
Formative Study
We conducted a formative study to observe how screen-rooted designers adapt their interfaces when
the designs enter a real space. Twelve designers prototyped interfaces for a multi-zone makerspace
and reviewed them as virtual screens on Apple Vision Pro from three viewing distances, iterating
in place. Their behavior yielded three findings—an element-level adaptation vocabulary, a struggle
to cover compounding conditions, and a dependence on being in place to judge thresholds and
transitions—from which we derive design goals DG1–DG3.

We recruited twelve designers (7 female, 5 male; 𝑀= 2.5 years of UI design experience, 𝑆𝐷= 1.4)
with design-related academic backgrounds, HMD familiarity, and prior XR use (Appendix A). Only
two had authored XR interfaces before, so the sample reflects the screen-interface practice AdapTest
targets. Each session closed with a 25-minute semi-structured interview on design choices, spatial
influences, and adaptation challenges (Appendix B); participants received approximately 40 USD
in local currency. We collected audio, interface designs, headset camera views, and floor-plan
markings of viewpoints and screen positions, and we analyzed 148 interface designs across 48
screen placements together with the transcripts through the six-phase thematic analysis [5]. Three
authors coded transcripts in English alongside the designs and floor plans, first in one joint pass
and then in rotation, so that each author layered codes over every session; the three code sets
were merged by discussion into the themes reported below. Each design was coded as one unit and
tagged with the adaptation strategies visible in it, and these tags yield the proportions (Figure 5).

3.3
Finding 2: Deciding Which Versions to Focus
Designers found it hard to decide which conditions deserved a distinct version and to treat the
same condition consistently across versions. Participants authored progressive series—P12 re-sized
text at 15%, 26%, then 41% of the window across three distances—and prioritization strained as
versions multiplied; P8: "It becomes difficult to decide which components to focus on first when there
are too many UI windows. Users must judge what to focus on, which takes mental energy." The same
condition also drew inconsistent treatments: P12 color-coded seat availability at 5.0m and 11.0m
but dropped color at 15.8m, while P6 set the threshold elsewhere: "When I’m far away, I just want
to know which ones are in use. I don’t think it necessarily needs to be color-coded." Participants were
further unsure which situations deserved a version at all; P10: "Should I know information about
that space when I’m not entering it?"
The task fixed how many versions each screen received, but which conditions each version
served, and whether sibling versions agreed, was the designers’ own work, and nothing in the
designers’ materials showed which conditions were handled and which were missed. This finding
motivates DG2: multi-condition preview comparison—render each rule’s condition-specific
state beside the default so coverage gaps and mismatched siblings become visible at a glance.

The decisions participants found hardest—when an adaptation should fire and how it should
change—were the ones they could not settle away from the space. Several preferred discrete, zone-
based switching; P12: "From the user’s perspective, thinking there’s something physical there, and when
they get closer it gets bigger—this feels more naturally acceptable." Others feared jarring changes near

AdapTest is an authoring tool for designing adaptive interfaces in the environments where
they will be deployed (Figure 6). It lets designers author adaptive behavior as rules over interface
elements, compare the condition-specific versions side by side, and test which becomes active
directly in the intended physical environment. The resulting design is a set of adaptive interface
variants together with the rules that define when each adaptation should occur.
The tool was structured in response to the findings of the literature review and formative
study, and designed with three goals: DG1) let designers author adaptation rules directly on the
elements of their imported design, stating what should change and under which environmental
condition; DG2) let designers compare each condition-specific variant beside the default design,
so overlapping and uncovered conditions stay visible; and DG3) let designers test which variant
becomes active in the real environment, under sensed or manually controlled values, and refine
the rules in place. A typical session runs as follows. A designer imports a multi-screen design, places
its screens around the room, and taps an element to make it a rule’s target (DG1). The designer sets

4.1
DG1: Element-Targeted Rule Authoring
Designers author each adaptation directly on the rendered design. Importing an interface opens a
spatial window per screen (Figure 6e). The designer places these windows around the room, scales
one with a two-hand pinch, and toggles each open or closed from the workspace bar (Figure 6d);
reopening a saved project restores its rules, variants, and test records. To set what changes, the
designer taps an element (e.g., the BOARDING GROUP B chip). To set when, the designer picks a
measure, a comparison, and drags a slider to a threshold such as distance > 3 m (Figure 7a). Pressing
+ outcome then gives the selected elements a visual change: hiding them or bringing them to the
front by selection, stepping size or opacity with a slider, or setting a text or background color with
a picker (Figure 7b). Each edit plays back in the preview attached to the rule (Figure 6h).
Each rule is an IF–THEN pair over the chosen elements, and + if condition adds conditions so
one rule can describe a compound situation, such as a far viewer in a dim room or a 2 to 4 meter
distance band. Distance and occlusion follow our formative study; brightness follows legibility
findings on dark-mode interfaces in optical see-through head-mounted displays [15, 31]. A THEN
output applies to the selected elements or to the whole screen, and one rule may carry several
outputs, so different elements respond differently under the same condition, and the output palette
(hide, bring-to-front, resize, opacity, text and background color) follows the adaptation strategies
designers used in the formative study. When a rule activates, its elements ease into the new state
over a short transition, so a hidden element fades and a resized one grows into place. The Rules
panel lists the rule set for renaming, enabling, disabling, duplicating, and deleting (Figure 6a); when
several rules match at once, the highest in the list takes effect, and designers reorder rules with
the arrows on each card. On the web editor, designers can also drag preset condition cards onto a
screen or element to seed a rule.

4.2
DG2: Multi-Condition Preview Comparison
As rules accumulate, designers need to see how the variants relate: where conditions overlap, clash,
or leave a situation unaddressed. Expanding a design window lays each rule’s preview beside
the unchanged default, so several variants read side by side (Figure 6h). When two rules could
fire together, a suggestion card appears(“Far Distance and Occlusion can occur together”), and the
designer accepts it to merge the rules or dismisses it (Figure 6b). When two rules set the same
property to different values, a conflict card appears, and the designer combines the rules, chooses
which wins, or dismisses it.
The coverage strips which ranges rules already cover and which remain open, with a marker at
the live value (Figure 6l, m). Tapping an open range closes it, and the designer chooses whether the
design eases gradually into the covered state or snaps to it; a measure with no rule at all prompts a
suggestion to add one. In the web editor, a designer can also select two variants and view a per-layer
list of all properties that differ between them. Every preview updates as its rules’ conditions, targets,
or outputs change, so overlaps, conflicts, and gaps stay visible on one authoring surface.
4.3
DG3: Situated Testing and Refinement
Fig. 8. AdapTest Test Mode: a) currently activated design version, b) current environmental values from live

Judging whether an adaptation is right, such as what counts as “far” or whether text stays legible
in the available light, requires being in the space. Test Mode therefore evaluates authored rules
in the physical setting where the interface will appear. Pressing Start on the Test panel folds the
editing controls away, and the screens begin reacting to the room: stepping closer, moving into
shadow, or blocking a screen with a hand switches it to the matched variant (Figure 8). The panel
names the active rule, shows the live distance, brightness, and occlusion values, and keeps a trace
of recent switches (Figure 8a, b). A Calibrate control re-baselines brightness to the current room,
and a Measure menu sets which screen the readings target.
When the room cannot reach a value, such as a viewing distance longer than the space, the
designer drags that measure’s slider to set it by hand while the others keep reading from the
room; releasing the slider returns it to live sensing (Figure 8b). Screens follow the highest-priority
matching rule and return to the default when none matches, and a new match takes effect only

AdapTest combines a React and TypeScript web client with a native SwiftUI application for
visionOS (Figure 9). The web client handles import, project management, and detailed rule editing;
the native application presents the rules panel, Test panel, and imported interfaces as spatial
windows. Both share one project model of screens and layers, variants, rules, window placements,
and test sessions.
On import, the web client extracts an interface’s layers as CSS selector paths and creates a
default variant per screen; each adaptive variant stores only the CSS properties that differ from the
default, and its rule records conditions, target variant, enabled state, and priority. Both applications
render the same HTML/CSS through WKWebView with the variant’s CSS injected over it. A typed
JavaScript-to-Swift bridge synchronizes project updates, rule edits, placements, sensor readings,
and active-rule changes as JSON messages; updates carry a source and timestamp so the latest
write wins, and edits made while disconnected replay on reconnection.
Rule evaluation runs in both TypeScript (authoring previews) and Swift (Test Mode) with the
same priority behavior. On the headset, a sensor loop runs every 150 ms, evaluates each open
screen’s enabled rules against its current distance, brightness, and occlusion, and shows the variant
of the lowest-numbered matching rule, restoring the default when none matches. A newly matched
variant commits only after it stays the best match for 300 ms, and variant changes patch the variant’s
CSS into the live document with a 400 ms eased transition. Suggestions and coverage strips derive
from the same rules: each rule’s conditions reduce to a value range per measure; compatible overlaps
become combined suggestions that merge both rules’ CSS, conflicting properties are surfaced for
the designer, and the strips mark the covered and open bands of each measure. Cross-screen reuse
maps source elements by semantic role (primary content, secondary content, status, action button)
before the designer confirms targets and a screen-specific copy is created.

5
User Study
We ran a within-subjects study comparing how designers author adaptive XR interfaces with
AdapTest against their current 2D practice. AdapTest offers three capabilities—element-targeted
rules, rule-attached previews with combination suggestions, and in-context Test Mode—and we
ask how designer behavior on three fronts differs between AdapTest and a Figma-based current-
practice baseline: RQ1. How explicitly do designers specify adaptive behavior—which element
changes and under which condition—and where does that specification live, in a working rule
or in text or speech? RQ2. How many environmental conditions, including compound ones, do
designers cover? RQ3. How do designers test their adaptations in place, and how do tests feed back
into thresholds and styling?
5.1
Participants
We recruited twelve designers (7 female, 5 male; ages 20–31, 𝑀= 25.0, 𝑆𝐷= 2.8) via university
community channels. Participants included UI/UX, industrial design, and HCI researchers and
graduate students in related domains. They had an average of 3.2 years of design experience
(𝑆𝐷= 1.7; range: less than one year to more than five years). Eligibility criteria included prior UI
design practice, at least one experience with an HMD or AR/VR, normal or corrected-to-normal
vision, and intact color vision. Seven participants also reported XR UI design experience through
coursework or research projects (Table 2). All twelve used Figma as a primary design tool and
reported high Figma fluency (𝑀= 6.0 of 7, 𝑆𝐷= 1.0); thus, the baseline condition used a tool with
which they were already proficient. Participants received approximately 35 USD in local currency

5.2
Study Procedure
Two authors attended each session: one moderated, while the other managed logging, observation,
and assistance. Upon providing consent, participants received an explanation of the research
background and completed an intake survey covering demographics and 7-point Likert-scale
self-ratings of UI and context-aware design experience, Figma-like tool fluency, and XR usage
familiarity. We then calibrated the Vision Pro’s eye and hand tracking for each participant, mirrored
the in-headset view to a Mac mini for observation and logging, and provided brief training on
moving, resizing, and deleting windows in visionOS.
Each participant then completed the two design tasks in counterbalanced order (six partici-
pants per order). After each task, participants briefly walked us through their artifacts, explaining
what they adapted, when each state should appear, and what they changed after previewing or
testing. They then completed the post-task survey; administering the debrief and survey after
each task reduced recency bias in the end-of-session ratings. The baseline required no training
because participants were already proficient with the tool. Before the AdapTest task, the authors
provided a ten-minute spoken walkthrough of each capability while observing the mirrored view.
This familiarized participants with the system’s basic operation before their task behavior was
measured. The session concluded with a 15-minute semi-structured interview. Having used both
tools, participants compared them, reflected on their own practices, and considered possible uses
of AdapTest (Appendix C). Surveys were administered in English, with verbal clarification in the
participant’s language upon request.
5.3
Conditions and Tasks
The two tasks used matched but distinct domains so that no participant authored the same arti-
fact twice. Task 1 (AdapTest) ran on a smart-home control dashboard (living-room overview,
thermostat, entry security) with the full system and suggestions enabled. Task 2 (baseline) used
workplace information panels (team board, meeting-room status, wayfinding) and represented
credible current practice [33]: participants edited on a desktop in Figma, inspected static PNG
previews floating on Vision Pro through a minimal view-only image viewer, and returned to Figma
between inspections to make revisions. Each domain comprised three screens built on the same
scaffold—a header, a primary reading, a subtitle, one rich element (slider, dial, or image), three chips,
and a primary button. The conditions therefore provided structurally equivalent authoring surfaces.
The viewer eliminated the file-transfer friction of everyday practice while holding window chrome,
passthrough blending, and logging constant. The baseline was consequently more convenient than
the workflow it represented. The baseline stands for current Figma-based practice, not for XR

they addressed each one is a measured outcome. A task was considered complete when every
screen included at least one working adaptive state and the participant had previewed or tested
the design at least once. The AdapTest build used in the study sensed occlusion from tracked
hands only, with scene-mesh occlusion disabled so that staging stayed predictable; distance was
the head-to-window distance minus a fixed 1.8 m offset, floored at 0.2 m, so authored thresholds sit
on that working scale; and brightness was ARKit light estimation normalized to the range seen

5.4
Data Collection and Analysis
We logged designers’ actions as structured events in both conditions: rules authored and their
selected targets (RQ1); coverage of the three staged factors, combined rules, and suggestion ac-
cept/edit/dismiss decisions (RQ2); Test Mode runs, threshold and style edits with their timing
relative to tests, test–refine cycles, and rule reuse across screens (RQ3). The Figma artifacts of
all twelve baseline sessions were retained and coded for variants and their intended conditions.
One author inventoried each file from its pages and layer names together with the walkthrough
recording, listing every on-page state as a variant and recording its trigger from a canvas annota-
tion, a layer name, or the participant’s spoken explanation; a second author reviewed all twelve
inventories, and uncertain cases were settled by discussion. A staged factor counts as covered when
at least one variant’s trigger names it, and a variant whose trigger names two factors counts as
a compound condition. With AdapTest, the same counts come from the authored rules, so the
baseline criterion is the looser of the two.

additionally carried tool-specific items on 7 point Likert scale—two workflow items in the baseline
block (“I could clearly communicate when the interface should change”; “I could directly check whether
the adaptation worked in XR”) and five capability items in the AdapTest block (element selection,
rules, previews, Test Mode, suggestions). Qualitative data comprise the audio-recorded per-task

Results and Findings
In the smart-home domain, designers authored more adaptive behavior with AdapTest than they
did in the office domain with their familiar workflow, at comparable workload and usability. With
AdapTest, all 12 participants captured how the interface should adapt as 55 rules (Table 3), and
every rule produced a working adaptive version of the design—a live, condition-specific version,
not just a note describing one. In the baseline, participants produced 38 adaptive versions as static
variants , with the conditions that should trigger them only written beside the design or spoken.
Because the two tasks ran on different time budgets by design , we read this comparison as an
authoring rate (Figure 10a): the two rates did not differ reliably1 . The volume difference thus
reflects sustained authoring over a longer session at a comparable rate. Self-reported workload was
comparable across conditions: raw NASA-TLX showed no reliable difference in overall workload .
Performance was the only subscale approaching a reliable difference, with participants reporting
marginally greater task success with AdapTest (𝑀= 7.2 vs. 5.8 of 10, 𝑝= .059, 𝑟= .39; all other
subscales 𝑝≥.23). SUS scores also did not differ reliably between AdapTest and the baseline .

6.1
Writing Adaptive Behavior on Specific Elements
With AdapTest, participants recorded how the interface should change as working rules attached
to specific elements. In the baseline, the same intent stayed in notes and speech. Of the 55 authored
rules, 44 bound specific elements from the original design (2 to 9 elements per rule, 𝑀= 5.6,
𝑆𝐷= 1.8) and 11 applied to a whole screen. Participants rated both mechanisms as helpful for
specification: selecting target elements helped them state what should change (𝑀= 6.4 of 7,
𝑆𝐷= 0.8), while writing IF/THEN rules helped them state when it should change (𝑀= 6.2,
𝑆𝐷= 1.1). P1 contrasted this with their usual practice: “Setting the IF/THEN was much easier; in
Figma you could really only write down in text what should happen in which situation, so specifying it
here was far easier.”
In the baseline artifacts, activation logic was written as text or verbally explained. We coded the
twelve baseline Figma files alongside the session recordings: seven participants recorded triggering
conditions as canvas annotations or layer names, while the other five explained them only during
the walkthrough—P8’s two-stage proximity fade, for example, existed as four overlapped static
states whose triggering logic was described in speech alone. Thresholds stayed symbolic where
AdapTest participants set numbers: P12 annotated the baseline variants with literal placeholders
(“distance > N m”), explaining that only testing could determine the value. Baseline participants
also reached beyond the staged factors: six of twelve built adaptive versions triggered by time,
touch, eye gaze, hand-reach, or the user’s own movement—trigger types their static tool could
neither encode nor fire. Consistent with this gap between intent and artifact, baseline participants
rated “I could clearly communicate when the interface should change using this workflow” at 𝑀= 4.4
of 7 (𝑆𝐷= 1.9), compared with 6.2 for the corresponding AdapTest rule item.
Rules could also be reused instead of remade as separate copies. Six of twelve participants carried
at least one rule condition onto a second or third screen (13 replicated conditions in the final projects,
and P9 applied one distance rule across all three screens). P1, whose five replicated conditions span
two screens, described the mechanism: “the other designs follow the same principle—reduce detail
when far—so after setting it once I could just paste the rules over to them.” Authoring inside the rules
still had limits. P11 found detailed content work harder in the headset (“With Figma, authoring the
window content itself was much easier—adding, editing, changing text”), but distinguished it from
adaptive authoring, describing Figma output as “making a pretty poster and sticking it up” whereas
AdapTest “makes you consider ...designing the interface around actions and spatial relations.”

6.3
Testing Rules in the Real Environment
Participants ran repeated tests during the session (Figure 12). They ran Test Mode 𝑀= 14.3 times
per session (𝑆𝐷= 10.5, range 2–38), completed 𝑀= 10.2 test–refine cycles (𝑆𝐷= 4.6), and edited
rule conditions and thresholds 𝑀= 46.1 times (𝑆𝐷= 27.3). Testing occupied about a quarter of
active session time (𝑀= 26%, 𝑆𝐷= 9%). Test Mode hides the editing panels while it runs, so a
participant who spots a problem during a test must stop the test before making the fix. We therefore
counted an edit as test-prompted when it occurred during a test or within 2 minutes after a test
ended: 430 of the 1,218 style and condition edits (35%) were test-prompted. Based on build–test
alternation, five participants interleaved testing throughout the session, each making at least 15
build–test switches, while seven concentrated testing in later verification passes.
The baseline afforded far less of this in-context checking. Participants could run AdapTest Test
Mode immediately during authoring (𝑀= 14.3 runs). In the baseline, testing required donning
the headset to inspect exported images and occurred much less frequently (𝑀= 2.7 inspections).
This difference in the frequency of in-context checking is descriptive (Figure 10b), because the
two measures represent different activities: running a live Test Mode session and donning the
headset to inspect a static image, and the baseline could not run a live test by construction. The two
workflows also placed testing at different times (Figure 13): with AdapTest, testing wove through
authoring, whereas the baseline separated a long stretch of off-headset Figma design from a few
late inspections.

Preferences Between Workflows
Participants rated all five AdapTest capabilities above the midpoint (previews, element selection,
rules, Test Mode, and suggestions, in order) and the interviews clarified why they preferred one
workflow over the other for a given kind of work. Four reasons recurred: immediacy, embodied
judgment of values, precision of content edits, and learning cost.
Participants placed the workflow inside practices they already knew. P5 recognized their own
habits: “Using the Vision Pro actually feels much more natural to me, because I usually design by
quickly roughing something out, placing it, and looking at it,” and P9 saw a round trip disappear:
“Designing VR content on a computer means you still have to re-verify it in VR anyway, so with a
good system doing it in VR is much better.” Learning cost and content precision favored the baseline.
Participants rated AdapTest higher on “I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going”
(𝑀= 3.0 vs. 2.1 of 5, 𝑝= .016, 𝑟= .49), and precise content edits such as changing text stayed
easier on the desktop. Looking beyond the study task, P7 identified where coverage work becomes
particularly important in practice: “For shipping products, companies imagine many very specific
edge cases, so this environmental control would be even more useful there,” and P5 noted the enabling
condition: “normally we never considered [distance and brightness] precisely because everyday sensors
can’t give exact distance; with accurate data the UI experience could get much better.”
7
Discussion
Our formative study surfaced three findings: designers already adapt concrete interface elements
with a small visual vocabulary, they lose track of the growing set of environmental cases, and they
can judge thresholds and transitions only in the space itself. AdapTest translated each finding into
a design goal: element-targeted rule authoring (DG1), multi-condition preview comparison (DG2),
and situated testing of the active rule (DG3). The comparative study then showed designers taking
up all three capabilities while keeping their own workflows: all twelve externalized adaptive intent
as working rules, they covered more of the staged factors, and testing wove through authoring
in two distinct rhythms, interleaved throughout or concentrated in later verification passes. We
discuss what these results mean for each design goal and close with limitations and future work.
7.1
Authoring Adaptivity as Designer-Owned Rules
Element-targeted rules kept the condition-appearance mapping in the designer’s hands, and design-
ers used that ownership. All twelve participants stated when and how their interfaces should change
as working rules, where the same participants’ baseline artifacts held that intent in annotations
and speech. They also rated their ability to communicate change conditions higher with rules (6.2
vs. 4.4 of 7). Prior work has advanced the complementary side of this division: automated systems
decide adaptations at runtime through solvers and optimizers [10, 16, 40], increasingly with user
preferences steering the objectives [28, 41], and even when designers pre-author the variants,
the runtime owns the mapping from context to variant [42]. Trigger-action tools established that

that visual designers, given concrete elements and continuous environmental triggers, author this
mapping fluently and treat it as part of the design itself—P11 described the shift as designing

The triggers designers reached for on their own show where rule authoring should extend
next. Six of twelve baseline participants authored states for triggers the study did not stage—time,
touch, eye gaze [29], hand-reach, and the user’s own movement—even though the task brief and
staging pointed only at environmental factors. These triggers fall into four families beyond the
environmental one AdapTest senses: interaction (touch, gaze, hand-reach), time of day, the user’s
own movement and proximity, and, in the fixture ideas below, the presence of other people. Each
family maps to a different use case, from a museum panel that reacts to a visitor’s approach to
a home panel that goes quiet at night. Several of these families have long precedents: proxemic
interaction research established distance, orientation, and movement as interaction dimensions
[35, 47, 59], and gradual engagement described the staged reveal-on-approach behavior that P8
rebuilt by hand from static states [46]. The sensing capabilities these triggers require (e.g., eye
tracking, hand tracking, and position tracking) are already available on the headset, and adaptation
drivers beyond the environment, such as privacy, are emerging in parallel [52]. Extending element-
targeted rules to these trigger families would let a single IF/THEN construct carry both interaction-
and context-driven adaptivity, keeping the rule set as a single inspectable record of both.
Designers also imagined interfaces that take on a role in the room, not only a position within it.
In both studies, participants pictured panels as interior fixtures (e.g., a glanceable clock blended
into a desk, or a panel by the door that becomes a video-doorphone as someone approaches) so
the interface’s anchor and the activity around it, alongside ambient conditions, shaped how it
should behave. This extends the reveal-on-approach of gradual engagement [46] to authored 2D
interfaces: treating an interface’s placement and the presence of people near it as conditions, and
its role—calm and glanceable at rest, interactive on approach—as an output, would let AdapTest
author interfaces that behave as ambient objects anchored in a space.
7.2
Seeing Coverage Before Deployment
With condition-specific previews, coverage became an inspection task, and in the baseline it stayed
a memory task . Participants addressed more of the staged factors with the tool (2.4 vs. 1.4 of
3), treated combined conditions as routine (nine of twelve authored combined rules), and rated
previews highest of the five capabilities (6.9 of 7)—editing directly from them. Comparing interface
alternatives side by side is an established aid to design judgment [13, 23, 56, 58]. Those comparisons
weigh alternatives a designer freely explores, and condition-indexed previews adapt the same aid
to a different question: whether one design stays legible and usable under each situation it will
meet—a concern readability research in AR documents across lighting, background, and distance
[6]. Coverage still did not become complete—brightness and occlusion trailed distance, and one
participant covered occlusion only in the baseline—so visible previews help designers inspect
coverage without ensuring they act on every gap. The shift from remembering conditions to
inspecting them is not specific to XR: any design that must hold across a space of conditions—
responsive layouts across screen sizes, light and dark themes, or accessibility variants—meets the
same risk of uncovered cases, and condition-indexed previews with coverage strips could carry the
same inspection aid to those settings, as keyframe authoring already spans screen size, ability, and
distance for 2D interfaces [61].
The suggestion results sketch how assistance might close those remaining gaps. Accepted
combination suggestions seeded five of the twelve combined rules, so acceptance did direct work.
At the same time, P12 saw seven suggestions, accepted none, and covered all three factors with

7.3
Testing as Part of Authoring
Situated testing became a design activity woven through authoring in the AdapTest condition,
where XR practice studies had described it as a costly separate step [1, 48]. Testing filled about
a quarter of active session time; 35% of edits followed a test within 2 minutes; and participants
described setting thresholds from observed values—P9 set a distance threshold by walking to a door
and reading 2m. Authoring rate did not differ reliably from the baseline while sessions ran longer,
so the added time went into testing and refining. The rhythms differed and the tool supported
each: seven participants concentrated testing in later verification passes, while five interleaved
tests from the start—at cadences from rapid edit–test alternation (P8 ran 38 tests around two rules)
to slower build–test cycles. In-situ authoring tools established creation in the target environment
[20, 27, 39, 44, 57], where testing reflects the conditions the room offers at that moment. The
active-rule Test Mode adds the ability to dial a condition on demand and know which authored
state is showing.
Seen across workflow types, the trade-off runs along where checking happens. A fully 2D
workflow, as in the baseline, keeps content edits precise and reuses the design system, and it lets
designers name any trigger they can imagine. Each condition, though, stays as text or speech, and
every check needs a headset round trip that P5 and P9 described as a separate step (Section 6.4). A
fully XR workflow, as in ShapesXR [57] or the Unity play loop P11 compared Test Mode to, gives
immediacy and spatial judgment from the start. It gives up the fine content editing and asset reuse
that participants missed in the headset. We did not evaluate such a tool, so this reading rests on
prior work and on participants’ comparisons. AdapTest sits between the two. The 2D design stays
the source, rules execute and are tested in place, and coverage is visible in previews, at the entry
cost participants reported. Which arrangement suits a project depends on whether its conditions
can be judged from the desk, how much fine editing remains, and how often the design must be
checked in the room. Participants’ requests draw a concrete design space for simulating conditions
the room cannot produce. P1 wanted a viewing distance beyond the room’s far wall, P2 wanted
more extreme conditions than the session offered, and P8 asked for a virtual distance control—yet
P8 insisted occlusion “should be tested by physically doing it,” and P11 could judge what 1m meant
only while moving. Simulation and embodiment thus split by condition: distance and lighting
invite virtual extension—simulating AR conditions from within VR has precedent [7], and headset
immersive environments could present a twenty-meter hall or a dark room inside a small lab—while
occlusion and threshold judgment draw their value from the body. Future situated-testing tools
could offer simulated ranges for the former while keeping the latter physical, widening testable
conditions without leaving the authoring session.
The same executable, in-place rules suggest a role in collaboration that our single-user sessions
did not test. Because an authored rule set runs and can be entered in Test Mode, a teammate
or developer could step into the space and experience the intended adaptive behavior directly,
where a mockup or written specification leaves that behavior to the imagination—a shared, situated
reference between designers and the developers who implement the result [4, 25, 48]. Shared rule
sets and semantic roles could likewise keep a team’s adaptive behavior consistent across screens
and products, and experiencing the behavior together might shorten the design–development
iterations that adaptive intent otherwise invites.

7.4
Limitations and Future Work
The main limitation of our study is that each tool was tested on its own fixed task domain, so
the comparison is not a pure tool contrast. We fixed this condition-to-domain pairing to keep the
physical staging comparable, and the results therefore compare AdapTest-on-home with baseline-
on-office under matched task structure. The shared screen scaffold and the first-block contrast
mitigate this, and a crossed replication would isolate the tool effect. The twelve single-session
participants likewise capture only first-contact use, representing one point in the broader space of
evaluation strategies [36], and the staged conditions were bounded by what one study room could
produce. Longer-term use—on designers’ own projects, with the learning cost amortized—remains
to be studied. The evaluation therefore supports claims about the authoring and testing process
and about participants’ perceptions in staged tasks. It does not show that the resulting interfaces
are more usable for end users, that deployed adaptations behave better, or that developer handoff
improves, since none of these was measured.
The tool itself has limitations that point to future work. Participants rated it higher in terms
of needing to learn before starting, and fine content edits remained easier on the desktop. Its
three sensing factors cover part of designers’ adaptive intent, and the simulation directions above,
together with the additional trigger families in Section 7.1, would broaden both the authorable and
testable ranges. The sensing itself is uncharacterized. Brightness is normalized to the range seen
since launch, so a threshold is relative to that session and to camera exposure. Occlusion in the study
came from tracked hands only, so physical obstacles and other virtual windows did not register.
None of the three readings was validated against an instrument, so thresholds are comparable
within a session and not across rooms. Environmental sensing also carries ethical weight: the
tool senses the surroundings only while the designer is testing, and the study stored structured
interaction events without camera frames, room reconstructions, or bystander recordings, but tools
that ship authored rules into everyday spaces will inherit the privacy questions of continuous
sensing [52]. Other people in the room would be sensed without having agreed to it. A version that
reads camera frames would also have to say what it keeps and for how long. Finally, the exported
rules and variants now travel back to desktop tools, and evaluating that handoff with the developers
and collaborating design teams who receive it—where XR development practice [4] and spatially
distributed collaboration [25] set their own requirements—would be the next step.
8
Conclusion
We presented AdapTest, an authoring tool that lets interface designers make their 2D designs
adaptive to the environments those designs will inhabit, through element-targeted rules, attached
condition previews, and situated testing of the active rule. Designers pick the elements to change
by tapping them on the rendered design, state when and how they should change as IF/THEN
rules over distance, lighting, and occlusion, compare each rule’s outcome in previews beside the
default design, and validate the behavior in place under manual controls or live sensing. The tool
accommodates designers’ own working rhythms, supporting testing woven through authoring as
well as verification concentrated at the end. AdapTest was designed from a formative study with
twelve designers and evaluated with twelve screen-rooted designers against their familiar desktop-
and-preview workflow. Participants took up all three capabilities, externalizing adaptive intent
as working rules, covering more of the staged environmental conditions, and refining thresholds
and styling from what they observed in tests, at workload and usability comparable to the tools
they already knew. Future work will extend rules to the trigger families designers already reach
for, simulate conditions a single room cannot stage, and follow authored rules into longer-term
practice and handoff.

Formative Study: Interview
The following questions guided the semi-structured interviews:
(1) Why did you choose to set the number of interfaces to be many/few or complex/simple?
(2) Did spatial structures or contexts influence your UI design decisions regarding environmental
factors?
(a) If the space were larger, more complex, or structured differently, would you have designed
the interfaces differently?
(3) What were your reasons for setting the interface size and composition at each viewing
location?
(a) What was the most important design factor you considered?
(4) You placed n interfaces in this space—if you were to redesign, would you change the number
of interfaces?
(5) Did you consider there to be an optimal window size, either in absolute physical dimensions
or relative to the user’s field of view?
(6) How did you design the UI components (videos, images, text, buttons) to change across
different viewing distances?
(a) What were your reasons for these component variations?
(b) Did you find certain component types to be particularly effective at specific distances?
(7) For designs targeting the same zone from different viewing positions, why did you make the
interfaces similar or different?
(a) : How would your interface design have changed if the viewing conditions differed (e.g.,
distances had been much closer or farther)?
(8) In what aspects did the environment between user and interface most significantly influence
your UI design decisions (size, position, content display method, component types, etc.)?
(a) If distance did not influence your design, what were the reasons?

User Study: Interview Protocol
We include here the full set of questions used in the user study:
Post-task walkthrough (baseline block)
(1) Please briefly walk us through what you created in this workflow, including anything you
expressed only as text notes.
(2) Which situation is this version designed to respond to?
(3) Looking at the artifact alone, how would one know when this version should appear?
(4) What could you check directly in this workflow, and what did you still have to imagine or
explain?
Post-task walkthrough (AdapTest block)
(1) Can you walk us through your final design as a whole?
(2) Choose one rule you consider important and explain it: why this target element, this envi-
ronmental condition, and this change?
(3) Did any part of the design change after you used the previews or Test Mode? What did you
observe that led to the revision?
(4) Did a suggested combined rule influence any design decision? If you accepted, edited, rejected,
or ignored one, why?
(5) Were there situations you noticed but chose not to address?
Semi-structured interview
(1) You used both workflows today. What felt most different between working in Figma and
working in AdapTest?
(2) Which parts made it easier or harder to specify what should change and when it should
change?
(3) Compared with your usual design practice, what felt familiar and what felt unfamiliar in
AdapTest?
(4) Was there anything you wanted to express in AdapTest but found difficult to express?
(5) In what projects or scenarios could you imagine using a tool like this?
(6) Conversely, in what situations would you prefer not to use it?
(7) If you could change one thing about the tool before real use, what would you change? (Probe:
at which point of the design–development process would it fit?)
