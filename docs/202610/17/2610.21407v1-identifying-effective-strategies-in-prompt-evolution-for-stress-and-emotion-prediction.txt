Abstract —Early stress detection and risk prediction are crucial for timely mental health assessment and support. While large language models have recently shown promise for social media stress prediction, relying on accuracy can obscure false positives. Also, prompt revisions yield inconsistent gains across models and tasks, with unclear links to strategy combinations and instruction changes. By comparing six methods on social media stress and emotion data across two model sizes, we found that GEPA and its extension combining instructions from two prompts (GEPA+merge) improved prediction over initial prompts in several settings. We also identified five prompting strategies associated with development gains. These strategies included directing attention to emotional evidence, distinguishing current from past distress, illustrating indirect cues, requiring emotional context, and guiding label selection. Moreover, we documented four observations across tasks and evaluation settings. In aseparate set of searches, binary stress prediction improved early, whereas multilabel emotion prediction initially declined and partly recovered. The other three concerned incorrect or conflicting instructions, gains on small comparison samples that did not hold on full development data, and changes in selected prompts’ development and reported test rankings. These findings identify effective prompt evolution methods, strategy combinations associated with gains, and task and evaluation limits, providing guidance for applying prompt evolution methods to social media stress detection. 

Index Terms —stress and emotion analysis, social media stress prediction, instruction analysis, strategy evaluation, prompt evo-lution, prompting strategies 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Early stress detection and risk prediction are crucial for timely mental health assessment and support. Recent studies following adolescents show why stress needs early attention: higher reported stress is associated with later mental health diagnoses in hospital records [1] and depressive feelings [2]. Randomized trials also show that digital stress management

and mindfulness programs can reduce employee stress [3], [4]. In one cohort, observed diagnosis rates over 12 to 16 months were about 4.8% for adolescents with high stress versus 0.5% with low stress [1], while employees receiving guided support averaged about 5 points lower than a waitlist group on a 0 to 40 stress scale after seven weeks [3]. These needs motivate predicting expressed stress from so-cial media text. Recent work applies large language models (LLMs) to predict stress in Dreaddit Reddit passages us-ing examples [5] or instructions refined with guidance from psychologists [6]. However, psychologist informed prompt refinement still misclassified about 11% of passages in one Dreaddit evaluation [6]. Also, accuracy alone can obscure false positives. Limiting these errors is important because false positives accounted for 90% of errors during initial prompt evaluation on Dreaddit [6]. Finally, prompt changes do not consistently improve prediction across models and tasks [5]. These studies leave open how instruction combinations evolve across revisions and relate to gains or declines [5], [6]. To address these limitations, we apply prompt evolution methods for stress and emotion prediction and evaluate ac-curacy alongside metrics that account for false positives and false negatives. Prompt evolution uses evaluation feedback to revise or combine an LLM’s instructions while keeping model weights fixed [7]. We trace strategy combinations and recorded instruction revisions alongside development gains and declines, considering how task and evaluation conditions affect their interpretation. These complete prompt compar-isons identify associations without isolating individual strategy effects or establishing reductions in false positives or false negatives. We compared six prompting methods, including prompt evolution, on Dreaddit for stress prediction [8] and GoEmo-tions for emotion prediction [9] across two model sizes. We measured overall correctness (accuracy, ACC), performance arXiv:2610.21407v1 [cs.CL] 15 Oct 2026 across labels (Macro-F1), and source support for explanations (Conformability). GEPA achieved the highest mean Macro-F1 on Dreaddit with Qwen3-8B, while GEPA+merge led on GoEmotions with Qwen3-32B. We then performed a detailed analysis of their recorded instruction revisions, supplemented by examples from separate historical searches. This analysis identified five strategies, including focusing on emotional evi-dence and distinguishing current from past distress, and traced their combinations across revisions. We also documented four observations, including early gains in binary stress prediction but initial declines in multilabel emotion prediction, and gains on small comparison samples that did not hold on full devel-opment data. We also discuss how these findings could inform future clinical applications, emphasizing clinician review and validation beyond social media data. In summary, we ask three questions. RQ1: Are prompt evolution methods effective for stress and emotion prediction tasks? RQ2: Which strategy combinations and instruction changes accompany gains or declines in development per-formance? RQ3: How do improvements and declines during prompt evolution vary across prediction tasks and evaluation settings? Our study makes three contributions:  

> •

We identified effective prompt evolution methods for stress and emotion prediction. Among six evaluated methods, GEPA achieved the highest mean Macro-F1 on Dreaddit with Qwen3-8B (67.81%), and GEPA+merge on GoEmotions with Qwen3-32B (25.14%) (Figure 2).  

> •

We identified five prompting strategies associated with development gains along golden routes for stress and emotion prediction. These strategies direct attention to emotional evidence, distinguish current from past distress, illustrate indirect cues, require emotional context, and guide emotion label selection (Table II). Golden routes, the paths from initial prompts to prompts selected at search end, combine several strategies, with performance measured for complete prompts.  

> •

We documented four observations on task differences, instruction errors, and evaluation limits in prompt evolution for stress and emotion prediction. Four ob-servations cover these task differences, incorrect or con-flicting instructions, gains on small comparison samples that did not hold on full development data, and changes in selected prompts’ rankings between development and reported test data. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Stress Detection in Social Media 

Stress detection must distinguish the author’s distress from topics, other people, and past experiences. Dreaddit labels Reddit passages [8], whereas Guntuku et al. relate users’ Face-book and Twitter language to survey measures of perceived stress [10], measuring users rather than individual passages. Turcan et al. incorporate emotion supervision into stress de-tection, obtaining performance comparable to BERT and using emotion predictions for interpretation [11]. Precision and recall distinguish false positives from false negatives, which a single F1 score cannot resolve. Turcan et al. describe BERT missing an author’s distress about a friend’s situation, while emotion informed models use crying as evidence of stress [11]. Xu et al.’s Mental-LLM study reports false positives when FLAN-T5, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 interpret a calmly recalled anxious job interview as current stress [5]. We compare overall scores across prompt revisions without measuring changes in false positive or false negative rates. 

B. Prompt Revision and Health Text Interpretation 

Prompt optimization revises instructions without changing model parameters. TextGrad uses written feedback, MIPROv2 searches instructions and examples, and GEPA uses reflection and component merging [7], [12]–[14]. For clinical adaptation and interpretation, TAMA coordinates generation, evaluation, and revision with clinician oversight [15]. SFT-TA uses super-vised fine tuning to train specialized coding agents, changing model parameters [16]. Auto-TA coordinates generation and feedback agents, describing optional reinforcement learning from human feedback as work in progress [17]. Yi et al.’s clinical framework refines codebooks and records source evi-dence and analytic decisions [18]. The clinical position paper emphasizes validity, reliability, and interpretability [19]. These principles support tracing evolving instructions and evidence, with clinical validation separate from benchmark prediction. 

C. Clinical Context and Patient Simulation 

O’Connor and Kirtley’s integrated motivational-volitional model distinguishes the emergence of suicidal ideation from the transition to suicidal behavior [20]. Sloan et al.’s ran-domized noninferiority trial compared five written exposure therapy sessions with 12 cognitive processing therapy sessions in 126 adults with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [21]. Craske et al. reviewed exposure therapy through inhibitory learning and retrieval across time and contexts [22]. Hoang et al.’s PSI-Bench preprint assesses depression patient simu-lators at turn, dialogue, and population levels. They evaluated seven language models across two simulation frameworks and compared benchmark results with mental health professionals’ judgments [23]. III. M ETHODS

B. Compared Methods 

Baseline uses the initial instruction pair without demon-strations or search. In context learning (ICL) keeps initial instructions and weights fixed, adding valid training demon-strations to both modules [25]. It checks the first eight training candidates in seeded order, retaining examples within the shared context limit whose labels match the reference, without development search. TextGrad revises both instruction texts as variables using textual gradients, written feedback to improve outputs [12]. We configured 20 update slots with three training examples each and full evaluation on all 100 development items at steps 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20. Failures or rejec-tions could reduce accepted updates. We selected the saved instruction state with highest available mean development score, choosing the earliest checkpoint on a tie. MIPROv2 

(MIPRO version 2) searches instruction and demonstration combinations for both modules [13], [14]. Each module had six instruction alternatives, including the original, and at most two demonstrations bootstrapped from model responses that passed task checks on training inputs. We configured the default and five trial combinations for evaluation on all 100 development items. Selection used the highest available full development score. Failed trials could end early. 

GEPA (Genetic-Pareto), our principal prompt evolution method, uses model reflection on task outputs and feed-back [7]. GEPA+merge adds the merging step in Figure 1. Both retain two modules and use the task backbone for feed-back and instruction revision. Only reusable prompt content changes, with model weights, task definitions, and reference data fixed. Different budget units do not imply equal model calls, tokens, or runtime. We used TextGrad 0.1.8, DSPy 3.0.3, and GEPA 0.0.7.

A. RQ1: Are Prompt Evolution Methods Effective for Stress and Emotion Prediction Tasks? 

GEPA and GEPA+merge improved mean stress prediction accuracy and Macro-F1 over Baseline on Dreaddit. With Qwen3-8B, GEPA achieved the highest Macro-F1 ( 67 .81 ±

2.58 ) and ACC ( 63 .50 ± 2.78 ), but lower Conformability than Baseline (Figure 2). With Qwen3-32B, GEPA+merge led Macro-F1 (71.80%) and ICL led ACC (68.50%). On GoE-motions, GEPA+merge exceeded Baseline in mean accuracy, Macro-F1, and Conformability with both model sizes, al-though leaders varied by model and metric. With Qwen3-32B, GEPA+merge led Macro-F1 ( 25 .14 ±0.61 ) and Conformability (83 .80 ± 3.70 ). GEPA led exact label set ACC ( 26 .00 ± 2.42 ), but its Macro-F1 remained below Baseline’s. With Qwen3-8B, MIPROv2 led Macro-F1 (16.82%) and ACC (23.70%). We next examine GEPA and GEPA+merge strategy com-binations and instruction changes accompanying development gains or declines (RQ2), and how these vary across prediction tasks and evaluation settings (RQ3). B. RQ2: Which Strategy Combinations and Instruction Changes Accompany Gains or Declines in Development Per-formance? 

Table II illustrates five strategies with saved prompt ex-cerpts and development scores from golden routes and other branches. The examples show changes and exceptions, not frequency rankings. Dreaddit rules distinguish the author’s emotion from topics or others’ experiences, current distress from past or resolved events, and distress from affection, help seeking, or coping. 

Strategy 1) Directing attention to emotional evidence pri-oritizes the writer’s expressed state over difficult circumstances alone. All three selected historical Qwen3-8B Dreaddit prompts distinguish the writer’s emotion from the topic. Seed 42 candidate 1 requires that “the label is based on the author’s emotional state, not on the topic”. Its development Macro-F1 is 76.14%, versus 84.96% for selected candidate 10 on a separate branch (Table II). Seed 43 candidate 11 instructs: “Focus on emotional tone and language, not the content of the passage itself”. Seed 44 candidate 10 notes that “The presence of challenging life events (e.g., family conflict, financial strain) does not automatically imply stress.” The shared rule alone does not explain the score difference. In the main Dreaddit/8B/GEPA/44 search, candidate 4 re-vises candidate 1, retaining its strategy categories but expand-ing emotional cues to behavioral changes and medical context. Development accuracy (ACC) on all 100 items rises from 56% to 61%. Emotional evidence occurs in stronger and weaker prompts, emphasizing complete instruction combinations. 

Strategy 2) Distinguishing current from past distress 

clarifies which time period matters. Dreaddit seed 42 candidate 2 adds a current emotional state rule alongside stress criteria and examples (Table II). Observation 1 relates this revision to the pattern across tree levels. 

Strategy 3) Illustrating indirect emotional cues provides examples of less explicit distress. Dreaddit seed 42 candidate 9 adds putting oneself down, feeling trapped, and deserving negative treatment to candidate 2’s cues. Macro-F1 rises from 79.80% to 81.88%. These illustrations are shorter than complete demonstrations containing a source text and labeled answer. 

Strategy 4) Requiring emotional context distinguishes a health concern from distress about it. Historical Dreaddit seed 42 candidate 10 requires emotional strain or anxiety for physical symptoms or medical concerns (Figures 3 and 4). Candidate 9 to selected candidate 10 adds physical and emo-tional decision boundaries, indirect distress examples, and temporal clarification among other edits, while development Macro-F1 rises from 81.88% to 84.96%. Child 18 retains this rule but removes five illustrative phrases, tightens a temporal rule, and reorders instructions. Macro-F1 falls to 78.52%, so retaining the rule is insufficient to preserve the higher score. 

Strategy 5) Guiding emotion label selection specifies when neutral or multiple labels apply. Historical GoEmotions seed 44 candidate 15 asks the model to “extract all emotionally charged words or phrases”, then match them to the inventory and use neutral if no clear emotional tone is present. Its parent already permits overlapping emotions. Table II contrasts this procedure with seed 43 candidate 3’s restrictive neutral rule. In GoEmotions/32B/GEPA+merge/44, selected candidate 3 combines candidate 1’s answer instruction with candidate 2’s source review instruction, both unchanged, creating a new prompt combination (Figure 1(b)). All main study roots requested grounding, concise output, and answer review, so these were not discoveries. Historical Dreaddit seed 42 candidates 1 and 12 respectively request quotations and explanations, and a label alone. Both revise root 0, not each other. Evolved prompts commonly used lists, emphasis, condi-tional rules, and restrictions. All contained commas. Median combined instruction lengths were 2,842 characters for GEPA and 3,270 for GEPA+merge. Quotation marks and field nota-tion served examples, output definitions, and other purposes without necessarily identifying source evidence. These struc-tural observations do not establish that formatting caused performance differences. 

C. RQ3: How Do Improvements and Declines During Prompt Evolution Vary Across Prediction Tasks and Evaluation Set-tings? 

Label number, coexistence, and existing instructions may explain task differences. 

Observation 1. Score trajectories differ by prediction task. In the separate search record, mean development F1 across seeds 42 to 44 rose steadily across tree levels for binary stress prediction but initially fell and partly recovered for emotion prediction with 28 labels. Golden routes were similar, with GoEmotions eventually exceeding its root (Fig-ure 3). Dreaddit’s mean gain from tree level 0 to 1 was 8.73 percentage points across three seeds ( p = 0 .0026 unadjusted, 

p = 0 .055 after 21 comparisons). No adjusted comparison met the 0.05 threshold. In three historical Qwen3-8B searches per dataset, all 19 scored Dreaddit root children gained 1.60 to 14.34 devel-opment Macro-F1 percentage points, expanding: “Classify whether the author expresses stress. Select exactly one label: 0=no stress, 1=stress.” Seed 42 candidate 2 on the golden route adds that “stress is determined by the current emotional state and expression in the text, not the past events themselves.” Macro-F1 rises from 66.56% to 79.80%. Beyond tree level 1, ten of 30 scored revisions improved, 19 declined, and one tied. All six scored children of seed 44 candidate 3 fell by 1.54 to 3.64 points. One root child and four later children lacked complete scores, including candidate 3’s seventh child. GoEmotions’ early revisions generally reduced perfor-mance: only two of eleven scored root children improved development Micro-F1, each by less than one point, while seven fell by more than four. All five scored seed 43 root children declined. Golden route candidate 3 specifies that neutral “should only be selected if the text explicitly reflects no strong emotional content,” while Micro-F1 falls from 31.62% to 24.18%. 

Observation 2. Some automatically revised prompts contained incorrect or conflicting task instructions. The first retained GoEmotions/8B/GEPA/42 candidate requests a single primary emotion, whereas the task allows multiple supported emotion labels. In historical Dreaddit seed 42, selected candidate 10 con-tains overlapping positive and negative conditions despite 84.96% development Macro-F1. Candidate 15 retains its par-ent’s no stress fallback but adds a competing rule against defaulting to neutral under ambiguity. Dreaddit seed 43 can-didate 16 references numbered example feedback without the complete example in its learned context. GoEmotions seed 42 candidate 7 maps joy and gratitude to 15, although their official identifiers are 17 and 15, respectively [26]. All three historical Qwen3-8B GoEmotions searches include a direct root revision with incorrect label identifiers. Seed 43 candidate 19 has only 3 correct identifiers among 28 entries and adds labels absent from the dataset, including “stress” and “terror.” Candidate 24 in seed 44 lists “anger” under seven identifiers and numbers the inventory from 1 to 28. These are their searches’ largest development Micro-F1 declines among scored parent and child comparisons, from 31.62% to 17.84% and from 29.30% to 18.05%. Seed 42 candidate 6 lists each of 15 names twice under identifiers 0 to 29, yet falls only from 29.30% to 27.41%. Retained candidate 6’s three descendants (19, 21, and 24) keep this inventory and score 27.54% to 28.87%. 

Observation 3. Comparison gains did not guaran-tee gains on the full development set. In GoEmo-tions/32B/GEPA/44, candidate 3 is retained after improving over parent 2 from zero to one correct item on the three item training comparison. Its full development score is 19%, versus its parent’s 20%. The final frozen candidate 5 scores 25% on development data. The search’s first attempted revision discourages multiple labels with overlapping meanings or when one suffices. Parent and revision both score zero on three comparison items. It is rejected without an identifier or full development evaluation. 

Observation 4. Among three selected prompts, the best development prompt ranked worst on the reported test. 

Among three selected historical Qwen3-8B Dreaddit prompts, seed 42 candidate 10 ranks first on development Macro-F1 (84.96%) but last on reported test Macro-F1 (75.99%). Seed 44 candidate 10 ranks last on development Macro-F1 (78.95%) and first on reported test Macro-F1 (79.99%). V. C LINICAL IMPLICATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

From a clinical perspective, the strategies associated with gains mirror clinical reasoning: distinguishing a person’s ex-pressed distress from difficult circumstances, separating cur-rent from past distress, and attending to indirect cues such as feeling trapped. Entrapment is central to suicide theory; the integrated motivational–volitional model proposes that defeat and entrapment drive the emergence of suicidal ideation [20]. Because the evolved prompts are written in plain language, clinicians can check them for errors. This checking is essential, since some prompts contained wrong labels and contradictory rules. These methods are not ready for clinical use, because they were tested only on social media posts and the study did not measure how often stress was falsely flagged or missed. Future work should test these methods in settings where text is already part of care. In written exposure therapy, which was noninferior to cognitive processing therapy for PTSD despite fewer sessions [21], patients write repeatedly about the same trauma. Emotion detection could help therapists track change across these narratives. Change across sessions is a better target than intensity within one, because exposure research indicates that neither the amount of fear reduction within a session nor the fear level at its end predicts treatment outcome [22]. Prompts would also need adapting, since a rule that discounts past events conflicts with writing about past trauma. A second direction is simulated patients for clinical training, where emotion detection could check realism; cur-rent depression simulators progress through therapeutic stages and toward positive emotion too quickly [23]. Finally, text-based stress and emotion detection may strengthen multimodal systems that also use speech and physiological signals, but transfer from social media posts to clinical speech must be tested first. VI. C ONCLUSION 

Among six evaluated methods, GEPA achieved the highest mean Macro-F1 for Dreaddit stress prediction with Qwen3-8B, and GEPA+merge for GoEmotions emotion prediction with Qwen3-32B. Across both model sizes, accuracy gains did not always coincide with better Macro-F1 or explanation support. Selected paths combined several of the five identified strategies also found in weaker prompts. Apparently useful revisions could reduce performance. Complete instruction combinations need evaluation in context, while isolated strategy effects remain unmeasured. Early gains in binary stress prediction contrasted with initial declines and partial recovery in multilabel emotion prediction, possibly reflecting task structure and initial instructions. Incor-rect or conflicting instructions, comparison gains that failed on full development data, and development versus reported test rank changes further qualified progress. The proposed clinical applications require controlled tests of individual strategies and combinations, measurement of false positives and missed stress, and validation on representative clinical data.
