Abstract 

Understanding the social intelligence of large language models (LLMs) is increasingly impor-tant for applications involving human-AI inter-action and multi-agent environments. Social intelligence comprises two aspects: cognitive intelligence, the ability to infer others’ mental states, and behavioral intelligence, the ability to select appropriate social actions based on these inferences to achieve social goals. Persuasion provides a natural testbed in which both aspects are simultaneously at play, yet prior bench-marks typically evaluate them in isolation us-ing synthetic scenarios. We introduce P ER SO,a benchmark that evaluates both aspects on real-world persuasion dialogues from Reddit’s ChangeMyView community, using an LLM-in-the-loop pipeline to construct the evaluation tasks, validated through human annotation. Our evaluation of diverse LLMs shows that perfor-mance generally scales with model size but varies across dimensions. The strongest LLM slightly surpasses the best observed human per-formance on the behavioral task, while the best human participant remains ahead on the cogni-tive task. 

1 Introduction 

As LLMs become increasingly integrated into so-cial contexts, the question of whether these models possess social intelligence has emerged as a central concern in NLP research (Zhu et al., 2025). Their deployment in direct interaction with humans and in multi-agent environments makes assessing their social intelligence a pressing research necessity (Li et al., 2024). Social intelligence has been defined in differ-ent ways over time. Thorndike (1920) charac-terized it as the ability to understand and man-age people and act wisely in human relation-ships. Marlowe (1986) emphasized understand-

ing people’s feelings, thoughts, and behaviors, including one’s own, and acting appropriately on that understanding in interpersonal situations. Albrecht (2006) defined it in terms of getting along with others and gaining their cooperation. Goleman (2007) distinguished social awareness— understanding others’ feelings and mental states— from social facility, or using that awareness to in-teract effectively. Together, these definitions characterize social in-telligence as understanding others’ mental states and acting appropriately to achieve social goals. Following Li et al. (2024), we distinguish two com-plementary aspects: (1) cognitive intelligence , the ability to infer others’ beliefs, emotions, and in-tents; and (2) behavioral intelligence , the ability to select and execute appropriate social actions based on these inferences to achieve social goals. A growing body of research has sought to evaluate and benchmark the social intelli-gence of large language models (Kim et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). However, existing benchmarks largely rely on synthetic or curated data rather than real-world interactions, and tend to focus on iso-lated aspects of social intelligence rather than cap-turing it in a multi-faceted manner. To address this gap, we introduce P ER SO, a benchmark for evaluating the social intelligence of LLMs across both cognitive and behavioral as-pects in persuasion scenarios. Persuasion provides a natural testbed for simultaneously assessing mul-tiple aspects of social intelligence (Li et al., 2024; Albrecht, 2006). In a successful persuasion inter-action, the persuader must anticipate the mental state of the counterpart—an ability that serves as a natural foundation for measuring cognitive intelli-gence. Furthermore, being able to judge which of two competing responses is more persuasive to that counterpart is itself a manifestation of behavioral intelligence. To ground P ER SO in real-world interactions, we use conversations from Reddit’s ChangeMyView (CMV) community, where users engage in struc-tured persuasion exchanges across diverse topics. To assess cognitive social intelligence, we construct multiple-choice questions targeting the persuadee’s beliefs, emotions, and intents, asking models to pre-dict each state in the persuadee’s subsequent reply. This evaluates the ability to infer others’ mental states from the preceding interaction. To assess behavioral social intelligence, we ask models to distinguish successful from unsuccessful persua-sion attempts by selecting the response that led to persuasion, as indicated by the persuadee’s delta award. This evaluates the ability to select an effec-tive social action toward the goal of changing the persuadee’s view. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We construct the P ER SO dataset from real-world persuasion dialogues and propose an LLM-in-the-loop pipeline for mental-state an-notation and question generation. • We design targeted evaluation tasks for assess-ing the cognitive and behavioral aspects of so-cial intelligence, with a single dataset jointly supporting the evaluation of both aspects. • We evaluate a diverse set of LLMs across vary-ing scales and architectures, revealing distinct patterns in social intelligence performance. 

2 Related Work 

Recent work has introduced several benchmarks for evaluating different aspects of machine social intelligence. One line of work focuses on cogni-tive intelligence, assessing the capacity of LLMs to infer mental states across dialogues and social contexts (Soubki et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024; Shinoda et al., 2025). Some studies evaluate mod-els’ ability to reason about others’ beliefs in con-versational settings (Kim et al., 2023). Others ex-amine how models track the evolution of beliefs, emotions, intentions across interconnected social scenarios (Xiao et al., 2025). More specifically, re-search on persuasive dialogues investigates mod-els’ ability to understand evolving beliefs, de-sires, and intentions throughout persuasion interac-tions (Yu et al., 2025). Most of these benchmarks ask models to recognize mental states from di-alogue that has already been observed. In con-trast, PERSO asks models to forecast the per-suadee’s mental state before seeing their follow-ing response, as shown in Table 1. Previous work on conversational emotions has also sepa-rated recognition from forecasting and found fore-casting to be more difficult when the target utter-ance is not available (Koga et al., 2024). Other works propose methods to improve social rea-soning through narrative-based Theory of Mind (ToM) tasks (Wu et al., 2023). More recent work also studies cognitive ability in multi-agent interac-tions rather than only static or two-person settings (He et al., 2023). Alongside cognitive evaluation, other bench-marks assess behavioral intelligence through mod-els’ ability to select appropriate social actions and achieve social goals. Prior work evalu-ates agents in interactive role-playing scenarios (Zhou et al., 2023), manually constructed narra-tives assessing goal achievement and interper-sonal abilities (Zhou et al., 2025), and individual or group level social interactions and multi-agent simulations (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). More recent work examines social goal com-pletion in multi-turn interactions alongside the inference of other agents’ private information (Mou et al., 2025). Unlike these primarily con-structed or simulated settings, P ER SO uses real human persuasion interactions to evaluate both cog-nitive and behavioral intelligence on the same data. 

3 Method 

3.1 Data Collection 

We construct our dataset using discussion threads from the Reddit CMV subreddit, spanning January 2024 to October 2025. CMV’s structured environ-ment for argumentation makes it an ideal source for naturalistic persuasion dialogues. In CMV, a delta (∆) is awarded when a user acknowledges that a comment changed their view. Reddit users can also upvote comments as a positive vote, and the re-sulting comment score provides a community-level signal of approval. 3.2 Task Definition 

To evaluate the social intelligence of LLMs in per-suasion scenarios, we design two distinct tasks, each targeting one of the aspects under considera-tion. The first task assesses the model’s cognitive intelligence through its ability to infer the mental state of the persuadee. The second task assesses the model’s behavioral intelligence through its ability to identify the more persuasive comment between two alternatives. Both tasks are constructed on the same dataset collected from CMV, enabling an in-tegrated assessment of both aspects on identical real-world data. The conceptual grounding of both tasks is detailed in Appendix A.1. The complete prompt templates are provided in Appendix A.9, Figures 15 and 16. Processed examples for each task are also provided in Appendix A.10, Figure 17.

3.3 Data Validation 

To validate the reliability of the constructed bench-mark, we conducted a human annotation study on a randomly sampled subset of 10% of the dataset: 40 instances, drawn from 200 threads containing 400 dialogues in total. Three independent annota-tors, denoted as A, B, and C, evaluated the same instances. All three were computer science stu-dents: one undergraduate and two graduate (Mas-ter’s level) students, all with prior experience in natural language processing. Unlike the models, which must infer the persuadee’s mental state with-out access to the final utterance, human annotators were shown the third utterance when making their judgments, allowing them to verify the gold answer and distractor options against the ground-truth text. For each dialogue, annotators were asked to iden-tify the correct option among the four candidates for each cognitive dimension. In addition to answer selection, annotators eval-uated the quality of the constructed options using two separate criteria. First, they rated how accu-rately the gold answer described the final utterance on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 ( com-pletely wrong ) to 5 ( completely correct ). Second, they rated the three distractor options based on their plausibility while remaining incorrect, using a scale from 1 ( bad distractors ) to 5 ( good distractors ). To measure the reliability of the human labels, we calculated pairwise agreement and Cohen’s κ be-tween annotators for the answer selection task. As shown in Table 4, the average pairwise Cohen’s 

κ values were 0.911, 0.623, and 0.709 for belief, emotion, and intent, respectively. These results indicate substantial agreement among annotators, particularly for belief and intent, and demonstrate that the human-annotated labels provide reliable supervision for evaluating cognitive reasoning ca-pabilities of LLMs. Additional agreement analyses and human assessments of gold answer correct-ness and distractor plausibility are reported in Ap-pendix A.2. To further examine the alignment of P ER SO with an established measure of social intelligence, all three participants completed the Tromsø Social In-telligence Scale (TSIS) (Silvera et al., 2001), a 21-item self-report instrument comprising Social Infor-mation Processing, Social Skills, and Social Aware-ness. Social Information Processing assesses per-ceived ability to understand others’ feelings and an-ticipate their behavior, while Social Skills assesses perceived competence in navigating social inter-actions. These subscales align conceptually with our cognitive and behavioral tasks, respectively. Ta-ble 3 reports their TSIS scores alongside P ER SO ac-curacies obtained before gold-label annotation us-ing the human evaluation protocol in Section 4.1.2. The behavioral ranking is identical across P ER SO

and TSIS Social Skills ( C > A > B ). Partici-pant A also ranks highest on Social Information Processing and all three cognitive dimensions, al-though the ordering of B and C differs between the two measures. These findings provide preliminary evidence of alignment with the targeted aspects of social intelligence within this small participant group. 

4 Evaluation 

4.1 Evaluation Setup 

We evaluate LLMs and human participants on the behavioral and cognitive tasks defined in Sec-tion 3.2, using accuracy to measure the selection of successful persuasion responses and the inference of persuadees’ mental states, respectively. The fol-lowing subsections describe the model evaluation settings and the human evaluation protocol. 

4.1.1 Model Evaluation 

For a comprehensive assessment of social in-telligence in large language models, we se-lect a diverse set of models spanning differ-ent model families and scales. Our evalua-tion set includes open-source models from the Gemma (Team et al., 2025), Qwen (Team, 2026), and DeepSeek (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) families at varying sizes, as well as closed-source models in-cluding Claude (Anthropic, 2025, 2026) and Gem-ini (Comanici et al., 2025). For the Qwen models, we additionally evaluate both reasoning and non-reasoning (with and without thinking) variants to examine the effect of this capability on social intel-ligence. Since the cognitive intelligence task was generated using Gemini 2.5 Pro, we additionally evaluate Gemini 2.5 Pro itself on the generated tasks to examine the potential for self-bias. To en-sure deterministic outputs, we set the temperature to 0 and top-k to 1 across all experiments. We also evaluate non-LLM baselines to situate model per-formance relative to surface-level cues; details of these baselines are provided in Appendix A.7. 

4.1.2 Human Evaluation 

To establish a human reference point for compari-son with model performance, we administered the same tasks to three human participants (A, B, and C), the same individuals who served as annotators in Section 3.3. This evaluation was conducted prior to the gold-label annotation stage described in Sec-tion 3.3. For the behavioral intelligence task, 20 instances (10% of the full dataset) were randomly sampled and given to all three participants. For the cognitive intelligence task, 40 instances (10% of the full dataset) were randomly sampled. The results for each participant are reported in Table 3.

4.3 Cognitive Intelligence Results 

Table 5 reveals several distinct patterns across the three cognitive dimensions. Most notably, emotion 

is consistently the most difficult dimension for all models. On a four-option question, chance-level accuracy is 25%, yet Gemma-3-4B and Gemma-3-12B reach only 20.5% and 23.25% respectively on this dimension, at or below chance, while the same models perform considerably better on belief and intent. This aligns with the human annotation data, where inter-annotator agreement was also lowest for emotion, suggesting that inferring emotional states is inherently harder than inferring beliefs or intents, for both models and humans alike. Another interesting observation concerns the spread of performance across dimensions. Emotion shows the widest variation, ranging from 20.5% to 59%, while most models cluster within a narrower 60–80.5% band on intent. This suggests that intent identification is less sensitive to a model’s specific capabilities and tends to be achieved at a relatively consistent level across different models. The positive scale–performance relationship seen in the behavioral task also holds here; for instance, within the Qwen family, belief accuracy rises from 66% for the 4B variant to 80.5% for the 397B variant. Interestingly, the DeepSeek family, which showed an irregular trend in the behavioral task, does not exhibit the same issue here. V4-Pro achieves the highest scores within the DeepSeek family on all three aspects. This contrast suggests that V4-Pro’s weaker behavioral performance is likely tied to a specific limitation in comparing two competing arguments, rather than a general weakness in understanding mental states. Finally, Sonnet-4.6 stands out on the emotion dimension with a score of 59%, more than 10 per-centage points above the second-best model on this dimension, Qwen-3.5-397B with thinking at 48.75%. This gap is not observed on either of the other two dimensions and may point to a stronger capacity in this model for detecting subtle emo-tional states. It is also worth noting that Gemini-2.5-Pro does not achieve the highest score on any of the three cognitive dimensions. It reaches 79.5% on belief, 37.25% on emotion, and 77.75% on intent, trailing Sonnet-4.6 on all three dimensions. It also trails Qwen-3.5-397B with thinking on emotion. Given that Gemini-2.5-Pro was involved in generating the cognitive task itself, its lack of consistent domi-nance provides evidence against systematic bias favoring this model in the evaluation. A comparison with the best human participant reveals relatively small gaps across the three cogni-tive dimensions. Sonnet-4.6, the strongest model on all three dimensions, scores 2 percentage points lower than the best human participant on belief, 6 points lower on emotion, and 4.5 points lower on intent. Thus, the best observed human perfor-mance remains higher across all three cognitive dimensions, although the differences are relatively small. Further comparisons with non-LLM-based mod-els are provided in Appendix A.7. 

4.4 Thinking Analysis 

To examine the effect of the thinking capability, we compared different variants of Qwen-3.5 with and without this capability enabled. Table 5 shows that thinking has different effects across dimensions, and no single conclusion can be drawn for all of them. The clearest pattern appears on the emotion di-mension. Enabling thinking improves accuracy for the 35B and 397B variants; the 35B variant goes from 35.75% to 41.25%, and the 397B variant from 41.0% to 48.75%. The 4B variant, however, remains almost unchanged, from 25.25% to 25.0%. Belief tells a different story: thinking lowers per-formance across all three sizes, with the 4B vari-ant dropping from 66.0% to 63.5%, the 35B from 71.75% to 69.75%, and the 397B from 80.5% to 79.0%. This difference likely comes down to the nature of each dimension. Emotion relies on subtle and implicit textual cues, and step-by-step reasoning can help the model interpret them more carefully. Belief, on the other hand, tends to be expressed more explicitly in the text, and thinking in this case adds complexity rather than clarity. For intent and the behavioral task, there is no clear pattern. On intent, all three variants show a slight drop with thinking enabled, though the de-cline is more pronounced for the 35B variant, from 73.75% to 66.5%. On the behavioral task, the re-sults are mixed: the 4B variant improves, while the 35B and 397B variants perform worse with thinking enabled. This inconsistency suggests that thinking offers no clear benefit for these two dimen-sions and may in some cases hurt performance. Overall, thinking is most effective where the text is more ambiguous and the model needs to rea-son more deeply. For dimensions where the rele-vant information is stated more explicitly, enabling thinking does not help much. 

5 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduced P ER SO, a benchmark for evaluating the social intelligence of large lan-guage models across cognitive and behavioral di-mensions, using persuasion scenarios as the evalu-ation testbed. P ER SO is built on real-world conver-sations from Reddit’s ChangeMyView community and enables an integrated evaluation of both dimen-sions on one dataset, addressing a key limitation of prior benchmarks that either evaluate these dimen-sions in isolation or rely on synthetic data. Our evaluation across a diverse set of LLMs reveals several consistent patterns. Model per-formance generally improves with scale, though not universally. The emotion dimension is consis-tently the most difficult to infer, both for models and human participants, while belief, intent, and overall persuasiveness prove comparatively easier. Enabling reasoning selectively improves emotion inference but offers little benefit, and occasion-ally slight harm, on the other dimensions. Finally, the strongest model slightly surpasses the best ob-served human performance on the behavioral task, while the best human participant remains above the top-performing model on all three cognitive dimensions. The gaps suggest that cognitive so-cial reasoning—particularly emotion—continues to present a meaningful challenge for current LLMs. Limitations 

Our human reference results are based on only three participants, which limits the statistical re-liability of any comparison between human and model performance. A larger and more diverse participant pool would be needed to draw more definitive conclusions about the relative standing of humans and models on these tasks. PER SO is constructed exclusively from the ChangeMyView subreddit, which, while a rich source of naturalistic persuasion dialogue, repre-sents a specific community with its own norms, de-mographics, and argumentative conventions. More-over, our data is limited to English-language con-versations. The extent to which our findings gener-alize to other persuasion contexts, languages, and cultural settings remains an open question. Fur-thermore, the dialogues in this work are limited to single-turn exchanges between the persuader and the persuadee. Investigating persuasion scenarios with a greater number of turns and longer conversa-tions (which may reveal distinct patterns of social intelligence) was not feasible in this work due to constraints on the volume of qualifying data, and represents a valuable direction for future research. 

Ethical Considerations 

The human annotation and evaluation studies re-ported in this work involved three volunteer par-ticipants who provided informed consent prior to participation. Participants were made aware of the nature of the tasks and their right to withdraw at any time. All individual results, including scores on the Tromsø Social Intelligence Scale (TSIS), are reported in anonymized form using labels A, B, and C, with no personally identifiable information disclosed. The TSIS scores reflect personal psycho-logical assessments and are included solely to pro-vide context for interpreting human performance; they should not be used to make judgments about individual participants. The Reddit data used in this work was collected from publicly available posts and comments on the ChangeMyView subreddit and does not include any private communications.

A.1 Conceptual Grounding of the Evaluation Tasks 

This section makes explicit the connection between the psychological definitions in Section 1 and the tasks in Section 3.2. The argument proceeds from the definition of social intelligence, to the abili-ties it involves, to their expression in persuasion, and finally to the observable decisions scored by PER SO.

Social intelligence and its cognitive and behav-ioral facets. The definitions discussed in the in-troduction share a connection between understand-ing people and acting effectively in interpersonal situations. Thorndike (1920) associates social in-telligence with understanding people and acting wisely in human relationships. Marlowe (1986) makes this connection explicit: social intelligence involves understanding people’s thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, including one’s own, and acting ap-propriately on that understanding. Goleman (2007) similarly distinguishes social awareness from so-cial facility : understanding another person and us-ing that awareness in effective interaction. These accounts motivate our focus on two com-plementary facets: cognitive understanding and behavioral effectiveness. Furthermore, we use the definitions proposed by Li et al. (2024) for these two facets. 

Cognitive intelligence: from understanding mental states to forecasting a recipient’s reac-tion. The cognitive facet concerns understand-ing another person’s mental states from avail-able social cues. This follows the understand-ing component of Marlowe (1986) and the social-awareness component of Goleman (2007). More specifically, Li et al. (2024) organize cognitive in-telligence around beliefs, emotions, and intents. These correspond to the three targets of our cogni-tive task: what the persuadee regards as true, how they feel, and what they seek to do or communicate. In a persuasion scenario, this understanding con-cerns a recipient who already holds a particular view and is presented with a message intended to influence it. Given the OP’s initial post uOi and a persuader’s response uPi,j , P ER SO asks the model to forecast the OP’s resulting belief, emotion, or intent by selecting among four candidate descrip-tions. The relevant judgment is therefore how this particular recipient will respond to this particular message. It is not whether the model itself endorses the argument or considers it objectively correct. The subsequent reply uO′ 

> i,j

supplies evidence for constructing the reference answer but is withheld during evaluation. This separates the evidence used to establish the target from the information avail-able to predict it. Consequently, the task evaluates anticipation of a mental state expressed in a future response, rather than extraction of that state from an already visible utterance. Forecasting is thus the operational form that mental-state understanding takes in our persuasion setting; it is not proposed as an exhaustive definition of cognitive intelligence. The reference answers remain interpretations of expressed states, supported by the annotation study in Section 3.3, rather than direct observations of private mental experience. 

Behavioral intelligence: from effective social action to response selection. The behavioral facet concerns using social understanding to com-municate and act effectively toward social goals (Li et al., 2024). It corresponds to acting on inter-personal understanding in Marlowe (1986) and to social facility in Goleman (2007). In a text-based persuasion exchange, a response is a communica-tive action, and changing the recipient’s view is the local social goal. Selecting between candidate responses therefore provides a constrained test of the action-selection component of this facet. Our behavioral task holds the OP and initial post fixed while presenting two naturally occurring re-sponses. The model assigns unequal persuasive-ness scores to the responses, thereby selecting the action it judges more effective for this recipient. The reference selection is grounded in the OP’s observed delta award, with both subsequent OP replies hidden from the model. An observed in-terpersonal outcome is therefore used to evaluate a prospective decision: given this recipient and these available responses, which response should be selected to achieve the persuasion goal? The criterion is recipient-specific persuasive suc-cess rather than an evaluator’s general preference for an argument. The additional upvote constraint aligns the selected outcome with a community-level signal; the delta remains the outcome label. A response without a delta lacks this explicit suc-cess signal, but may still have affected the recipient. Likewise, the task assesses judgment about which action to select, rather than the ability to generate and execute a persuasive response in live interac-tion. 

Why persuasion brings the two facets to-gether. The connection to persuasion is sup-ported by the cited accounts of social intelli-gence. Albrecht (2006) emphasizes gaining co-operation and describes clarity in terms of com-municating ideas effectively, including persuasion. Goleman (2007) includes influence within social facility, and Li et al. (2024) place persuasion under behavioral intelligence. Persuasion consequently supplies a natural interpersonal setting with an iden-tifiable recipient, a communicative action, and a social goal. Within this setting, understanding the recipient’s reaction and selecting an effective re-sponse are complementary questions. The processed example in Figure 17 illustrates the distinction. After the medical-progress argu-ment, the OP concedes the need for further med-ical advances while retaining a broader concern about well-being. The cognitive questions target this selective belief revision, the receptive emo-tional stance, and the intent to qualify the original claim. The behavioral question instead asks which of the two responses achieved the acknowledged view change. Thus, the tasks evaluate different aspects of the same interaction: the recipient’s re-sulting mental state and the effectiveness of a com-municative action. 

From the task definitions to the measured con-struct. The reasoning chain is therefore as fol-lows. Established definitions connect interpersonal understanding with effective social action. Per-suasion gives these abilities a concrete recipient, context, and goal. Mental-state forecasting oper-ationalizes the understanding component, while outcome-grounded response selection operational-izes the action-selection component. Evaluating both on the same interactions therefore provides a definition-grounded assessment of complementary facets of social intelligence. This establishes the conceptual relevance of the tasks without equating their scores with complete psychometric validation of social intelligence. The tasks need not be cognitively independent, and their scores do not establish that a model internally uses its forecast when selecting a response. Nor does persuasive success alone establish social wisdom or competence in every interpersonal setting. Hu-man annotation, the exploratory TSIS comparison, and the baseline analyses provide additional evi-dence with different purposes. Within these bound-aries, P ER SO measures mental-state forecasting and goal-directed response selection as theoreti-cally grounded cognitive and behavioral facets of social intelligence in persuasion.

A.3 Behavioral vs. Cognitive Intelligence 

A cross-task comparison reveals that, within most model families, models that perform better on the cognitive intelligence task also tend to perform bet-ter on the behavioral intelligence task, suggesting a degree of shared underlying capability. However, this correlation does not imply that the two tasks measure identical aspects of social intelligence, as the magnitude of improvement across model sizes within a family varies substantially between the two tasks. In the Gemma family, the behavioral dimension shows a larger performance spread across model sizes (10.5 percentage points from 4B to 27B) com-pared to the average spread across cognitive di-mensions (approximately 8.4 percentage points), suggesting that behavioral intelligence is more sen-sitive to scale within this family. The pattern re-verses in the Qwen and Claude families: Qwen models without thinking exhibit a larger margin on cognitive dimensions (approximately 15 percent-age points on average across belief, emotion, and intent) than on the behavioral task (12 percentage points), while in the Claude family this contrast is even more pronounced: the behavioral gap be-tween Haiku-4.5 and Sonnet-4.6 is only 2.0 per-centage points, whereas the cognitive gap averages approximately 15.7 percentage points across the three dimensions. The DeepSeek family presents a notably differ-ent picture: whereas V4-Pro is the weakest of the three variants on the behavioral task (62.0%, com-pared to 67.5% for V3.1 and 69.0% for V3.2), it consistently achieves the highest scores among its family variants on all three cognitive dimensions (80.0% on belief, 39.75% on emotion, and 75.0% on intent). This reversal in the performance or-dering between the two tasks indicates that the behavioral and cognitive dimensions of social intel-ligence can be dissociated, and that architectural or training differences between model variants may selectively affect one dimension while leaving the other largely unaffected. Taken together, these observations suggest that behavioral and cognitive social intelligence, while positively correlated at a broad level, are distinct capabilities that scale differently across model fam-ilies and sizes. A high score on one dimension should therefore not be taken as a reliable proxy for performance on the other.

Cognitive task.
