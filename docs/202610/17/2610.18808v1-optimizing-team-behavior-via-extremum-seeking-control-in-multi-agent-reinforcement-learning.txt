# Optimizing Team Behavior via Extremum-seeking Control in Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning 

Svar Rajankumar Patel 

Department of Computer Science University of Massachusetts Lowell Lowell, Massachusetts, USA 

Kshitij Jerath 

Department of Mechanical and Industrial Engineering University of Massachusetts Lowell Lowell, Massachusetts, USA 

Abstract —In cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learn-ing (MARL), agents must learn to coordinate their actions to achieve shared objectives. A key indicator of effective coordination is behavioral diversity, i.e., the agents’ potential to enact distinct yet complementary control policies given the same set of observations. Although behavioral diversity can enable role specialization and richer joint behaviors, its appropriate level is highly context- and task-dependent and often costly to tune. We propose Adaptive Diversity Control (ADiCo), a two-timescale framework that adapts behavioral diversity online during training. On a fast timescale, Diversity Control (DiCo) regulates system diversity toward a target value. On a slower timescale, an Extremum Seeking Controller (ESC) adjusts this target using only mean episodic returns, without requiring an explicit model or analytical gradient of the diversity-performance landscape. We evaluate ADiCo on six configurations across two cooperative MARL tasks, where it maintains task-appropriate diversity levels: low diversity when homogeneous policies are beneficial and higher diversity when heterogeneous policies are required. Through this adaptation, ADiCo matches or exceeds the mean performance of DiCo (best grid-searched diversity target) and Unconstrained DiCo (diver-sity allowed to emerge without target regulation). These results demonstrate that incorporating classical feedback control into MARL training enables effective coordination by providing a systematic alternative to task-specific manual selection of the diversity target. 

Index Terms —Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning, Behav-ioral Diversity, System Neural Diversity, Extremum Seeking Control, Model-Free Adaptive Control 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) [1], [2], a growing body of work has shown that behavioral diversity among agents can provide significant benefits, including the emergence of complementary roles, improved exploration, and greater resilience to disruptions [3]–[7]. Under the Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm, centralized information may be used during training while agents execute decentralized policies based on local observations. The resulting degree of behavioral diversity is shaped by architectural and learning-design choices such as parameter sharing, auxiliary objectives, and intrinsic rewards [8]. However, these approaches generally induce or encourage diversity without explicitly regulating its resulting magnitude to a desired, measurable level. Consider a team of delivery drones: if all packages are destined for the same neighborhood, similar behaviors may be sufficient, resulting in low diversity. In contrast, if packages must be delivered to different parts of a city, agents may need to specialize their routes, requiring greater behavioral diversity. The appropriate degree of diversity therefore depends on the task, motivating methods that can explicitly measure and regulate behavioral diversity according to task requirements. In this work, we measure behavioral diversity using Sys-tem Neural Diversity (SND) [9], which aggregates pairwise differences between agents’ policies into a single system-level measure. To regulate this diversity, we use Diversity Control (DiCo) [4], which represents each agent’s policy as the sum of a shared component and a dynamically scaled agent-specific component, allowing system diversity to be controlled toward a prescribed SND target. However, the appropriate diversity target is task-dependent and generally unknown a priori. We therefore formulate its selection as a feedback optimization problem and use Extremum Seeking Control (ESC) [10], a model-free optimization technique used across domains including robotics, manufacturing and energy systems [11], [12]. ESC applies small sinusoidal per-turbations to the diversity target and processes the resulting variations in task performance to estimate the local direction of improvement, which is then used to adapt the nominal diversity setpoint. Building on these components, we propose Adaptive Diversity Control (ADiCo), a two-timescale framework in which DiCo regulates diversity while ESC adapts the diver-sity target on a slower timescale. ADiCo preserves DiCo’s compatibility with on- and off-policy actor-critic algorithms using deterministic or stochastic continuous-action poli-cies [4], including IPPO [14] and MADDPG [15], without modifying the underlying MARL learning objective. The main contributions of this work are as follows:

II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Behavioral diversity in cooperative MARL has been en-couraged through a range of architectural and learning mech-anisms. Selective parameter-sharing and agent-specific archi-tectures allow policies to specialize to different degrees [16], [18]–[20], while methods based on latent variables, role representations, and intrinsic objectives explicitly encour-age diverse behaviors or specialization [21]–[23]. These approaches primarily enable or promote heterogeneity, but generally do not expose the resulting magnitude of behav-ioral diversity as an explicit control variable. System Neural Diversity (SND) [9] addresses the measurement problem by quantifying policy-level heterogeneity through pairwise behavioral distances, while Diversity Control (DiCo) [4] uses this measure to regulate the system toward a prescribed diversity target, SND des . Although DiCo outlines an outer-loop procedure for adjusting desired diversity toward a user-specified performance level, it does not address the problem of locating an unknown reward-maximizing diversity target. This motivates adapting the desired diversity directly from task-performance feedback during training. Adaptive hyperparameter optimization has also been stud-ied within reinforcement learning. Population-Based Training (PBT) jointly adapts model parameters and hyperparame-ter schedules using a population of concurrently trained agents [26], while Hyperparameter Optimization on the Fly (HOOF) adapts policy-gradient hyperparameters within a single training run by evaluating candidate policy updates using off-policy estimates [24]. Meta-gradient methods in-stead differentiate through the learning process to adapt differentiable hyperparameters online [25]. In our setting, the desired diversity is a control input to DiCo, and its effect on task performance is unknown and may change as policies learn. We therefore require a model-free controller that can adjust this target during training using scalar performance feedback, without access to an analytical gradient of the diversity-performance relationship. Extremum Seeking Control (ESC) meets these require-ments by applying small periodic perturbations and using the resulting performance variations to estimate a local direction of improvement [10], [11]. ESC has previously been applied to dynamical systems [12] and to refining sampled actions during reinforcement learning [13]. In ADiCo, ESC instead adapts a system-level diversity target on a slower timescale than the underlying DiCo/MARL process, seeking a locally high-performing diversity level along a single training run. C. Diversity-Performance Landscape 

Although DiCo regulates behavioral diversity toward a pre-scribed target θ, the target that maximizes team performance is generally unknown and task-dependent. To formulate its selection, we introduce the diversity–performance map J(θ),representing the expected team performance associated with a diversity target θ. This relationship may change as the policies learn. We therefore consider it on the slower ESC timescale, with the underlying MARL/DiCo training process treated as the faster subsystem. At each cycle k, the diversity target is held fixed while agents collect experience and update their policies. The updated policies are then held fixed and evaluated over M

complete episodes to obtain the mean episodic return Rk.This return provides a stochastic performance measurement at the current target, which ESC uses to adjust the target for the next cycle. Thus, diversity adaptation occurs after each training and evaluation cycle, rather than at every environment timestep. Using this feedback, ADiCo seeks a locally optimal di-versity target θ⋆ within the admissible range Θ = [0 , θ max ].Because the diversity–performance map is unknown and its analytical gradient is unavailable, we treat target selection as a black-box optimization problem. ESC addresses this problem by perturbing the target and processing the resulting changes in Rk to estimate a local direction of performance improvement. 

D. Extremum Seeking Diversity Adaptation 

Having formulated diversity-target selection as a black-box optimization problem, ADiCo uses ESC to estimate a local direction of performance improvement through periodic perturbations. At each ESC cycle k, a sinusoidal perturbation with amplitude a and phase ϕk is applied to the nominal setpoint ˆθk. The perturbed value is projected onto Θ to obtain the diversity target θk supplied to DiCo. The phase advances according to ϕk+1 = ϕk + ω∆t, where ω is the perturbation angular frequency and ∆t is the interval between ESC updates. After training and evaluation at θk, the measured return 

Rk is processed to estimate the local performance response to the perturbation. A high-pass filter (HPF) attenuates slowly varying components of the return, demodulation extracts the response correlated with the perturbation, and a low-pass filter (LPF) attenuates the remaining oscillatory components. The resulting signal ˆξk estimates the local direction of performance improvement. ESC uses ˆξk to update the nominal setpoint ˆθk, with a positive integrator gain η controlling the adaptation rate and projection onto Θ enforcing the admissible range. These updates seek a locally high-performing diversity level. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1. IV. E XPERIMENTS

A. Environments and Task Configurations 

We evaluate ADiCo on two cooperative continuous-control tasks from the Vectorized Multi-Agent Simulator (VMAS) [31]: Navigation and Balance (Fig. 2). All ex-periments use three agents under partial observability, with episodes lasting at most 100 steps. We keep the team size fixed to simplify the analysis, although ADiCo is not limited to a fixed number of agents.

For each configuration, we compare ADiCo with two baselines. Grid-searched DiCo uses the best fixed SND target from {0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.3, 1.5, 1.8, 2.0}, with a separate 12M-frame training run for each target. Unconstrained DiCo 

fixes the agent-specific scaling factor at one, allowing diver-sity to emerge without an explicit SND target. These base-lines test whether online adaptation can match or improve upon both the best grid-searched target and freely emerging diversity. All methods use the same MARL algorithm, net-work architecture, and 12M-frame budget per training run. 

B. Training and Evaluation 

All our approaches use IPPO [14], [28] and are trained for 12M frames over five random seeds. Training is divided into cycles, with each cycle collecting 60,000 frames from 600 parallel environment instances followed by the MARL policy update. The updated policies are then evaluated over 

M = 200 episodes, and the mean episodic return Rk is used by ADiCo to update the diversity setpoint before the next cycle. V. R ESULTS AND ANALYSIS 

In this section, we evaluate ADiCo across three key aspects: performance across task configurations, diversity adaptation from different initial targets, and training behavior under continuous ESC perturbations. 

A. Performance Across Task Configurations 

We evaluate ADiCo across six configurations of two co-operative tasks. In Navigation, we vary the number of goals (1, 2, or 3) to change the need for specialization, using 3A-

xG to denote three agents with x goals. In Balance, we vary ball Mass (2, 3, or 4) to change task difficulty while keeping the coordination structure the same, using 3A-xM to denote three agents with ball Mass x. In Navigation (Fig. 3, top row), ADiCo adapts to high diversity (SND ≈1.2–1.3) for 3A-3G, moderate diversity (SND ≈0.8) for 3A-2G, and near-zero diversity for 3A-1G. It achieves reward comparable to grid-searched DiCo, with lower reward variance than Uncon-strained DiCo. In Balance (bottom row), ADiCo maintains low diversity across all Mass configurations and matches the performance of DiCo with SND = 0. For Unconstrained DiCo, increasing diversity accompanies declining reward, with performance collapsing in the 3A-4M configuration. These results show that ADiCo automatically adapts to-ward low diversity when similar behaviors are beneficial and higher diversity when specialization helps. It achieves performance comparable to manually tuned DiCo without requiring configuration-specific grid search, while avoiding the excessive diversity observed with Unconstrained DiCo.

L IMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

While ADiCo uses mean episodic return to adapt diversity, changes in return are not always caused by diversity adjust-ments alone. Factors such as policy learning, environmental shifts, and adapting opponents also influence performance, complicating adaptation in adversarial or mixed-motive do-mains. Consequently, future work could explore alternative feedback signals and evaluation methods that better isolate the specific effect of diversity. Furthermore, because ADiCo relies on extremum seeking control, it risks converging to a local optimum of the performance signal rather than the globally optimal diversity target for the task. This limitation is compounded by the fact that ADiCo currently tunes only a single team-wide SND target, whereas distinct agents or roles may benefit from different diversity levels. Extending the framework with multidimensional ESC could allow multiple diversity targets to be adjusted from a shared performance signal, enabling more detailed control over team behavior. Finally, ADiCo adapts diversity exclusively during training rather than runtime execution. Because teams often need to alter behavior as conditions change within an episode, integrating ADiCo with event-based policy adaptation [32] could allow agents to dynamically adjust their diversity in response to runtime events. VII. C ONCLUDING REMARKS 

In this work, we introduced ADiCo, a framework that adapts team diversity during training using team-performance feedback. Across two cooperative tasks, ADiCo adjusts di-versity to task-appropriate levels. Experiments with different initial setpoints further show that, in the tested configuration, users can begin with a rough diversity estimate and let ADiCo refine it without an explicit model of team dynamics. More broadly, ADiCo illustrates how model training can be viewed as a process whose adjustable components are optimized through feedback, much like machines in industrial systems.
