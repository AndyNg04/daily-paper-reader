Abstract. Communication in shared space interweaves verbal and non-verbal signals, and among them pointing gestures anchor language to the environment: “put the cup on that one” is uninterpretable without the gesture that fixes the referent. For embodied agents to produce such gestures, their evaluation must ask whether a gesture indicates the intended object, yet no common framework for this exists; distributional metrics reward a gesture aimed at the wrong object as long as it looks natural. We introduce a benchmark for spatially grounded gesture generation. It supplies three missing pieces: a paired data release of ∼2K pointing-annotated clips from naturalistic VR dialogue, each labelled with a ground-truth referent in a 3D scene graph, alongside 1K scripted single-target clips with shared actors; a task formulation in which systems receive spontaneous conversational speech and a 3D target and must decide when to gesture, how to move, and how to aim, with no oracle apex frame; and an evaluation protocol separating temporal alignment from spatial grounding and reporting their product, complemented by a crowdsourced naturalness study. We evaluate a flow-matching baseline, an independently developed retrieval-based system, and a ground-truth reference run. The retrieval-based system achieves substantially stronger spatial grounding than both the baseline and captured motion, while being rated no more natural than the baseline. Temporal evaluation likewise separates the systems, but comparison with captured motion depends on the temporal scoring parameters. These results expose a gap between geometric task metrics and perceived motion quality, motivating evaluation that reports spatial, temporal, and perceptual factors separately.

1. Introduction 

Human communication in shared physical space runs on more than words. Speakers maintain a representation of their surroundings and index it as they talk—through stance and body orientation, through gaze, and through pointing— so that meaning is distributed across the verbal and bodily channels rather than carried by either alone. Reference to the environment is the clearest case: “put the cup on 

that one” is uninterpretable without the gesture that fixes what “that one” denotes, and the gesture is uninterpretable without the room. Embodied agents that share space with people—virtual avatars, social robots, AR assistants—need both halves of this ability: to interpret such signals, and to produce them. Our concern here is production. A gesture genera-tion model can produce fluent, speech-synchronised motion and still lack the capability that makes communication in space work: indicating something about the scene. Current co-speech gesture generation mostly addresses the distribution-matching problem in motion synthesis. Models are trained to reproduce the statistical properties of human motion and evaluated accordingly, with metrics such as Fréchet Gesture Distance [1] and diversity, as estab-lished by the GENEA Challenge [2] for community-wide benchmarking. For beat and iconic gestures, where the 1arXiv:2610.03105v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 goal is natural-looking motion synchronised with speech, this is appropriate. Referential gestures pose a fundamen-tally different problem: a pointing gesture directed at the wrong object can score well under distributional metrics while being a complete communicative failure. Referential gestures need to be both natural and referentially accurate. What has been missing is the data-driven counterpart: a corpus, task, and metric set that let speech-conditioned generative models be compared on referential accuracy the way they are already compared on motion realism. Evalu-ating referential gestures therefore requires complementing distributional fidelity with task success: did the gesture indicate the intended object? The shift has been blocked by three missing pieces: datasets pairing human gesture with 3D scene context, a task formulation for spatially grounded referential ges-ture generation, and metrics that measure spatial ground-ing in gestures. We address all three, building on MM-Conv [3], whose referring expressions are each annotated with a ground-truth referent in a 3D scene graph, thereby enabling evaluation of whether generated motion geomet-rically indicates the intended object. This paper defines the benchmark and validates its proto-col using a flow-matching baseline, an independently devel-oped system (RePointer [4]), and a ground-truth reference run. Our contributions are: 1. A paired data release : ∼2K pointing-annotated clips from MM-Conv’s naturalistic dialogue and 1K clean single-target pointing clips from SGS-HSI with shared actors (§3). The two sources provide complementary regimes: spontaneous pointing in dialogue and con-strained single-target pointing. 2. A task formulation for referential gesture in contin-uous dialogue. Systems receive, per test item: audio with a word-level transcript; a 3D target coordinate in pelvis-centred and world frames; the dominant point-ing hand; a static SMPL-X body shape; and the room’s scene graph (per-object category, bounding-box cen-tre, extent). They must generate SMPL-X motion containing a pointing gesture directed at the target. Ground-truth motion, apex frame, referent identity, and evaluator labels are withheld, so the system de-cides for itself when the gesture peak occurs. No oracle apex frame or motion template is provided at inference time. Phrase intervals are released for train-ing only and are not consumed by the scorer. 3. An evaluation protocol with a ground-truth refer-ence run . The objective half separates when a system gestures (temporal alignment, Score 𝑎 ) from where it points (spatial accuracy, Score 𝑏 , with tolerance from the annotated referent’s extent) and combines them multiplicatively (STG, §4.2); the subjective half rates rendered motion for naturalness in a crowdsourced study (§5.3). 4. A flow-matching baseline , MM-Conv-Flow: a co-speech diffusion-transformer backbone pretrained on BEAT2 [5], adapted to the benchmark with target-conditioned residual branches, relational pointing losses, and an inference-time directional refinement (§4.3). It is trained on the released training split and evaluated under the released protocol, so it is a repro-ducible reference point for future systems. 5. A validity analysis of the protocol. The ground-truth reference run exposes complementary limitations of the objective metrics. RePointer exceeds captured motion on spatial grounding, showing that evaluator-aligned geometric precision can exceed that exhibited by human pointing, while its comparison with GT on combined grounding is sensitive to the temporal scor-ing parameters. Together with the perceptual study, these results motivate reporting spatial, temporal, and naturalness measures separately rather than relying on a single ranking score (§6). 

2. Related Work 

The most relevant prior work comprises spatially grounded gesture generation models and datasets that pair commu-nicative motion with explicit scene referents. 

2.1. Spatially Grounded Gesture Generation 

Data-driven co-speech gesture generation has progressed from deterministic regression to probabilistic generative se-quence models, with diffusion [6, 7] and flow-matching [8] models now producing convincing co-speech motion. Tracking this progress has been supported by the GE-NEA Challenges [2], which fixed a shared corpus and a large-scale crowdsourced protocol for human-likeness and speech appropriateness, and by objective measures such as Fréchet Gesture Distance [1] and beat alignment that score the motion distribution and its relation to audio. These models primarily focus on modelling prosodic and seman-tic alignment rather than grounding gestures in the sur-rounding scene. Human–scene and human–object motion generation instead grounds motion through contact and af-fordance [9–14], but does not address communicative ref-erence. Work combining these requirements remains limited. A physics-based pointing agent jointly optimises motion imi-tation and referential accuracy [15]; subsequent work fine-tunes OmniControl [16] for spatially conditioned point-ing [17]; and SIG-Chat [18] conditions conversational ges-ture generation on audio, text and 3D target trajectories. These approaches use different datasets and evaluation pro-tocols, preventing direct comparison. Our benchmark pro-vides a common corpus, task and evaluation protocol. 

2.2. Referential Gesture Datasets 

Evaluating referential gesture requires motion paired with scene geometry and an intended referent. General-purpose embodied datasets provide related signals: Nymeria [19] combines full-body motion with egocentric video, gaze and 3D geometry, while Embody3D [20] additionally captures 2dialogue. Synthetic datasets such as ScanERU [21] and Ges3ViG [22] provide explicit pointing and referent geom-etry, but construct pointing instances rather than capture their natural occurrence in conversation. Our benchmark builds on MM-Conv [3]: 6.7 h of dyadic VR dialogue in five AI2-THOR rooms, in which an instruction-giver describes the scene to a partner across short role-play scenarios while full-body motion, speech with word-level alignment, gaze, and the ground-truth scene graph are captured. Each of its 4,211 referring ex-pressions is manually linked to its referent object; nearly half (49%) are pronominal (“it”, “that one”), so in many cases non-verbal and contextual cues are needed to resolve the referent. 

3. Corpus and Annotations 

MM-Conv [3] is the primary data source: 6.7 h of dyadic VR interaction across five AI2-THOR [23] rooms (Fig. 2), with SMPL-X motion capture at 30 fps, word-level tran-scripts, and per-room scene graphs (42–61 objects). Each referring expression is labelled with its ground-truth ref-erent. Capture is dyadic: the addressee’s pose is also recorded throughout. We add and release a per-reference pointing annotation layer. A random-forest classifier using arm-kinematic, scene-context and speech-timing features guided manual review; human-assigned gesture tiers deter-mined inclusion. The released benchmark contains 1,842 MM-Conv pointing clips with dominant-hand annotations, divided into training and test splits as shown in Table 1. Reference apex frames were estimated automatically from motion kinematics; manual review established gesture pres-ence rather than apex timing. This motivates evaluation in continuous dialogue rather than assuming a gesture at every reference. 

SGS-HSI [24] adds 1,138 isolated single-target point-ing clips: clean geometry, one target coordinate and apex frame per clip, no scene graph. Motion is real capture, aug-mented and retargeted to the MM-Conv actors. Speech is synthesised and matched to the gestures, so phrase–gesture alignment is constructed rather than naturally occurring. 4. Benchmark 4.1. Task Formulation 

Systems receive, per test item: audio with a word-level tran-script; a 3D target coordinate in pelvis-centred and world frames; the dominant pointing hand; a static SMPL-X body shape; and the room’s scene graph (per-object category, bounding-box centre, extent). They must generate SMPL-X motion containing a pointing gesture directed at the tar-get. Ground-truth motion, apex frame, referent identity, and evaluator labels are withheld, so the system decides for itself when the gesture peak occurs. Since the target coordinate is supplied, the scene graph is optional. It can be used for reasoning about distractors or occlusion, or ig-nored. Supplying the coordinate isolates gesture generation from referent resolution; the harder variant that withholds it will be the focus of future benchmark releases. 

4.2. Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluate two complementary aspects of generated ges-tures: motion quality and referential grounding. Standard motion-quality metrics characterise the motion distribu-tion, dynamics, and audio–motion relation, but do not measure whether a gesture successfully refers to the in-tended object. We therefore evaluate referential grounding through two components: temporal alignment (Score 𝑎 ), measuring the timing of the detected gesture apex relative to the reference apex, and spatial grounding (Score 𝑏 ), mea-suring whether the gesture indicates the intended 3D target. Their product gives the combined spatio-temporal ground-ing score (STG). All grounding scores are computed auto-matically from generated SMPL-X motion and are bounded in [0, 1]; systems provide no apex frame or other evaluation metadata. 

Temporal alignment (Score 𝑎 ). As with the tempo-ral parameters, 𝜎 is a benchmark design parameter rather than an empirically estimated perceptual threshold. 

Combined grounding (STG). STG = Score 𝑎 ×Score 𝑏 .The product penalises errors in either temporal or spatial grounding, while reporting the two factors separately ex-poses the source of a low combined score. 

Motion quality. We report motion quality separately from grounding using EMAGE [5] Fréchet Gesture Dis-tance (FGD), average jerk, L1 velocity diversity, and beat alignment. These metrics characterise complementary dis-tributional, kinematic, and audio–motion properties of gen-erated motion; they do not measure whether a gesture suc-cessfully refers to the intended target. FGD is computed against the training-reference feature distribution.

5. Evaluation 

We evaluate the baseline, one independently developed sys-tem, and captured motion. 

5.1. RePointer 

RePointer [4] is a spatially conditioned retrieve–generate– score–select system. It first retrieves a full-body SMPL-X anchor from a memory of natural pointing clips, indexed by target geometry, pointing hand, predicted gesture tim-ing, and apex ratio, with donors from the held-out fold excluded. Realisation operators then adapt the anchor: an apex-preserving temporal warp that resamples pre- and post-apex frames separately to the query timing; a body-shape-aware target-ray inverse kinematics step minimising

Root translation, lower body, and the non-active arm are preserved from the anchor unchanged, so aiming is achieved by arm articulation alone. Cross-fitted scorers rank the anchor against generated hypotheses using only public test-time inputs and motion-derived fea-tures, and a selector returns one candidate unchanged. No test ground-truth motion, apex, object identity, or evalua-tor labels are used. Eq. (12) makes forearm–target align-ment an explicit inference-time objective—the same quan-tity Score 𝑏 reads.

In MM-Conv-Flow, temporal placement is left to the speech-conditioned generative backbone; the larger timing error shows that this model does not consistently place the referential apex precisely under the benchmark’s detector-based criterion. This advantage is robust to the temporal metric design: RePointer scores higher across all 806 tested combinations of 𝜏 and 𝜎 𝑡 (Appendix A). GT obtains Score 𝑎 =0.941; because the reference apex is kinematically defined and the same detector is applied to captured motion, we treat this value as a detector/reference diagnostic rather than a human timing baseline. 

Combined Spatio-Temporal Grounding. STG is the per-clip product Score 𝑎 ×Score 𝑏 , averaged over clips. Un-der the primary temporal parameters, RePointer and GT obtain nearly identical mean STG (0.719 and 0.720, re-spectively), while MM-Conv-Flow obtains 0.361. Re-Pointer therefore substantially exceeds MM-Conv-Flow on the combined measure. In contrast, the RePointer–GT or-dering changes with the temporal tolerance and decay pa-rameters (Appendix A). We therefore draw no conclusion about RePointer relative to captured motion from STG and report the temporal and spatial factors separately. 

5.2.2 Motion Quality. 

RePointer obtains the lowest EMAGE FGD (2.364), com-pared with MM-Conv-Flow (4.551) and held-out GT (4.738), using the same training reference. This advan-tage should be interpreted in light of its retrieval-based construction: RePointer initialises from human motion in the retrieval base, which overlaps the FGD reference distri-bution, and preserves the root, lower body, and non-active arm. Its average jerk (0.543) lies between MM-Conv-Flow (0.687) and GT (0.261). RePointer has substantially higher L1 velocity diversity than both MM-Conv-Flow and GT (0.760 vs. 0.178 and 0.246, respectively), while its beat alignment is moderately higher than both (0.380 vs. 0.272 and 0.318). Despite these differences, RePointer is not rated more natural than MM-Conv-Flow (MOS 2.95 vs. 3.11, 𝑝 = .071; §5.3). Thus, its lower FGD and higher di-versity and beat-alignment scores do not establish greater perceived naturalness; the metrics capture different proper-ties of motion. 

5.3. Perceptual Evaluation 

Objective grounding scores do not capture whether gen-erated motion looks human-like. We ran a crowdsourced MOS study to complement the spatio-temporal metrics. 

5.3.1 Protocol. 

We sampled 23 referring-expression clips from the held-out test room and rendered each under three conditions: ground-truth motion capture (GT), MM-Conv-Flow, and RePointer. All conditions use the same speech audio, avatar, room, and camera; only body motion varies. Con-ditions were counterbalanced with a cyclic Latin square: participants in rotation 𝑔 saw clip 𝑖 in condition 𝑐 (𝑖 +𝑔 ) mod 3 ,so each participant rated every clip exactly once and, across three rotations, every clip ×condition cell was covered. Trial order was randomised with no more than two consecutive trials from the same condition. Participants were recruited on Prolific (English first lan-guage, desktop) and rated each clip on a five-point scale (1 = very unnatural, 5 = very natural) after watching it in full; playback controls were disabled, and the rating was gated on ≥ 85% of the clip having been played. Each ses-sion contained 23 stimulus trials plus two attention checks. Sessions took a median of 5.5 minutes and were paid £1.25.

6. Validity Analysis 

The benchmark exposes two limitations that are important when interpreting referential-gesture metrics. 

Geometric grounding is not a proxy for perceived nat-uralness. RePointer achieves Score 𝑏 =0.905, above cap-tured motion (0.767), by explicitly optimising forearm– target geometry at inference time. Score 𝑏 therefore rewards geometric precision beyond that exhibited by the captured gestures, but it does not establish that this precision is com-municatively useful: human pointing is not aimed along the elbow–wrist ray, and observers may resolve referents from coarser cues. At the same time, RePointer is not rated more natural than MM-Conv-Flow, so its geometric advantage does not transfer to perceived motion quality. Geometric grounding and human-like motion quality must therefore be evaluated separately, and neither substitutes for a direct test of communicative success. A qualitative failure mode of the retrieval-based system is that the retrieved motion prior can carry gesture semantics unrelated to the current utterance. For example, we ob-served a case (Appendix E, clip 298) in which the retrieved anchor contained an iconic object-interaction-like motion from the training corpus, while the subsequent geometric adaptation redirected the arm toward the correct referent. Such cases can obtain good spatial grounding despite se-mantically inappropriate residual motion, illustrating that Score 𝑏 evaluates target-directed geometry rather than the semantic appropriateness of the complete gesture. 

Combined grounding depends on temporal metric de-sign. Under the primary temporal parameters, RePointer and GT have nearly identical STG (0.719 and 0.720), but their ordering changes as the temporal tolerance and decay are varied (Appendix A). In contrast, RePointer remains above MM-Conv-Flow across all tested temporal parame-terisations. Thus the system comparison is robust, whereas an aggregate RePointer–GT comparison is not. Reporting Score 𝑎 and Score 𝑏 separately, together with raw timing error, makes the source of an STG result explicit. 

7. Limitations 

Data. The benchmark rests on five rooms of one VR cor-pus, with a single held-out room supplying all 100 test clips. SGS-HSI extends the dataset with clean single-target pointing, but its speech is synthesised and phrase–gesture alignment is constructed, so it does not extend the naturalis-tic referential domain. Larger referential corpora spanning more scenes (beyond indoor household rooms) and more speakers would benefit the benchmark. 

Task. Supplying the target isolates gesture generation from reference resolution. Future variants could require joint generation of language, speech, and motion as a benchmark task for referential communication in embodied agents. 

Protocol. Score 𝑏 measures target-directed forearm ge-ometry. The retrieval-based system shows that this quan-tity can be optimised directly at inference time, so Score 𝑏 

should be read as a geometric precision measure, not as evidence of communicative success. The spatial score also depends on estimated elbow and wrist positions; sensitivity to pose-estimation or calibration errors remains unquanti-fied. Score 𝑎 likewise depends on a frozen apex detector and a fixed tolerance that are benchmark design choices rather than perceptual thresholds. 

Perceptual evaluation. The MOS study measures natu-ralness only. Whether observers can identify the intended referent from generated motion—the communicative ques-tion the benchmark ultimately targets—is not tested here. The current test set is not suited to such a study: items 8were sampled for speaker balance and target diversity, not for controlled distractor structure, so many targets have no comparably placed alternatives and a forced-choice task would be trivial on some items and ill-posed on others. A referent identification study requires a dedicated item set in which each target has matched distractors at controlled angular separations, so that chance level and difficulty are known. We consider this a necessary complement to Score 𝑏 

and plan it as the next benchmark release. 

8. Discussion Neither system learns end-to-end spatial grounding. 

Both evaluated systems use the supplied target coordinate to improve spatial grounding at inference time. MM-Conv-Flow’s directional refinement (§4.3) redirects an arm ex-tension already produced by the generator, whereas Re-Pointer’s IK solver directly optimises forearm–target align-ment. Their different Score 𝑏 values therefore reflect, in part, the strength and constraints of these geometric opera-tors rather than solely learned scene-aware gesture genera-tion. A harder future benchmark should withhold the target coordinate and require systems to infer the referent from the scene and utterance. 

Temporal evaluation requires an explicit tolerance. 

Unlike spatial grounding, gesture timing has no natural exact-frame target: the reference provides one kinemati-cally defined apex, while communicatively acceptable tim-ing may span an interval. We therefore use a fixed toler-ance for all clips and report raw timing errors alongside Score 𝑎 . The RePointer–MM-Conv-Flow ordering is stable across a broad sensitivity sweep, but absolute scores and the RePointer–GT STG comparison are not. The tolerance should consequently be understood as part of the bench-mark definition rather than as an empirically established perceptual threshold. 

Geometric grounding and naturalness are complemen-tary. RePointer substantially exceeds MM-Conv-Flow on spatial grounding, but the naturalness study found no statis-tically significant difference between the two generated sys-tems. Conversely, distributional motion metrics also dis-agree with perception: RePointer obtains the lowest FGD and highest velocity diversity and beat alignment with-out a corresponding MOS advantage. No single objective score therefore captures referential gesture quality. Future benchmarks should retain separate measures of geomet-ric grounding, temporal alignment, and perceptual motion quality. 

9. Conclusion 

We introduced a benchmark for spatially grounded ges-ture generation: pointing-annotated clips from a naturalis-tic conversational dataset with 3D referents, a task in which systems must determine when and where to point within continuous co-speech motion, and a protocol that reports temporal alignment, spatial grounding, and perceived natu-ralness separately. Evaluating a flow-matching baseline, an independently developed retrieval-based system, and cap-tured human motion shows why this separation matters: the retrieval-based system exceeds captured motion on spa-tial grounding and distributional motion metrics without being rated more natural. Geometric precision, timing, and perceived motion quality are therefore distinct task di-mensions, and no single score captures referential gesture quality. The released data, baseline, and scorer provide a fixed reference point for future work on referential commu-nication in embodied agents; a referent-identification study on a dedicated distractor-controlled target set is the next step toward measuring communicative success directly.

B. Apex Detector 

The frozen apex detector is a logistic-regression classifier (𝐶 = 1, balanced class weights), trained on recordings dis-joint from evaluation. Frames within ±3 frames of the au-tomatically estimated reference apex are labelled positive; all other frames are negative. Each frame is represented by 12 values obtained through maximum and mean pooling of six kinematic features over a ±10-frame window. The ±3-frame supervision window is distinct from the scoring tol-erance 𝜏 = 10 frames. The sensitivity analysis above varies the scoring parameters while keeping detector predictions fixed; it does not independently validate apex detection.
