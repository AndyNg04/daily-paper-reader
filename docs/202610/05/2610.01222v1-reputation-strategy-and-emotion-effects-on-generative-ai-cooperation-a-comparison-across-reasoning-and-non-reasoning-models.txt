Abstract
As generative AI (Gen AI) systems take on increasingly autonomous roles in economically and so-
cially consequential interactions, understanding their propensity to cooperate – and the signals that shape
this propensity – has become essential. We examine cooperative behavior in frontier Gen AI models us-
ing the iterated prisoner’s dilemma, manipulating counterpart reputation (positive, unknown, negative),
strategy (extortion vs. generosity), and non-verbal emotional signaling (facial expressions conveying
competitive or cooperative appraisals). In a first study with non-reasoning models (Claude 3.5, Gem-
ini 2.0 Flash, GPT-4o), cooperation was systematically shaped by all three factors, paralleling patterns
long documented in human behavioral research, though models varied substantially in how heavily each
factor was weighted. A second study with reasoning models (Claude 4.6, Gemini 3, GPT-5.2) revealed
a more concentrated reliance on strategy and reputation, a near-elimination of the Potemkin effect ob-
served in non-reasoning models (evidenced by near-uniform cooperation in a diagnostic harmony game),
and a more conditional role for emotion consistent with a hierarchical cue-weighting strategy rather than
a simple loss of social sensitivity. Reasoning models also showed heterogeneous end-game behavior,
ranging from sustained cooperation to systematic last-round defection effect, revealing model-specific
exploitability profiles with direct practical relevance for deployment in negotiation and other multi-round
interactions. Together, these findings characterize Gen AI models as increasingly sophisticated, though
heterogeneous, social actors, and underscore the practical value of developing standardized coopera-
tion benchmarks to inform the responsible deployment of Gen AI in interactive, socially consequential
settings.

Introduction
Cooperation is a pervasive feature of human interaction: when interests conflict, exploiting others can yield
immediate personal gains, yet mutual cooperation frequently emerges and provides substantial benefits to
both individuals and societies Axelrod [1984], Rand and Nowak [2013], Trivers [1971]. As artificial intel-
ligence (AI) becomes increasingly integrated into society, these sustained patterns of cooperation may be
reshaped or even fundamentally disrupted. This challenge is particularly salient with the emergence of Gen-
erative AI (Gen AI) systems, which exhibit powerful emergent capabilities Bubeck et al. [2023], including

sophisticated social reasoning Lightman et al. [2023], Mann et al. [2020], Schick et al. [2023], Wei et al.
[2023], Yao et al. [2023]. Recognizing the growing societal role of these systems, governments and inter-
national bodies have called for ensuring these systems reflect prosocial values Parliament [2024], UNESCO
[2021], NIST [2023], Wu et al. [2020]. Although designing AI that promotes cooperation has been studied
for some time de Melo and Terada [2019], de Melo et al. [2023], Paiva et al. [2017], Gen AI introduces new
challenges due to its scale, complexity and emergent behavior Bommasani et al. [2021]. In this article, we
study cooperative behavior in frontier Gen AI models. As these systems become increasingly pervasive, it is
essential to understand how they navigate social dilemmas Kollock [1998], characterize their heterogeneous
cooperation patterns, and assess their propensity to promote, or undermine, social welfare.
Several mechanisms underlie the emergence of cooperation Axelrod [1984], Rand and Nowak [2013],
Sigmund [2006], Trivers [1971]. Here, we focus on three: direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, and non-
verbal signalling (emotion expression). In direct reciprocity, cooperation emerges when individuals engage
in repeated interaction, thus introducing an opportunity to punish wrongdoing or return a favor (“you scratch
my back, I scratch yours”) Trivers [1971]. Conditional strategies, such as tit-for-tat and the zero-determinant
generosity strategies (i.e., a repeated-game strategy that ensures the opponent always earns at least as much
as the strategist), have been shown to be successful in encouraging cooperation among individuals Hilbe
et al. [2013], Press and Dyson [2012], Stewart and Plotkin [2013].
However, cooperation can also emerge in the absence of any prior interaction via indirect reciprocity Alexan-
der [1987], Bicchieri [2005], Hitoshi et al. [2020], Kandori [1992], Leimar and Hammerstein [2001], Milin-
ski [2016], Nowak and Sigmund [1998, 2005], Ohtsuki and Iwasa [2004], Panchanathan and Boyd [2003]
wherein an individual’s reputation is communicated to others through informal transmission (e.g. gossip)
Dunbar and Dunbar [1998], Giardini and Conte [2012], Giardini and Vilone [2016], Sommerfeld et al.
[2007] or formal reputation mechanisms Milinski et al. [2002], Semmann et al. [2004], Wu et al. [2016].
When such reputational information is available and reliable, cooperation among strangers can emerge even
in one-shot interactions. Decades of behavioral studies show that direct and indirect reciprocity reliably
promotes cooperation among humans; yet, it remains less understood in Gen AI models.
Thirdly, a growing body of experimental evidence shows that emotion expressions can shape others’
decision making de Melo and Terada [2019, 2020], de Melo et al. [2014a, 2023], Fonseca et al. [2025],
Keltner and Lerner [2010], Lerner et al. [2015], Scherer and Moors [2019], van Kleef et al. [2010]. Recent
studies show that facial displays of emotion can promote or hinder cooperation, according to the context in
which they are shown de Melo and Terada [2019, 2020], de Melo et al. [2014a], van Kleef et al. [2010]. This
evidence is in line with the argument that emotion expressions serve important social functions, including
communicating one’s beliefs, desires, and intentions to others Keltner and Haidt [1999], Keltner and Lerner
[2010], Morris and Keltner [2000]. Emotions are argued to arise from appraisals of ongoing events relative
to an individual’s specific goals Frijda [1986], Scherer and Moors [2019]; thus, different emotions can be
elicited for the same event, depending on how it is appraised. Emotions, subsequently, result in concomitant
physiological reactions, action tendencies, and expressions. Reciprocally, experimental evidence shows that
people are able to infer others’ mental states, including intentions, from emotion displays by reversing this
appraisal process de Melo et al. [2014a], Hareli and Hess [2010], Parkinson and Simons [2009]. In fact,
emotion expressions have been argued to be an important signal to detect cooperators Frank [2004]. More
recently, studies also show that emotion expressions influence social norms defining how reputations are
updated, revealing a long-term effect of emotion on cooperation de Melo et al. [2023]. Here we present,
to the best of our knowledge, the first study on the effect of emotion expressions on cooperation in Gen AI
models.
Gen AI systems are trained on vast corpora of text to produce human-like language outputs through
next-token prediction Brown et al. [2020], Zhao et al. [2023]. In recent years, Gen AI has advanced
rapidly in their capacity for complex reasoning, social inference, and strategic decision-making, making
them increasingly relevant as objects of behavioral research Bubeck et al. [2023], Horton et al. [2023].

A theoretically important distinction in this literature concerns the cognitive architecture underlying model
inference. Drawing on Kahneman’s (2011) dual-process framework Kahneman [2011], though only as a
metaphor, researchers have differentiated between “System 1” and “System 2” modes of processing in Gen
AI models Hagendorff et al. [2023], Zhang et al. [2026]: System 1 processing refers to the model’s default
inference mode — a single forward pass through the network that generates responses rapidly and without
deliberate intermediate reasoning; System 2 processing, by contrast, involves extended chain-of-thought
reasoning prior to output generation, allocating additional computational resources at inference time to
support more deliberate, multi-step problem solving Wei et al. [2023], Snell et al. [2024]. This distinction
maps meaningfully onto current model generations. Earlier models — including GPT-4o OpenAI [2024],
Gemini 2 Flash Google [2025a], and Claude 3.5 Anthropic [2024] — operate primarily in a System 1 mode,
generating responses through rapid, single-pass inference without access to extended reasoning mechanisms.
More recent models — including GPT-5.2 OpenAI [2025], Gemini 3 Google [2025b], and Claude 4.6
Anthropic [2025] — integrate System 2 capabilities natively, supporting configurable extended thinking
that allows the model to engage in structured internal deliberation before producing a response. To be fair,
these models differ in more than the presence of extended thinking, with other meaningful changes including
training data, RLHF procedures, scale, and safety tuning. For convenience and drawing on terms commonly
used by researchers and practitioners to refer to the models pre- and post-generational shift, we refer to the
former as non-reasoning models and the latter as reasoning models. In this article, we present two studies
that look into this generational contrast in the context of cooperative behavior in a canonical experimental
economic game, the Prisoner’s Dilemma (Fig. 1).
The prisoner’s dilemma is an economic game that has been widely used to study emergence of cooper-
ation in humans Rapoport and Chammah [1965]. In this game, two players make a simultaneous decision,
without communication, to either cooperate or defect. If they both cooperate, they each receive a payoff
R. If they both defect, they receive a payoff P that is lower than R. However, if one cooperates and the
other defects, the defector earns the highest possible reward (T) and the cooperator the lowest (S), i.e.,
T > R > P > S. In the iterated version, players engage in multiple rounds of the game with the same
counterpart. If the number of rounds is finite, the rational prediction is that players should always defect von
Neumann and Morgenstern [1944]; however, in practice, people often cooperate Rand and Nowak [2013].
Fig. 1B shows the payoff matrix used in the experiment. Here, Gen AI models engaged in the iterated
version of this dilemma for a total of 20 rounds; before proceeding to the next round, the models learnt the
outcome for the previous round.

Methods
The experimental design was based on a prior study conducted with human participants de Melo et al. [2023]
and follows several behavioral science best practices Hertwig and Ortmann [2001], Pruitt and Kimmel
[1977], in line with prior calls on the value of drawing from human-subject experiments when studying Gen
AI Binz and Schulz [2023], Moment`e et al. [2025], Fontana et al. [2025], Zhou et al. [2025]. Our methods,
which generally sought to increase experimental control and reduce bias, include designing a cover story for
the task, ensuring task comprehension, leveraging ecologically valid experimental stimuli (e.g., virtual agent
facial expressions of emotion de Melo et al. [2014b,b]), and creating appropriate conditions of anonymity.
All prompts given to the Gen AI models followed IRB-approved methods and instructions given to human
participants in the prior human-subjects study de Melo et al. [2023]. Further details are provided in the
Supplementary Information (SI), Section A.
Prior to engaging in the task, Gen AI models were informed about the counterpart’s reputation, which
could be negative, unknown, or positive (Fig. 1C). These reputations were presented as having been calcu-
lated from a prior questionnaire and were characterized as follows: cooperative, tend to pursue an outcome

The counterparts made decisions consistent with a cooperative or competitive intention. To accomplish
this, we relied on two zero-determinant strategies Hilbe et al. [2018], Press and Dyson [2012], Stewart and
Plotkin [2013] (Fig. 1D): extortion, which unilaterally enforces a linear relationship between the players’
payoffs, while ensuring the counterpart cannot earn more than the extortionist by exploiting cooperation
often while only cooperating enough to encourage the counterpart to cooperate; generosity, which always
rewards cooperation while showing lenience towards defection.
Regarding the emotion expression manipulation, counterparts were represented by virtual faces that
could express emotion, using typical facial configurations that were validated in prior work de Melo et al.
[2014a] (see the SI, Section A.2, for further details on the benefits and ecological validity of using virtual
faces for this type of manipulation). After the outcome of each round, a picture of the emotion expression
was shown to the Gen AI model, thus requiring it to use its visual interpretation capability to make sense
of the image – no text caption or translation were provided for the images. Building on prior work, the
displayed expressions were appraisals of the preceding round’s outcome rather than generic positive or
negative displays (Fig. 1E). Cooperative counterparts displayed joy following mutual cooperation and regret
after exploiting the Gen AI model, whereas competitive counterparts displayed joy after exploiting the Gen
AI model and regret following mutual cooperation (as if they had missed an opportunity to exploit). In
both of these expressive conditions, the counterpart displayed anger after the model exploited it (i.e., the
model defected on a cooperating counterpart), signalling retaliation, and a neutral expression after mutual
defection; the neutral condition always displayed the neutral expression.

Cooperation in Non-Reasoning Models
In the first experiment, the aforementioned experimental design was run with three non-reasoning fron-
tier models – Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 Anthropic [2024], Google’s Gemini 2.0 Flash Google [2025a] and
OpenAI’s GPT 4o OpenAI [2024] (but see the SI, Section B.5, for results with open-source models). The
experiment followed a 3×2×3 between-participants design: reputation (negative vs. unknown vs. positive)
× strategy (extortion vs. generosity) × emotion (competitive vs. neutral vs. cooperative). Each Gen AI
model engaged in 39 runs (of 20 rounds each) per condition, for a total of 702 runs per model. This number
of runs was comparable to the sample size for the human data de Melo et al. [2023]. Before each run, the
model was reset and we always used the default parameters for the models. Additionally, we ran five runs
per condition per model, where we asked the model to provide an explanation for its decisions after each
round. This allowed us to get further insight on the models’ rationale for the decisions. Given prior evidence
that encouraging Gen AI models to self-reflect or explain its behavior can change the behavior Renze and
Guven [2024], we kept this set of rounds separate from the main set of rounds, for which we did not request
an explanation. See the SI for further details on the default parameters for each model, the prompts, costs,
and other experimental methods.

pareto-efficient Nash equilibrium. This contrast is diagnostically powerful: a model that truly understands

explanations (Fig. 5) it is clear that the models were able to process all information, including from facial

all models cooperating 100% of the time independently of the experimental condition (see further details,
including a variation where thinking depth was lowered, in the SI, Section. B.4).
In the last round, interestingly, reasoning models showed heterogeneous behavior (Fig. 4-B). In general,
models defected in the last round when faced with extortionist others; however, there were marked differ-

5.2 always defected, revealing a clear terminal opportunism effect Embrey et al. [2018], Lave [1965], Nor-
mann and Wallace [2012]; Gemini 3, by contrast, showed conditional behavior, always cooperating when
the reputation was positive but, when the reputation was negative, cooperating more when the counterpart
showed cooperative rather than competitive emotion.
5
Discussion
Across two studies spanning multiple model families and generations, we find that Gen AI models’ coopera-
tive behavior in the iterated prisoner’s dilemma is systematically shaped by reputation, direct reciprocity (via
counterpart strategy), and non-verbal emotional signaling. Models cooperated more with counterparts who
had a positive reputation, who played a generous rather than extortionist strategy, and who displayed posi-
tive rather than negative emotion. This pattern echoes decades of human behavioral research on (direct and
indirect) reciprocity Axelrod [1984], Rand and Nowak [2013], Sigmund [2006], Trivers [1971], Alexander
[1987], Bicchieri [2005], Hitoshi et al. [2020], Kandori [1992], Leimar and Hammerstein [2001], Milinski
[2016], Nowak and Sigmund [1998, 2005], Ohtsuki and Iwasa [2004], Panchanathan and Boyd [2003] and
emotion’s role in shaping cooperative decisions de Melo and Terada [2019, 2020], de Melo et al. [2014a,
2023], Fonseca et al. [2025], Keltner and Lerner [2010], Lerner et al. [2015], Scherer and Moors [2019],
van Kleef et al. [2010], and is consistent with prior findings in humans de Melo et al. [2023]. We emphasize
that this correspondence is best understood as a point of reference rather than a benchmark of success: our
aim is not to establish that Gen AI models cooperate the way humans do, but to characterize how these
systems navigate social dilemmas in their own right, using the extensive human literature as an interpretive
anchor for a domain that otherwise lacks one. Notably, models achieved this without any explicit training

At the same time, our results reveal substantial heterogeneity, both within and across model generations,
that cautions against treating “Gen AI” as a monolithic actor. Within a generation, models diverged sharply
in how heavily they weighted each factor: strategy dominated for Claude 3.5 and GPT-4o but far less so for
Gemini 2.0 Flash, while GPT-4o was comparatively more driven by reputation than either of its contempo-
raries. This heterogeneity is consequential: it means a counterpart’s optimal way of eliciting cooperation
from a Gen AI system — for instance, whether to invest primarily in reputation-building or in early recip-
rocal generosity — would differ by model. Characterizing these model-specific “cooperation profiles” is
therefore not a peripheral detail but a central practical requirement for anyone deploying these systems in
interactive economic contexts.
Across generations, reasoning models showed more homogeneous overall cooperation rates but a more
concentrated reliance on strategy, alongside a more conditional role for emotion. In particular, there was
no main effect of emotion for reasoning models overall, in contrast to non-reasoning models. Rather than
interpreting this as reasoning models simply becoming less socially attuned, the findings (including in the
SI) suggest a more precise account: emotion maintained its effect on expectations of cooperation (SI, Sec-
tion. B.2, retained a conditional effect specifically when other signals were incongruent or uninformative
(e.g., a counterpart with a negative reputation who nonetheless behaved generously) (SI, Section. B.1, and
Gemini 3 in particular showed continued sensitivity to emotion especially when reputation was negative.
This pattern is consistent with a cue-weighting strategy in which lower-validity or redundant signals are
down-weighted when higher-validity cues already resolve the decision, and are recruited when those cues
conflict or are absent – a pattern with parallels in the human judgment-and-decision-making literature on cue
redundancy Schmitt and Dudycha [1975], Singh et al. [2024], Jones and Pearce [2015]. Under this reading,
the disappearance of a main effect of emotion in reasoning models may reflect more sophisticated, context-
11


sensitive cue integration, which is further reflected in the reasoning models’ detailed verbal explanations for
its decisions.
Reasoning models also showed a marked reduction in the Potemkin effect Mancoridis et al. [2023]
observed in non-reasoning models, cooperating close to uniformly in the harmony game regardless of ex-
perimental condition. This is preliminary evidence that reasoning models are better able to discriminate
between social-dilemma structures that superficially resemble one another but differ in their underlying
game-theoretic properties. The harmony game is, nevertheless, an initial diagnostic tool. So, this result
should be read as an initial, encouraging signal rather than conclusive evidence of deep conceptual under-
standing; harder tests involving multiple equilibria or weakly dominant strategies should be conducted in
future work to establish the boundaries of this capability.
These findings carry practical implications beyond scientific characterization. As Gen AI systems are
increasingly deployed as autonomous or semi-autonomous agents in negotiation, procurement, and other
economically consequential interactions, understanding their propensity to cooperate – and the specific sig-
nals that move that propensity – becomes a matter of practical risk assessment, not merely academic interest.
Our finding that end-game behavior diverges sharply across reasoning models (Claude 4.6 sustaining coop-
eration with generous counterparts through the final round, GPT-5.2 reliably defecting, Gemini 3 behaving
conditionally) illustrates a concrete, model-specific exploitability profile: a counterparty aware of which
model underlies an agent could time defection accordingly. This argues for the development of standardized
cooperation benchmarks – analogous to existing safety and capability benchmarks – that characterize how
successive model generations and different model families navigate social dilemmas under reputation, reci-
procity, and affective manipulations. Such benchmarks would offer practical value to developers selecting
or fine-tuning models for interactive deployment, and could support the broader governance aims already
articulated by regulatory and standards bodies calling for AI systems that reflect prosocial values Parliament
[2024], UNESCO [2021], NIST [2023], Wu et al. [2020].
Limitations
The work has limitations that offer opportunities for valuable future work. First, the “reasoning” versus
“non-reasoning” grouping, while reflecting a distinction now in common use among researchers and prac-
titioners, is confounded with other differences between model generations, including training data, RLHF
procedures, scale, and safety tuning. Whereas extended reasoning may be the more relevant factor and parsi-
monious explanation, supplementary analyses in which reasoning models’ extended-thinking budgets were
reduced showed that these models still outperformed the prior generation on the harmony game, suggesting
the observed differences are not purely a function of additional deliberation at inference time on a given
trial, and may partly reflect capabilities acquired during training. This is informative but not conclusive:
a within-model manipulation that directly toggles extended thinking on a fixed model checkpoint, ideally
with a graded range of reasoning budgets, would be needed to more cleanly isolate the causal contribution
of inference-time reasoning from other concurrent changes across generations.
Second, our counterparts were scripted to follow fixed zero-determinant strategies and did not adapt to
the Gen AI models’ behavior, whereas real-world counterparts — whether human or AI — often adjust their
own strategy dynamically. Future work should examine Gen AI cooperation against adaptive or learning
opponents, including other Gen AI agents, to assess whether the patterns reported here generalize to more
realistic, non-stationary strategic environments.
Third, our experimental materials, cover story, and manipulations were derived from a single prior
human-subjects study [15] conducted with a particular participant population and set of framings; the
generalizability of our findings to other cultural contexts, task framings, or languages remains an open
question, particularly given known cross-cultural variation in cooperative behavior and emotion display
norms de Melo and Terada [2019].
12


Finally, our reliance on models’ verbal explanations to interpret their decision processes carries the
usual caveat that such explanations may not faithfully reflect the underlying computation and could partly
constitute post-hoc rationalization rather than a transparent window into the model’s actual decision process;
this is a general limitation of interpretability approaches based on model self-report.
Coda
Notwithstanding these limitations, our results offer an encouraging picture. Gen AI models – even with-
out additional explicit training for this purpose – already display a capacity to track reputation, reciprocate
strategically, and interpret non-verbal social signals in ways that shape cooperative outcomes, and this ca-
pacity appears to be strengthening, not weakening, as models gain more sophisticated reasoning abilities.
As these systems take on a growing role in mediating economic and social interactions, both between hu-
mans and AI and among AI systems themselves, the tools of behavioral science offer a concrete, tractable
way to measure and ultimately steer these tendencies over time. Continued characterization of Gen AI’s
cooperative dispositions – alongside deliberate benchmark development and governance efforts already un-
derway Parliament [2024], UNESCO [2021], NIST [2023], Wu et al. [2020] – offers a genuine opportunity
to help ensure that as these systems become further embedded in society, they act as a force that sustains
and strengthens human cooperation, rather than eroding it.

A
Supplementary Methods
In this appendix, we describe several best practices from the behavioral sciences literature Hertwig and Ort-
mann [2001], Pruitt and Kimmel [1977] implemented in our experimental methods to increase experimental
control and minimize confounding factors. We further present details on data analysis methods.
A.1
Task
To minimize bias (e.g., participants’ desire to be perceived as picking the cooperative choice in experimental
studies), the prisoner’s dilemma task was recast as an investment game Kiesler et al. [1996] and described as
follows to the Gen AI models: “You are going to play a two-player investment game. You can invest in one
of two projects: project green and project blue. However, how many points you get is contingent on which
project the other player invests in. So, if you both invest in project green, then each gets 5 points. If you
choose project green but the other player chooses project blue, then you get 2 and the other player gets 7
points. If, on the other hand, you choose project blue and the other player chooses project green, then you get
7 and the other player gets 2 points. A fourth possibility is that you both choose project blue, in which case
both get 3 points”. Thus, choosing project green corresponded to cooperation, and project blue to defection.
Finally, to assess task comprehension, prior to the actual task we presented the Gen AI models with four
types of comprehension quizzes (game rules, anonymity, reputation types, and the matching system). In all
quizzes, every model achieved 100% accuracy.
A.2
Conditions
Gen AI models were instructed that they would be engaging with a “participant from an online pool”;
however, to increase experimental control the responses from the counterparts were always scripted. Similar
methods have been implemented in prior IRB-approved human studies de Melo et al. [2014a], de Melo
and Terada [2020], de Melo et al. [2023], Hertwig and Ortmann [2001]. Furthermore, to have precise
control over the facial expression manipulation, counterparts were presumably engaging in the task via
virtual agents (or avatars). Virtual agents are anthropomorphic computer characters that have virtual bodies
and can express themselves through them in the same way people can Gratch et al. [2002]. Previous research
has shown that people can treat virtual agents like people Reeves and Nass [1996] and be socially influenced
by them Blascovich and McCall [2013]. Moreover, virtual agents have been argued to be an appropriate tool
for research in social psychology Blascovich et al. [2002]. The virtual face was of a young Caucasian adult
that was designed to have balanced gender features de Melo and Terada [2019]. The facial expressions were
created based on prototypical expressions for the corresponding emotions and were validated empirically in
prior studies de Melo et al. [2014a], de Melo and Terada [2020], de Melo et al. [2023].
A.3
Anonymity
In human studies, it can be important to create experimental conditions that preserve anonymity to minimize
any reputation effects, such as participants’ concern for retaliation due to decisions made in the experi-
ment Hertwig and Ortmann [2001]. To the extent that these concerns may have been learnt implicitly by
Gen AI models, we described the experiment to be fully anonymous and the counterparts were always
referred by anonymous names (e.g., “Anonymous43”).
A.4
Financial Incentive
To increase the ecological validity of economic games, researchers have noted the importance of having
appropriate financial incentives for participants that are contingent on their performance in the games Her-
19


twig and Ortmann [2001]. Accordingly, in the original study de Melo et al. [2023], participants were paid
for participation ($2.50), but also had the chance to earn extra money based on their performance as the
total amount of points earned in the task, summed across all rounds, was converted to lottery tickets for a
$30.00 lottery. To maintain consistency with the original study, the same instructions were also provided to
the Gen AI models; however, the lotteries had no actual consequence to the models per se (e.g., financial
rewards were not promised to be allocated towards compute resources for the model). The topic of defining
appropriate incentives to study Gen AI behavior, thus, remains an important avenue for future research.

Supplementary Results
B.1
Cooperation Under Incongruent Signals
The interplay of the different factors is particularly salient when looking at the conditions with one incongru-
ent signal (e.g., counterpart had negative reputation, played the extortion strategy, but showed cooperative
emotion). The results for non-reasoning models are shown in Fig. 6 and for reasoning models in Fig. 7.
When the reputation was incongruent, regarding non-reasoning models (Fig. 6-A), Claude 3.5 was par-
ticularly successful in overriding this initial expectation based on the counterpart’s strategy and emotion,
while GPT 4o was the least successful. For reasoning models (Fig. 7-A), on the other hand, all models were
able to recover from the initial reputation, thought GPT 5.2 was more conservative when engaging with
generous others with a negative reputation.
When the strategy was incongruent, for non-reasoning models (Fig. 6-B), Gemini 2.0 Flash tended to
cooperate ∼40% of the time, GPT 4o tended towards low cooperation, and Claude 3.5 punished extortion-
ists and rewarded generous counterparts. Reasoning models (Fig. 7-B), by contrast, were equally able to
adapt to the counterparts’ strategy.
Finally, when the emotion was incongruent, for non-reasoning models (Fig. 6-C), GPT 4o appeared to
mostly ignore the signal, whereas Gemini 2.0 Flash tended to move cooperation slightly in the direction of
the emotion signal. Reasoning models (Fig. 7-C), generally, ignored the emotion signal in this case, further
emphasizing that emotion had a weaker effect overall than the other factors.

model’s beliefs about the counterpart prior to the next round’s decision. We ran mixed model × reputation
× strategy × emotion × round analysis of variance (ANOVAs) on expectations of cooperation, splitting the
data by reasoning and non-reasoning models.

B.3
Belief Learning Analysis
To further characterize cooperation behavior, we drew on belief learning models Ho et al. [2007], Ido et al.
[2010], Cheung and Friedman [1997] to capture how players (Gen AI, in this case) form and update their
expectations about their counterparts’ behavior in a dynamic or repeated game. Unlike simple reinforcement
learning, which adjusts choices based only on past rewards, belief learning explicitly models how agents
form beliefs about others’ likely future actions and adapt their strategies accordingly. This framework is
particularly valuable in social interaction settings, where anticipating and responding to another’s behavior
22


Figure 10: Main effects of emotion expression on expectations of cooperation.
is central to strategic adaptation. In our case, rather than estimating belief-updating dynamics directly, we
follow the approach of Ioannou and Romero Ioannou and Romero [2014] who proposed a generalized
approach for belief learning in repeated games, including the prisoner’s dilemma. This approach consists
of describing players’ behavior in the constrained, yet expressive, space of finite automata (Fig. 11-A).
For instance, for the finite automaton corresponding to the well-known tit-for-tat behavioral rule Axelrod
[1984], the player always repeats the counterpart’s move in the previous round. Other relevant well-studied
rules include All-C (always cooperate), grim trigger (cooperate until the first defection) Friedman [1971],
and All-D (always defect). By mapping players’ strategies into this formal space, we can capture how
belief-driven adaptations unfold over time in structured social interactions.
We ran this analysis for every non-reasoning model for each experimental condition (Fig. 11-B). The
results reinforced that Gen AI models engaged in distinct behavior, with Claude 3.5 showing a pattern
for conditional cooperation (highly cooperative versus generous others, and tit-for-tat versus extortionists);
GPT 4o was characterized by more competitive rules; and, Gemini 2.0 falls somewhere in the middle, often
engaging in tit-for-tat.
When applied to reasoning models, under unknown and positive reputation conditions, belief learning
analysis revealed a more homogeneous pattern across the three models (Fig. 12). When facing extortionist
counterparts, the models generally adopted tit-for-tat, indicating a reciprocal response pattern. When facing
generous counterparts, they generally adopted All-C, indicating stable cooperation. One exception was GPT
5.2 under the condition of unknown reputation, a generous counterpart, and competitive emotion, where it
was the only model best fit by Grim-Trigger. This suggests that, when reputation information was incom-
plete, GPT 5.2 may have been particularly sensitive to competitive emotional cues and therefore shifted
toward a more defensive strategy. Under negative reputation conditions, there was a bit more heterogeneity
across models. Against extortionist counterparts with negative reputations, Claude 4.6 and Gemini 3 mostly
defected (All-D), but GPT 5.2 often adopted a tit-for-tat strategy. When faced with generous counterparts,
Claude 4.6 tended to start with a defection and then quickly shift to cooperation, whereas the others tended
to play tit-for-tat.

B.5
Cooperation in Open-Source Models
Complementing the analysis in the main text, we ran our experimental design with several frontier open-
source models (5 runs per model per condition):
Google’s Gemma 41, Z.ai’s GLM 4.6V2, Shanghai AI Laboratory’s Intern-vl-3.5.-GPT-OSS3, Moonshot
AI’s Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking4, Meta’s Llama 45, and Alibaba’s Qwen 3.5-VL-Thinking6. Studying these
models is useful for various reasons. First, open-weight models offer a degree of transparency and repro-
ducibility that closed, API-served frontier models cannot. Second, open-source models are increasingly
deployed at scale in real-world applications — embedded in local, on-device, or self-hosted systems where
cost, latency, privacy, security or customization needs favor open weights over proprietary APIs — meaning
their cooperative tendencies carry direct practical relevance for a large and growing share of actual Gen AI
deployments. Third, because open-source models span a wider range of parameter counts, training corpora,
and post-training procedures than the proprietary frontier systems examined in our main studies, they allow
us to probe whether the reputation-, strategy-, and emotion-sensitivity patterns we observe are a general
property of sufficiently capable Gen AI systems or are instead particular to the specific architectures and
alignment procedures used by a small number of well-resourced labs. Finally, studying models developed
by a broader and more geographically diverse set of organizations helps guard against conclusions that are
inadvertently specific to the training philosophy or safety tuning practices of one commercial ecosystem,
and offers a more representative picture of the cooperative behavior likely to characterize the broader Gen
AI landscape as these models proliferate across sectors and regions.
The results for the prisoner’s dilemma experiment are shown in Fig. 14. Across the set, direct reci-
procity (counterpart strategy) remained broadly influential, with cooperation generally higher under gener-
ous than extortionist counterparts. However, the strength and reliability of this effect varied considerably:
InternVL3.5-GPT-OSS and Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking showed the cleanest, most consistent strategy separa-
tion, with cooperation reliably higher under generosity regardless of other conditions, while Llama4 showed
a strategy effect that was weak and in some cells reversed, suggesting comparatively unreliable tracking of
the counterpart’s behavior.
Reputation processing, by contrast, revealed substantially more cross-model divergence than either pro-
prietary study. Some models (Gemma4, Qwen3.5vl-Thinking) appeared to treat negative reputation as a
near-absolute floor on cooperation, suppressing cooperative responses even when the counterpart’s actual
strategy was generous — a pattern suggesting reputation can override direct behavioral evidence rather
than being integrated with it. Others (InternVL3.5-GPT-OSS) showed the expected graded staircase (posi-
tive ¿ unknown ¿ negative), consistent with the proprietary reasoning models. Still others (Kimi-VL-A3B-
Thinking, Llama4) showed a non-monotonic or largely flat reputation effect, with positive reputation some-
times producing lower cooperation than unknown reputation — a reversal not observed in either proprietary
study and one that warrants further scrutiny.
