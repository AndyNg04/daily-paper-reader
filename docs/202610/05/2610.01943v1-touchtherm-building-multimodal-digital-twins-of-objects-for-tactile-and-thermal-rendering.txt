# TouchTherm : Building Multimodal Digital Twins of Objects for Tactile and Thermal Rendering 

Yitao Zhang, Hong Ying, Haoran Guo, Xiaoying Zhou, Guanyu Chen, and Chenxi Xiao †

Abstract — Robotic simulation and virtual reality increasingly require object assets that capture not only visual geometry, but also the physical cues underlying tactile and thermal interaction. However, existing 3D datasets and reconstruction methods primarily represent object-scale geometry and vi-sual appearance, overlooking the microscale surface structure needed for high-fidelity haptic rendering and the transient temperature dynamics required for temperature-aware inter-action. We present TouchTherm, a framework for constructing simulation-ready visuo-tactile-thermal object assets from real-world objects. For visual and tactile reconstruction, we combine a high-fidelity handheld structured-light scanner with multiview normal maps obtained from photometric stereo. The normal maps are registered to the scanned geometry and transformed into tangent space to recover local micro-height fields, which are queried by an optical tactile renderer, while the coarse mesh handles collision detection. For thermal reconstruction, we capture synchronized multiview infrared videos of natural cooling following controlled heating and reconstruct a physics-regularized dynamic thermal field. These representations are integrated with the visual geometry into a unified, simulator-ready object asset. Experiments on 20 objects show that the reconstructed micro-height fields preserve dominant surface structures and recover higher-frequency details beyond the coarse geometry, while the dynamic thermal fields achieve held-out surface-temperature MAEs of 0.465 ◦C and 0.592 ◦C

at 30 s and 45 s, respectively. The resulting tactile assets enable high-fidelity tactile simulation, including synthetic-to-real object recognition from tactile sensor observations, while a glove-based VR system demonstrates spatially and tempo-rally varying thermal feedback. These results highlight the potential of TouchTherm for multimodal sensory simulation and temperature-aware virtual interaction.

For thermal simulation, the recon-structed dynamic thermal field supports time-varying surface-temperature queries for robotic and virtual interactions. A. Multimodal Data Collection 

Existing object reconstruction pipelines are largely vision-centric. Structured-light scanning and multi-view stereo re-cover metric geometry [6], while NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting enable view synthesis and high-fidelity render-ing [7], [8]. However, these methods mainly capture object-scale geometry and appearance, overlooking the contact-scale surface relief and transient thermal properties needed for multimodal sensory rendering. Non-visual sensing provides complementary physical in-formation. Tactile sensors capture local surface geometry and contact responses [5], [9], [19], while thermal sensors capture spatial temperature distributions and heat-transfer dynamics [13]. Yet, these measurements are typically lo-calized observations rather than object-level representations registered to the object geometry. We therefore construct a unified representation that spatially registers visual, tactile, and thermal information as reusable simulation assets. 

B. Datasets and Simulation Assets 

Geometry and appearance assets. Existing robotics and embodied-AI datasets primarily provide object-scale geometry and visual appearance. YCB provides standardized physical objects with RGB-D scans [15], Google Scanned Objects contains reconstructed household objects suitable for physics simulation [16], and large-scale collections such as Objaverse and Digital Twin Catalog provide extensive 3D assets for learning and simulation [20], [21]. These resources substantially expand the diversity of available digital objects, but generally lack spatially registered contact-scale tactile relief and dynamic surface-temperature information. 

Multisensory object assets. ObjectFolder and its ex-tensions associate objects with visual, acoustic, and tactile observations or implicit representations [17], [18], [22], while X-Capture collects synchronized RGB-D, tactile, and impact-audio measurements of physical objects [23]. These datasets primarily support learning from existing multisen-sory observations. However, none combines registered tactile microgeometry with spatiotemporal thermal responses in a unified object asset. 

Simulation-ready tactile and thermal assets. Tactile simulators synthesize contact observations from object ge-ometry or height-field representations [3], [4], [10], [24]. Coarse meshes are efficient for collision and contact sim-ulation but may miss tactile-relevant surface details [6], [25], while dense geometric representations of microscale structures can be used for haptic rendering but increase simulation and rendering costs [4], [10]. Beyond tactile-texture simulation, thermal simulation is less developed: prior work mainly uses thermal cues for material recogni-tion or user feedback, rather than constructing object-level dynamic thermal fields. TouchTherm bridges these gaps by combining coarse collision geometry, registered tactile micro-height fields, and an object-specific dynamic thermal field within a shared object coordinate frame. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Pipeline Overview 

Our goal is to convert a real object into a simulator-ready multimodal asset comprising a coarse mesh, tactile micro-height fields, and a dynamic thermal field. As shown in Fig. 2, the pipeline aligns object scans, multiview normal maps, and infrared videos to a common object frame, then reconstructs tactile microgeometry and the dynamic thermal field in two parallel branches. The resulting representations are integrated into a unified object asset. According to this pipeline, the following sections describe the acquisition setup (Sec. III-B), tactile reconstruction (Sec. III-C), and dynamic thermal field reconstruction (Sec. III-D), respectively. 

B. Hardware Setup 

As shown in Fig. 2(a), we use an EinScan Pro 2X V2 structured-light scanner to acquire an object mesh. For appearance and normal estimation, a fixed Redmi K70 smart-phone captures a stack of RGB images at each viewpoint under manually varied illumination directions. The pretrained SDM-UniPS [26] model then estimates an image-aligned normal map from each illumination stack. For thermal acqui-sition, HIKMICRO P09 thermal cameras record the natural cooling process following controlled heat-gun excitation. The RGB and thermal cameras are calibrated, and regis-tered to the object frame using manually selected 2D–3D correspondences. Finally, a GelSight Mini provides tactile measurements for validation and calibration of the micro-height amplitude of the reconstructed surface. Fig. 2. Overview of the TouchTherm reconstruction pipeline. (a) Structured-light scanning, multiview photometric stereo, and thermal imaging acquire geometry, normals, and infrared videos. (b) Coarse geometry is decoupled from registered microgeometry, reconstructed into contact-conditioned height fields for tactile rendering. (c) Infrared observations support physics-regularized dynamic thermal reconstruction and parameter identification for rollout. (d) The asset comprises a coarse collision mesh, normal maps, tactile micro-height fields, and a dynamic thermal field. 

C. Reconstruction of Tactile Microgeometry for Rendering 

As shown in Fig. 2(b), we decouple contact geometry from the tactile appearance on the scale of tactile sensor observa-tions. Specifically, the coarse mesh provides the geometric foundation for visual rendering and physical collision, while registered local micro-height fields encode high-frequency surface relief exclusively for tactile rendering. 

1) Coarse Geometry and Microgeometry Decoupling: 

The scanned mesh is first cleaned by removing support surfaces, isolated fragments, and reconstruction artifacts. For simple geometries, measured dimensions can alternatively be used to construct a cleaner mesh. The resulting mesh 

M = ( V, F ) is exported to Isaac Sim as the visual and collision geometry. Next, contact-scale surface texture is represented separately as local micro-height fields, thereby preserving high-frequency tactile detail without increasing the geometric or collision complexity of the simulation mesh. Detailed process for obtaining texture is provided as follows. 

2) Normal Estimation and Registration: For each RGB view i, the SDM-UniPS model [26] estimates an image-aligned normal map Ni from multi-illumination images. We manually annotate sparse 2D–3D correspondences between the RGB & normal image and the coarse mesh, and estimate the object-to-camera pose (Ri, ti) using PnP. D. Dynamic Thermal Field Reconstruction 

We reconstruct the object’s surface temperature field from multiview thermal videos and estimate two parameters: the surface-diffusion coefficient α, which controls heat spreading along the surface, and the ambient-relaxation coefficient h,which controls heat exchange with the environment. These parameters drive the subsequent thermal simulation.

We correct pixel temperatures for temporal drift and viewing-angle bias following [30], then project them onto the surface. We evaluate TouchTherm from four perspectives: object asset quality (Sec. IV-A), tactile rendering fidelity (Sec. IV-B), thermal prediction accuracy (Sec. IV-C), and downstream utility in perception and interaction tasks (Sec. IV-D). 

A. TouchTherm Assets Overview 

We constructed digital twins for 20 real objects spanning flat, curved, cylindrical, and free-form geometries, as shown in Fig. 3. These objects exhibit smooth, granular, embossed, and anisotropic surface patterns. As summarized in Fig. 2(d), each visuo-tactile asset comprises a cleaned metric mesh, registered multi-view normal maps, and reconstructed micro-height fields, which together support simulated tactile contact Fig. 3. Overview of the 20 real objects used in our dataset . The collection spans flat, curved, cylindrical, and free-form objects with diverse materials and surface relief. and rendering. Excluding object bottoms, planar objects require one registered RGB/normal view, whereas 3D objects use at least two, typically 5–7, and up to 12 for complex geometries. For thermal acquisition, synchronized infrared cameras record natural cooling following heat-gun excitation, fol-lowing Sec. III-D. Constructing the visuo-tactile assets and acquiring the thermal data took approximately two to three weeks per modality for the full collection, corresponding to roughly half a day per object per modality.

Thermal Feedback in VR . We further evaluate the usability of the reconstructed thermal properties. Ten consenting, self-reported healthy participants (8 male and 2 female, aged 18–28) used the wearable platform in Fig. 8 to explore cold, warm, hot, and continuously cooling regions. On a seven-point scale (with 7 denoting the highest rating), visual– thermal consistency, cooling consistency, response timeli-ness, realism, and comfort received scores of 6.30 ± 0.95 ,

Overall, all participants recognized the gradual cold–warm–hot changes across different object regions, with 7 participants rating the rendered cues as highly clear (the highest rating). All ten participants reported no discomfort, indicating that the system can provide intuitive and comfort-able thermal feedback. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented TouchTherm , a framework for construct-ing simulation-ready multimodal digital twins that integrate coarse visual geometry, tactile microgeometry, and a dy-namic thermal field. By separating tactile micro-height fields from collision geometry, the framework preserves contact-scale surface details without increasing collision complexity. It also reconstructs a dynamic thermal field from multiview infrared observations, enabling spatiotemporal temperature Fig. 8. Thermal-feedback VR system . (a) VR and thermal-feedback hardware and (b) interaction with a reconstructed dynamic thermal field. queries. Experiments on 20 objects demonstrate improved tactile rendering compared with coarse-geometry and image-space baselines, as well as accurate thermal predictions on held-out data, with surface MAEs of 0.465 ◦C and 0.592 ◦C

at 30 s and 45 s, respectively. In synthetic-to-real tactile object recognition, TouchTherm consistently outperforms coarse geometry across Top-1, Top-3, and Macro-F1. An application using a wearable glove further demonstrates spatially and temporally varying thermal feedback in VR. 

Limitations and future work. The current framework uses microgeometry only for tactile rendering and does not model its effects on contact mechanics. Its dynamic thermal field focuses on natural cooling and does not capture bidirectional heat transfer during human or robotic contact.
