Co-Designing AI For Mental Health Support With Young Adults of Color (YOC):
Needs, Expectations, and Implications for AI Literacy

Young adults of color (YOC) face heightened mental health challenges and barriers to care while navigating developmental and
life transitions. Situated between youth-oriented safeguards and adult-oriented AI systems, little is known about how they use AI
chatbots for mental health and well-being support or how sociocultural contexts shape their expectations, concerns, and design
preferences. We conducted a two-day co-design workshop with 13 Asian, Black, and Hispanic/Latino/a young adults aged 18–24.
Participants found generic chatbot advice to flatten their lived experiences; rather than making incorrect assumptions, they wanted
more opportunities for identity-informed disclosure. Preferences for YOC-centered personalization also revealed gaps in privacy and
AI literacy. Participants negotiated different therapeutic roles for chatbots and sought greater AI accountability and user agency,
highlighting blurred boundaries between clinical and non-clinical AI-mediated support. Findings suggest directions for integrating AI
literacy with mental health literacy and centering YOC’s experiences in the privacy calculus.

Introduction
Young people are increasingly using consumer-facing AI applications for mental health and well-being support,
including general-purpose large language model (LLM)-based chatbots and applications designed for social uses (e.g.,
AI companions) [68, 69, 72, 86]. For young adults—aged 18 to 24 years in this study—the adoption of AI chatbots for
such support is unfolding within the broader developmental period of emerging adulthood, characterized by identity
exploration, ongoing cognitive development, and major life transitions that can reconfigure social support systems
[6, 7, 106]. In the U.S., young adults experience comparatively high rates of mental illness, yet underutilize traditional
mental health treatment [77]; they also report about twice the rates of anxiety and depression compared to teens [67].
Meanwhile, young adults today face many demands and “less latitude for failure” [46, 67]. Against this backdrop, AI
chatbots may be perceived as accessible sources of support while also creating potential harms, especially in vulnerable

as emotional, psychological, and social well-being [3, 49]. Such use [99] ranges from coping with stressors and mental
health symptoms (e.g., ADHD, trauma) [49, 66] to seeking interpersonal advice [101].
For racially and ethnically diverse young adults, these needs can be compounded by heightened stressors [46] and
structural barriers to accessing appropriate mental health care [78, 82]. Recent data, which include ages 18–21, also
indicate that they may be using AI chatbots for mental health advice at comparatively higher rates and frequency
[69]. Yet, research on conversational AI-mediated mental health support has insufficiently centered cultural and
identity-based contexts [103]. This gap is significant because AI systems are not specifically designed for the critical
developmental periods young adults navigate [46], despite their vulnerability to AI-related harms; those harms are
further intensified for young adults of color (YOC) when AI systems reproduce identity-specific biases, stereotypes, and
stigma [13, 34, 43, 79, 83, 99]. As cultural backgrounds can also shape attitudes toward AI conversational agents [32, 36],
YOC’s culturally situated knowledge and lived experiences are essential to understanding how they use and make
sense of AI chatbots. Accordingly, we collaborated with YOC to examine both age-related experiences and identity- and
culturally informed perceptions and practices surrounding AI use for mental health and well-being support.
We acknowledge a central challenge: While the risks of using AI chatbots for mental health support are increasingly
recognized, discouraging use alone is unlikely to address the needs and circumstances that motivate young adults to
seek such support. In addition, AI use for mental health could be further pushed underground, especially since younger
users tend not to disclose such use to people in their lives [69]. At the same time, much of AI safety discourse frames
“young people” primarily as minors, such as age-specific safeguards in commercial platforms like ChatGPT for Teens
[81]. This framing leaves young adults in an ambiguous position—treated as autonomous adult users despite ongoing
psychological and cognitive development and mental health needs. Meanwhile, young adults have unique preferences,
attitudes, and utilization patterns compared to the general adult population for chatbot-enabled mental, emotional, and
social support [8, 56]. Instead, we build on and extend prior participatory studies that emphasize working with people
who use AI systems to foster safer, more informed engagement [19, 53, 60, 94, 98]. This entails identifying young adults’
valued design priorities, as well as behavioral and knowledge gaps, to inform AI literacy education that empowers
them to navigate their diverse needs. To this end, we ask the following research questions:
• RQ1. What are YOC’s needs, expectations, and concerns regarding AI chatbot use for mental health and well-being
support? In what contexts do identity and culture shape these accounts?
• RQ2. What AI chatbot features do YOC envision for supporting more responsible and mindful mental health-
related use?
• RQ3. What AI literacy implications emerge from YOC’s experiences for future mental health contexts?
We conducted a two-day, in-person co-design workshop with 13 YOC who self-identified as Asian, Black, or Hispanic
or Latino/a. We chose co-design and design workshops as an avenue for racially diverse young adults to envision the
role of technology in their own futures [41]. The workshop combined individual and group reflections, AI conversation
exercises, and lightweight, iterative design activities. These activities allowed participants to share contextual knowledge
[16] and personal experiences related to mental health, while collectively reflecting on their understanding of and
uncertainties about conversational AI.
Throughout the workshop, YOC participants expected AI chatbots to provide mental health and well-being support
that is more responsive to their sociocultural contexts. They perceived generic chatbot advice as reducing complex
concerns—such as parental pressure, mental health stigma, and immigration experience—to typical young-adult

“individual” problems; however, such advice is often less relevant to challenges connected to YOC’s cultural backgrounds
and community norms (RQ1). Rather than having systems fill in gaps with incorrect assumptions, participants designed
opportunities for identity-informed disclosure (RQ2). For example, they envisioned an initial set of intentional questions
(e.g., ethnicity, household dynamics) that would contextualize subsequent conversations. However, with the desire
to disclose more of their personal backgrounds, participants rarely discussed the privacy implications of in-depth
self-disclosure (RQ3).
Additionally, participants wanted chatbots to clearly communicate the forms of mental health support they can or
cannot provide from the outset. They envisioned chatbots as serving multiple functions across the help-seeking process,
including culturally resonant therapeutic roles (RQ1). Their designs foregrounded features intended to strengthen AI
accountability and user agency (RQ2). At the same time, participants struggled to reach consensus about the appropriate
role of AI conversational agents within the formal mental health support landscape and to grasp what system “pushbacks”
and safeguards look like (RQ3). Drawing on these findings, we conceptualize how gaps in AI literacy may amplify
existing gaps in mental health literacy, motivating a more integrated approach to AI literacy for young adults engaging
with AI-mediated mental health and well-being support.
This paper makes three contributions:
1. An empirical account of how YOC’s identities, lived experiences, and mental models of AI shape their uses and
perceptions of AI chatbots for mental health and well-being support. Drawing on participatory design approaches,
we contribute to a more population- and domain-specific account of AI use, safety, and literacy that moves
beyond individualistic models of AI adoption and use.
2. A set of design principles for AI chatbot features that support YOC’s vision of mindful and culturally responsive
engagement with AI chatbots for mental health and well-being. These principles help identify opportunities to
extend broad AI safety approaches by attending to more identity-specific vulnerabilities.
3. A conceptual framework for AI literacy, mental health literacy, and privacy considerations. We show why informed
and safer engagement with AI chatbots for mental health support cannot be reduced to technical knowledge or
prompting skills alone; it also requires capacities to navigate AI chatbots’ structural affordances and relational
dynamics, critically assess their limitations and harms in mental health contexts, and recognize culturally situated
risks.

Background and Related Work
2.1
Young Adults’ AI Chatbot Use for Mental Health Support and Its Limitations
As more and more young adults, especially YOC, turn to AI chatbots for mental health and well-being support, their
wide-ranging perspectives and practices should be further explored. This is pressing given that AI chatbots are rapidly
evolving and being adopted at a pace that currently exceeds our understanding of their appropriateness and effectiveness
for mental health support. Further, much more should be done to translate these experiences into designing technologies
and evaluating the resulting impact.
2.1.1
Use of Conversational AI for Mental Health and Well-Being Support. “Mental health” encompasses both mental
illness and subjective emotional, psychological, and social well-being, including people’s capacity to manage stressors
and navigate relationships [3, 49]. Consistent with this broad conceptualization, a recent review including participants
aged 18–24 found that they rarely hold a single, shared definition of mental health; rather, they understood mental
health to cover emotions, coping, relationships, identity, and life satisfaction [11]. Given this, mental health-related

conversations with AI chatbots span concerns with different meanings and risk profiles—affective or interpersonal
concerns (e.g., navigating relationships [101]), clinically framed help-seeking (e.g., managing mental health conditions
[66]), and high-risk or crisis conversations—and accordingly require different forms of monitoring and safeguards [70].
Young adults may turn to AI chatbots for diverse mental health concerns, particularly given they already view
AI-enabled systems as a potential means of navigating gaps in the help-seeking process [84]. However, less is known
about how young adults specifically use and perceive general-purpose and consumer-facing AI chatbots for such
concerns [27]. Young adulthood entails distinctive developmental and social circumstances [6, 46], including patterns of
support-seeking outside traditional sources that often intersect with digital technology use [51, 75]. Together, these
contexts may shape young adults’ needs and expectations for mental, emotional, and social support from conversational
AI in ways that differ from those of older adults and younger teens [8, 56]. For instance, they report greater familiarity
with and use of AI than other-age adults [52]. Further, among surveyed teens and young adults, respondents aged 18–21
had greater and increasing use of AI chatbots for mental health advice relative to younger respondents [68, 69].
This gap is especially salient for YOC. Available evidence suggests that racially and ethnically diverse teens and
young adults report high and frequent use of AI chatbots for mental health support [69, 71], yet YOC’s specific
practices, perceptions, and strategies for navigating chatbot-mediated support remain poorly understood. Furthermore,
cultural contexts can shape users’ attitudes toward conversational AI and their expectations of these systems, including
preferences for AI autonomy and relational connection [32, 36]. Further research is therefore needed to examine how
YOC’s diverse, culturally situated experiences shape mental health-related interactions with AI chatbots.
2.1.2
Emerging Research on Limitations of AI-Mediated Support. The growing use of general-purpose LLM chatbots
for mental health support is concerning because these systems are not designed or clinically validated to provide
therapeutic care [108]. Documented limitations and harms include inadequate detection and protections in high-risk or
crisis situations, with increasing attention on younger users [21, 29, 58]. Further, when users bring clinically framed
concerns to AI chatbots, these systems can respond with stigma [76], provide unauthorized medical advice [64], and
respond inappropriately to common symptoms [76]. Even in affective or interpersonal conversations, risks can arise
through structural components, such as data collection and privacy practices, and relational dynamics, such as emotional
reliance [4, 42, 70].
However, limited research examines young adults as a distinct developmental group in defining and evaluating AI
safety. Emerging research focused on those under 18 illustrates the importance of developmentally informed safety
approaches. For example, the Youth-Centered GAI Risks (YAIR) taxonomy identifies risks—including risks to mental
well-being and social development—that existing AI safety frameworks do not fully capture [109]. Yet, it is unclear
the extent to which youth-centered safety frameworks extend to young adulthood in light of the distinctive forms
of autonomy, identity exploration, changing social networks, digital support-seeking, and life transitions—on top
of ongoing cognitive development [6, 7, 46, 75, 106]. More work should focus on building upon developmentally
informed approaches, especially in vulnerable contexts like mental health support-seeking. Such work must also
account for the experiences of YOC, for whom racial bias, stereotypes, and stigma reproduced or amplified by AI
systems [13, 34, 43, 79, 83, 99] may compound existing inequities in mental health contexts [2, 82].
Given these concerns, studies have begun to design interventions intended to promote more mindful and safer AI
chatbot use, though evidence of their effectiveness remains limited. Examples include warning labels and other safety-
oriented interface features [45, 57, 85, 98]. In addition to the lack of evidence of their effectiveness, existing interventions
and platform safeguards have rarely been designed with, or rigorously evaluated among, young adults—particularly

and harms that have already unequally impacted their experiences in mental health and technology design.
2.2
Approaches to Supporting AI Use for Mental Health among Young Adults
Beyond addressing gaps in YOC-centered design of AI chatbots, we argue that it is also important to understand
whether young adults have the AI literacy needed to use conversational AI mindfully and responsibly for mental
health and well-being support. Such literacy empowers users to recognize how AI systems work, and this work starts
with reviewing their current limitations and making AI literacy contextually appropriate. Prior work has generally
pursued two approaches: (1) developing AI literacy frameworks and programs, and (2) engaging young adults through
participatory design methods.
2.2.1
Existing AI Literacy Frameworks for Young Adults. Over the past decade, AI literacy scholarship has expanded
alongside AI’s growing integration into everyday life, identifying the knowledge and competencies needed to help
people understand, evaluate, and use AI systems [65, 80]. Early work articulated foundational concepts, including
five “big ideas” for AI education [100], and defined AI literacy as competencies needed for critically evaluating AI,
communicating and collaborating with AI systems, and using them across varied contexts [65]. With the widespread
availability of generative AI, more recent frameworks have incorporated practical competencies (e.g., prompting and
detecting AI-generated content) alongside sociotechnical and ethical awareness [5, 47].
Nevertheless, it remains unclear how well existing AI literacy education equips young adults to navigate conver-
sational AI in personal and higher-stakes contexts, including mental health and well-being support. Research on AI
literacy has mainly focused on K–12 [17, 100, 105] or higher-education settings [38, 61]. Although these efforts provide
an important foundation, young adults specifically may encounter AI literacy unevenly—some in K–12 or university
settings, whereas others may have limited access to formal AI literacy education or no longer be situated in educational
institutions. Moreover, even when available, classroom-based approaches may not address the personal, relational, and
emotionally consequential uses of conversational AI that occur outside the academic or professional uses [107].
A further limitation of conventional AI literacy education concerns the distinctive nature of mental health support-
seeking with conversational agents. Existing AI literacy competencies may help users understand AI capabilities and
limitations or identify bias and misinformation [5, 100]; however, they may be insufficient for recognizing interactional
risks that emerge over time. For example, recognizing harmful language in chatbot output does not necessarily enable
users to identify emotional dependence, escalating reliance, or blurred boundaries across repeated interactions with an
AI chatbot [109]. Emerging K–12-focused initiatives begin to connect AI literacy with well-being [24], though their
applicability to young adults and to mental health-related chatbot use remains unclear.
These gaps may be further heightened for YOC, who encounter racialized and culturally misaligned biases in AI
systems [34, 43, 79, 83], yet report comparatively high use of AI chatbots for mental health advice [69]. Their interactions
with chatbots may also be shaped by diverse sociocultural contexts and culturally situated understandings of mental
health that remain under-examined. More research is needed to identify the knowledge and behavioral gaps that YOC
experience while using conversational AI for mental health and well-being support, including how these needs vary
across sociocultural contexts and patterns of use.

2.2.2
Participatory Design Methods for AI Literacy and Young Adults. AI literacy research increasingly draws on
participatory design to foreground people’s situated knowledge and everyday experiences of AI, in topics such as AI
fairness and critical AI literacy. Emerging studies have, for example, extended this approach to teens [19, 53, 60, 94].
Parallel work in digital mental health has applied co-design methods with young adults to identify their needs and
preferences for technology-mediated mental health support [23, 31, 73]. However, limited research brings these bodies
of work together by engaging specifically young adults as active contributors to the design of AI literacy. This gap stands
out given limited attention to demographic factors—for example, ethnicity and socioeconomic backgrounds—within
digital mental health research that includes young adults [12]. It also highlights the need to move beyond designing
for young adults toward designing with them with more empathy-driven design [90]. Building on these works, this
study takes a participatory design approach, and the Methods section further details how and why this approach was
operationalized.
3
Methods
We conducted a two-day, in-person co-design workshop with young adults who self-identified as Asian, Black, or
Hispanic or Latino/a (n = 13, aged 18–24, M = 20, SD = 2.29) and five facilitators. Co-design entails a process of “joint
inquiry and imagination” [95], grounded in participatory design commitments to mutual learning and shared power
and decision-making [87]. For young adults, participatory design positions participants as co-constituents—rather than
merely subjects—of mental health research that intersects with digital technology, given they are digital natives and
value agency [73, 90]. Accordingly, we used co-design to extend participatory approaches in the context of AI-mediated
mental health and well-being support. Our approach also builds on prior work that has centered young people, such as
teens, as active problem-solvers in AI knowledge production [19, 53, 60, 94].
We developed the workshop as a pilot model for engaging YOC in conversations about AI chatbots and mental
health. Given the topic’s sensitivity, we first conducted the workshop with young adults and plan to adapt the approach
for other populations, such as younger teens and individuals living with mental health diagnoses. We operationalized
co-design through workshops, a common HCI format for eliciting participants’ values, needs, and non-design expertise
around emerging technologies [40]. Our workshop incorporated lightweight and iterative design activities, such as
storyboarding and paper-based prototyping. We also incorporated activities and facilitation practices intended to foster
psychological safety, build trust, and support individual reflection to help minimize emotional burden on participants.
We pilot-tested the workshop with three young adults outside the participant pool. We also iteratively reviewed
and refined the workshop program and materials with a panel of six experts with extensive experience conducting
youth-centered design research. The study was approved by the University’s Institutional Review Board.
3.1
Participants
We recruited participants through previous Youth Participatory Action Research [54] programs, community organi-
zations, and word of mouth. Participants were eligible if they: (1) were 18-24 years old; (2) self-identified as a person
of color (defined as identifying with a non-White race); and (3) used AI chatbots regularly for any purpose (defined
as at least once a week). We did not restrict candidates from joining if they had never used an AI chatbot for mental
health and well-being support to accommodate a broad range of perspectives and attitudes. The workshop took place
during Summer 2026 in a major metropolitan area on the West Coast of the U.S. with a racially and culturally diverse
population. Of 29 people who expressed interest, 13 were invited to participate based on eligibility, availability to join
in-person, and complete interest form.

critically examining AI: Recognize (identifying potential AI harms), Analyze (understanding sociotechnical dimensions
of harms), and Deliberate (developing recommendations for improvement) [93] (Figure 2). We adapted this approach for

Day 1’s activities created opportunities for participants to recognize their own uses of AI chatbots, share perspectives
with peers, and identify initial concerns, needs, and expectations related to chatbot use for mental health and well-being
support (RQ1). These activities prepared participants to transition to analyzing ChatGPT interactions for mental
health support more critically. We postponed any informational session on problematic AI behaviors until Day 2. This
sequencing was intended to avoid prematurely framing participants’ interpretations.
3.2.1
Warm-up and Group Reflection on Ideal AI Chatbot for Mental Health. Day 1 began with participants completing
a pre-participation survey and generating their definitions of mental health and well-being. Rather than imposing a
predefined clinical or expert definition, we encouraged a broad conceptualization [11] from participants. Responses
included mood regulation, stable emotions, low distress, inner peace, self-confidence, life quality, and holistic health.
Participants then discussed why they or their peers might seek mental health and well-being support from AI chatbots,
as well as their initial concerns about this form of AI use.
After the warm-up, participants worked in groups on an art-based activity informed by value-sensitive design [33].
They were asked to consider the following prompt: “If a friend were going to use an AI chatbot for mental health,
what would you hope the chatbot does well?” Groups identified values they hoped such a chatbot would embody,
then brainstormed an object, space (e.g., a room), or character that could represent those values. Each group drew one
example and the values it reflected. This activity served as a throughline for the remainder of the workshop: participants
frequently returned to the values they identified during this early exercise when navigating AI conversations and
design sessions. Final examples included a garden, a library with a lighthouse, and a scale.

3.2.2
AI Chatbot Conversations, Journaling, and Group Discussion. Participants engaged in two 15-minute conversations
with ChatGPT1, individually or in pairs depending on their preference. In the first conversation, participants responded
to: “What would another young person of color ask an AI chatbot for their mental health and well-being? Feel free
to draw on your identity and backgrounds to inform your prompts.” They explored topics including social anxiety,
stress and workload management, parental pressure, interpersonal conflict, and immigration experience. In the second
conversation, they considered when an AI chatbot might push back in a mental health context and how users might
still attempt to elicit cooperation; they explored scenarios such as asking ChatGPT to act as a therapist (e.g., diagnose
symptoms, conduct CBT), seeking validation for cheating on an exam, and navigating moral ambiguity in relationships.
Before the activity, we reviewed safety guidelines, including avoiding illegal or harmful topics and emphasizing that
ChatGPT is not a clinically trained provider. After each conversation, participants wrote a brief product-style review of
their interaction as part of individual journaling (Appendix A) and consented to donate their in-session chat log data.
Day 1 concluded with a semi-structured group discussion of participants’ observations and reflections (Appendix B).
3.3
Day 2 – Analyze and Deliberate: Translating Gaps into Design Solutions
Day 2 dedicated more time to analyzing current gaps and potential harms that hinder mindful and relevant use of
AI chatbots for mental health and well-being support and transitioned to deliberating what AI chatbot behaviors and
features participants wanted to see (RQ2). We underscored ideation, rather than perfection, in group debriefs and
design sessions, so that we could use participants’ discussions to surface knowledge and behavioral gaps to highlight
AI literacy implications (RQ3).
3.3.1
Wishlist Brainstorming and Taxonomy of Problematic AI Behaviors. Building on Day 1 reflections, participants
started Day 2 by collaboratively brainstorming a “wishlist” of AI chatbot behaviors to support mindful and culturally
relevant mental health and well-being use. They defined these concepts in their own terms, identified current pain
points, and proposed desired chatbot behaviors (Figure 4). After initial brainstorming, we introduced a taxonomy
of potentially problematic or inappropriate AI behaviors for young adults in mental health contexts (Appendix C),

3.3.2
Co-design of AI Behaviors and Solutions. We introduced storyboarding and low-fidelity prototyping to scaffold
participants’ design process. Four teams selected an AI behavior from their wishlists and developed a storyboard around
a self-selected scenario (e.g., “young adults asking ChatGPT for advice on how to come out to their family”). Teams
then selected a user goal (e.g., “process emotions and organize thoughts”) for the storyboard’s first panel and translated
their storyboards into low-fidelity mock-ups. An undergraduate student with extensive UI/UX experience served as a
design consultant, supporting participants’ visualization of their ideas and, when appropriate, using generative AI tools
to guide this process. The workshop concluded with a presentation of and peer feedback on the mock-ups (Appendix F),
and a post-participation survey.
3.4
Data Collection and Analysis
We collected multiple forms of workshop data. Four facilitators audio-recorded group discussions and took extensive
field notes during and immediately after workshop activities. We also collected individual responses from warm-up
activities, journaling exercises, donated in-session chat logs, and survey responses.
We conducted an inductive thematic analysis of these data sources, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase process
[14]. An inductive approach enabled us to better foreground participants’ own perspectives on using conversational AI
for mental health and their desired AI behaviors. Furthermore, there was no established theoretical or design framework
to adequately and consistently guide a deductive analysis of YOC’s experiences with AI chatbots for mental health.
At the same time, our research questions, workshop activities, and facilitators’ follow-up prompts shaped the topics
participants were invited to discuss and, consequently, the data available for analysis. We therefore treated participants’
accounts as situated within the workshop context rather than as exhaustive representations of their lived experiences.

Throughout and following the workshop, the facilitators engaged in iterative discussions of preliminary observations
and field notes. Two authors then familiarized themselves with the data by reviewing field notes, audio recordings, and
all other data sources and generated initial codes inductively, which were then iteratively organized and refined into
themes and subthemes. They met weekly to discuss emerging findings from the data. To mitigate potential biases, they
continuously shared and critically examined their interpretations with the broader research team. Still, we acknowledge
that authors’ lived experiences and professional and educational backgrounds can shape interpretation of the data. We

We organize our findings around two themes. First, participants found generic mental health advice and incorrect

“Don’t Assume, Just Ask”: Uplifting Lived Experiences through Identity-Informed Disclosure
4.1.1
AI’s Generic Advice and Incorrect Assumptions for YOC-Specific Needs (RQ1). AI’s “Textbook” Answers Flatten
Lived Experiences: Participants recognized that while current AI chatbots can offer practical mental health advice,
chatbots were often insufficiently responsive to their sociocultural contexts. P06, an Asian international student,
explained that ChatGPT “didn’t ask about my background, so the advice was somewhat general” when seeking advice on
navigating academic stress and language barriers at an American university. ChatGPT produced general strategies that
an average student may find helpful for balancing their workload, such as reducing context-switching between tasks.
During the AI conversation exercise, a Black participant (P01) sought ChatGPT’s advice about social anxiety related
to past trauma stemming from interactions with others as a minority in school. ChatGPT offered broad encouragement,
such as “Aim for connection, not perfection. Most people aren’t looking for someone who is endlessly witty or charismatic.
They’re looking for someone who is friendly, interested, and authentic.” The chatbot, however, did not ask any questions
to clarify the participant’s circumstances or other identity-based contexts that may contribute to their anxiety. After
this interaction, P01 critiqued ChatGPT’s advice, saying that “AI chatbot [cannot] think about your situation in the lens

human empathy.” P01 added that this type of “one-size-fits-all” advice is why they intentionally never used ChatGPT
for mental health and well-being support.

health challenges into problems typically expected among young adults. P04 explained that ChatGPT was “excellent at
providing comfort and suggesting next steps,” but “not perceptive enough to detect more complex cultural experiences.” They
added that these answers were fine for a “quick pick-me up conversation” and “temporary relief,” but “not a long-term

mental health stigma and collectivism, that YOC find deeply rooted in their lives.

5
Discussion
Our workshop findings show that YOC participants wanted AI mental health and well-being support to better recognize
the identities, sociocultural contexts, and lived experiences that shape their needs. In particular, they designed for more
opportunities for identity-informed disclosure to mitigate generic advice or assumption-making from conversational
AI; this desire aligns with prior research finding that teens and young adults value chatbots’ interpretive follow-up
questions in mental and emotional support (e.g., “what else?”) [39]. At the same time, participants struggled to form
stable mental models of AI chatbot roles within the broader mental health support landscape. Despite uncertainties,
they aligned on and designed for balancing user agency with meaningful AI accountability. Together, these pain points
and proposed designs reveal important knowledge gaps that future AI literacy education and supporting systems design
should address in mental health contexts. In the Discussion section, we first examine these findings through privacy
calculus versus personalization (Section 5.1) and consider how AI literacy and mental health literacy can be bridged in
YOC-centered ways (Section 5.2). We conclude by reflecting on participatory approaches for engaging YOC in future
human-AI interaction research, discussing study limitations, and outlining an emerging research agenda.
5.1
Negotiating the Fine Line between Privacy and Personalization
Our findings (particularly Section 4.1) reveal tensions between YOC’s desire for personalized mental health support
and their understanding of privacy in human–AI interaction. Below, we discuss the theoretical, design, and AI literacy
implications of these findings.
Manuscript submitted to ACM


19
5.1.1
Theoretical Implication: Privacy Calculus is a Tilted Scale for YOC. Across workshops, participants repeatedly
expressed a willingness to proactively share personal information in exchange for AI support that was more personalized
and culturally relevant. Some were willing to share “as much as possible” and envisioned features that would solicit
such information, including identity-informed follow-up questions and background intake forms. These findings reveal
two related dynamics.
First, participants sought to move from passive datafication [102], in which platforms collect and transform users’
interactions into data, toward more user-driven data collection. Rather than remaining as subjects of data collection,
they wanted to shape what information AI systems solicit and use in personalization. In doing so, they wanted to
reclaim some agency over data collection from the platform.
Second, participants weighed disclosure against anticipated benefits: they expected personal information to yield
support that was more culturally grounded, affirming, and responsive to their circumstances. This expectation reflects
privacy calculus, in which people evaluate the expected benefits of disclosure against potential privacy costs [63].
Although participants’ reasoning broadly aligns with privacy calculus theories, our findings suggest that the scale is
tilted for YOC and rarely relies on rational calculus alone [55].
On the benefit side, disclosure does not reliably produce the value participants expect. Even after sharing identity-
relevant context, they sometimes received generic advice or responses. Thus, YOC may willingly “pay” for personalization
with their personal data without receiving the culturally relevant support they expect in return. Moreover, even when
systems attempt to personalize responses, they may infer identities from explicit disclosures or implicit cues and
reproduce stereotypical or biased information [50, 83]. Thus, personalization may require YOC users to make themselves
more visible to a system without ensuring that it will interpret that information accurately or safely.
On the risk side, participants simultaneously expressed strong concerns about privacy and willingness to disclose
extensive personal information. While conventional privacy literature might characterize this tension as a privacy
paradox [9, 97], our findings suggest that it may also reflect a limited conception of privacy in AI-mediated interactions.
Participants’ concerns often focused on interpersonal privacy—for example, peers gossiping about or judging sensitive
disclosures—rather than the broader risks associated with AI systems’ collection, inference, storage, and downstream
use of personal data. Consequently, the privacy calculus YOC perform may not account for the full spectrum of privacy
risks involved in human-AI interaction.
5.1.2
Design Implication: Supporting Mindful Privacy Calculus by Enhancing AI Literacy. These findings suggest that
AI design should not simply discourage disclosure. Instead, systems should support mindful disclosure: helping users
identify what they seek from an interaction, determine which contextual information is relevant, and share no more
than is necessary to pursue that goal.
Participants did not express indiscriminate willingness to disclose. They remained cautious about highly identifying
information, but wanted AI to understand cultural backgrounds, community norms, social contexts, and personal
histories that extend beyond conventional demographic data. Future systems can help users distinguish between
contextual information that may clarify a support need and information that is sensitive, identifying, or unnecessary.
Such scaffolding could begin by prompting users to articulate their goals for AI support, then offer guidance about
what context, if any, may be useful. Systems should also clarify that more disclosure does not necessarily yield more
personalized or culturally responsive support.
This distinction is particularly important in mental health contexts. Many participants turned to AI because openly
discussing mental health was uncommon or difficult within their communities. Advice such as “talk to your friends and
Manuscript submitted to ACM


20
Jeon et al.
family” may therefore overlook the very conditions that make AI support appealing. Mindful disclosure could help
users communicate relevant constraints—for example, community norms around mental health or boundaries within
existing relationships—without requiring them to reveal their identities or other sensitive details. In this sense, effective
personalization may depend less on knowing who a user is than on understanding the sociocultural contexts within
which their support needs arise.
Mindful disclosure support should also operate in situ, as sensitive information can emerge incidentally rather than
through deliberate decisions to disclose. For example, P08 inadvertently shared her home address while brainstorming
cooking ideas after the chatbot suggested having ingredients delivered. Building on research on privacy nudges
[1, 104], we identify three interaction-level opportunities: First, explain-why-before-asking: Before requesting personal
information, systems should explain why it is being requested, whether it is necessary, and how it may shape the
current response or future personalization. Such explanations can help users assess whether the expected benefit
justifies disclosure. Second, offer disclosure alternatives: Systems should make clear that disclosure is not all-or-nothing.
For example, prompt templates can help users communicate relevant sociocultural context at an appropriate level
of abstraction while omitting names, locations, and other unnecessary identifiers. Third, surface third-party pri-
vacy: Descriptions of personal challenges can also reveal information about family members, friends, peers, and
communities. When a disclosure could identify or expose others, systems could gently surface these third-party
implications, broadening privacy reflection beyond the individual user.
Finally, systems could make the relationship between disclosure and personalization more legible by indicating which
user-provided context informed which response. Such feedback may help users develop more calibrated expectations of
personalization. Together, these designs shift privacy support from simply asking users to disclose less toward helping
them disclose with greater intention.
5.1.3
AI Literacy Implication. AI literacy education should equip YOC to critically negotiate disclosure tradeoffs within
the affordances and limitations of AI-mediated mental health support, rather than simply teaching them to disclose less.
Our findings point to three priorities.
Not everything contributes to personalization. Because our workshop used researchers’ ChatGPT accounts and
temporary chats to protect participant privacy, chatbots had limited access to prior conversational contexts. While we
acknowledge this as a limitation of our study design, it also surfaced an interesting insight: several participants were
surprised that temporary chats generated responses similar to those from their personal accounts, despite expecting
their accounts to “know” them better. AI literacy efforts should therefore clarify that sharing more information
does not necessarily mean that every piece of information will inform subsequent, personalized responses, nor does
personalization operate to the extent users imagine.
Be mindful of LLM prompt sensitivity. Seemingly minor prompt changes can meaningfully change an LLM’s output.
Disclosing more personal information can therefore affect a response in ways that are not helpful or anticipated. Below
are AI literacy concepts particularly relevant to our findings; we encourage future work to expand this list:
1. Overreliance on prompt cues. LLMs may give disproportionate weight to prominent identity or contextual details,
even when those details are only marginally relevant to the support needed.
2. Shortcut-based inferences. As discussed above, LLMs may treat demographic or cultural attributes as proxies for
other characteristics, values, or experiences [39]. For instance, a model may make stereotypical assumptions about
family relationships after implicitly picking up on user’s cultural background, rather than responding to the user’s
particular circumstances, introducing additional biases during AI responses .
Manuscript submitted to ACM


21
3. Spurious associations. Irrelevant contextual details can supply signals that prompt models to form unhelpful or
unsupported connections, potentially distorting rather than improving support.
Users must retain agency in deciding what context matters. AI systems cannot reliably determine which aspects of
a user’s experience are relevant to a particular support need. Users therefore need agency not only over whether
to disclose but also over what context should shape the interaction. AI literacy education can cultivate this agency
through practical questions: “What do I want from this interaction? What does the AI actually need to know to help me?
What can I leave out?” Such reflection positions users not as passive sources of data, but as active decision-makers in
AI-mediated support. However, this also requires some mental health literacy (Section 5.2) to understand how identities
and sociocultural contexts, among other factors, shape one’s mental health help-seeking process.
Together, these literacy goals shift the emphasis from teaching YOC to simply “share less” toward helping them
develop a more critical mental model of how disclosure, personalization, and privacy operate in AI-mediated mental
health support.
5.2
Bridging AI and Mental Health Literacy among YOC
Throughout the workshop (particularly Section 4.2), participants often preferred AI behaviors resembling clinical
practices (e.g., symptom assessment, diagnosis-like judgments, personalized advice based on extensive history), despite
such functions exceeding the appropriate scope of non-clinical, consumer-grade AI applications. This difficulty in
distinguishing AI-delivered support from licensed clinical care suggests intersecting gaps in AI and mental health literacy.
Participants’ expectations reflected uncertainty not only about chatbots’ roles but also about therapeutic care itself;
for example, requests for “objective” answers contrast with a therapist’s role in supporting their clients through more
exploratory and collaborative processes. In light of these insights, we discuss an integrated conceptual approach to AI
and mental health literacy, as well as implications for future AI literacy and systems design.
5.2.1
An Integrated Conceptual Model: AI x Mental Health Literacy. Responsible AI use in mental health contexts
requires helping young adults understand AI’s capabilities and limitations alongside a broader understanding of mental
health help-seeking, including self-efficacy for self-help and to seek professional help [74]. Relatedly, there should
also be efforts to improve their understanding of differences in the roles of mental health professionals (e.g., licensed
clinicians versus mental or behavioral health coaches). This should better frame how young adults view AI and ground
their expectations and interactions (e.g., prompting) with AI chatbots in mental health conversations [59].
In addition to the structural conditions, such as data privacy practices, future research should examine how gaps in
mental health literacy—and the expectations YOC bring to AI-mediated support—are shaped by the relational dynamics
of conversational AI. As AI systems have evolved, experts have increasingly called attention to “relationship-based
deceptive patterns,” interactive patterns that “exploit the human impulse to build and tend relationships’ [42]. These
dynamics complicate existing understandings of mental health support landscape—and the empathy, expertise, and role
within it, for instance. Accordingly, AI literacy in mental health contexts should move beyond a narrowly skills-based
model toward an interactional and sociotechnical approach that equips young adults to recognize, interpret, and
critically navigate the relevant relational cues and structural conditions (Figure 9).

clinical and non-clinical support more legible to young adults—relevant to those beyond YOC as well. Building on
emerging proposals for creating AI “nutrition labels” [25] that communicate a system’s intended role, evidence base,

limitations, and appropriate uses in accessible terms, we posit that AI nutrition labels tailored to young adults should,
at the minimum, communicate three core messages and reciprocally support their growing AI-mental health literacy:
1. Clarify that general-purpose AI is not appropriate for every role. The labels should identify explicit “red lines” around
functions reserved for licensed professionals—like how vitamin labels should clarify that supplements are not a
substitute for a healthy diet.
2. Distinguish technical limitations from role- or regulation-based constraints. Without this distinction, young adults
may interpret an AI refusal behavior or disclaimer as a technical obstacle that can be circumvented through further
prompting, rather than as a meaningful boundary on what AI should provide.
3. Explain the intended scope of mental health and well-being support. In addition to stating what an AI chatbot cannot
do, labels should describe in plain language what mental health and well-being support entails. Similar to how a
vitamin label explains its intended purposes (e.g., calcium supports bone health), a conversational AI label should
specify the purpose to manage expectations and clarify what other “supplements” are needed in addition to AI
support.
Making the rationale for these boundaries visible could simultaneously help young adults develop AI literacy and learn
about the roles of mental health professionals and the broader healthcare system as they explore what AI can and
cannot do. A potential approach is to communicate a 2 × 2 mental model (Figure 10) that distinguishes what AI can
versus cannot technically do from what it is versus is not allowed to do within clinical and regulatory boundaries. Such
a framework could help young adults recognize that technical capability does not imply appropriate use, while bridging
AI literacy and mental health literacy in the context of AI-mediated support.
5.2.3
Design Implication: Designing for Proactive Role Clarification. Participants consistently emphasized that AI
should clearly communicate its role and boundaries at the outset of an interaction. Yet, role clarification alone may be
insufficient: we observed participants finding it particularly difficult to understand and adapt to shifts in AI behavior
when a system initially presented itself as supportive but later pushed back on certain requests.

Young adults could benefit from AI-mediated mental health support that avoids event-triggered role clarification,
particularly when clarification occurs only after users disclose mental health risk factors. According to our participants’
responses, such delayed boundary-setting can often be interpreted as an AI system’s “accountability gap” (i.e., specific AI
behaviors designed to offload accountability), rather than as reflecting its true technical capabilities. This interpretation
could inadvertently encourage users to circumvent the boundary (e.g., by reframing prompts to minimize perceived
consequences) or seek similar forms of support from AI again during highly vulnerable moments. Instead, systems
should communicate role boundaries proactively based on a tiered taxonomy of risks associated with mental health and
well-being topics [70], for instance, and maintain behavioral consistency throughout interactions. Beyond one-way
disclosure, designers might also explore co-constructing interaction norms with young adults, allowing users and AI
systems to establish expectations about the forms of support AI will and will not provide.
5.3
Engaging YOC to Actively Shape Human-AI Interactions
In addition to implications for AI and mental health literacy and design choices, we reflect on how to continue engaging
YOC in future human-AI interaction research. The co-design methodology helped surface YOC’s knowledge and
behavioral gaps in AI in a psychologically safe environment among peers with shared backgrounds. Future research
should consider combining this approach with other methods to complement data collection and insight generation.
For example, a longitudinal study design can capture the evolving trajectory of human-AI interactions via chat log data
and integrate reflective prompts (e.g., diary) [44]. Furthermore, as AI chatbot use for mental health support becomes
more taboo [69], we need more participatory methods centered on young adults to normalize the topic. Otherwise, the
more stigmatizing a topic becomes, the harder it will be to study and address associated risks and harms. Finally, while
this workshop was designed and conducted as a pilot model, we see great potential to extend it to other population
groups (e.g., younger teens, individuals with mental health diagnoses), as well as specific topics related to mental and
emotional support. We envision further participatory research around specific mental health topics relevant for YOC
(e.g., mental health destigmatization) or designing YOC-centered AI literacy education.
5.4
Limitations
The study’s findings are contextual, emerging from a two-day workshop with 13 YOC living in a racially and culturally
diverse large U.S. metropolitan area. Accordingly, they are not intended to represent all YOC perspectives in the U.S. or

elsewhere. Rather, the findings represent the situated perspectives and experiences of the participating young adults
and present implications for the design and use of AI chatbots for mental health support for other young adults who
may share similar sociocultural contexts and age-specific experiences.
The workshop also condensed substantial material—including mental health concepts, co-design activities, and AI
risks and harms—into approximately eight hours. Participants entered with varying levels of familiarity with these
topics. Future iterations, particularly those involving other age groups or communities, should examine how workshop
duration, pacing, sequencing, and preparatory materials can better support participants’ orientation and sustained
engagement within a bounded time frame.
As previously mentioned, the workshop relied on researcher-provided ChatGPT accounts and temporary chats to
protect the privacy of participants. We acknowledge that in-session chatbots had limited access to prior conversational
contexts, though participants who use ChatGPT regularly and personally did not note noticeable differences in the
context of the workshop activities. Future research should examine whether YOC’s perceptions and practices differ
when interacting with AI chatbots through their personal accounts, while developing ethically appropriate ways for
studying sensitive mental health-related interactions.
Finally, as in many facilitated research and co-design settings, facilitators may have shaped discussion through the
topics they prompted, the examples they introduced, and the ways they responded to participant contributions. These
dynamics may also have encouraged socially desirable responses. Future work could explore peer-facilitated formats or
other approaches to reduce any power dynamics in the sessions

Conclusion
Taken together, this study suggests that responsibly supporting YOC’s use of AI chatbots for mental health and well-
being requires more than improvements to AI safety and systems design alone. Instead, an emerging agenda must
address the intertwined challenges of AI design, AI literacy, mental health literacy, and YOC’s broader ecosystems of
care and support.
First, future research should develop approaches that empower YOC to critically navigate AI-mediated support while
remaining oriented toward their own well-being goals. This requires addressing gaps in AI and mental health literacy
simultaneously. In particular, YOC may need support distinguishing among technical limitations, institutional or
regulatory boundaries, and the often ambiguous promises of “personalization”—and what this means for YOC in terms
of biases and stereotypes [39, 50, 83]. Beyond understanding AI’s role, YOC may also need opportunities to reflect on
their own well-being goals. Our observations suggest that participants often discovered what they wanted from AI as
conversations unfold, while chatbots’ follow-up questions could steer interactions in unanticipated directions. Future
participatory and literacy programs could therefore develop reflective scaffolds that help YOC articulate the well-being
goals they hope to pursue and recognize when an AI interaction has drifted away from those goals.
Second, this work calls for a shift from reactive safeguards toward the co-construction of safety in human-AI interaction.
Rather than intervening only after a user has disclosed sensitive information, for example, systems should support
YOC in establishing and revising expectations, boundaries, preferences, and definitions of supportive interaction from
the outset. In-the-moment and retrospective interventions could then help users notice and reflect on whether an
interaction is drifting from their intended goals, prompting more disclosure than needed, or displacing connections to
existing sources of support.
Finally, future work should situate AI chatbot use within YOC’s broader ecosystems of care rather than treating AI
support as a private dyadic relationship. Seeking support from AI need not be a solitary path pursued only when “there
Manuscript submitted to ACM


25
are no better options.” Instead, AI could function as one resource within a broader network of care, complementing
other forms of care. Future research should explore designs that actively connect AI use with these existing support
systems, such as peers [39].
Participatory research remains essential to this agenda: it can foreground the perspectives of YOC and other
communities underrepresented in both AI and mental health research, despite their vulnerability to AI-related risks and
mental health challenges. In our study, co-design surfaced not only participants’ design priorities, but also knowledge
gaps with implications for future AI literacy education and systems design. Ultimately, ensuring responsible and safe use
of AI chatbots for mental health and well-being support requires treating YOC as situated experts and active partners
in defining how AI can—and cannot—participate in their lives.

Positionality Statement
Given this study’s focus on the mental health of young adults of color (YOC), we recognize that our identities, disciplinary
training, and relationships to the topic shaped the research process. Our interdisciplinary team brought expertise in
HCI, participatory design, behavioral social science, AI safety, clinical mental health, and public health, including prior
experience working with YOC. One author is an undergraduate student.
Most authors identify as Asian or Asian American, with diverse national, ethnic, and immigration backgrounds. The
first author is a first-generation Asian immigrant whose past experience with mental health and cultural background
informed the study’s conceptualization. At the same time, our experiences do not fully represent the situated knowledge
of the Asian, Black, and Hispanic young adults who participated in this study, particularly across differences in age,
race and ethnicity, culture, and life circumstances with rapidly emerging AI technologies.

challenges to our interpretations from the wider team and retained participant quotations, where relevant, to foreground
YOC perspectives and distinguish participants’ accounts from our interpretations.
