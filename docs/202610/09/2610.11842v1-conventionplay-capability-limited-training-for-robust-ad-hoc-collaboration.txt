ABSTRACT 

Ad-hoc collaboration often requires agents to identify and adhere to some shared convention within a cooperative task. Existing work on reinforcement learning (RL) for ad-hoc collaboration focuses on training agents that adapt to the conven-tions established by their partners. These methods fail to consider the possibility that while some partners might follow only a single fixed convention, others may themselves be capable of adapting to multiple conventions. Here we present Con-ventionPlay , an RL-based approach that teaches agents to discover their partner’s optimal convention by training against a learned population of partners that exhibit different degrees of adaptability across conventions. Some of these partners follow a single, fixed convention, while others are able to adapt to a subset of the pos-sible conventions for the task in question. The existence of partners that support a limited subset of conventions forces agents trained against this population to actively probe their partner’s capabilities, and steer their partner towards the most effective joint strategy that they are capable of following. Our experimental results demonstrate that agents trained via ConventionPlay achieve superior performance to existing ad-hoc collaboration methods against test populations of partners that are compatible with multiple conventions. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Cooperative multi-agent tasks often admit a variety of different solutions, with success depending on the agents’ ability to coordinate their strategies on a single solution. In such tasks, joint strategies can exhibit different levels of compatibility, with performance deteriorating when individual strategies are misaligned. We describe collections of mutually compatible strategies as “conventions”. Within the broader challenge of designing agents to collaborate with unseen teammates (Mirsky et al., 2022), the ability to recognize and align with established conventions is crucial for effective ad-hoc collaboration (Stone et al., 2010; Shih et al., 2021; Lerer & Peysakhovich, 2019). In this work, we propose an approach to training collaborative agents for a novel setting – interacting with partners that have varying levels of proficiency over known conventions. As a motivating example, consider an agent joining a cooperative setting such as a mechanic shop, a football pitch, or a professional kitchen. In each of these domains team members may possess a diverse and partially overlapping set of skills—e.g. in a kitchen, most chefs know how to both chop and sauté vegetables, but only some may be well versed in using a specialized tool. The skills of the individual agents determine the set of joint strategies available to the team, one of which they must actually commit to. A new chef joining the team then has two options. They could infer the status quo, and adapt to an established convention. Alternatively, they could act on an opportunity to produce a better outcome for the team by changing the joint strategy—for instance, reassigning tasks among the cooks—in the hope that the rest of the team would be able to adapt accordingly. We consider how prior methods for ad-hoc collaboration would approach this scenario. A common approach (Strouse et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023; Carroll et al., 2019) trains collaborative agents through a two-step process: First, a diverse “population” of agents is trained for the target task—e.g. teams of cooks where each team assumes a certain task allocation. Then, a “generalist” agent is 1arXiv:2610.11842v1 [cs.MA] 8 Oct 2026 Preprint. Under review. trained to coordinate with any member of that population. Each member of the initial population effectively acts as a “leader,” committed to a fixed convention and expecting its partner to adapt to it. The generalist agent trained against these partners then becomes a “follower” that adapts to its partner’s convention. A shortcoming here is that the generalist defers unconditionally to its partner’s convention, however sub-optimal, since it has never encountered a partner who could themselves adapt to a different convention. This does not reflect realistic populations, where partners vary in their capacity for adaptation—some follow only one convention, others several. Because existing methods do not model this variation, the resulting agents remain purely reactive, with no mechanism for recognizing when a partner could instead be steered toward a higher-value convention. In this work, we introduce ConventionPlay , a reinforcement learning-based approach to training adaptive agents that are capable of coordinating with partners that may themselves be adaptive. The novelty of this algorithm lies in discovering how a population partitions into conventions, and using this partition to construct a structured training population of generalist followers —agents designed to adapt to specific, restricted subsets of conventions. Exposing our agent to these adaptive partners forces it to move beyond purely reactive behavior; it must actively probe its partner to determine whether to steer the team toward a higher-value convention, or defer to the partner’s established one. Our experimental results demonstrate that ConventionPlay agents outperform state-of-the-art methods (Strouse et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2022; Sarkar et al., 2023) in identifying the most effective convention its partner can follow in a number of complex ad-hoc collaboration scenarios. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Population Diversity The most common method for achieving robust ad-hoc collaboration is by anticipating the range of possible solutions to the task. A number of methods focus on computing a diverse set of solutions and training a generalist agent against this set of strategies. Fictitious Co-Play (FCP) (Strouse et al., 2021) provides behavioral diversity using intermediate checkpoints from a self-play training process, simulating partners of varying skill levels. Carroll et al. (2019) extract diversity from human interaction data in an offline dataset. TrajeDI (Lupu et al., 2021) incentivizes policies to generate distinct trajectories, while MEP (Zhao et al., 2023) uses an entropy bonus to encourage the population to cover the entire state-action space. LIPO (Charakorn et al., 2023), CoMeDi (Sarkar et al., 2023) and ADVERSITY (Cui et al., 2023) aim to learn policies that are incompatible with each other, yet individually optimal in self-play. Our work focuses not on discovering the task’s solutions, but on leveraging these solutions to train a generalist agent that coordinates with partners of varying proficiency. To that end, our proposed method is compatible with any of the diversity-generation techniques discussed above. We contrast our approach against two additional methods that structure diversity hierarchically. TBS (Ni et al., 2026) analyzes population diversity with a return-based similarity measure to train an ensemble of best-response agents and select among them online via a theory-of-mind classifier. SyKLRBR (Cui et al., 2022) implements a cognitive hierarchy, training agents at each level as best-responses to a uniform distribution over all lower levels. Neither method, however, considers the range of adaptive behaviors a real-world partner may exhibit. 

Convention-Free Strategies Unlike methods that generate diverse solutions, another line of work focuses on zero-shot coordination (ZSC), aiming to avoid conventions that an independently-trained partner is unlikely to discover on its own. Other-Play (Hu et al., 2020) addresses the equilibrium selection problem by computing “focal” policies that are invariant to task symmetries. We argue this is unrealistic for human-AI coordination: humans do not always converge on such focal policies even when they exist, instead settling on conventions shaped by personal context and prior interactions. Off-Belief Learning (Hu et al., 2021) suppresses arbitrary conventions—e.g. secret handshakes that emerge to convey a partner’s private state information in partially observed settings. Our emphasis is not on partial observability of the environment state, but on the ad-hoc agent’s uncertainty about which equilibrium its partner prefers. Instead of avoiding conventions, our approach embraces them, aiming to construct agents capable of coordinating with any convention present in a population. 

Partner Shaping We distinguish our method from explicit partner-shaping approaches. Jaques et al. (2019) employ intrinsic rewards to explicitly encourage policies that influence the actions of other agents, rewarding influence itself as an objective. With our method, influence arises naturally 2Preprint. Under review. from a best-response objective against a population of partners with latent capabilities, requiring the agent to explore which capabilities a given partner supports. LOLA (Foerster et al., 2017) and M-FOS (Lu et al., 2022) similarly learn strategies for shaping a partner’s adaptive behavior. LOLA however requires a predefined model of the partner’s learning process. While M-FOS does not require such a model, it must repeatedly interact with a specific partner to learn one, with the ability to “reset” the partner’s learning process multiple times. Unlike these methods, ConventionPlay makes no assumptions about the specific learning algorithm the partner uses to adapt its strategy to different conventions, and learns a strategy that shapes a partner’s behavior within a single episode. Instead, 

ConventionPlay uses the structure of the task itself to identify the behaviors a partner might be capable of implementing, allowing it to quickly identify the best-response to a new partner within a single episode by probing this partner with a limited set of strategies.

Intuitively, a “convention” is a feature of the joint behavior of two agents, the knowledge of which is sufficient for these agents to coordinate. For example, the convention that all cars should drive on the right allows vehicles to avoid collisions without knowing where each one is going. Here we formalize a system of conventions as a collection of subsets C = {C1, . . . , C q } of the policy space Π

(with Π = Smi=1 Ci). Each convention is identified with a set Ci ∈ C of strategies that implement 

that convention. Note that the sets Ci need not be disjoint, as there may be adaptive policies that can implement multiple conventions depending on which partner they are paired with. A useful system should allow us to determine whether two agents are compatible based solely on the conventions that they implement. For example, in a task requiring natural language communication between agents, each convention in C could correspond to a specific language (e.g., English or French). Any two policies π and π′ belonging to a single convention Ci ∈ C will be able to successfully cooperate because they speak a common language. In this work, we define compatibility in terms of return alone, with compatible policies (those that implement compatible conventions) achieving high joint return when paired together, while pairs of incompatible policies yield low returns. Crucially, all conventions may not be equally good, with some yielding substantially higher joint return than others. ConventionPlay leverages the cross-play matrix of its initial training population P to identify conventions. For a finite population of m policies, the cross-play matrix X ∈ Rm×m is defined such that Xij = J(πi, π j ) for each i, j ∈ { 1, . . . , m }.

4 DISCOVERING CONVENTIONS 

To simplify the task of discovering a system of conventions over the full policy space Π (Section 3.3), we focus on partitioning a known, finite population of policies into disjoint conventions. We implement two algorithms for convention discovery that compute partitionings based on the cross-play matrix induced by these populations. 4.1 PARTITIONING INTO CONVENTIONS 

Given a finite population P of m policies, we first compute a cross-play matrix X, which captures the varying degrees of compatibility between members of the population. We consider two approaches to identifying an underlying system of conventions C, both of which attempt to find systems that serve as a compact representation of X. The first approach clusters the rows (or equivalently the columns) of 

X into discrete sets which become the conventions. The second uses a matrix factorization technique to compute a system of conventions that can be used to reconstruct X.

Spectral Clustering A useful system of conventions should allow us to determine appropriate responses to a policy based only on the knowledge of the convention that policy is following. A natural approach to convention discovery then is to cluster policies that have similar compatibility structures, and treat these clusters as conventions. We treat each row Xi, : as a feature vector representing policy 

πi’s interaction profile, and apply spectral clustering (Ng et al., 2001) to partition policies into groups with similar interaction signatures (Ni et al., 2026). Conventions emerge naturally as clusters of policies that achieve comparable returns with the same partners, such that a single policy should be able to achieve high return when paired with any member of a given cluster. The clustering approach requires specifying the number of conventions q as a hyperparameter, which can be determined through eigengap analysis, silhouette analysis, or domain knowledge about the task structure.

We leverage the discovered convention system to improve the effectiveness of the generalist policy learned via best-response training, which depends on the distribution of policies within P. The existing diversity methods discussed in Section 2 train against the empirical distribution of a learned policy population, which may be skewed across convention labels – common conventions over-represented while sub-optimal or hard-to-discover conventions under-represented. However, human biases over conventions may differ substantially from the biases that emerge in RL-discovered populations. We posit that a test-time partner is equally likely to follow any convention for the task, and use a discovered convention system to correct this potential imbalance in the training population. Given a population P and its convention partition {C1, . . . , C q }, we replace sampling π ∼ P with a rebalanced procedure: first sample a convention Ci uniformly from {C1, . . . , C q }, then sample a policy uniformly at random, with replacement, from Ci. This gives every convention equal weight in the training distribution regardless of its size or frequency in P. A best-response agent is then trained against the rebalanced population P′ rather than P. Experimental results on convention-rebalanced training are discussed in Section 7. 

5 CONVENTION PLAY 

Different approaches to training policies for ad-hoc collaboration can be distinguished by the as-sumptions they make about the target population of potential partners that they will eventually need to interact with. Convention-rebalanced training (4.2) models our assumption that a partner may hold any single convention with equal likelihood, but still assumes each partner is limited to just one convention. We now consider that different partners will have varying degrees of ability in coordinating across conventions. The population model of ConventionPlay is described using a variant of the Cognitive Hierarchy (CH) (Camerer et al., 2003) model. Informally, policies at level 

k ≥ 1 of the CH model are best-responses to a mixture over policies at the previous levels 0, . . . , k −1.

ConventionPlay assumes that members of the target population will sit at levels 0 or 1 of the hierarchy, and that the final policy it outputs sits at level 2. We denote a level-k policy as π(k), and construct populations at each level iteratively. We describe the assumptions made at each level of the hierarchy below, and highlight how the assumptions made by ConventionPlay differ from those made by the FCP (Strouse et al., 2021) and SyKLRBR (Cui et al., 2022) baselines; this is illustrated in Figure 1. 5Preprint. Level-0 ( K0): Fixed Convention Agents. We would expect a shared convention to emerge among a single population of agents that routinely interact with one another. These agents are brittle: they expect a partner to match their specific mode of coordination. Multiple isolated populations can in turn converge on distinct conventions. The K0 population, P(0) , represents this aggregate: diverse as a population, but composed of individually fixed-convention agents. Population diversity methods such as MEP, LIPO, and CoMeDi generate and train against a K0 population alone. In our implementation of ConventionPlay the members of the K0 population are trained using PPO in self-play. 

Level-1 ( K1): Adaptive Agents with Limited Capabilities. Training only against a K0 population ignores the possibility that some partners will be able to adapt to multiple conventions. A solution is to incorporate agents at the K1 level of the hierarchy into the training population. SyKLRBR, for example, trains against a K1 population that includes best-responses to the uniform distribution over the full K0 population P(0) . Realistic partners, however, may have varying capabilities: they may adapt to multiple conventions, but not all. ConventionPlay captures this possibility by assuming that each K1 agent only encounters a restricted subset of the possible conventions for the task. For each

This objective incentivizes π(2) to adopt a probing-and-steering strategy; it must interact with a partner to infer whether they are a restricted K0 agent, requiring strict adherence to a fixed convention, or an adaptive K1 agent, allowing the K2 agent to steer the team toward the highest-value convention within this partner’s specific capability set; the full training procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1 (Appendix C). 

6 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

This section introduces the environments and evaluation protocol used to train and test our ad-hoc agents; further details and visualizations for all environments are provided in Appendix A. 6.1 ENVIRONMENTS 

We evaluate our approach on multiple versions of three multi-agent environments where coordination on shared conventions is critical. To test robustness across different payoff structures, we configure three variants of each environment: uniform payoffs where all conventions yield equal returns ( non-diff ), partial differentiation where some conventions dominate others ( partial ), and full differentiation where each convention has a unique value ( full ). 

Repeated Matrix Game. A coordination task where agents must select matching actions across twenty trials. Agents observe a limited three step action history of their partners. Figure 2 shows payoff matrices for uniform and fully differentiated variants. Here, the expected convention is a single coordinated action. 

Point Mass Rendezvous (PMR). A spatial coordination task (Charakorn et al., 2023) where agents must rendezvous at one of six pre-configured landmarks. Agents observe their position, velocity, and distance to landmarks, along with their partner’s velocity. The reward signal is sparse, forcing agents to commit to rendezvous at a specific landmark before observing rewards for it. Here, the expected 6Preprint. Under review.

8 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

In this work, we have introduced ConventionPlay , a reinforcement learning-based approach for ad-hoc collaboration that moves beyond purely reactive partner adaptation. By introducing strategic diversity into the K1 layer of a cognitive hierarchy, agents learn to dynamically balance leading and following. Our experimental results demonstrate that when partners themselves are capable of adapting across multiple conventions, ConventionPlay outperforms existing methods by actively probing a partner’s capabilities and steering the team toward high-value conventions. 

Limitations A primary limitation in our approach is the reliance on cross-play returns for convention identification. Incorporating other behavioral signals—state-action trajectories or policy similarity (Lupu et al., 2021)—could provide more nuanced representations of agent capabilities, which would in turn improve the construction of capability-limited K1 training sets. Additionally, our evaluation focuses on relatively small populations of conventions; scaling to environments with many overlapping or complex convention structures remains an open question. 9Preprint. For spectral clustering, we first normalize the cross-play matrix X based on the expected reward range for the environment, yielding ˜X. We compute an affinity matrix A ∈ Rm×m using a standard RBF kernel over each pair of agents’ normalized feature vectors, Aij = exp( −γ∥ ˜Xi, : − ˜Xj, :∥2)

For both convention partitioning approaches (spectral clustering, and matrix factorization), we set the expected number of conventions q based on domain knowledge (detailed in Table 6, Appendix B.3). When constructing the K1 training population, we sample capability sets Kj for each of the n agents such that 1 < |K j | < q . To ensure diversity in partner flexibility while maintaining computational efficiency, we prioritize smaller capability sets during sampling. Specifically, we sample capability sets in order of increasing size: we first sample agents with 2-convention capability sets, then 3-convention sets, and so on, until we have constructed the full K1 population of size n. This stratified sampling strategy ensures that the final K2 agent encounters partners with varying degrees of adaptability, from those capable of adapting across just two conventions to those capable of adapting across nearly all conventions. For each sampled capability set Kj , we then construct the training population Pj by uniformly sampling m policies from each convention in Kj , where m is the number of policies per convention. This ensures that each K1 agent receives balanced exposure to the conventions in its capability set. Setup We construct a variant of the matrix game whose payoff structure is designed to make some conventions harder to discover than others: every convention here yields the same reward, but differs in how many compatible actions it spans (1, 1, 2, and 6 actions respectively—shown in Figure 7a). This mirrors the hard-to-find CMG-H setup of Charakorn et al. (2023), in which smaller compatible-action sets are less likely to be discovered by self-play under random exploration. As expected, training a K0 population under self-play skews heavily toward easy-to-find conventions (Figure 7b), with the hardest-to-find conventions represented by only a small number of policies. Because our ad-hoc setting assumes a test-time partner is equally likely to follow any convention, we construct a 

P(0)  

> test

D.4 ANALYSIS OF PROBING IN PMR Figure 10 shows ConventionPlay against both a fixed-convention and adaptive partners in PMR. As in the matrix game, the ad-hoc agent “probes” with movement away from the partner’s implied landmark and toward a higher-value one, in an attempt to induce the partner to adapt toward that convention. D.5 EFFECT OF K1 POPULATION SIZE 

A critical design parameter is the size of the K1 training population. With a low number of K1 agents, 

ConventionPlay should approximate naive best-response training. As population size increases, diversity of adaptive partners should enhance probing capability. Figure 11 reveals an inflection point in this relationship: performance gains are substantial up to a moderate K1 size, after which additional diversity yields diminishing returns. The exact inflection point varies by environment and convention discovery method, suggesting complex interactions between convention decomposition approach and K1 population size. A thorough analysis of these interactions remains an important direction for future work. 20 Preprint. Under review.
