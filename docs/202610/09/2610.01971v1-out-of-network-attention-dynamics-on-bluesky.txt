Abstract. Personalized social media commonly relies on explicit follow graphs to shape what content users encounter; yet how much atten-tion crosses ties they have not formed remains largely undocumented at scale. We study this question on Bluesky, a large decentralized microblog-ging platform whose default feed relies on a simple, reverse-chronological content recommender. We analyze 173 million user–author interactions (likes, reposts, replies, and quotes) collected from a near-complete plat-form dump between February and September 2023. We decompose each interaction by attention-path length (already followed, relayed by a fol-lowed account, reachable within two follow-hops, or beyond) and find that 74 .5% of interactions reach the user through an account they al-ready follow. Measured by distance in the follow graph rather than by route, 80 .6% of interaction lands within two follow hops, far beyond the 22 .4% an expected-degree null predicts. We then characterize how exploration varies across users and over tenure. A broad-reaching minor-ity generates three quarters of all exploratory activity, while aggregate declines in exploration with tenure mask three distinct individual tra-jectories. Finally, attention reaching beyond two hops converts into new follow ties at less than one third the rate of two-hop-local exploratory attention. Together, these results depict a platform where out-of-network exploration is substantial in volume but strongly constrained by network proximity and unlikely to translate into new social ties. 

Keywords: Bluesky, social media, user behavior, homophily 

## 1 Introduction 

Most large social media platforms mediate content exposure through ranking algorithms whose operation is only partially observable from the outside. This makes it difficult to disentangle two forces that can structure online attention: the social neighborhood users construct through explicit ties and the content that recommender systems choose to surface from within or beyond that neigh-borhood. Bluesky offers an unusual setting in which to study this distinction. Built on the open AT Protocol [9], it uses a default Following feed that presents posts from followed accounts in reverse chronological order. Other recommen-dation mechanisms exist, but they are user-selectable alternatives, and existing arXiv:2610.01971v1 [cs.SI] 1 Oct 2026 2 Andrea Failla et al. 

large-scale studies suggest that their uptake remained limited relative to the platform’s user base [6, 15]. The Bluesky follow graph therefore provides an un-usually meaningful structural baseline for asking how far users’ attention extends beyond the social neighborhood they have explicitly constructed. In this work, we use attention to refer to the behavioral traces through which users engage with content. Following the literature on collective attention, likes, reposts, replies, and quotes provide observable signals of where users direct their attention [16, 10]. This allows us to ask a basic structural question: when users engage with content, how much of that attention remains close to the accounts they already follow, and how much reaches beyond their existing network? The question matters because social ties are themselves selective. Homophily, i.e., the tendency of similar individuals to connect, implies that the follow graph already constitutes a non-random information neighborhood [11], while algorithmic cu-ration can further concentrate exposure around existing interests and relation-ships [14, 4]. Understanding how much of that attention the follow graph itself accounts for, before attributing any narrowing to recommendation, is therefore important for separating the role of network structure from that of content-ranking mechanisms. At the same time, attention that moves beyond one’s im-mediate neighborhood may be especially consequential. Classic work on weak ties argues that boundary-spanning connections provide access to novel infor-mation and social circles [8]. Out-of-network attention may play an analogous role: it exposes users to accounts outside their established neighborhood and may, when followed by tie formation, redraw the boundary of that neighborhood itself. Despite this, out-of-network attention remains unexplored to date. To ad-dress this gap, we organize the analysis around three research questions: RQ1: 

How much observable attention escapes the follow graph? RQ2: How is explo-ration distributed across users, and does it change as users spend more time on the platform? RQ3: Does attention that escapes the follow graph convert into new social ties? Using a near-complete collection of 173M interactions by 1.1M users, we find that most of what users act on reaches them through accounts they already follow: 74.5% of interactions arrive either from a followed author or by reshare from a followed account. 80.6% of interaction lands within two follow hops, sub-stantially above what produced by an expected-degree null model. Exploration is nevertheless substantial in absolute volume and highly heterogeneous across users: a broad-reaching minority generates most exploratory activity, while ag-gregate declines with tenure conceal distinct upward, downward, and transient individual trajectories. Finally, structural distance is associated with markedly lower tie formation: interactions beyond the two-hop neighborhood convert into follows at less than one third the rate of local exploratory interactions. ## 2 Related Work 

Network structure and attention. Online attention is shaped not only by platform design but also by users’ own social and informational preferences. Ho-mophily leads individuals to form ties with similar others [11], while selective exposure and confirmation bias make people more likely to seek out and engage with information consistent with their existing beliefs and interests [13, 7]. These mechanisms can produce substantial self-selection into socially and information-ally homogeneous neighborhoods even before algorithmic ranking is considered. On social media, such tendencies are reflected in the emergence of clustered and sometimes ideologically segregated interaction patterns [4]. Most existing work, however, characterizes this concentration at the level of communities, ideology, or information diffusion rather than measuring how far individual acts of at-tention extend from a user’s own follow neighborhood. We take this structural distance itself as the object of study. 

Boundary-spanning interaction and tie formation. Attention outside an established social neighborhood is potentially important because weak and bridg-ing ties can connect otherwise distant parts of a network and facilitate access to novel information [8, 2, 17]. Yet boundary-spanning contact does not necessarily become a persistent structural relation. Work on evolving online networks shows that tie formation is shaped by prior network position, interaction, and mech-anisms such as triadic closure [1]. We therefore distinguish between exploration 

— interaction with accounts outside the immediate follow neighborhood — and its subsequent conversion into a new follow tie. This connects the literature on information reach with that on network evolution. 

Bluesky. Existing work on Bluesky has primarily characterized the platform’s growth, network structure, and forms of participation. Failla and Rossetti [6] pro-vide a large-scale description of activity, users, and the follower network; Quelle and Bovet [15] study network topology, political structure, and the ecosystem of algorithmic feeds. More recent work examines how Starter Packs affect onboard-ing and diffusion [5] and how participation differs in intensity and behavioral style [12]. These studies describe who uses Bluesky, how they participate, and how platform mechanisms structure activity. We instead focus on the spatial relation between interaction and the follow graph. 

## 3 Methods 

Data Collection. Data for our study are collected via the Bluesky API. Bluesky stores user data in Personal Data Servers (PDSes), publicly accessible reposito-ries containing the full history of a user’s activity on the platform. Starting 4 Andrea Failla et al. 

from a sample of approximately 5 million users [6], we query their PDSes for all available activity records up to January 2025. The dataset includes multiple ac-tion types, among them follows, likes, posts, reposts, and blocks; for a complete description we refer the reader to the official documentation [3], and to [9] for an architectural overview of the protocol. For our analysis to remain computa-tionally tractable, we restrict it to the first 8 months of platform activity, from 2023-02 to 2023-09. 

Attention-Path Length. We operationalize attention as observed interaction. The primary unit of analysis is a user–author interaction du,a : the interaction between user u and a piece of content authored by a, over four types (likes, reposts, replies, quotes). We argue that an interaction is sufficient evidence that 

u attended to a’s content; on the other hand, it is not necessary evidence of attention’s absence, since exposure a user does not act on (e.g., a scroll, a read) leaves no trace in this data. Every quantity reported under this axis is therefore a lower bound on attention proper. We measure that attention along the short-est observed path between a user and the content they act on. Every interaction falls into exactly one of four tiers. An interaction is in-network if u already fol-lows a at the time of interaction, or if u = a, and out-of-network otherwise. An out-of-network interaction is relay-mediated if an account followed by u re-posted or quoted the target post p before du,a , with both the follow and relay preceding the interaction. This identifies a possible route to the post rather than its actual delivery mechanism; conversely, the absence of a relay does not rule out recommendation or search. Out-of-network interactions without such a relay are exploratory . We further distinguish exploratory interactions as two-hop local 

when a directed follow path u → i → a exists, and distant otherwise. 

Clustering. We characterize how exploratory behavior is distributed across users with two complementary clusterings, fit and validated by the procedure detailed in Appendix A. The first clusters users on the composition of their ex-ploration — rate, breadth, and repeat contact — to characterize what kind of explorer a user is. The second clusters each user’s own eight-week exploration-rate trajectory , z-scored to remove overall level, to characterize whether that behavior is rising, falling, or transient over their early tenure. 

Conversion Rate Analysis. We treat a user–author pair’s first follow after 

t0, if any, as the outcome of interest and model it in two complementary ways. Descriptively, we report the share of pairs converting by 7, 15, and 30 days, over-all and split by the scope of the pair’s first contact (two-hop local vs. distant) and crossed with first-event type. Adjusted, we fit pair-level logistic regressions of 30-day conversion on first-event and user-covariate features (full specification in Appendix A); this isolates the association between scope and conversion net of the user characteristics we can measure, without claiming a causal effect of exploring further from one’s network. ## 4 Results 

4.1 RQ1: How Much Attention Escapes the Follow Graph? 

Of 1 .1M users with at least one analyzed interaction, 575K contribute an ex-plored pair. Fig. 1 (top) decomposes the 173M interactions by attention-path length: 62 .3% involve an already-followed author, 12 .2% are relay-mediated by a followed account, 18 .3% reach an author two follow-hops away, and 7 .2% ex-tend beyond two hops. These tiers support two distinct summaries. First, 74 .5% of interactions are routed through an account the user already follows, either as author or relay. This concerns possible delivery routes, not graph distance: a relay does not imply a two-hop follow path to the original author. Second, considering follow-graph distance and excluding relays from the distance count, 80 .6% of interactions reach authors within two follow hops: 62 .3% at one hop and 18 .3% at two. Because two-hop reachability is evaluated only for non-relayed interactions, this is a lower bound: some relay-mediated interactions may also lie within two hops. To assess whether this concentration exceeds what degree structure alone would imply, Fig. 1 (bottom) compares the observed share with an expected-degree null preserving users’ in- and out-degree in expectation. 4.2 RQ2: How is Exploration Distributed Across Users, and Does it Change with Tenure? 

We first ask, at a coarse grain, whether the users who follow and interact most are also the ones whose exploratory attention stays closest to home. Ranking users into deciles by total interaction volume, and separately by out-degree (ac-counts followed), the first decile holds the tenth of users with the lowest value on that measure and the tenth decile the tenth with the highest. Local share of exploratory interaction climbs from 20 .1% in the first activity decile to 74 .3% in the tenth, and from 3 .3% to 80 .4% across out-degree deciles: the users who interact and follow most are the ones whose exploration stays closest to home, a between-user gradient we return to with the clustering below. We then move to two complementary clusterings of user behavior. 

Composition clustering. We cluster users on what their exploration looks like – exploration rate, breadth (distinct explored authors), and repeat contact per author, each standardized before clustering for the 246 K users with at least ten explored events. This yields K = 4 clusters. Each feature singles out a different cluster against a shared baseline. Cluster 3, the 53 .3%-of-users majority (“nar-row, occasional, low-rate” in Figure 2), is low on all three features; each other cluster departs from that baseline on exactly one. Breadth singles out the “broad, occasional, low-rate” cluster (22 .7% of users, reaching 293 .9 authors on average, five to fourteen times as many as any other cluster); repeat contact singles out the “narrow, repeated, low-rate” cluster (5 .2% of users, revisiting each explored author 5 .2 times on average against 1 .4–1 .8 for the rest); exploration rate singles out the “narrow, occasional, high-rate” cluster (18 .8% of users, 66 .7% of whose activity is exploratory) despite it reaching only 31 .6 authors on average, among the narrowest of the four. We can see that breadth and exploration rate are not necessarily positively related: the highest-rate cluster is one of the narrowest-reaching, while the broadest-reaching cluster explores at a middling 29 .7% and seldom returns (1 .76 events per author). The broad, low-repeat cluster is 22 .7% of users but 74 .4% of all explored events, while the narrow, occasional majority contributes 9 .1%: exploration volume is concentrated in users who reach widely and lightly, not in those who explore most intensively per interaction. 

Trend-shape clustering. A complementary view asks whether that rate itself moves with tenure, and in what direction. Figure 3 shows a Downward cluster (34 .6% of users, 38 .1% → 20 .7% over the window), an Upward cluster of nearly the same size (35 .0%, 16 .3% → 25 .0%), and a Transient cluster that rises then falls within the window (30 .4%, peaking at 26 .7% in week 2 before ending at 16 .9%). The pooled rate over these users does decline, from 24 .3% at t = 0 to 21 .0% at t = 7, but that aggregate obscures real heterogeneity: a third of users are trending the other way entirely.

## 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

Our results show that attention on Bluesky is strongly anchored to the social network users have already constructed. Although one quarter of interactions es-cape the follow graph, most remain structurally nearby: 80 .6% of all interactions Out-of-Network Dynamics on Bluesky 9

land on an author within two follow hops, and a further 12 .2% reach the user by reshare from an account they follow. The large gap from the expected-degree null — 80 .8% against 22 .4% on the scored interactions — further shows that this concentration is not a trivial consequence of degree structure. In a platform where the follow graph constitutes a meaningful baseline for content access, net-work proximity remains a powerful correlate of where interaction occurs. Exploration beyond this neighborhood is nevertheless substantial and highly un-even. A relatively small group of broad-reaching users generates most exploratory activity, while longitudinal patterns show that exploration is not a single sta-ble behavioral tendency: declining, increasing, and transient trajectories coexist. The aggregate decline with tenure therefore conceals considerable individual heterogeneity. More importantly, structural distance appears consequential for network evolution. Contacts beyond two hops convert into follows at only 3 .3%, compared with 11 .9% for local exploratory contacts, and the difference remains after adjustment for observed user characteristics. Distant attention is therefore not simply less common; it is also less likely to become incorporated into the user’s subsequent social network. Concluding, these findings suggest a distinction between exploration as exposure 

and exploration as structural change . Users frequently interact beyond accounts they already follow, but most such activity remains close to the existing graph, and encounters that travel further rarely produce new ties. This qualifies the in-tuitive idea that boundary-spanning attention necessarily expands users’ social neighborhoods: on Bluesky, exploration is substantial in volume, but its capacity to reshape the follow graph appears limited. These conclusions remain subject to several limitations. Interaction records cap-ture acted-upon attention rather than exposure itself, two-hop locality is recon-structed from the available follow graph rather than from exact delivery paths, and the conversion models are observational and do not establish a causal ef-fect of network distance. The study also covers an early period of Bluesky’s growth, so the magnitude of these patterns may change as the platform and its recommendation ecosystem evolve. Future work should test whether the same relationship between network proximity, exploration, and tie formation holds in later periods and on platforms where algorithmic ranking plays a more central role.

Sensitivity. There are three ways the scope effect could be an artifact. The first is confounding by the target: distant authors might simply be harder to follow. Adding the author’s in-degree, out-degree and received-interaction volume as of 

t0, each estimated on the sampled side of the collection, moves the odds ratio from 0 .351 to 0 .289. Author-side connectivity therefore does not explain away the association between structural proximity and subsequent tie formation. The second is that the effect belongs to one kind of user: someone who follows few accounts has a small two-hop neighbourhood, so almost everything they touch counts as distant by construction. Refitting on fifteen overlapping subsets, by activity, degree, tenure, exploration rate and position in the observation window, leaves the odds ratio between 0 .331 and 0 .380, every interval excluding one. The penalty on distant contact is therefore a regularity of tie formation across the population, not an artifact of users whose neighbourhoods are too small to reach locally. The third is that scope only stands in for how the pair developed: distant pairs might convert less simply because they never interacted again. Replacing the indicator with five measures of the pair’s own event sequence over the thirty days after t0 — its volume, its spread across days, its composition by event type, and the share of those events that were exploratory and two-hop local — puts that local share at 1 .682 across its full range, well short of the 1 /0.351 = 2 .849 redundancy would imply.
