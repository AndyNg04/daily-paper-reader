## Abstract 

Recent research has revealed that accessibility in virtual collabora-tion is not only a technical problem but also depends on allyship: the informal, interpersonal practices through which people support others’ accessibility needs. Yet little research has examined how artificial intelligence (AI) might support allyship in collaborative accessibility contexts. To address this gap, we conducted a study where 18 participants (10 disabled, 8 non-disabled) reflected on four allyship scenarios with novel AI tools. Participants valued AI designs that they anticipated could help teammates express, in-terpret, and coordinate around access needs by reducing repeated disclosure and making allyship more actionable. At the same time, participants raised concerns when AI acted without consent, mis-represented users’ intentions, or displaced the interpersonal work of allyship. Based on our findings, we introduce allyship-support tools as a class of accessibility technologies, offer design guidelines, and discuss AI in particular as a means of supporting allyship.

## 1 Introduction 

Virtual collaboration has become a fundamental part of how people work together. Tools like virtual meeting platforms, messaging tools, and shared documents allow team members to connect across distances and offer flexibility. However, virtual collaboration also introduces challenges, especially for people with disabilities. Prior work has documented how virtual collaboration creates barriers to participation, coordination, and access to shared information across a range of disability contexts. For example, studies with blind and low vision professionals highlight how screen sharing and visually oriented interfaces limit access to meeting content [16]. Research with neurodivergent professionals points to challenges such as sensory overload and the need for flexible communication norms in remote meetings [22, 81]. These barriers can affect job performance, career growth, and sense of belonging on a team [1, 21]. In response to these barriers, researchers have long explored technologies to support accessibility. These technologies focus on improving individuals’ technical access to virtual collaboration, such as screen readers and captioning systems [22, 48, 70]. Compa-nies have extended these approaches through AI-powered features that automatically generate captions or describe visual content [28, 29, 57, 82]. While essential, these approaches focus on tech-nical access to tools. However, research on mixed-ability teams suggests that accessibility is not only determined by technical ac-cess, but also shaped by interpersonal and relational dynamics [33, 45, 77, 79]. In recent years, research has revealed that accessibility in team-work is not just a technical problem, but depends on team norms, expectations, and practices, including how members share access needs, coordinate support, and take on accessibility-related labor [2, 33, 45]. In these settings, accessibility is not simply provided to individuals, but is collectively negotiated through ongoing interac-tions between disabled and non-disabled teammates [5, 11, 73, 77]. Jung et al. [33] further found that allyship , the actions taken to support disabled team members, is foundational to enabling these practices. Allyship is enacted through everyday team interactions, such as recognizing access needs, offering support, and coordinat-ing accommodations, that shape how accessibility is collaboratively negotiated over time [33]. Despite this, little work has examined how technology might support the relational dynamics through which accessibility is enacted in collaborative work. arXiv:2610.09061v1 [cs.HC] 6 Oct 2026 ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal Jung et al. 

Meanwhile, artificial intelligence (AI) has shown promise to support collaborative work, such as for creative tasks and decision-making. Human-AI interaction research suggests that AI tools and agents can shape collaboration by helping teammates coordinate, communicate, and navigate group dynamics. For example, Suh et al. [69] found that AI tools shape social dynamics in collaborative settings by functioning as a co-creative mediator that seeds shared starting points, sustains creative momentum, and reduces inter-personal friction. Related work on autonomous AI agents further highlights how AI can influence group processes, as Johnson et al. [32] explored proactive AI agents as teammates that contribute to group discussions by introducing perspectives, facilitating co-ordination, and shaping team reasoning processes. This creates an opportunity to ask whether AI with agentic capabilities might also support allyship in teams, since it can identify opportunities for support in moment-to-moment interactions [32, 61]. Yet, little work has examined how AI might support allyship in accessibility contexts. To address this gap, we explore how AI might be designed to support allyship. We focus on AI because allyship often depends on recognizing when support may be needed, identifying what form of support may be appropriate, and coordinating responses as collab-oration unfolds. AI with agentic capabilities may be able to support these moment-to-moment processes by surfacing relevant context, suggesting possible actions, or mediating coordination between teammates. Prior work demonstrates that this is a particularly chal-lenging problem. Jung et al. [33] found that although allyship is an important component of strong teamwork in mixed-ability teams, it is often difficult to enact in practice. For disabled teammates, disclosing access needs can carry professional risks, such as stigma and being perceived as less capable. At the same time, non-disabled teammates may want to help but remain uncertain about when sup-port is needed, what form it should take, and how to offer it without overstepping. Together, these tensions make allyship a complex interpersonal process that directly shapes teamwork. Therefore, we ask: What designs of AI can support allyship in virtual collaboration? 

We conducted a scenario-based study with 18 participants who collaborate in mixed-ability teams, including ten who identified as disabled and eight who identified as non-disabled. A scenario-based study allowed us to explore future designs of AI and surface partici-pants’ expectations, critiques, and concerns [15]. We developed four scenarios depicting situations in which teammates gave or received allyship using novel AI tools in virtual collaboration platforms. We conducted 60-minute sessions with each participant over a virtual meeting platform. In each session, we presented the four scenarios one at a time; for each, the researcher first walked through the scenario and then conducted a semi-structured interview to un-derstand participants’ reactions, perceptions, and preferences for designs of AI to support allyship. Our findings suggest that AI was perceived as most promising when it helped teammates express, interpret, and coordinate around access needs while preserving human judgment and relational con-trol. Participants valued designs that reduced repeated disclosure, surfaced opportunities to provide support, and distributed allyship-related labor across the team. However, participants were less re-ceptive to AI that acted without consent, risked misrepresenting users’ intentions, or weakened the authenticity and interpersonal meaning of support. These findings suggest that AI can best sup-port allyship not by automating it, but by making support more actionable while preserving agency, authenticity, and the relational nature of teamwork. Building on these findings, we argue that supporting accessi-bility in virtual teams requires rethinking the role of technology: from a tool that provides individual access to one that scaffolds the interpersonal and organizational processes through which access is negotiated, enacted, and sustained. We propose allyship-support tools as a class of accessibility technologies that scaffold how team-mates recognize, communicate, coordinate, and respond to access needs within ongoing collaborative relationships. We make two contributions. First, we present findings from a scenario-based study with disabled and non-disabled participants examining how participants perceived possible AI designs for sup-porting allyship in virtual collaboration. This empirical contribution shows how participants anticipated AI might help teammates ex-press, interpret, and coordinate around access needs, while also surfacing tensions around agency, authenticity, and control. Sec-ond, building from these findings, we introduce the concept of 

allyship-support tools as a broader class of accessibility technolo-gies. This conceptual contribution shifts the focus of accessibility from individual access to the relational processes through which support is enacted in teamwork. We offer design guidelines that emphasize actionability, consent, and the preservation of human judgment, offering guidance for how AI-supported systems, and allyship-support tools more broadly, might support allyship without displacing the interpersonal work that sustains inclusive teamwork. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Accessible Virtual Collaboration 

Virtual collaboration presents substantial accessibility barriers for people with disabilities. Prior work has identified challenges such as inaccessible screen-sharing interfaces, absent or unreliable cap-tions, and shared documents that are incompatible with assistive technologies [1, 16, 21, 22, 36, 37, 45, 68, 81]. To navigate these bar-riers, people with disabilities use assistive technologies and often undertake additional preparatory work such as requesting materials in advance, adjusting platform settings, or developing workarounds for inaccessible interfaces and documents [11, 16, 22, 58, 81]. However, these workarounds and preparatory work are still of-ten insufficient because many accessibility barriers stem from the social organization of collaboration rather than from the software itself. A screen reader cannot compensate when a teammate shares visual content without describing it, and captions do not resolve the difficulty of following overlapping or unstructured discussion [16, 45]. As a result, accessibility in virtual collaboration often de-pends on what teammates do for one another. Prior work shows that teammates may support access by sharing materials before meetings, describing visual content, checking communication pref-erences, or making space to discuss needs [40, 45, 73, 79]. These practices are often essential, but ad hoc and uneven, depending on whether teammates know about others’ needs, understand what support would be useful, and are willing to act [33, 45, 68, 79]. points to the need for a more structured understanding of how accessibility can be supported in virtual collaboration. 

## 2.2 Allyship in Disability Contexts 

Allyship refers to actions taken to support or advocate for mem-bers of marginalized groups [12]. In disability contexts, allyship often takes the form of interpersonal support rather than formal ac-commodations, which are typically institutional and policy-driven [33, 45, 55, 56, 77]. Prior work on collaborative accessibility and interdependence similarly shows that access is negotiated through interaction between disabled and non-disabled collaborators rather than simply provided by one person to another [5, 11, 73]. These practices include describing visual content, checking communica-tion preferences, and adjusting collaboration behaviors to support access needs [5, 33, 45, 73]. Yildiz and Subasi [77] further describe how members of mixed-ability teams engage in collective access and ongoing dialogue about one another’s needs. Jung et al. [33] brought this conversation more directly into mixed-ability virtual teamwork, showing that accessibility practices shaped not only access, but also coordination, participation, and collaboration. They identified two conditions for effective allyship: disabled teammates must be able to express their needs, and allies need clear, actionable ways to respond. At the same time, prior work highlights tensions that can make allyship difficult to enact. Disabled individuals may hesitate to disclose disabilities or request support because of stigma, power dynamics, or concerns about being perceived as burdensome [16, 42, 58, 72], while allies may be unsure when and how to help without overstepping [33, 45]. Thus, prior work has established the importance of allyship for strong collaboration and identified conditions for its success or failure. However, it has not examined in depth how AI might support allyship. 

## 2.3 Technology that Supports Interpersonal Dynamics in Collaboration 

While there has been little research investigating allyship in HCI and CSCW, researchers have explored broader ways that technolo-gies can support interpersonal dynamics in collaboration. One line of work examines how systems support people in supportive roles. For example, Lee et al. [39] studied people who remotely supported family members and friends with mental health conditions and identified opportunities for technology to help them maintain com-munication, coordinate care, and manage emotional strain. Sim-ilarly, Wang et al. [74] examined peer counselors and identified opportunities for systems to help supporters better understand others’ situations and coordinate appropriate responses. However, this literature assumes relatively stable and unidirectional support roles, focusing on how supporters carry out their role, rather than on how allyship is negotiated between collaborators in reciprocal, ongoing relationships. Since allyship is often enacted through everyday interpersonal dynamics such as communication, participation, coordination, and trust, a second relevant line of work examines how collaborative technologies shape these aspects of teamwork [61, 62]. For example, researchers have explored communication features that support more inclusive interaction, such as backchannels for additional com-munication during meetings [51], visual communication layers for coordinating attention and participation [35], and tools that support disclosure and help teammates better understand communication needs [40]. Building on this broader research on technologies that shape in-terpersonal dynamics in collaboration, recent work examines how AI, including agentic capabilities, can influence social dynamics in group work. This line of work is especially relevant as it shows that AI can affect how collaborators align, relate to one another, and nav-igate trust. Suh et al. [69] found that generative AI could sometimes act as a social connector by helping collaborators align quickly. At the same time, other work shows that AI can also introduce interpersonal tensions rather than simply improving connections. For example, Johnson et al. [32] found that proactive AI agents in synchronous teamwork raised concerns about trust, inclusivity, and social integration. Hwang et al. [31] showed that AI agents speaking on a person’s behalf raised concerns about authenticity, trust, and autonomy in social interaction. Fu et al. [26] found that while users valued AI-mediated communication tools for reduc-ing anxiety, they also expressed concerns about inauthenticity and the erosion of personal voice, particularly in close relationships. Prior work has explored proactive and context-aware AI systems that guide collaboration, but these have not been examined in the context of accessibility and allyship in mixed-ability teams. This literature demonstrates that technology can shape how collaborators communicate, coordinate, and relate to one another. However, this work has primarily focused either on one-directional support within structured roles or on collaboration dynamics more generally. By contrast, allyship in mixed-ability teams is peer-based, reciprocal, and embedded in ongoing teamwork. Supporting it re-quires attention not only to coordination, but also to how teammates recognize access needs, decide how to respond, and negotiate the interpersonal meaning of support. Our work addresses this gap by exploring the design of AI to support allyship. 

## 3 Method 

To answer our research question, we conducted a scenario-based study to examine disabled and non-disabled people’s preferences for AI to support allyship. Our method draws on scenario-based design, which uses narratives of future use as low-fidelity design artifacts to explore how technologies may be experienced before they are implemented. Unlike interview studies, which primarily elicit reflections on past experiences, scenario-based studies present participants with hypothetical situations, allowing researchers to surface expectations, critiques, concerns, and values for technolo-gies that do not yet exist but may be feasible in the near future [15]. To operationalize this approach, we developed a set of hypothet-ical collaboration scenarios that depict how AI might be used in mixed-ability teams. Each scenario was presented as a storyboard il-lustrating a concrete collaboration situation together with a form of AI. These storyboards enabled participants to react to and reflect on potential uses of AI in the context of teamwork [18, 20, 44, 66, 80]. We first conducted a formative study to inform the development of our scenarios. ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal Jung et al. 

## 3.1 Formative Study: Informing the Creation of Scenarios 

Our formative study helped us prepare the main scenario-based study in two ways. First, it refined the problem space by identifying realistic allyship-related situations in mixed-ability teamwork that the scenarios should depict. These situations involved recurring in-terpersonal or coordination challenges and could plausibly benefit from AI. Second, it narrowed the design space by surfacing partici-pants’ preferences for how AI should be represented, presented, and behave within those situations. Since the main study aimed to elicit participant reactions to AI support for allyship, the scenarios for the main study needed enough structure for participants to reason about specific design possibilities without becoming an open-ended design study. To identify the problem space, we asked participants to reflect on allyship-related challenges they had experienced in mixed-ability teamwork and to describe situations in which AI might be valuable. Prior work has shown that accessibility in mixed-ability teamwork involves ongoing negotiation of support, including challenges in articulating access needs, determining when and how to offer help, and balancing support with respect for autonomy [33]. We built on this work by using these previously identified challenges as a starting point for the formative study, probing how they appeared in participants’ lived experiences and whether there were additional allyship-related situations in which AI might be useful. To explore how AI might support these allyship-related situa-tions, we designed formative storyboards that varied how AI was represented, how visible its actions were to the team, and how proactively it behaved. We looked to research on AI with agentic capabilities in social and collaborative contexts. We focused on AI with agentic capabilities because allyship unfolds through moment-to-moment interactions [33]. Unlike static or user-initiated features, AI with agentic capabilities can identify opportunities for support in context as collaboration unfolds [32, 61]. Prior work highlights several key dimensions: embodiment, vis-ibility, and initiative. Embodiment defines how AI is represented, which can influence users’ sense of social presence and expecta-tions. For example, virtual representations can enhance telepres-ence, while highly anthropomorphic forms may raise expectations that, if unmet, reduce perceived effectiveness [54]. Visibility refers to how visible the AI’s interactions are within team interactions. Prior work shows that different levels of visibility, from subtle to overt, can shape trust and coordination by improving users’ un-derstanding of the system’s behavior and intentions, as well as accountability for actions taken within the collaboration. However, increased visibility can also lead to over-reliance, misinterpreta-tion, and added cognitive or social burden [17, 24, 63]. Initiative describes the extent to which AI acts proactively versus passively; mismatches between expected and actual levels of initiative can undermine user trust and acceptance, and appropriate levels de-pend on task context and team dynamics [41, 49, 53]. We used these dimensions to structure and narrow the design space. 

3.1.1 Participants and Procedure. We recruited six participants (four disabled and two non-disabled) with experience working in mixed-ability teams, whose ages ranged from 21 to 44 years old. Participants were recruited through mailing lists, social media, and snowball sampling. Participants were invited to self-identify any disabilities, and disclosure was entirely voluntary. Each 60-minute session had two chronological parts. First, we asked participants to reflect on allyship-related challenges they had experienced in past teamwork and to describe situations in which AI might have been valuable. Second, we presented storyboards depicting different forms of AI to support allyship in virtual collab-oration. These storyboards helped participants compare concrete versions of three design dimensions that would have been difficult to evaluate abstractly: embodiment (text-based versus anthropomor-phic representations), visibility (private versus public interactions), and initiative (proactive versus passive behavior). These formative storyboards were not the same as the four scenarios used in the main study; rather, they were storyboards used to compare possible AI design dimensions, which informed the final scenario designs described in Section 3.2. We used semi-structured interviews to elicit participants’ reac-tions, as well as examples of situations in which such systems might be useful. We analyzed the sessions through open descriptive codes, focusing on participants’ descriptions of preferences, concerns, and prior experiences that informed their reactions to the storyboards. 

3.1.2 Findings. While prior work has examined mixed-ability team-work more broadly and surfaced allyship-related challenges [2, 33, 45], our formative study specifically asked about allyship chal-lenges. We asked about how participants experienced allyship-related breakdowns and where they imagined AI support could be valuable. Participants described difficulty articulating their acces-sibility needs and identifying appropriate forms of support. They also noted that requesting support could feel uncomfortable or exposing, particularly in group settings, highlighting the poten-tial value of anonymity or mediated communication. In addition, participants described uncertainty about when and how to offer help, even when motivated to be supportive, indicating opportuni-ties for systems that surface appropriate moments for intervention. Finally, participants emphasized tensions in how support is com-municated, including concerns about overstepping, paternalism, or misinterpreting others’ needs. In addition, participants expressed preferences regarding how AI should be integrated into teamwork, which shaped the final scenarios. They favored text-based interactions over anthropomor-phic representations, private communication channels over public interventions, and systems that behaved passively unless users had specified preferences in advance. We set aside design alternatives such as more anthropomorphic AI representations, public-facing interventions visible to the team, and proactive AI behavior be-cause participants expressed concerns that such design choices could heighten social risk or reduce user control. Together, these findings helped us establish (1) the problem space by identifying which allyship-related situations are most appro-priate for AI, and (2) design preferences for how AI should be represented, presented, and behave when supporting allyship. We elaborate on these in the following section.

Scenario 1: Accessibility Profile Support. This scenario ex-plored how AI might help make accessibility preferences clear. This design responds to our formative study finding that teammates sometimes struggled to articulate what kinds of support would be helpful and found examples useful for recognizing potentially helpful accommodations. In the scenario, Alex, an autistic team member who experienced difficulty meeting deadlines, prepared to collaborate on a project. Rather than explaining his needs from scratch to each teammate, Alex interacted privately with an AI to create an accessibility profile. The AI used information Alex provided about his disability and work-related challenges to suggest possible support options, such as receiving reminders before deadlines, scheduling coworking ses-sions, allowing flexibility with deadlines, or enabling captions. Alex selected the options that reflected how he preferred to work. Only the preferences Alex selected were saved to his profile and made available to teammates, allowing them to understand his support preferences and consider how to respond when coordinating work together. 

Scenario 2: Anonymous Support Request. This scenario ex-plored how AI might mediate requests for support when directly asking a team member could feel uncomfortable or exposing. In the formative study, participants raised anonymity as a potential means of reducing discomfort and privacy concerns around requesting support, consistent with existing features in platforms like Zoom that allow users to request captions anonymously. In the scenario, Casey was a new team member with ADHD. He attended a meeting where Jordan, the facilitator, shared multi-ple documents on the screen. Casey had previously indicated that receiving shared materials helped reduce cognitive overload. The AI used this preference and the meeting context to determine that the materials might be helpful. It privately asked Casey whether he wanted help requesting them. After Casey said yes, the AI of-fered several routing options, including asking the whole group, privately notifying a trusted ally, or asking the meeting facilitator. Casey chose to notify Jordan, and the AI prompted Jordan to share ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal Jung et al. 

the links or email the materials without requiring Casey to ask directly in the group. 

Scenario 3: Support Opportunity Prompts. This scenario ex-plored how AI might help with establishing and coordinating team-mates’ support preferences. Prior work has proposed technology-mediated reminders and coordination aids for accommodation-related support [45], while our formative study findings suggest that teammates may hesitate to intervene when unsure whether help is wanted or what form it should take, and that preferences might be used by AI to make suggestions. In the scenario, Riley was a team member with a chronic illness and ADHD. She had previously indicated in her accessibility pro-file that coworking sessions helped her with accountability. Riley shared in a team chat with Ethan and Sam that she was worried about forgetting to update a draft before the next day’s meeting. The AI recognized this as a possible support moment based on Riley’s message and saved preference. It first privately offered to set a reminder for Riley, which she accepted. The AI then privately prompted Ethan and Sam to consider offering a coworking session and disclosed that the suggestion was based on Riley’s stated pref-erence. After Ethan and Sam agreed and offered the session to Riley, she accepted, and the AI scheduled it. 

Scenario 4: Phrasing Alternatives for Offering Help. This scenario addressed the challenge where well-intentioned help can be perceived as overstepping or paternalistic [33]. Our formative study participants described these challenges when offering sup-port as an ally. Drawing from disability etiquette principles that emphasize autonomy and agency [19, 23, 52, 71, 75], the design explores how AI might encourage more reflective communication among teammates. In the scenario, Taylor was a blind team member who mentioned in the team chat that she was having difficulty with the images in a set of presentation slides. Wanting to help, Noah, a teammate, began typing, “I’ll take care of that.” Before he sent the message, the AI used Taylor’s message and Noah’s draft response to determine that Noah’s phrasing might unintentionally overstep by taking control of the task. The AI privately suggested alternative phrasings, such as, “Would you like me to take on part of this task?” or “Which im-ages do you need help with?” It also explained that these responses might be more appropriate because they left room for Taylor to express what kind of support would be helpful. Noah then revised the message accordingly, which created space for Taylor to specify her preferences. 

## 3.3 Participant Recruitment and Demographics 

To be eligible for the study, participants had to (1) be part of a mixed-ability team that included both disabled and non-disabled members, (2) regularly use virtual meetings for teamwork, and (3) for disabled participants, self-report having experienced challenges in performing responsibilities due to their disability in virtual meet-ing contexts. These criteria ensured that participants had relevant, lived experience with accessibility and collaboration in virtual team settings, allowing us to ground our findings in real-world practices and challenges. We recruited participants through mailing lists for people with disabilities and people interested in accessibility topics, social me-dia sites, online groups for people interested in accessibility topics, posters on our university’s campus, and snowball sampling. Re-cruitment materials described the study as seeking people with experience working in mixed-ability teams that collaborate virtu-ally. To determine eligibility, interested individuals first completed a survey in which we asked about their disability status, whether they worked on a team with both disabled and non-disabled mem-bers, and their use of virtual meetings. Participants were invited to self-identify any disabilities, and disclosure was entirely voluntary. Additionally, to respect privacy and considerations around disability status, we clarified that participants were not required to disclose the specific disabilities of their team members. Instead, participants were only asked to confirm whether their teams included members with disabilities. This approach allowed us to identify mixed-ability teams without requiring personal or detailed information about other team members’ disabilities. This study and all recruitment materials were approved by our university’s Institutional Review Board. We recruited 18 participants (5 male, 13 female). Ten participants identified as having a disability (e.g., blind or low vision, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety, attention deficit disorder (ADD)). Eight participants identified as not having a dis-ability. Participant ages ranged from 20 to 44 years (mean = 27.2, standard deviation = 5.7). Table 2 shows detailed participant demographics. We refer to non-disabled participants as N# and disabled participants as D#. 

## 3.4 Procedure 

We conducted 60-minute scenario-based study sessions with each participant via a virtual meeting platform. Prior to the session, participants completed a demographic survey (e.g., age, gender, and current occupation). All interviews were audio-recorded, and participants received a $30 gift card for their time. See Table 2 for participant details. We used the same protocol for both disabled and non-disabled participants, since in mixed-ability teams, both disabled and non-disabled members may act as allies in different situations, and non-disabled participants may also experience temporary or situ-ational needs. Using one protocol allowed us to examine how all participants understood, enacted, and responded to allyship across different roles, rather than treating disabled participants solely as recipients of support and non-disabled participants solely as allies. During the session, we presented four scenarios (see Section 3.2), one at a time. For each scenario, the researcher first walked the participant through the storyboard step by step and also described it verbally to ensure accessibility. This walkthrough introduced the collaboration context, the AI, and each panel of the storyboard. We then conducted a semi-structured interview about that scenario. For each scenario, the walkthrough and interview took around ten to fifteen minutes in total. We began with the same broad question, “What is your gut reaction to this design?” to capture participants’ initial reactions. For example, if a scenario depicted a teammate receiving AI support, we asked how they would feel if they were in that teammate’s position or if that person were their teammate (e.g., “If Riley were your teammate, how would AI nudges to others affect your confidence or hesitation to reach out?”). 

• Disclosure and willingness to express access needs. For scenarios involving the expression of access needs and pref-erences, we asked disabled participants additional questions about how the design might shape their willingness to dis-close needs or request support. For example, in Scenario 1, which involved creating an accessibility profile, we asked, “How might a design like this change how you think about expressing your needs?” 

• Broader applicability of allyship and support. We in-vited participants to reflect on allyship more broadly across scenarios, including situations in which non-disabled team-mates might need support due to illness, caregiving respon-sibilities, or other temporary or situational challenges. Throughout the session, we elicited details about participants’ prior experiences that informed their answers. For example, we asked questions such as, “Can you recall a recent instance when. . . ” We probed for details about these experiences, including who was involved, what needs were addressed, what actions were taken, how others responded, and what challenges or tensions emerged. 

## 3.5 Data Analysis 

All sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed. During the ses-sions, the research team took notes and debriefed after each one to discuss initial observations. We analyzed transcripts from dis-abled and non-disabled participants together to examine allyship as a relational practice that emerges through interactions between teammates, rather than as separate experiences. We conducted our analysis in two phases. In the first phase, two researchers performed descriptive open coding to summarize the data without interpretation [60]. During this process, we priori-tized segments that included concrete examples of interactions or ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal Jung et al. 

situations, excluding abstract or generalized statements about what participants believed they should do or felt socially expected to do. This helped us analyze how allyship was enacted in practice rather than described only in the abstract. The researchers also wrote analytical memos to note emerging observations. To ensure consis-tency and minimize bias, the two researchers independently coded the same transcript and reconciled discrepancies. They repeated this process with a second transcript to ensure alignment before dividing and coding the remaining transcripts independently. In the second phase, we conducted interpretive open coding to identify the concepts emerging from the data [60]. We followed the same process: two researchers independently coded the same transcript, met to discuss, repeated with a second transcript, and then divided the remaining transcripts. We adopted this two-phase approach to first identify and focus on concrete accounts of partici-pants’ experiences, and then develop interpretations of participants’ experiences. After all transcripts were coded, we conducted a cross-case com-parison analysis [50] to identify patterns across participants. We constructed a matrix with 26 interpretive codes as rows and 18 participants as columns. In each cell where a participant was as-sociated with the code, we described how the code manifested for that participant. This allowed us to examine how each construct manifested across participants, identify variations and edge cases, and surface broader themes. We then reviewed the matrix to identify higher-level patterns and relationships across codes, grouping related constructs into broader thematic categories that informed our two high-level find-ings themes: (1) reducing the labor of expressing, interpreting, and coordinating access needs, and (2) preserving human judgment, control, and team dynamics. Throughout the analysis, the research team met regularly to discuss emerging themes and maintain align-ment. Our analysis resulted in two higher-level themes, six second-level themes, and 26 interpretive codes. For example, we applied the code “AI mediation as threat to authenticity” to the quote, “If Riley later gets to know that [her teammate] was prompted by an AI to do that, she might feel that it’s coming from an inauthentic place” (N7). A summarized version of the matrix with themes is provided in Tables 4 and 5 in Appendix B. 

## 4 Findings 4.1 Reducing the Labor of Expressing, Interpreting, and Coordinating Access Needs 

4.1.1 Addressing Breakdowns in Interpreting Needs and Responses. 

Both disabled and non-disabled participants, but especially non-disabled participants, wanted to support others but struggled to determine what someone needed or whether intervening would be appropriate at all. This uncertainty was often tied to concerns about overstepping or appearing patronizing. For example, N14 worked for several years at a global human rights organization on a large distributed team. She explained that “sometimes it doesn’t feel like there’s a forum to ask what people might need,” making it difficult to clarify needs directly. In the absence of clear information, she relied on inference, which could lead to misdirected or unwelcome support. She described a situation where she inferred, based on a colleague’s behavior, that he might be neurodivergent, and began sending him deadline reminders as a form of support during project coordination. She later learned that he used a wheelchair due to a mobility disability, meaning her accommodation did not address his actual needs. As she put it: “I sort of started accommodating him by texting him reminders... and I was like, oh no, he’s in a wheelchair. Not what I thought at all.” Reflecting on Scenario 1 (Accessibility Profile Support), she noted that a structured way to communicate needs could help avoid this kind of misinterpretation by making appropriate forms of support more explicit. When reacting to Scenario 3 (Support Opportunity Prompts), non-disabled participants noted that AI-generated suggestions would be difficult to act on without sufficient context about why support was needed or whether it would be welcome. Similarly, for Sce-nario 4 (Phrasing Alternatives for Offering Help), N18 was unsure how to offer help without overstepping, worrying about sounding overly benevolent. N17, a software engineer on a fast-paced devel-opment team, described this uncertainty through an interaction with his manager, who had a motor disability. When N17 asked him to quickly log in and fix an issue, his manager explained that he could not type as fast. N17 was unsure how to respond: “I did not know if I should apologize for what I had asked him to do, or if I should be sorry for his situation, or if I should not, because I thought that would be insensitive.” He also hesitated to ask more questions, unsure whether that would feel caring or intrusive. At the same time, disabled participants described suppressing needs in order to avoid negative judgments from teammates. For Scenario 1 (Accessibility Profile Support) and Scenario 2 (Anony-mous Support Request), participants noted that making needs pub-licly visible could threaten perceptions of competence or profes-sionalism. D10, an art director in the advertising industry with ADHD, described difficulty keeping up with fast-paced meetings, including tracking multiple points of discussion, prioritizing infor-mation, and identifying next steps. To manage this, she relied on AI tools such as meeting transcription and summarization systems that generated notes and action items after meetings. However, she hesitated to ask teammates for support, explaining, “I know I have ADHD, but sometimes I don’t want to let them know I have ADHD, even though I need a little bit of support and help.” Instead of requesting clarification or additional support from teammates, she used AI privately to compensate for these challenges. This meant that potential forms of interpersonal support, such as teammates slowing down, sharing materials, clarifying priorities, or checking in on understanding, were not enacted. Her decision to rely on AI independently reduced the need for disclosure, but also limited opportunities for teammates to recognize and respond to her needs. In addition to challenges in identifying and sharing needs, dis-abled participants sometimes failed to recognize whether they needed support even when they were already implementing ac-commodations and strategies. D16, a software engineer with ADD, already relied on tools such as Pomodoro timers to stay focused and task trackers and post-it notes to keep herself accountable. 4.1.2 Reducing the Burden of Expressing and Coordinating Access Needs. In response to Scenario 1 (Accessibility Profile Support), participants emphasized that the profile could reduce the repeated labor of explaining preferences, needs, or accommodations to dif-ferent collaborators over time. This was not only raised by disabled participants, but also by non-disabled participants who compared the design to other situations in which their teammates had to repeatedly clarify how they worked, communicated, or wanted to be understood. For example, N13 compared the accessibility profile to existing profile features in Slack, such as name pronunciation, which help teammates know how to address someone correctly without requir-ing repeated explanation. She also drew a parallel to a manager she had worked with who routinely told colleagues at the start of meetings that her note-taking helped her stay focused and did not mean she was disengaged. Drawing on these examples, N13 sug-gested that an accessibility profile could similarly make interaction preferences or support needs visible up front, so teammates would better understand how to work with someone without needing repeated clarification. Disabled participants also shared similar sentiments. D10 em-phasized the value of not having to repeatedly explain her needs to others, noting, “Rather than explaining my situation to each person individually, I think this function is really helpful.” 

Beyond reducing repetition, both disabled and non-disabled par-ticipants also described how making needs persistently visible could enable more direct and effective support. N18 explained that clearly stated preferences made it easier to respond appropriately: “it makes it really explicit, allows me to give the kind of support that the person needs in a clear-cut manner, without the need to sidestep around the issue.” In this sense, participants anticipated that profiles could not only store information, but also translate needs into actionable forms of support. Preset support options also carried social meaning for disabled participants. D16 explained that seeing these options signaled that accessibility was already normalized within the team or organiza-tion, influencing whether she felt comfortable selecting them. 

## 4.2 Preserving Human Judgment, Control, and Team Dynamics 

4.2.1 Distributing the Labor of Allyship. Non-disabled participants shared how they noticed that the work of tracking, coordinating, and responding to access needs was often concentrated in a small number of individuals, rather than distributed across the team. When reflecting on Scenario 1 (Accessibility Profile Support), N13 described her experience working on a fully remote software de-velopment team with disabled team members. For example, one teammate she worked with closely had ADHD. The teammate had accessibility needs, including shorter meetings, access to captions or transcripts, and receiving materials and summaries to support following discussions. Responsibility for coordinating her team’s needs was largely taken on by the project manager, who served as the primary point of contact. The project manager helped adjust meeting structures, shared materials in advance, and ensured that notes and action items were distributed after meetings. While this support benefited the team, N13 noted that it became increasingly difficult for one person to manage as team demands grew. Following a company merger, meetings became longer and required coordination across multiple teams, increasing the complexity of maintaining these practices. As she explained, “[our project manager] was kind of the point of contact. . . I did notice that at some point she was having more trouble accommodating all needs. . . she’s the one trying to do all the things that this tool would handle, but herself.” N13 found value in AI that can help track accessibility needs over time and support their coordination across the team, rather than relying on one person to manage them alone. Non-disabled participants also emphasized that an ally’s ability to provide support was shaped by their capacity, resources, and role within the team. In response to Scenario 3 (Support Opportunity Prompts), participants pointed to the value of distributing respon-sibility by prompting multiple teammates when support might be needed, rather than relying on a single attentive individual. N1 described this limitation from the perspective of a team member without formal responsibility for coordination. He explained, “I might not have the support myself (as an ally) to be able to accommo-date those people,” particularly in situations where multiple support needs arise at once or when he lacks the time or resources to re-spond to them. He highlighted that even well-intentioned allies may lack the time or bandwidth to consistently take on this work. Similarly, N7 described how the support she provided depended on her role within the same broader team context. In her lab, which included members and collaborators with a range of disabilities, including motor impairments, low vision, blindness, and hearing-related access needs, she participated both as a collaborator and as an organizer of a seminar series. In her organizer role, she viewed accessibility as part of her formal responsibility. For seminars con-ducted over Zoom, this included proactively ensuring that captions were enabled, sharing materials ahead of time, and checking with attendees about their usual accommodations and any additional support they might need. As she explained, she would “100% reach out to everybody [attending the seminar].” 

In contrast, when participating as a fellow lab member or col-laborator, N7 did not see herself as the primary person responsible for coordinating accessibility, especially when someone else, such as the meeting facilitator, was already responsible for those needs. In those situations, she described taking a less proactive role by default, explaining, “I don’t personally feel liable to go an extra mile unless somebody’s needs are not being met satisfactorily.” N7’s re-sponsibility for accessibility was shaped by team role: she would step in when unmet needs became apparent, but did not see herself as the person who should initiate accommodation efforts when that responsibility already belonged to someone else. 

4.2.2 Accounting for Organizational and Social Constraints. Dis-abled participants emphasized that allyship depended on the broader organizational and social context in which collaboration occurred. These constraints shaped both those requesting support and allies in distinct ways, influencing whether and how allyship could be enacted. 4.2.3 Preserving Control, Authenticity, and Human Judgment. Non-disabled participants worried that when AI mediated too much of an allyship interaction, it could weaken the intention, personal ex-pression, and interpersonal learning that made allyship meaningful in the first place. For many, support was not only about whether help occurred, but its provenance. The support would be welcomed if it were recognizably grounded in another person’s own care, judgment, and effort. This concern appeared in responses to Scenario 3 (Support Op-portunity Prompts). N7 explained that if Riley later learned a team-mate had only acted because of an AI prompt, the support might feel less genuine: “If Riley later gets to know that [her teammate] was prompted by an AI to do that, she might feel that it’s coming from an inauthentic place.” D11 raised a related concern as an ally, explaining: “I’d be like, ‘Oh, does this person not feel comfortable reaching out to me directly (for help)?’ And that might worry me a little bit.” Participants valued the interpersonal act of offering and receiving support as much as the support itself, not just the out-come of the help. Mediation by AI raised questions about whether support reflected the ally’s own intention or merely compliance with a prompt. D3 similarly emphasized that “The best role tech-nology can play is making the steps of interaction a little bit easier, instead of taking that very human-to-human interaction out.” 

Both disabled and non-disabled participants expressed similar concerns about AI mediating how support was communicated. In reactions to Scenario 4, they worried that AI-generated rephras-ing could misrepresent tone and intentions, reducing control over self-expression or introducing biased interpretations. N14 worried that repeated rephrasing could create “a warped idea of the kind of person you are,” gradually shifting how someone came across. D16 likewise emphasized that “when asking for someone’s help, the delivery matters. I would personally write messages in my own tone and in my own intentions.” N17 said AI-suggested phrasing made ex-changes feel “not natural anymore,” while N18 cautioned that such tools would depend heavily on their sensitivity to social nuance and could otherwise come across as “really blunt.” 

Participants also questioned whether AI-mediated support would help people grow into better allies over time or simply substitute for that process. D11 explained: 

“If you’re too reliant on AI being able to say things that you’re not as comfortable saying to the group, then maybe you’ll unintentionally train yourself to not ask questions outside of [using the AI].” 

N17 similarly reflected on a difficult conversation with his man-ager, who had a motor disability. The interaction was difficult for N17 because he needed to respond appropriately after learning about his manager’s needs, without clear guidance on what would be respectful. He explained, “I was way more comfortable in the later conversation that we had, and I could feel that even my manager was way more open and comfortable having those conversations with me.” 

For him, learning came from working through the initial discomfort and uncertainty, rather than avoiding the interaction altogether. N18 raised a broader concern, whether AI risked “only performa-tively helping allyship” without fostering substantive change in how teammates relate to one another. 

4.2.4 Supporting Coordination Without Overstepping Social Bound-aries. Both disabled and non-disabled participants preferred AI for low-stakes coordination, but were less comfortable when it acted on matters with greater interpersonal or logistical consequences. This distinction appeared in responses to Scenario 3 (Support Opportu-nity Prompts). N7 explained that she would be comfortable with AI supporting simple accommodations, such as prompting someone to turn captions on, because these actions felt easy to carry out and did not require much personal commitment. However, she felt differently when the requested support imposed a more substantial demand on the other person’s time or schedule: 

“If AI is reaching out to turn captions on, I’d be com-fortable with that. But something which requires me to block time on my own calendar... for those kinds of events, I would feel more comfortable directly talking to Riley.”

## 5 Discussion 

We found that participants valued designs that they anticipated could reduce repeated disclosure, surface opportunities to provide support, and distribute allyship-related labor across the team. How-ever, participants were less receptive to AI that risked misrepresent-ing users’ intentions or weakened the authenticity and interper-sonal meaning of support. Together, these findings suggest that AI may best support allyship not by automating it, but by scaffolding recognition, coordination, and reflection while preserving agency, authenticity, and the relational nature of teamwork. Building on our findings, we argue that supporting accessibility in virtual collabo-ration requires a different technological framing: allyship-support tools. We outline design guidelines for these tools and examine the considerations for AI in this space. 

## 5.1 Toward Allyship-Support Tools as a Class of Accessibility Technologies 

Accessibility research has emphasized two models of support: as-sistive technologies that help an individual user interact with in-accessible systems [16, 22, 40, 76, 81], and human-mediated ac-cessibility systems such as crowdsourcing and friendsourcing [6– 10, 14, 27, 30, 38]. Although both have substantially advanced acces-sibility, neither fully captures how support operates in teamwork, where accessibility often depends on how teammates recognize needs, coordinate responses, and negotiate support within ongoing relationships. Prior work on collaborative accessibility and interdependence has shown that access is often relational and co-constructed through interactions among people, technologies, and environments [4, 11, 73, 77]. We build on this foundation, but focus on a more spe-cific collaborative problem. In our findings, the central challenge was not only that access was shared, but that collaborators had to determine when support was needed, how to offer it without overstepping, how to disclose needs without incurring stigma, and how to distribute accessibility-related labor across the team. In this context, allyship names the collaborative practice through which this relational accessibility is enacted. We therefore propose allyship-support tools as a class of accessi-bility technologies. Unlike traditional assistive technologies, these tools are not designed solely to help one disabled user access con-tent or interfaces. Unlike crowdsourced or friendsourced systems, they do not primarily route accessibility work to another person to complete on the user’s behalf. They also differ from technologies de-signed for caregiving or counseling, which generally assume more stable and asymmetrical support roles [39, 74]. Instead, allyship-support tools scaffold the relational and coordinative work around support while keeping the people involved central to the interaction (see Table 3). At the same time, framing these systems explicitly as accessibil-ity tools may not always be desirable. Several participants described wanting support without necessarily disclosing a disability, suggest-ing that allyship-support tools should also accommodate people who prefer to express work preferences, communication needs, or support preferences without labeling them as disability-related. Existing non-AI practices already support some of this work, in-cluding “work with me” statements [13, 47], personal user manuals [3], and team working agreements [64]. These tools allow people to describe how they collaborate best, what kinds of support are useful, and how teammates should communicate or coordinate with them without requiring disclosure. Connecting allyship-support tools to these broader practices may make them useful for a wider range of interpersonal needs, including temporary illness, caregiving re-sponsibilities, or other situational constraints. However, broadening the framing in this way introduces a tension. When tools describe access needs in general terms, disability-specific barriers can go unnamed and unaddressed, and teams lose the moments of explicit discussion through which they learn about and take ownership of accessibility. This distinction also points to when AI may be useful versus when it may overstep. Participants’ reactions suggest that AI may be more appropriate for low-stakes coordination that depends on timing or context, such as reminding teammates of an expressed preference, helping route a request for materials, or prompting an accessibility action that might otherwise be forgotten. By con-trast, participants were more cautious when AI moved into socially weighty actions, such as committing someone’s time, initiating support without situational consent, or mediating interactions that required direct interpersonal negotiation. Thus, systems should not simply add AI to allyship-support tools wherever support is possible. Designers should consider whether a particular use case requires AI’s contextual and coordinative capabilities, or whether it can be better handled through non-AI interface and workflow de-sign. ## 5.2 Design Guidelines for Allyship-Support Tools 

Design for actionability, not visibility alone. Prior work on ac-cessibility in collaborative settings has emphasized the importance of making access needs visible and discussable, whether through disclosure, shared communication norms, or explicit discussion of support needs [33, 40, 45, 77]. However, our findings suggest that visibility alone is insufficient for enabling effective support. Even when teammates were aware that someone might need sup-port, allyship often stalled because collaborators remained uncer-tain about what action would actually be helpful, appropriate, or welcome. In this sense, our findings extend prior work on collab-orative accessibility by identifying a gap between awareness and response: knowing that support may be needed does not neces-sarily make that support actionable [33, 45, 73]. This reframes the design problem from one of disclosure to one of awareness and coordination. Allyship-support tools should therefore do more than surface needs; they should help convert those needs into concrete, context-sensitive forms of action. Examples include structured sup-port preferences, examples of accommodations others have found useful, and prompts that clarify what kinds of responses would be helpful in a given situation. 

Reduce coordination burden while preserving user control. 

Prior research on accessible collaboration and AI-assisted systems has shown that technology can reduce the burden of repetitive support work, including disclosure, coordination, and reminders [33, 39, 74]. Our findings revealed similar insights, but an impor-tant limitation: participants were hesitant when systems acted au-tonomously without explicit, situational consent. This extends prior work on AI proactivity by showing that, in accessibility contexts, initiative is not only a matter of efficiency or convenience, but also one of agency, disclosure, and social risk [32]. A system that over-steps may expose someone in ways that feel unsafe, misrepresent their preferences, or undermine their control over how they are perceived by others. These risks are shaped by workplace power dynamics: an accessibility profile or AI-generated prompt may be experienced very differently depending on who can see it, and a support preference shared among peers carries different stakes than Supporting Allyship in Virtual Collaboration with Artificial Intelligence: A Scenario-Based Study ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal 

the same preference visible to a manager or accessible to HR. D6’s concern that disclosed needs could surface in a performance review illustrates how visibility that feels safe among peers can become a professional risk when it extends up a reporting hierarchy. Consent, therefore, cannot be treated as a single choice to make information visible or not; it must account for who gains access. Thus, the value of AI in this domain lies less in acting on users’ behalf than in reducing the repeated labor around coordination while preserving users’ authority over when and how support is enacted. Designers should therefore favor editable profiles, opt-in requests, and consent-based coordination mechanisms that give users control over role-differentiated visibility, allowing them to specify which information is shared with peers, supervisors, or the broader organization. Systems should also incorporate governance safeguards that limit how disclosed information can be accessed, retained, or repurposed, so that tools intended to support access do not become mechanisms for workplace surveillance or evaluation. 

Scaffold human judgment rather than replace it. CSCW research has long shown that collaborative systems can shape how people communicate, coordinate, and participate in group work [35, 51, 61, 62]. Our findings extend this perspective by showing that allyship involves not just coordination, but also ongoing in-terpersonal judgment about how to provide support in ways that are respectful, appropriate, and non-presumptive. Participants re-sponded most positively to scenarios that helped them understand possible accommodations, surfaced relevant context, or suggested more reflective ways of responding, and were less comfortable with designs that replaced human expression or made socially conse-quential decisions on their own. This suggests that the role of AI in allyship-support tools is not to determine the “correct” social ac-tion, but to scaffold the process through which collaborators make better judgments themselves. In contrast to automation-oriented approaches that aim to reduce user involvement, allyship-support tools should preserve human responsibility for socially meaning-ful decisions while reducing the uncertainty surrounding those decisions. In practice, this means designing systems that suggest, prompt, or explain rather than systems that fully automate requests, offers, or decisions. 

Preserve authenticity and opportunities for interpersonal skill-building. Prior work on accessibility and collaboration has often focused on reducing burden and improving the efficiency of support practices [40, 45, 73]. While our findings align with these goals, they also surface a complementary concern: partici-pants cared not only about whether support occurred, but about how it was enacted and what it signaled about the relationship. This extends prior work on AI-mediated social interaction, which has shown that AI can affect perceptions of social connection and authenticity in collaboration [31, 32, 69]. Designing for allyship, therefore, requires preserving opportunities for interpersonal en-gagement and skill-building. Rephrasing tools, reflection prompts, and communication aids may still be useful, but they should be framed as scaffolds for learning and self-reflection rather than sub-stitutes for interpersonal work. In this sense, allyship-support tools should help people become better at navigating difficult support interactions over time, rather than simply performing those in-teractions for them or removing the productive friction [34, 59] through which collaborators learn how to understand and support one another. Together, these guidelines demonstrate that allyship-support tools should support accessibility as an ongoing collaborative prac-tice. Allyship-support tools should help teams make needs action-able, reduce the labor of coordination, and support better judgment, while preserving the agency, authenticity, and trust through which inclusive collaboration is sustained. Rather than replacing the inter-personal work of allyship, the role of these tools is to better scaffold the conditions under which that work can happen. 

## 5.3 Considerations for AI in Allyship-Support Tools 

While the guidelines above outline how allyship-support tools can be designed, introducing AI with agentic capabilities into this role raises additional questions for future work. We began this project with the goal of designing an AI agent that could act as an ally. However, through our formative study and design process, we did not arrive at a fully autonomous agent. Instead, we developed a form of AI with agentic capabilities that supports allyship through text-based, private interactions and remains passive unless users have specified preferences in advance. In our study, this AI guided profile creation, mediated how requests were made, prompted teammates to act, and intervened in how support was communicated. A non-agentic version of these same ideas might take the form of a manual anonymous request option or user-invoked writing suggestions. By contrast, a more fully agentic AI might decide who should provide support or act on a teammate’s behalf. This distinction highlights that not all allyship-support workflows require AI; rather, AI’s distinctive value lies in cases where support depends on recognizing, timing, or initiating opportunities as collaboration unfolds, such as identifying when a stated access preference becomes relevant in a particular interaction or prompting teammates when support might otherwise be missed. The use of AI and the extent of its agentic capabilities raise several questions for future work, which we discuss below. First, how should responsibility be structured when AI partic-ipates in coordination? Our findings show that responsibility for accessibility is already uneven and context-dependent, and can be-come diffuse in larger teams. When AI actively prompts teammates, recommends who should respond, or helps coordinate support, it does not simply make information available; it participates in assigning attention and action. Seeber et al. [65] identify account-ability as one of the central unresolved tensions in designing AI as a teammate, noting that when systems take on coordination roles previously held by humans, questions of who bears responsibil-ity for outcomes become difficult to answer. In our context, this tension is heightened by the social stakes of accessibility: if an AI recommends the wrong person to respond, misjudges how urgent a need is, or prompts support in a way that unintentionally exposes a teammate, the result is not just inefficiency but potential harm to trust and inclusion. As with many emerging technologies, especially those intro-duced into socially consequential settings, AI systems for allyship support would require oversight and verification rather than being trusted to coordinate support on their own. If AI prompts teammates ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal Jung et al. 

to provide support, future systems would need ways to determine whether the support was actually offered, whether it addressed the person’s need, and whether the AI’s interpretation of the situation was appropriate. This may require human-in-the-loop mechanisms, such as allowing the person requesting support to confirm whether the need was resolved, enabling teammates to correct or reject AI-generated suggestions, or designating a human coordinator for unresolved requests. Thus, future work must examine how such systems can be monitored, verified, and corrected. Second, when should AI intervene? Because an agentic AI can decide when to surface a need, when to prompt others, and how strongly to frame its recommendations, it risks being experienced as authoritative even when its read of the situation is incomplete. Flathmann et al. [25] show that people often adapt their behavior to align with AI recommendations through social influence processes, even when those recommendations may not be optimal: a risk that is especially consequential in socially sensitive tasks such as allyship decisions. Zhang et al. [78] further found that an AI teammate’s communication style significantly shapes who feels empowered to act. Shen et al. [67] argue for a bidirectional approach to alignment: rather than designing AI systems that simply act on users’ behalf, future systems should help people understand, critique, and verify AI outputs while supporting calibrated reliance and opportunities for human intervention. In our context, this might mean AI that surfaces its own uncertainty about when to intervene, or that invites teammates to calibrate how strongly it should prompt, thereby preserving human judgment while still reducing coordination burden. Finally, what happens to relationships when AI takes on more of the interpersonal work involved in supporting allyship? AI that notices needs, suggests responses, or mediates communication may scaffold processes for allyship, but it may also reduce opportunities for teammates to practice the interpersonal judgment and direct communication through which allyship is developed. Fu et al. [26] found that users of AI-mediated communication tools grew con-cerned about inauthenticity and overreliance, worrying that the tools would erode their personal voice and communicative agency. Prior work on AI in creative collaboration offers a parallel: Suh et al. [69] found that generative AI can act as social glue, helping collaborators establish common ground and reduce interpersonal friction, while in some cases shifting interaction toward evaluating AI outputs rather than deeply building on each other’s ideas. In a higher-stakes context like accessibility, this consideration has addi-tional consequences. Jung et al. [33] and Das et al. [21] both show that accessibility in mixed-ability teams is accomplished through ongoing negotiation, not simply through information transfer or task completion. An agent that intervenes too readily may short-circuit exactly the relational processes through which teammates build shared understanding of each other’s needs. Taken together, these questions highlight that designing AI with agentic capabilities for allyship is not only about introducing new features, but about carefully defining the AI’s role in collaboration. The challenge is to determine when the agent should guide, prompt, or coordinate, and when it should step back to preserve human judgment and interaction. The risks identified here are not unique to disability contexts, but they are intensified by the social and technical stakes of accessibility work. Future work should explore the role of AI specifically in allyship-support tools. 

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Work 

We used a scenario-based study, which, like any method, carries inherent limitations. While this approach is valuable for under-standing participants’ perceptions of emerging design spaces before technologies are built and deployed, it cannot reveal how systems would actually be used in practice. As a result, our findings reflect participants’ perceptions, values, and concerns about using AI for allyship support, rather than how such tools would actually be used, negotiated, or resisted in ongoing collaboration. Because the scenarios were hypothetical, participants could only describe how they imagined they might respond, not how they would act in real-time team settings. As a complement, future work should therefore examine prototype or deployed systems in real mixed-ability teams to understand how AI designed to support allyship is used in prac-tice, including how such tools build trust, redistribute access labor, support accountability, or introduce new coordination burdens over time. Our scenarios focused on one part of the design space for AI-supported allyship by emphasizing text-based, private, and passive or consent-driven AI interactions. This focus was informed by our iterative design process and formative study. While this let us exam-ine these design assumptions in depth, it also left other possibilities unexplored, such as anthropomorphic AI representations, more proactive AI behaviors, more public forms of support, and mul-timodal interactions. Participants’ reactions should therefore be interpreted in relation to the specific scenario configurations we presented, including what information the AI appeared to know, what it inferred from context, what it disclosed to teammates, and whether it sought consent before making a recommendation. Differ-ent configurations, such as more assertive recommendations, public prompts, or AI that acts without situational authorization, could be perceived as more intrusive or risky. Future work should explore opportunities and limitations of alternative design approaches. We also note that our recruiting process likely favored partici-pants with certain characteristics. We recruited participants with experience in mixed-ability teams and framed the study around allyship in virtual collaboration. As a result, participants may have been more comfortable discussing disability and accessibility, or more attentive to these issues, than the broader population. This may limit how widely our findings apply, especially for teams with less experience engaging with accessibility. Future work should examine perspectives from a wider range of participants, including those with less prior exposure to accessibility practices. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We conducted a scenario-based study with 18 participants (10 dis-abled and 8 non-disabled) to examine what AI designs can support allyship in virtual collaboration. Participants perceived AI as most useful when it helped teammates express, interpret, and coordinate around access needs, and less acceptable when it displaced user control, human judgment, or the relational meaning of support. Addressing Breakdowns in Interpreting Needs and Responses Uncertainty about when and how to help Allies hesitated due to lack of clarity about whether support was needed or appropriate. Fear of overstepping or being patronizing Participants avoided acting to prevent appearing intrusive or condescending. Lack of a clear forum for discussing needs There were no socially acceptable mechanisms to ask about accessibility needs. Misinterpretation from inference In the absence of explicit information, participants guessed needs, sometimes incorrectly. Gap between awareness and actionabil-ity Knowing a need exists did not translate into knowing what to do. Needs are not always fully knowable Participants, especially disabled participants, described diffi-culty identifying or articulating their own needs. Reducing the Burden of Expressing and Coordinating Access Needs Repeated disclosure as labor Participants described repeatedly explaining needs as emo-tionally and cognitively taxing. Preference for persistent accessibility profiles Storing needs once reduced repeated explanation across chang-ing collaborators. Making needs actionable for others Explicit preferences enabled more direct and effective support from teammates. Preset options signal legitimacy of ac-commodations Predefined options conveyed that accommodations were nor-malized and acceptable.

First-Level Theme Second-Level Theme Interpretive Codes Cross-Case Pattern (Summary) Preserving Human Judgment, Control, and Team Dynamics 

Distributing the Labor of Allyship Centralization of accessibility labor Accessibility work was often concentrated in a single individ-ual, such as a manager or organizer. Need to redistribute coordination work Participants valued distributing responsibility across team-mates or systems. Ally capacity constraints Even willing allies lacked time, attention, or resources to con-sistently provide support. Role-dependent allyship responsibility Willingness to act varied based on role, such as organizer versus collaborator. Accounting for Organizational and Social Constraints Professional risk in disclosure Participants hesitated to request support due to fear of evalu-ation, stigma, or retaliation. Organizational culture shapes safety Tools were only useful when the environment felt safe and supportive. Group size affects accountability Larger teams diffused responsibility, reducing the likelihood of action. Structural constraints on coordination Visibility of needs did not guarantee follow-through without clear responsibility. Preserving Control, Authenticity, and Human Judgment AI mediation as threat to authenticity AI-mediated support risked feeling less genuine or meaning-ful. Loss of control over self-presentation Rephrasing tools could misrepresent tone, intent, or identity. AI may weaken interpersonal learning Overreliance on AI could reduce opportunities to develop allyship skills. Value of human intention in support Participants emphasized that support should reflect genuine care and judgment. Supporting Coordination Without Overstepping Social Boundaries Effort-based boundary for AI involve-ment AI was acceptable for low-effort tasks but not for high-effort or socially complex ones. Preference for human negotiation in high-stakes support Requests involving time or commitment required direct inter-personal interaction. Need for explicit, situational consent Participants wanted control over when and how AI acted on their behalf. Maintaining agency for both parties Both the requester and the ally should retain control over support interactions.
