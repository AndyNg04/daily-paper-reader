Abstract 

This study examines the construct validity of LLM-based generative agents in simulating reactive, proactive, and co-occurring aggression in children. Four distinct agents were instantiated using a theory-driven parameterization grounded in the social information processing model. A total of 1,920 simulation runs were conducted across eight social scenarios, employing a hybrid blind-coding pipeline to extract 32 quantitative behavioral indicators. Results demonstrate robust discriminant validity relative to a non-aggressive baseline, with large effect sizes. High cross-seed reliability confirms that behavioral differentiation is driven by underlying psychological parameters rather than model stochasticity. Qualitative narrative analyses further converged with established empirical literature. Overall, these findings indicate that theory-parameterized LLM agents can accurately reproduce distinct aggression subtypes, offering a scalable, highly controllable framework for hypothesis generation, intervention piloting, and the refinement of psychological measurement tools.

“Hot-Blooded” vs. “Cold-Blooded”: Simulating the Behavioral Phenotypes of Childhood Aggression via Generative Agents 

Introduction 

Aggression is not a unitary construct but can be distinguished into two subtypes, namely reactive and proactive aggression (Dodge & Coie, 1987). These subtypes have distinct underlying mechanisms according to the social information processing model (Crick & Dodge, 1996). Despite the strong theoretical foundation of this classification, empirical research continues to face persistent constraints. Reactive aggression is triggered by provocation, which makes it ethically challenging to manipulate in experimental settings. Conversely, proactive aggression is premeditated, covert, and often socially masked, rendering it under-detected in standard observational paradigms (Raine et al., 2006). While questionnaire surveys, such as the Reactive-Proactive Aggression Questionnaire, are reliable and efficient, they remain susceptible to social desirability bias and informant recall errors. These limitations have created a pressing need for computational approaches capable of simulating complex social behaviors under controlled, manipulable, and ethically sound conditions. 

The recent emergence of large language model (LLM)-based generative agents offers a promising opportunity for simulating human social behavior, as LLMs possess extensive knowledge of human behaviors derived from training on massive datasets of human societies (Bommasani et al., 2021). Consequently, these agents hold great potential as a powerful complementary tool for examining reactive and proactive aggression that are difficult to observe in the real world. However, given their “black-box” structure, current LLM simulations often rely on face validity – the surface-level outputs – rather than construct validity which concerns the underlying mechanisms the model is intended to capture (Larooij & Törnberg, 2025b). The present study aims to address this gap by testing whether LLM-based agents can reproduce the behavioral profiles of children with reactive and proactive aggression. Construct validity is evaluated through three criteria: discriminant validity across quantitative indicators, cross-seed reliability, and convergent validity with narratives. By grounding the validation in a well-established theoretical framework, this study seeks to determine whether theory-based LLM agents can serve as a stable and valid tool for investigating reactive and proactive aggression. 

The Social Information Processing Model 

The social information processing (SIP) model provides a primary theoretical framework for understanding the cognitive deficits underlying aggressive behaviors (Holtzworth-Munroe, 1992). According to the SIP model, individuals process social information through five sequential stages: encoding social cues, interpreting those cues, accessing and organizing a response, evaluating and deciding upon the response, and finally, enacting the behavior (Dodge & Crick, 1990). 

Reactive and proactive aggressors exhibit distinct cognitive distortions at different stages of this process (Crick & Dodge, 1996). Reactive aggressors, often described as “hot-blooded”, typically struggle with the first two stages (Dodge & Crick, 1990; Fanti et al., 2024). They are characterized by impulsivity, overt physiological displays of anger, and elevated emotional arousal (Hubbard et al., 2002). A key feature of reactive aggressors is hostile attributional bias, that is they tend to focus on negative social cues and misinterpret others’ intention as hostile in ambiguous situations (van Dijk et al., 2018). Consequently, they are more likely to resort to aggressive behaviors in self-defense or retaliation. Forgiveness can help prevent revenge, as it 4

involves a shift toward prosocial motivation that facilitates the management of interpersonal conflicts and significantly reduces anger (Van der Wal et al., 2017; Watson et al., 2017). 

In contrast, proactive aggressors are described as “cold-blooded” (Fanti et al., 2024). They are generally capable of interpreting and comprehending social cues; however, their distortions occur in the third and fourth stages of the SIP model. They evaluate aggression positively and adopt aggressive behaviors instrumentally to obtain their personal goals and desired outcomes (Fung, 2019).They show little concern for others and fail to exhibit remorse or repentance toward their victims (Fung, 2019). In childhood, these individuals are associated with callous-unemotional traits, narcissism, and psychopathy (Hubbard et al., 2002), reflecting a deficit in affective empathy (Van Noorden et al., 2015). 

LLM-Based Agents 

With the rapid development of LLMs, generative agents can interpret natural-language scenarios, maintain consistent personas over multi-turn interactions, and produce believable behaviors (Park et al., 2023). However, validation practices in generative social simulation remain underdeveloped (Larooij & Törnberg, 2025b). Many studies rely on surface-level or subjective assessments of model behavior rather than systematic tests of whether simulated behavior matches theoretically specified psychological constructs (Larooij & Törnberg, 2025a). Although a small body of research has begun to apply psychometric methods to LLM behavior, findings remain inconsistent. Some studies have demonstrated that, under appropriate prompting configurations, personality measures in LLM outputs can be reliable and valid, and that LLM personas’ self-reported personality scores are consistent with their designated personality types (Jiang et al., 2024; Serapio-García et al., 2025). In contrast, other studies have found that LLM-generated behavior systematically diverges from human patterns in important ways. For instance, LLMs are less sensitive than humans in decision-making (Xiao & Wang, 2025), and even model scaling fails to produce more human-like choices (Gao et al., 2025). In survey design, most prominent LLMs fail to consistently mimic human psychological biases, often responding in opposite directions or reacting erratically to meaningless formatting changes (Tjuatja et al., 2024). 

The Present Study 

These inconsistent findings suggest that validation must be construct-specific. It is not sufficient merely to test whether an LLM appears “human-like” in general; rather, validation requires examining specific, theoretically grounded evidence that defines the target construct. Building on this premise, the present study tests whether LLM-based agents can reproduce the distinct behavioral profiles of children characterized by reactive, proactive, or co-occurring reactive-proactive aggression. Construct validity is evaluated through three criteria: discriminant validity across quantitative indicators, cross-seed reliability, and convergent validity with qualitative narratives. The research questions are as follows: 

1.  Can LLM-based agents produce behaviors that distinguish among reactive, proactive, and co-occurring reactive-proactive aggression? 

2.  Are these differences stable across independent random seeds? 

3.  Do the simulated behavioral narratives converge with qualitative descriptions of the aggression subtypes in the existing literature? 

4.  Which scenarios most effectively discriminate among the subtypes of aggression, and which psychological dimensions drive that discrimination? 5

Methods 

Agent Construction 

The simulations were implemented using Concordia (Vezhnevets et al., 2023), an open-source framework developed by Google DeepMind for constructing and executing generative agent-based models that simulate social interactions within rich physical, social, or digital environments. Four distinct child agents were instantiated, each conditioned on an explicit psychological profile corresponding to one of the aggression subtypes or a non-aggressive baseline control (Table 1). Agent profiles were parameterized according to the SIP model and features of the two subtypes of aggression (Crick & Dodge, 1996; Hubbard et al., 2010). Profile parameters were operationalized on a normalized continuum ranging from 0 (low) to 1 (high). For example, reactive aggressors are characterized by deficits in the early stages of the SIP, high dispositional anger, and low anger control (Dodge & Crick, 1990). These values served two functions: guiding the LLM’s role-playing prompt and computing internal emotion updates during the simulation. Rather than functioning as empirical epidemiological estimates, these parameters represent theory-driven ordinal boundaries. 

Agent-level psychological conditioning was operationalized by configuring Concordia’s modular architecture—specifically its identity, associative memory, and generative reasoning components—with detailed natural-language descriptions reflecting each agent’s cognitive, affective, and behavioral tendencies (Vezhnevets et al., 2023). These trait descriptions remained invariant across all scenario vignettes and simulation trials (Table 1).

Scenarios 

Eight hypothetical scenarios were developed to investigate the process of social information in children exhibiting reactive, proactive, and co-occurring reactive-proactive aggression. Hypothetical scenarios are a well-established instrument for examining children’s behavior and social dynamics, including aggressive tendencies (e.g., Poulou & Bassett, 2018; Verhoef et al., 2022). The eight scenarios comprise a physical push during an argument, a hungry class monitor, a classmate in distress, a restroom ostracization incident, an accidental displacement, a reward distribution, a playground impact incident, and an unattended pencil case (Table 2). Among these, Scenarios 1, 5, and 7 specifically evaluate intent attribution. These particular scenarios were designed with situational ambiguity, as children displaying reactive aggression tend to exhibit a hostile attribution bias under ambiguous circumstances (van Dijk et al., 2018). Furthermore, these three scenarios evaluated emotional arousal and retaliatory tendencies in reactively aggressive children, as such individuals are easily provoked and frequently engage in retaliation as a mechanism to express their anger (Quan et al., 2022). Scenarios 2, 6, and 8 were designed to assess proactive aggression by measuring children’s inclination to deploy instrumental aggression for resource acquisition (e.g., obtaining food, candy, or a coveted pencil case). Scenarios 3 and 4 were designed to assess children’s cognitive and affective empathy. Specifically, Scenario 3 evaluated participants’ empathic responses and examined their inclination to engage in helping behaviors. Scenario 4 examined proactively aggressive children’s empathy and their outcome evaluations regarding bullying behavior. 

Meanwhile, qualitative data were collected via individual interviews and focus groups to establish a baseline for distinguishing reactive and proactive aggression in real-world contexts (Fung et al., 2026; Fung et al., 2023). Participants were presented with the same eight scenario vignettes subsequently deployed in the agent-based simulations. For each vignette, interviewees were asked to describe their thoughts, feelings, and behavioral responses. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and coded using a structured coding scheme aligned with the quantitative indicators extracted from the simulations (e.g., anger, retaliation, hostile attributional bias, empathy). Inter-rater reliability was established on a subset of transcripts before full coding proceeded (Fung et al., 2026; Fung et al., 2023). 

Procedure 10 

All simulations were executed using the DeepSeek-V3 model, with the sampling temperature held constant at T = 0.7 to balance response consistency and generation diversity (0.0 = deterministic output, 1.5 = high variance). Each agent was simulated in a four-turn interaction chain across all scenarios: (1) the agent ’s initial reaction, (2) a peer response or environmental scene development, (3) the agent ’s action following the peer ’s response, and (4) the agent ’s final reaction or reflection. This sequential architecture enabled the dynamic observation of behavioral unfolding over time rather than relying on a static, single-snapshot measurement. The peer in turns two and three was generated by the same language model but operated independently of the four focal agents: it served as a typically developing eight-year-old classmate whose responses were dynamically conditioned on the target agent’s preceding 

behavior. To ensure statistical reliability and account for stochasticity in model generations, each of the four child agents was simulated three times across the eight scenario vignettes using 20 distinct random seeds, resulting in a total sample size of 1,920 simulation runs (4 agents  × 3replications  × 8 scenarios  × 20 seeds).

Data Analysis 

To evaluate behavioral outcomes, a blind coding pipeline extracted structured quantitative indicators from agents ’ generated behavioral narratives following each trial. Thirty-two quantitative indicators were extracted through blind LLM-based coding of the generated behavioral text (Table 3). These indicators were classified into three categories: continuous 0 –

10 rating scale, count-type, and difference indicators. This classification ensured that each indicator was analyzed with statistical methods appropriate to its underlying structure. 

A majority of indicators were rated on a 0 –10 scale (n = 26) (Table 3). Given these indicators are continuous and interval-scaled, they are suitable for parametric analyses, including means, standard deviations, t-tests, and Cohen’s d. There are four indicators covering number of items allocated in the reward distribution scenario (Scenario 6). These indicators included the number of candy packs given to oneself, Leo, and two other teammates. They are integer counts, not 0 –10 ratings, and therefore capture a different behavioral dimension. 

There are two indicators computed from other measures rather than directly rated (Table 3). Attribution shift was calculated as the difference between initial hostile attributional bias and public accusation (initial hostile attributional bias minus finial accusation). Aggressive action was a composite score derived from multiple aggressive behavior ratings. Because they are derived scores, their scale may differ from the 0 –10 ratings, and they were interpreted as composite rather than as direct ratings. 

Indicators that were categorical (e.g., action type, help type, solution type) were excluded from these 32 indicators because they are nominal variables. The final set of 32 indicators thus consisted of 26 continuous ratings, 4 count indicators, and 2 composite indicators. This classification guided the statistical analysis of each indicator: continuous ratings were analyzed with parametric tests, count indicators were summarized descriptively, and composite indicators were interpreted as difference scores. 12

Results 

Cross-Round Reliability 

Cross-round reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way random effects, absolute agreement, single measurement) with subject defined as the scenario × subtype combination and rater defined as the random seed (20 seeds) (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). ICC values were defined as follows: ≥ 0.75 excellent, 0.60 – 0.74 good, 0.40 –

0.59 moderate, and < 0.40 poor (Koo & Li, 2016). Twenty-one indicators achieved excellent reliability (Table 4). The most reliable indicators were affective empathy (ICC = 0.99), prosocial behavior (ICC = 0.99), dominance (ICC = 0.97), care (ICC = 0.96), and empathy for the victim (ICC = 0.95). Other indicators with excellent reliability included repeat offending likelihood (ICC = 0.95), remorse (ICC = 0.94), selfishness (ICC = 0.94), help (ICC = 0.93), retaliation (ICC = 0.93), hostile attributional bias (ICC = 0.92), pain-amplified anger (ICC = 0.91), likelihood of aggression (ICC = 0.91), and forgiveness (ICC = 0.91). Four indicators showed good reliability: guilt (ICC = 0.72), impulsivity (ICC = 0.71), outcome evaluation (ICC = 0.61), and number of packs for self (ICC = 0.61) (Table 4). Three indicators showed moderate reliability: cognitive empathy (ICC = 0.59), concern for consequences (ICC = 0.47), and attribution shift (ICC = 0.46) (Table 4). Four indicators showed poor reliability: number of packs for Leo (ICC = 0.39), number of packs for Teammate B (ICC = 0.17), number of packs for Teammate C (ICC = 0.11), and intensity of temptation (ICC = 0.08).

The coefficient of variation (CV = SD /M) was computed to assess the reliability of each simulated behavior across repeated runs (Figure 1). Specifically, the CV was calculated across the 26 continuous 0 –10 rating indicators within each subtype × scenario cell. The CV has been established as a sound indicator of experimental error in simulation studies. Lower CV values indicate greater stability and reproducibility of the simulated behavior across replications, whereas higher values indicate greater variance and the need for more replications to obtain stable estimates(Lorscheid et al., 2012). 

The reactive aggressive child agent showed the most stable behavioral profile, with CV values ranging from 0.19 to 0.44 across all scenarios. Stability was highest in Scenario 3 (CV = 0.19) and lowest in Scenario 1 (CV = 0.44). The proactive aggressive child agent showed substantially greater variability, particularly in Scenario 1 (CV = 1.26) and Scenario 4 (CV = 1.95). The reactive –proactive co-occurring aggressive child also showed elevated variability, with CV values exceeding 1.0 in Scenario 1 (CV = 1.05), Scenario 3 (CV = 1.35), and Scenario 4 (CV = 1.25). The non-aggressive baseline showed its highest variability in Scenario 1 (CV = 1.51) and Scenario 5 (CV = 0.94). 

Overall, stability varied systematically by subtype and scenario. Reactive aggression was the most consistent subtype, whereas proactive and reactive-proactive co-occurring aggression showed greater cross-round variability, especially in scenarios involving direct provocation (Scenario 1), ambiguous harm (Scenario 5), and moral transgression (Scenario 4). The baseline was most stable in Scenarios 3 and 8 (CV = 0.15 and 0.18, respectively) and least stable in Scenario 1 (CV = 1.51). 14

Discriminant Validity 

Discriminant validity was measured using eight indicators. These indicators were selected for inclusion in the radar chart based on three criteria: the features of subtypes of aggression, statistical robustness, and visual clarity (Figure 2). First, these indicators encompass the four core domains distinguishing subtypes of aggression: emotional arousal (anger, retaliatory motivation), instrumental motivation (dominance, selfishness), empathy (affective empathy, cognitive empathy), and prosociality (care, prosocial behavior). These dimensions yield a comprehensive behavioral profile for each agent subtype. Second, all selected indicators demonstrated strong psychometric properties. Each was measured on a 0 – 10 scale across multiple scenarios, yielding significant group differences, robust effect sizes, and high cross-seed reliability. Third, radar charts function best with five to eight dimensions. Incorporating further metrics degrades interpretability through overlapping lines and label clutter. The remaining variables from the full 32-indicator set were excluded because they were scenario-specific (e.g., remorse, hostile attribution bias), categorical (e.g., action taxonomy), count-based (e.g., resource allocation frequency), or psychometrically unstable (e.g., outcome evaluation). 15

Divergent Validity 

The divergent validity was assessed by Welch’s t-tests with Bonferroni correction. The t-tests were conducted to compare each subtype with the baseline on the 10 indicators (Table 4). These 10 core indicators were selected since they represented the main theoretical domains distinguishing subtypes of aggression: impulsivity (anger, retaliation), instrumental motivation (dominance, selfishness), empathy (affective, cognitive), and prosociality (help, care, forgiveness, prosocial behavior). These indicators were continuous on a 10-point scale, showed adequate reliability, and mapped directly onto the SIP model. The remaining 22 indicators were either scenario-specific, categorical, count-based, or psychometrically unstable. Discussion 

The present study examined whether LLM-based agents can reproduce theory-driven behavioral profiles of aggression. Across 1,920 simulated interactions, the aggression subtypes and the non-aggressive baseline showed distinct quantitative patterns that aligned with the SIP model. Cross-seed reliability was extremely high for most core dimensions, indicating that these differences were stable across random seeds. Qualitative excerpts further supported these patterns. Together, these quantitative and qualitative findings suggest that theory-informed LLM agents can capture the core features of aggression subtypes in a reliable and theoretically consistent manner. 

The findings provide solid empirical support for the SIP model in a computational context. By successfully isolating the “hot-blooded ” (reactive) from “cold-blooded ” (proactive) aggression, this study has demonstrated that these distinct underlying mechanisms can be operationalized as discrete, stable parameters within a generative agent. To be specific, the reactively aggressive agent exhibited high-level of anger and retaliation, yet maintained levels of empathy and prosocial behavior comparable to the non-aggressive baseline. This dissociation underscores that reactive aggression stems primarily from deficits in emotional regulation and social cue interpretation rather than a lack of empathy, consistent with existing literature (Dodge 22 

& Crick, 1990; Euler et al., 2017). The qualitative narratives further support the “hot-blooded ”

profile of children with reactive aggression. The qualitative narratives further support the “hot -

blooded” profile of reactively aggressive children. Although the reactive child agent’s affective 

empathy was statistically significantly lower than the baseline, their absolute mean values remained close. A potential explanation is that the baseline empathy parameter was set relatively low; consequently, small numerical differences reached statistical significance across multiple simulation runs. 

The proactively aggressive agent displayed “cold-blooded” behavior, characterized by high dominance and selfishness coupled with near-zero anger and deficits in affective empathy. The dissociation of cognitive and affective empathy supports findings that proactive aggressors are characterized by callous-unemotional traits (Hubbard et al., 2002), and that those with preserved cognitive empathy are better equipped to manipulate others and act calculatingly (Gordon et al., 2014). These results suggest that LLM-driven simulations enable researchers to isolate and manipulate specific cognitive distortions, allowing them to observe their behavioral effects without the confounding variables inherent in human subjects. The reactive and proactive co-occurring aggressive agent displayed the most pervasive and severe deficits, combining high levels of both aggressive subtypes. This mixed profile suggests that co-occurring aggression is not merely an additive effect of the two individual subtypes, but rather a distinct, severe profile marked by both emotional instability and callous traits (Chan et al., 2013; Maneiro et al., 2024). 

Beyond the psychological findings, this study addresses the “circularity problem ” and the lack of rigorous validation prevalent in current LLM-based social simulations. The implementation of a hybrid blind-coding pipeline – where behaviors are generated by persona-conditioned agents but evaluated via neutral, theory-blind coding prompts – establishes a robust framework for mitigating confirmation bias. This ensures that the observed results reflect the 

agent’s genuine behavioral output rather than a direct artifact of the system prompt ’sinstructions. 

Furthermore, the high cross-seed reliability observed across core constructs suggests that LLM-based psychological simulation has moved beyond mere “stochastic mimicry ” to function as a stable, reproducible measurement tool. The capacity to execute thousands of simulation trials with high consistency offers a scalable alternative to traditional vignette-based methodologies, enabling researchers to systematically examine rare behavioral cases and extreme personality configurations that are statistically underrepresented or unfeasible in human samples. 

Scenario discrimination power analyses further indicate that affective contexts (e.g., ambiguous harm and direct provocation) are significantly more effective at eliciting aggressive behaviors than neutral contexts (e.g., resource allocation). This pattern aligns with empirical findings from focus group discussions and individual interviews (e.g., Fung et al., 2026; Fung et al., 2023). Whereas real-world human behavior remains undifferentiated in neutral contexts, the simulation appears to over-discriminate between reactive and proactive aggression. This hyper-sensitivity under neutral conditions highlights a current limitation of persona-conditioned agents, which may enforce overly discrete behavioral boundaries compared to the more flexible, noise-tolerant responses of human subjects. Additionally, social desirability bias may account for why 23 

neutral scenarios failed to differentiate aggression subtypes in human interviews, as participants tend to offer socially acceptable answers rather than disclosing their genuine thoughts or behavioral tendencies. 

Implications 

The capacity to reliably simulate distinct subtypes of aggression offers significant opportunities to advance experimental paradigms in developmental psychology. First, LLM-based generative agents provide a valuable methodological benchmark for researchers designing scenario vignettes to assess aggression in both child populations and computational models. Because affective contexts proved significantly more effective at eliciting aggressive behaviors than neutral ones, future diagnostic tools can optimize scenario design by embedding affective triggers to better evaluate socioemotional development. Second, generative agents can serve as in silico pilot-testing platforms for behavioral interventions. Given that large-scale field interventions are notoriously time-consuming and resource-intensive, preliminary simulation trials can help predict treatment outcomes and identify whether specific aggression subtypes exhibit resistance to particular therapeutic modalities. Third, generative agents could supplement traditional randomized controlled trials (RCTs). While RCTs remain the gold standard, executing them in real-world educational or clinical settings often encounters severe ethical and logistical barriers; synthetic control groups could provide a low-risk, scalable benchmark for evaluating treatment efficacy. Fourth, future research can extend this framework to multi-agent ecological simulations to model dynamic group behaviors — such as peer bullying within school environments — by incorporating the interacting roles of victims, followers, bystanders, and teachers. 

Limitations 

There are three major limitations in this study. First, this study relied exclusively on a single foundation model (DeepSeek-V3), which limits the generalizability of the findings across different LLM architectures. Because agent behavior can vary based on pre-training corpora, fine-tuning methodologies, and parameter scales, future research should evaluate these frameworks across a broader spectrum of proprietary and open-weight architectures (e.g., the GPT, Claude, and Llama series) to determine whether these aggression profiles remain consistent across model families. 

Second, the findings highlight a key divergence between computational simulation and empirical reality. While the generative agents cleanly differentiated all eight scenarios, human interview data exhibited adequate scenario discrimination in only five. This hyper-consistency suggests that explicit persona conditioning may over-amplify discrete theoretical boundaries that remain naturally fluid, nuanced, or noise-affected in real-world human populations. Future studies should systematically benchmark simulation outputs against larger, multi-modal human empirical datasets to better calibrate agent sensitivity. 

Third, the study relied strictly on hypothetical scenario vignettes, which may fail to capture the real-time, emergent complexities of dynamic social interactions. To enhance construct and ecological validity, future research should assess generative agents using a broader 24 

suite of diagnostic instruments, such as interactive virtual interviews, standardized psychometric surveys, and longitudinal multi-agent sandboxes. 

Conclusion 

This study provides robust empirical evidence that theory-informed LLM agents can reliably reproduce the complex behavioral profiles of reactive and proactive aggression. By effectively dissociating the “hot” (reactive) and “cold” (proactive) dimensions of aggression, this research demonstrates that generative agents can serve as a powerful complementary paradigm for investigating aggression and socioemotional development. While computational simulation is not a substitute for traditional empirical research, it offers a safe, highly controllable, and scalable environment for hypothesis generation, measurement tool refinement, and the preliminary evaluation of interventions that would otherwise be ethically unfeasible or logistically impractical in real-world human populations.
