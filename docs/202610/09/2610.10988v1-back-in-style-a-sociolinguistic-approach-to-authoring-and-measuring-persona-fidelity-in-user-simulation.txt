## Abstract 

As agentic systems gain commercial popular-ity, user simulators increasingly serve as mea-surement instrument for their evaluation. How-ever, the fidelity of simulated users in compar-ison to real human users is generally low, and typically assessed by costly, subjective LLM judges. In this pilot study, we ask whether fidelity can instead be measured deterministi-cally by treating a user persona sociolinguisti-cally : as a social type that emerges from ob-servable linguistic style, rather than one pre-dicted by labels or descriptions a model must extrapolate into behaviour. We author personas as concrete stylistic rates, which lets us trans-fer two established, model-free instruments — authorship-verification stylometry and lexicon-based content analysis — as fidelity diagnos-tics. We A/B-test the sociolinguistic schema against a flat descriptive baseline across five task-oriented customer-service agents. Results show that the sociolinguistic schema improves both stylistic adherence and stylometric distin-guishability for most of the tested models, with a caveat that persona style fidelity does not nec-essarily equal persona “naturalness”. We argue that a sociolinguistic approach to persona de-sign is a promising path towards more diverse and representative user personas, and that these metrics are most valuable in an error-attribution analysis, localizing where fidelity breaks down. This is a first step towards interventions that move user simulations closer to faithful render-ings of diverse and variable linguistic outputs. 

## 1 Introduction 

User simulators are becoming a standard measurement instrument for evaluating agentic systems (Abdulhai et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Hathidara et al., 2026). For production evaluation systems, a simulated user exercises the full system end to end at scale: it drives complete multi-turn interactions through the agent’s tool calls and the downstream side effects they produce, which can surface integration and data-flow errors that single-turn or unit tests alone do not reach. However, simulation also introduces its own conversational arti-facts and failure patterns unlike those of real users, and evaluating agents on simulated users can misrepresent real-world capability, especially across diverse user pop-ulations (Seshadri et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). This makes the simulator’s own fidelity a core priority in agentic evaluation pipelines. This is further complicated by how personas are con-ventionally specified (i.e. what instructions are supplied to the simulator for its rendering of a given user), typ-ically as lists of adjectives or demographic labels the simulator must extrapolate into behaviour. We argue that a sociolinguistic approach to persona schema de-sign offers a promising path to designing more faithful and diverse user personas. Where labels or prose-level descriptions treat identity as predictive of behaviour (the model infers how a specified type, e.g. "a confused client", would act), a sociolinguistic approach treats identity as emergent from linguistic expression. In this view, a persona is a social type constituted by recurrent stylistic choices and discursive stance-taking (Du Bois, 2007; Kiesling, 2009, 2018; Ochs, 1993). Treating ob-servable linguistic style as the substrate lets us author a user persona as concrete, measurable features such as rates of hedging, politeness, filler words, and emoji or internet-speak usage. This also makes persona fidelity legible to model-free, spec-mapped metrics. Testing this grounding poses two coupled questions: (a) does persona specification as linguistic style mea-surably improve a user simulator’s fidelity, and (b) can that improvement be read off deterministically, without requiring a calibrated LLM judge or human annota-tor? We treat the second as fundamentally a problem of metric transfer, taking two established deterministic instruments (authorship-verification stylometry (Aggaz-zotti et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024) and lexicon-based content analysis (Hutto and Gilbert, 2014; Tausczik and Pennebaker, 2010)) and applying them on the style sub-strate as an exploratory fidelity diagnostic. We test whether they yield actionable signal: can they local-ize which components of a persona’s linguistic struc-ture a simulator does and does not render faithfully? Such localization can then be used as a lever: targeted behavioural interventions on simulated users (e.g. in-structing them to limit politeness) have been shown to mitigate gaps for the most miscalibrated user groups (Seshadri et al., 2026). ## 2 Related work 

2.1 Defining a user persona for simulation 

Most task-oriented LLM-based user simulators con-verge on one of two types of persona steering. The first is to specify a persona as a profile or attribute list, e.g. goals, demographics, or personality tags, which the model is asked to render into user behaviour (Chen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Du et al., 2025; Abdulhai et al., 2025). The second representation steers a single trait along a control knob and checks that the output moves with it (Chen et al., 2025; Han et al., 2026); role-steering studies show that LLM personas can be pushed along social dimensions (Song et al., 2026), but also collapse back toward a default assistant voice under sustained interaction (Xiao et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). Both rep-resentations share a gap, as they do not specify explicitly the quantity that a fidelity metric must measure. Sociolinguistic research has long treated surface-level linguistic forms as exactly this kind of observable quan-tity. In sociolinguistic terms, a persona is not simply a collection of speaker qualities, but a social type that emerges from recurrent choices of linguistic expression. A speaker takes stances in discourse – evaluative, af-fective, and epistemic positions toward the topic of talk and their interlocutor – and as these stances recur, they accrete into a durable linguistic style that constitutes the persona (Du Bois, 2007; Kiesling, 2009, 2018; Ochs, 1993). Under this account, style is the medium in which the persona itself is constituted: the social type resides in observable language, rather than behind it. Crucially, a social type is a relational category, con-stituted in how a speaker positions themselves toward the talk and toward others, and not a demographic one, though it can intersect with identity categories without being reducible to them (e.g. Podesva (2007)’s account of a queer speaker enacting a “gay diva” persona among close friends shows how the persona is assembled from stylistic choices such as falsetto phonation rather than derived from demographic membership). Elements of this view have begun to surface in simulation studies: for example, Jeong et al. (2026) apply fine-grained soci-olinguistic control to in-vehicle assistant simulation, and linguistic signals have been used to probe persona sta-bility and collapse (Han et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026). Yet the persona is still typically modelled as something behind the words, rather than recognizing the words themselves as evidential (Figure 1). 

2.2 Evaluating persona adherence 

In task-oriented user simulation, the main fidelity signal largely remains an LLM-judge tasked with determining whether a simulated user is consistent with its persona, either through direct role-consistency scoring (Abdulhai et al., 2025), rubric-guided alignment (Liu et al., 2026), persona-adaptive prompting of the evaluator (Purwar and Choudhary, 2026), or dedicated benchmarks for persona and role-play quality (Bao et al., 2026; Tu et al., 2024; Peng and Shang, 2024). As a step towards nar-rowing the "sim2real" gap (Zhou et al., 2026), assessing user simulation faithfully requires instruments that are reproducible, deterministic, and able to localize where fidelity breaks down. However, deterministic, model-free measures rarely appear as the primary signal for persona fidelity. Aggregate human-likeness (Hathidara et al., 2026), single-attribute knob-tracking (Chen et al., 2025), or lexical analysis (e.g. LIWC, (Tausczik and Pennebaker, 2010)) have been offered as a descriptive lens to complement an LLM judge (Han et al., 2026), though not as the headline metric. The stylometric tech-niques we adopt here have been developed in author-ship verification and attribution (Aggazzotti et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024; Man et al., 2026), and in stylometric fingerprinting used to tell role-constrained LLM agents apart (Dietrich, 2026); fidelity itself has been checked behaviourally against real user decisions (Chen, 2026), but not on the stylistic substrate where a persona is realized. To our knowledge, no prior work uses de-terministic, spec-mapped stylometric measures as the primary fidelity signal for a multi-attribute persona in task-oriented customer-service simulation across text and voice. Because low fidelity can fall unevenly across user populations (Seshadri et al., 2026; Haq and Saldías, 2026), a reproducible way to detect and localize it is a prerequisite for trustworthy evaluation. While detecting a disparity is not the same as removing it, detection is a necessary first step toward any targeted intervention, especially for production systems that serve a linguisti-cally diverse user base. 

## 3 Methodology 

3.1 Evaluation system 

Our study sits inside an end-to-end evaluation pipeline designed for task-oriented conversational tool-use agents. It consists of an LLM-based simulator imper-sonating the user, interacting with an agent harness. The agent harness has access to tool APIs, and a scoring layer with LLM-as-a-judge and deterministic checks aimed at its outputs. The user simulator is given a per-sona schema, a task scenario, and details specific to the scenario such as name or contact details. The simulator interacts with the agent via WebRTC and produces a multi-turn dialogue. Crucially, in this paper we hold the agent fixed and evaluate only the simulated user’s turns in a transcript. 

3.2 Sociolinguistic personas 

The sociolinguistic persona is authored as a set of stance-linked stylistic features with corresponding in-structions directing the LLM to instantiate them. We designed eight personas that instantiate example cus-tomer archetypes intended to be plausible across five service verticals (see 3.4), with each persona’s per-feature bands set by hand by a sociolinguist. An exam-ple persona schema is provided in Appendix A. In this schema (henceforth the definition schema), a persona is specified as ordinal rate targets over eight linguis-tic features: question rate, message length, hedging, politeness markers, filler, all-caps, computer-mediated communication (CMC) acronyms such as lol, omg, wtf 

etc., and affect valence. Each is then compiled to a band (NONE < LOW < MEDIUM < HIGH ) in the instruc-tion prompt. We add per-modality targets for markers specific to text or voice (e.g. emoji and CMC acronym use are text-only). This schema also contains a “be-havioural_contract” block with a set of rules translating sociolinguistic markers into commands and rules coun-teracting the LLM’s default assistant behaviour. 2 The 

archetype baseline describes the same personas in the conventional way: as a set of defining adjectival traits, communication style, situational motivations, and ex-ample phrases. The A/B test isolates the measurable and instructive “what to do” versus the implicit and de-scriptive “what you are” persona design (§3.4). Stating the target as an observable rate is what makes a persona both authorable at feature granularity and directly leg-ible to the model-free metrics of §3.3, where we also define how each observed rate is computed. 

3.3 Persona Fidelity metrics 3.3.1 Stylometric distinguishability (authorship-verification ROC–AUC) 

We adapt authorship verification as an unsupervised separability test, a framing that mirrors how Bao et al.

(2026) operationalize simulator consistency. Each con-versation’s simulated-user turns are concatenated and fingerprinted with a character n-gram TF-IDF vector (char_wb , n ∈ [2 , 4] ), a standard authorship-verification representation (as in the PAN shared tasks 3) dominated by orthography, morphology, and function-word pat-terns rather than topics (Aggazzotti et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024). We compute the cosine similarity of every conversation pair, label each pair same- or different-persona, and report the ROC-AUC of similarity as a predictor of the same-persona label. AUC is the proba-bility that a random same-persona pair is more similar than a random different-persona pair: 0.5 denotes in-distinguishable personas, and 1.0 perfect separability. Because the statistic rewards both between-persona dis-tinctiveness and within-persona consistency, a simula-tor that collapses to a bland neutral voice scores near chance. Crucially, the metric is feature-agnostic: it pre-supposes none of the authored style features (§3.3.2), providing an independent check that distinctiveness is not an artifact of the features we chose to author. Four properties make the estimate more trustworthy. (i) A label-permutation control (shuffle persona ids, re-compute AUC) collapses to ≈ 0.5, confirming the met-ric reads genuine persona identity rather than a shared baseline. (ii) Because every persona runs an identical scenario set, topic is crossed with persona identity and therefore cancels in the same-minus-different similarity gap the AUC rewards. (iii) The measurement is a fixed transform (character n-gram TF-IDF with cosine simi-larity) with no trained parameters, held identical across the archetype and definition arms, so any difference in AUC is attributable to the simulated text rather than the detector. (iv) We report a bootstrap 90% CI over conver-sations and size the definition −archetype schema effect with a paired bootstrap on the AUC delta; alongside the AUC we report the within- versus between-persona sim-ilarity gap, which separates within-persona consistency from between-persona distinctiveness. 

3.3.2 Stylistic adherence (Kendall’s τb)

Distinguishability certifies that personas differ, but it does not check that they differ as specified. Stylistic adherence is a rank-based test of spec-to-behaviour alignment. For each authored feature f , we map each persona’s specified band ( NONE < LOW <

We use τb for its tie-tolerance and robustness on the small per-persona n typical of a pilot, and report Spearman’s ρ alongside. Because the metrics are sensitive to different things, reading them jointly allows us to localize where fidelity breaks down. And since they are deterministic measures, they are sta-ble enough to hold as a fixed instrument while we vary what they measure. 

3.4 Experimental design A/B factorial. We use factorial A/B design to test our persona schema in a two-part experiment. First, we cross the persona schema (archetype vs. definition) with the simulator model in a 2×2, so a change in fidelity can be attributed to schema or model rather than confounded. Second, we ran the schema A/B in a voice modality to test whether the effect replicates in a realtime voice pipeline. The schema factor’s main effect measures how much of a fidelity gap is attributable to spec underspeci-fication. The model factor’s main effect tests whether a newer, more capable generation or higher reasoning effort makes it easier to steer, and the schema ×model in-teraction tests whether the richer schema pays off more for newer and stronger models. Finally, we include the voice mode to test whether the schema effect holds up under a different-modality pipeline. 

Personas. We test eight customer archetypes: the Anxious Inquirer, Busy Multitasker, Frustrated Com-plainer, Methodical Researcher, Social Chatterbox, Tech-Savvy Expert, Technophobe, and Vague Story-teller, shared across the two schemas under test. Each were chosen to span the stance and style dimensions encoded in the definition schema (§3.2). Across the set, affective baselines range from anxious and angry to calm and cheerful, epistemic stance from deferential and low-certainty to confident and authoritative, and each of the eight stylistic features from NONE to HIGH .

Agents and corpus. We run five task-oriented customer-service agents across 5 verticals (real es-tate, healthcare, agricultural, automotive, and technical-support), spanning tasks such as appointment booking, eligibility and identity checks, knowledge-base lookups, and request routing. Each bot interacted with eight dis-tinct user personas. To ensure a consistent baseline, we drew from a larger gold-standard set to create a topic-matched, manually verified subset of 5 scenarios, which were reused across all personas and test arms, yielding five conversations per persona, per arm. We scrubbed the data of the conversations that failed due to technical errors such as connection or time out issues, and dis-carded arms with a high failure rate. This resulted in 4103 clean conversations across all arms. 

Prompt encoding. All 9 text models tested are instruction-tuned assistants and receive the identical role-play prompt: persona schema, scenario goal, scenario-specific information (e.g. desired appointment time), and user-profile content (e.g. name, email ad-dress, phone number, etc.; all synthetic), wrapped into the same explicit turn-taking and end-of-conversation directives. The voice arm takes the same input, but uses the gpt-4o-realtime-preview model’s native speech pipeline with the default settings.

## 5 Discussion and conclusion 

Do the metrics transfer? Both metrics yield action-able, internally consistent signal in simulated user per-sona fidelity measurement for task-oriented dialogue. They also detect what the design set out to test: the sociolinguistically-grounded definition schema (specify-ing each persona as concrete stylistic rates with ‘what to do‘ instructions) produced more faithful and stylistically distinct simulated users than a schema based on descrip-tive terms with ‘what you are‘ instructions, improving both spec adherence and inter-persona distinguishabil-ity over the baseline for six of tenmodels tested. They also enabled localization of the schema effect to spe-cific stylistic features. A small scale human preference analysis (Appendix E) shows that the metrics generally align with human judgment at the extremes, with some exceptions among smaller effects. 

Specifying persona sociolinguistically improves user simulator fidelity, but faithfulness ̸ = naturalness. 

Grounding personas in specific linguistic rates makes them inherently measurable and directly informs the prescriptive (“what to do”) instructions. Compared to broader descriptive archetypes (“who you are”), this combined schema produces more linguistically diverse and faithful renderings. However, while the definition schema reliably raises scores on both metrics, qualitative analysis reveals a critical caveat: rate-based adherence certifies that a feature appears at the specified relative frequency and ordering , not that it is realized naturally .The model renders some markers formulaically, reduc-ing politeness to repetitive “Thank you so much!” / “I hope that’s okay!”, and disfluency to a predictable “um” at the start of a sentence. We can partially combat this by adding the measures of lexical diversity (e.g. Type-Token Ratio, Shannon Entropy) or conditioning them on specific dialog acts, but it remains difficult to capture the full nuance of persona authenticity with deterministic scoring only. mulation; the metrics are a useful tool for determining which is most performant, and which makes a poor simulator. 

The metrics work as a design instrument for author-ing personas. Part of the modest distinguishability also reflects genuine overlap between personas. Qualita-tively, a chatty “social” persona bleeds into a rambling “storyteller,” and an anxious persona into a technophobic one. They are distinct in specification but convergent in observable style. Thus, low pairwise separability flags persona definitions that should be pulled apart to maxi-mize diversity, closing the loop from measurement back to authoring. 

Conclusion. Treating persona fidelity as a determinis-tic, spec-mapped diagnostic on observable style turns an opaque, judge-dependent quality signal into a feature-level, reproducible, and attributable one. The proposed metrics have proven most useful in error-attribution anal-ysis, localizing where fidelity breaks down, as a first step towards interventions that would move user simu-lations towards more faithful renderings of diverse and variable linguistic outputs. That is both a prerequisite for trusting a simulator as a measurement instrument, and a concrete step toward narrowing the sim2real gap. 

## Limitations 

Scale and scope: This is a pilot study that covers five agents from a single domain (customer service) on one platform, with eight personas and five conversations per persona per arm; per-agent confidence intervals overlap, so the result rests on cross-agent consistency rather than any single significant effect. 

Model coverage: All models in our study are instruction-tuned and aligned to act as "helpful assis-tants". We can not determine whether this underly-ing alignment inherently limits achievable persona fi-delity. We originally planned to test a user-aligned model U SER LM-8 B to isolate this effect, but it proved too unstable as a next-turn predictor and was left for future work. 

Construct validity: The metrics measure stylistic fi-delity via eight engineered features and a character-n-gram fingerprint. This operationalization of “style” does not capture task/goal success, semantic appropriateness, or stylistic dimensions outside the feature set. Follow-up experiments will mine the sociolinguistic and stylomet-ric literature for broader markers (e.g. intensification, discourse markers, syntactic complexity, etc.) and ver-ify if our findings hold. We will also isolate the effects of specific stylistic markers and other parts of the spec, e.g. specific types of instructions. 

Static persona specification: The persona is supplied only once, as a prompt at the start of the conversation, with no in-dialogue reinforcement; the simulator must sustain it unaided across many turns, the very condition under which aligned models tend to drift back toward a default “assistant” voice (Xiao et al., 2026). Our results therefore characterize fidelity under one-shot prompting. Testing live, in-conversation persona steering is reserved for future research. 

Length-dependent distinguishability: The ability to distinguish between personas relies heavily on conver-sation length. Mean transcript length correlates with the definition-arm AUC at r = 0.79 (and with the schema effect at r = 0.73) across the five agents. Longer con-versations naturally provide more opportunities to drop stylistic markers, and thus distinguishability is driven both by the persona schema and the speech the agent elicits from the persona. Follow-up experiments will incorporate length normalization to better isolate this effect.

The paper discusses simulation fidelity using LLM-simulated users and hand-authored lexicons, with no human subjects data involved. We access the assets used in this study according to their stated licenses and providers’ APIs. Annotation in the preference align-ment study was performed in-house, without external annotation services.

## E Human Preference Analysis 

We run a small scale blinded human preference analysis (archetype vs. definition vs. no preference; 10 randomly sampled transcript pairs per model), with two annotators rating pairs of transcripts produced by the two persona schemas under otherwise identical settings. 

Per-model trends. Directionally, human preference tracked the metrics at the extremes. For the two strongest effects on the automated metrics – 

claude-opus-5 (∆AUC = +0 .105 , the largest adher-ence gain in the study) and claude-sonnet-5 (∆AUC =+0 .095 ) – annotators expressed a definite preference in most cases, favoring the definition schema. At the other end, gemini-3.5-flash (low) ’s near-zero effect cor-responds to "no preference" in 85% of cases. An inter-esting exception is gemini-3.5-flash (high) , where the automated AUC delta was flat ( +0 .001 ) but human annotators expressed a definite preference in 62% of cases and favored the definition arm. 

Per-persona trends. The two personas with low-est distinguishability (tech_savvy_expert and 

busy_multitasker ) across every model were also rated as mostly "no preference" by the annotators. Interestingly, frustrated_complainer , with the lowest "no preference" rate, leaned towards archetype schema while the automated metrics showed modest gains for definition schema. The sample size for each persona is small and inconsistent, and these findings should be taken with a grain of salt.
