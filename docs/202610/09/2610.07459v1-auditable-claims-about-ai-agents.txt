Abstract. Organizations make claims about their AI agents: a person ap-proves every external email, every action is logged, an evaluation shows the agent is safe to deploy. Article 12 of the EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to allow the automatic recording of events but does not say which records settle a given claim. The position is one sentence: to be checked, a claim about an agent must first name its policy, its scope, the records that would settle it, and who writes them. Adapting the preconditions of an assurance engagement, we call a claim auditable when these elements and a decision rule are fixed before any verdict and the records are obtainable. This extends the Policy Checkability dimension of our Auditable Agents framework from single actions to claims. Agents add three conditions: coverage by an independent record, authorization bound to each action’s arguments, and completeness beyond integrity. Under an explicit model, we prove that support is impossible without each wherever its hypotheses hold. A claim-check table applies the method to six common claims, anchored in cur-rent NIST, IETF, and OWASP drafts. A worked case follows one claim through five evidence states. We close with a practice box and steps for operators, buyers, auditors, and standard setters. 

Keywords: AI agents · Auditing · Auditable claims · Audit evidence · Authori-zation · Human oversight · AI standards. 

## 1 Introduction 

Claims about AI agents appear in system documentation, procurement answers, and incident reports, and others may later need to check them. Consider a firm whose doc-ument agent summarizes a matter folder and emails documents. The firm states that “a person approves every external email”. On 6 March 2026, the agent sends email tx-23 , with an attachment, to press@example.net after a confirmation in its chat inter-face. The mail gateway logs the delivery, but the log of the firm’s authorization server holds no grant that covers it. Three questions become central: what exactly the claim covers, which records would settle it and who writes them, and what stays out of reach even when every record is present. 2 Y. Zhao et al. 

Rules about records are arriving. Article 12 of the EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to “technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the life-time of the system” [9]. Its Article 14 requires that overseers be able to understand the system’s limitations and stay aware of automation bias. These duties apply from 2 De-cember 2027 for Annex III uses and from 2 August 2028 for Annex I products [10].

Standards bodies are drafting guidance on logging [13], documentation [1], evalua-tion [20], and agent authorization [14]. These documents say what a system should rec-ord or enable. Those we could read do not say, for a particular claim, which records would settle it or what stays out of reach. Auditors already test claims this way: an assurance engagement checks a statement against suitable criteria, with evidence that must be obtainable [11]. We adapt that method to claims about agents. Agents change where the evidence comes from. Their actions may leave no record outside the agent’s own log, the agent under control often records its own approvals, and signed logs are offered as audit trails.

## 2 Auditable Claims 

An audit here starts from a claim, such as “every email with an attachment that the agent sent outside the firm in March met policy P-7”. Whether that claim can be checked depends first on the claim itself. 

Definition 1. A claim about an AI agent is auditable when four things are specified: policy and version, actions and period covered, required records obtainable from named writers, and the decision rule. 4 Y. Zhao et al. 

A claim such as “our agent is safe” fails the definition before any record is read. Even a claim that meets it can be UNDECIDABLE when a required record is lost. Defini-tion 2 gives the decision rule that C2 to C4 in Table 2 and the worked case share. C1, C5, and C6 keep the rules in that table. 

Definition 2. A claim that every in-scope action meets a stated policy is (i)  

> CONTRADICTED

when the required records establish that an authenticated in-scope ac-tion violated the policy. Where the violation is an absence, such as a missing grant, the record that should hold it must be complete for the relevant period. The claim is (ii)  

> SUPPORTED

when coverage evidence shows that every in-scope action was captured (Condition 1) and each one conforms. Otherwise it is (iii) UNDECIDABLE , and the audit names the missing record. 

Auditable Agents [17] judges one action: under a stated policy, its verdict is comply or violate. A missing required field can make that assessment undecidable, however many other events are logged. The chapter judges a claim over many actions, so support also needs coverage evidence. Records differ in when they exist and in who writes them. CatchBench [26] distin-guishes the declared configuration before a run (PRE), the steps recorded so far during the run (LIVE), and the record of the finished run (POST). A configuration can show what an agent could do; only a run record shows what it did. The writer may be the agent, an authorization server that issues grants, or a gateway that carries the action’s effect. The definition names writers because whether a record can be trusted depends on who wrote it and who could change it. 

What we borrow. The definition adapts the preconditions of an assurance engagement. Before accepting one, a practitioner under ISAE 3000 asks whether the criteria are suit-able and whether the evidence needed for a conclusion can be obtained [11]. Our policy and version play the role of criteria, the actions and period the role of subject matter, and the records the role of evidence. ISA 315 separates occurrence, that recorded events happened, from completeness, that every event was recorded [12]; the case in Section 4 tests both. We borrow this structure only. Our verdicts are not assurance opinions, and we leave aside materiality, sampling, and auditor independence, which a professional engagement would address. 

Related work. Brundage et al. proposed institutional, software, and hardware mecha-nisms that make claims about AI development verifiable, audit trails included [2]. Ojewale et al. link tamper-evident audit trails for language model systems to govern-ance records such as approvals, waivers, and attestations [18]. In a safety case, a devel-oper argues that a system is safe [6]; an auditable claim is a statement that a third party checks against records. Broader frameworks cover the internal audit process [21], governance, model, and application audits [16], and auditor access [3], and for agents, visibility [5]. We work one level below them and ask what a single claim about a deployed agent needs in order to be checked. Auditable Claims about AI Agents 5

## 4 Worked Case: One Claim, Five Evidence States 

This case continues the external-email scenario of Auditable Agents [17]; its records are constructed for teaching, and no agent was run. 

Setting. A firm deploys a document agent that may read one matter folder, summarize it, and email documents. The firm uses the OAuth architecture AIMS describes [14], with the agent as an OAuth client. Its authorization server issues each grant as a signed token, one of the formats AIMS allows. The mail gateway computes the hash of every attachment it delivers (a fingerprint that changes if the file does). Policy P-7 requires each external email with an attachment to fall within a grant that a person approved before sending (Table 3). 

Making the claim auditable. The operator states that “a person approves every external email”. Table 3 rewrites it as an auditable claim: every email with an attachment that the agent sent to an external address in March met P-7, version 3. The claim is narrower than the statement. It leaves out emails without attachments, counts a prior batch ap-proval, and covers only this version. Its three records come from three writers. Figure 2 shows two records the auditor receives.

State A: configuration only (PRE). The tool manifest (the list of tools the agent may call) grants send_email with no limit on the recipient domain and configures an ap-proval step. This settles a configuration question: the agent may email any domain, although its task involves one outside counsel. The claim about March is UNDECIDABLE ,because a configuration records no transmissions. 

State B: three records agree. For email tx-19 , the agent log names grant g-5531 , and the authorization server logged at 10:02:11 that user:alvarez approved that grant for the same recipient and hash. The gateway logged delivery to that recipient at 10:02:15, with a hash it computed that matches. The token verifies and was valid at sending. This email met P-7, and the finding rests on three separately written records (C2, C3). The March claim still needs every other delivery matched. 

State C: confirmation without authorization. Email tx-23 carries a confirmation from the agent’s chat interface and names no grant. The gateway logged its delivery to an external address, with a hash matching the agent’s record. Complete for the period in which a grant could have been issued, the server’s log holds none that covers it. This one authenticated exception makes the March claim CONTRADICTED , provided the server log is complete. The record supports only a narrow statement: the interface logged a confirmation under the user’s account. AIMS states that such confirmations “do not by themselves constitute authorization” and must be bound to a grant from the authorization server (§10.7). The requirement that each external email fall within an approved grant comes from P-7; AIMS leaves such policy to the deployment (§12). The verdict does not show that the user lacked intent to send. Auditable Agents [17] asks that approvals be rec-orded; the case adds the distinction between a local confirmation and a verifiable grant, which here decides the verdict. 

State D: a missing field. For email tx-31 , the agent log names no grant and lacks the correlation identifier, and the server’s log for that hour was lost in a storage migration. The grant P-7 requires is absent from every surviving record, so the verdict for this email is UNDECIDABLE [17]. Because that absence is in the record and may not be in the world, reporting a P-7 violation would be an error. The lost log is a record-keeping finding in its own right. 8 Y. Zhao et al. 

State E: a signed log and an extra email. The supplied agent log is hash-chained (each entry includes a hash of the previous one) and signed, and it verifies. The gateway, however, shows four external emails with attachments from the agent’s account, and the log records three. Verification authenticates the supplied entries; without an inde-pendent checkpoint, it cannot rule out a rewritten chain. The agent may have sent the fourth email outside its logged path, the log may have dropped its entry, or a person may have used the same account. The server holds no grant matching that email, so its verdict stays UNDECIDABLE until another record, such as the gateway’s client identifier, shows who sent it.

## 5 Three Conditions Agents Add 

The case turned on three tests that audit practice already applies. Agents change how each can be met, so we state each as a condition on the evidence. 

Model. The audited party , the agent and the team that runs it, writes and may sign its own records. It may omit an in-scope action but never records one it did not take. An 

independent writer , such as the mail gateway or the authorization server run by other teams, is outside its control and records accurately what passes through it. The audited party cannot alter or forge such records. An approval such a writer records for a signed-in account is its holder’s. A complete record holds every event of its kind in its period. A world is one history of the period, records included, that fits this model. A rule is Auditable Claims about AI Agents 9

sound if, from the records it reads alone, it returns SUPPORTED only when the claim of Definition 2 holds and CONTRADICTED only when it fails. 

Lemma 1 (Same records). A sound rule returns UNDECIDABLE in two worlds where it reads the same records if the claim holds in one but not the other. 

Proof. The rule returns one verdict for both. SUPPORTED would be wrong where the claim fails, and CONTRADICTED where it holds. □ In Fig. 3, the agent log hides the difference between the worlds; the independent gateway records the omitted send c.

Proposition 1 (One exception suffices). Let the policy require a grant for each in-scope action, as P-7 does. Suppose the records show an in-scope action, and the grant log, complete for the relevant period, holds no grant covering it. Then CONTRADICTED 

is correct in every world with these records. 

Proof. Independent writers are accurate and the audited party fabricates no action, so the action occurs in every such world. The complete grant log, which the audited party cannot alter, would hold any covering grant, so the claim fails. □ In State C, the gateway shows the delivery and the agent’s own entry shows that it sent tx-23 , so the proposition applies if the server log is complete. 

1. Coverage. Was every action recorded? Support needs evidence that rules out an un-recorded violating action; a record of each action that the audited party does not write is one way to provide it. Auditors test the completeness assertion [12] by tracing from independent sources, such as confirmations from third parties, into the books. An agent’s log has no independent counterpart by default: the agent writes it, and nothing else records each action. In State E, the agent log records three of the four emails the gateway shows. That record can be a record of effects or the log of a gateway that every relevant action must pass (State B). Reconciling the agent log with it in both directions tests 10 Y. Zhao et al. 

occurrence and completeness together. Condition 1 refines the action coverage that Au-ditable Agents measures under Action Recoverability. 

Corollary 1 (Coverage). Suppose every world could gain a violating in-scope action without changing the independent records a sound rule reads, as when it reads only the audited party’s records. Then it never returns SUPPORTED .

Proof. If the claim fails, SUPPORTED is wrong. Otherwise, add that action, which the audited party omits. The claim fails on the same records, so Lemma 1 applies. □ 

2. Authorization. Did a person approve these arguments? The evidence must establish the approval and the argument scope that the policy requires; P-7 requires a prior user-approved grant naming the recipient and attachment hash. A common control separates the person who approves a payment from the person who makes it, with the approval recorded independently. South et al. argue that agents need authenticated delegation, so that others can verify who authorized an agent to act [23]. Yet an agent often records its own approvals. In State C, the agent’s interface wrote the confirmation, which rec-ords only an interface event attributed to an account. Neither a confirmation nor a grant shows what the person understood. For high-risk systems, Article 14 requires that over-seers be able to understand the system’s limitations and stay aware of automation bias [9]. A confirmation log is evidence of neither. Condition 2 refines Lifecycle Cov-erage, where Auditable Agents asks that approvals be recorded. 

Corollary 2 (Authorization). Let the policy require that a person approve each in-scope action with its effect-setting arguments. Suppose the independent records do not show such an approval for some action, as when only the audited party records it or they omit a value. Then no sound rule returns SUPPORTED .

Proof. If the claim fails, SUPPORTED is wrong. Otherwise, take a twin world in which no person gave that approval. The independent records do not show it and can stay the same, as can the audited party’s, so Lemma 1 applies. □ 

3. Integrity versus completeness. Is a verified log complete? Tamper-evident logs are often offered as audit trails; AIMS requires them (§11). A signature authenticates the entries supplied, and a hash chain anchored with an independent party exposes later edits to its entries [7,22]. In a recent preprint, a hash chain over the log that an agent harness (the software running the agent) keeps about itself flagged none of 28 injected omissions and fabrications. A log kept outside the harness flagged all 28 [8]. Condi-tion 3 refines Evidence Integrity, which Auditable Agents grades from none through append-only and hash-chained to signed [17]. 

Corollary 3 (Integrity versus completeness). Under the premise of Corollary 1, sig-natures, hash chains, and independent checkpoints of the entries the audited party sub-mits cannot justify SUPPORTED .

Proof. An omitted violating action changes neither the audited party’s signed entries nor the checkpoint of those entries, and both still verify. The two worlds remain con-sistent with the model, so Corollary 1 applies. □ Auditable Claims about AI Agents 11 

Asymmetry. Contradiction can rest on one authenticated action and a complete grant log (Proposition 1). Support needs independent records that would show any violating ac-tion (Corollary 1). The corollaries give necessary conditions only; sufficiency needs more, such as a gateway that no action can bypass. 

Limits of a verdict. A verdict concerns a stated policy; it does not by itself decide legal compliance or liability. 

Alternative views. We answer three objections. A signed, tamper-evident log is enough :it cannot show events it never recorded (Corollary 3). A person should approve every action one at a time : per-action approval may invite automation bias, and a grant for a scoped batch stays checkable (Condition 2). Article 12 logging suffices : it requires re-cording but names no records or writers that settle a claim (Definition 1).

## 6 What Each Reader Can Do 

Auditable Agents proposed adoption paths for papers, benchmarks, frameworks, and skill ecosystems [17]. Claims about deployed agents concern four other groups of read-ers. 

Operators and developers. Write each public claim in the form of Definition 1 before publishing it, and keep the filled worksheet with it. Route consequential actions through a component the agent does not control, such as a gateway, and let that component write the counterpart record. Ask a person to approve a scoped batch of actions through the authorization server, rather than confirm each action in the agent’s interface. Publishing the Auditability Card beside the claim tells readers which records exist. 

Buyers and procurement teams. Ask vendors to state claims in that form and to name the writer of each record. Ask whether the buyer, or an auditor it appoints, can obtain those records. Read “tamper-proof logs” as claim C5 and ask which record the agent does not write. For an evaluation claim, ask for results per data source against a trivial baseline, with the evaluated version named (C6). 

Auditors. Agree on the decision rule with the operator before examining records, as an assurance engagement requires [11]. Report UNDECIDABLE together with the record that would have settled the question, and report a lost log as a record-keeping finding apart from the verdict (State D). 

Standard setters. Three additions to current guidance would make more claims decid-able. The second and third qualify the book roadmap’s advice to escalate high risks to human authorities and to log all interactions [15]. 1. Versioned system controls (C1). NIST AI 300-1 leaves entire systems to future work, and its profiles may add fields but not change the base templates [1]. Approval gates and execution policies thus have no defined field. The 300-1 comment pro-posed asking what decides whether an action the model emits takes effect [25]. 2. Authorization bound to arguments (C3). AIMS binds a local confirmation to a grant and notes that “Additional specification or design work may be needed” for interactions with the user during execution (§10.7). A profile could bind each ap-proval to the exact arguments it covers, and the AIMS minimum audit fields could include them. 3. A named second writer (C2, C5). Guidance could ask each deployment to name a second writer, such as the authorization server or a gateway, for every class of con-sequential action, and to reconcile the two routinely. That record should keep only the fields the decision rule needs, under access and retention limits (AIMS §10.9). Accountability logs kept by smart contracts, which the roadmap names as one can-didate for assigning responsibility [15], need the same second writer. Auditable Claims about AI Agents 13 

## 7 Summary, Limitations, and Discussion Questions 

Summary. A claim about an AI agent is auditable when its policy, scope, records with their writers, and decision rule are fixed before the check, and the records are obtaina-ble. Agents add three conditions: coverage, authorization bound to arguments, and com-pleteness beyond integrity. Under the model and each corollary’s hypotheses, support is impossible without them. The filled worksheet in Table 3 records one such check, and Section 6 turns the conditions into requests that operators, buyers, auditors, and standard setters can make. 

Limitations. The anchors are drafts, and logging standards are still moving. We could not read ISO/IEC 24970, at the final-draft stage [13], or prEN 18229-1, under ap-proval [4]. The Article 12 and 14 duties discussed here apply to high-risk AI systems, and whether an agent qualifies depends on its use or product. The chapter omits privacy and data minimization [17], responsibility across chains of delegating agents, and se-mantic policies that record schemas cannot decide. Our case measures nothing, and the box covers only the offline PRE board of our own benchmark. We did not test the method. A test would seed known violations with complete reference records, then delete fields, records, or the independent record and re-sign a trimmed log (States D and E). It would score correct verdicts, correct  

> UNDECIDABLE

answers, false CONTRADICTED verdicts, and time per check. 

Discussion questions. 

1. Claims. Rewrite a public statement about an AI agent’s approval feature as an au-ditable claim, using the worksheet of Table 3. Which of the four elements did the statement lack, and which evidence would change your verdict? 2. Silence. Who should bear the uncertainty when a record is silent, and should silence ever count as a violation? 3. Confirmation. In State C, the interface logged a confirmation but no grant exists. When, if ever, should that confirmation carry legal or organizational weight? 4. Second writers. Who in your setting could keep an independent record of an agent’s actions, and what privacy obligations would that record create? 5. Evaluation. A benchmark reports one pooled score for a safety detector. What would you ask before using it in a procurement decision?
