# From Token-Max to Outcome-Max: How You Use AI Determines Its Productivity 

Chen Xu 1, Mengqiao Liu *2, Beibei Li 1 and Chenyan Xiong 1 

> 1Carnegie Mellon University, 2Amazon

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) models can perform increasingly complex tasks, yet greater AI usage does not necessarily translate into proportional productivity gains. We identify token-max as one source of this inefficiency: when token consumption is treated as productive effort, agents are encouraged to over-exert and expend computation beyond what is necessary. We instead propose outcome-max , which rewards independently verified task completion per unit cost and induces a principled stopping rule. Then, to study these objectives, we develop a three-level simulation framework spanning immediate interaction, long-run behavioral adaptation, and organizational collaboration. Across all three levels, outcome-max improves the efficiency of AI-assisted production while largely preserving verified task performance. To further align these incentives with outcome-max, we introduce OutcomeShare , an incentive mechanism. Theory and simulation show that OutcomeShare can induce participation while generating shared gains for employees, firms, and LLM providers. Together, our results suggest that AI productivity not just depends on model capability, but also on the objectives governing AI use.

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) can improve productiv-ity in software development [Cui et al., 2026] and professional writing [Noy and Zhang, 2023]. Yet realizing these gains re-quires balancing greater adoption with its costs. New reports of token-usage leaderboards [Roose, 2026] and Meta’s plans to limit employee token spending [Mann, 2026] reveal a tension between encouraging use and controlling costs: why can greater AI usage fail to improve productivity? 

We argue that one source of this inefficiency is the token-max 

policy, which treats greater token consumption as evidence of productive effort. Yet more tokens do not necessarily produce better outcomes. Figure 1 illustrates this pattern in both our sim-ulations and real developer sessions: success initially increases but then plateaus or declines as interactions grow longer. The rea-son is due to over-efforting effect: excessive interaction expands the reasoning context, encourages unnecessary and redundant checking and revision. These observations motivate an alternative objective. Inspired by economic theory for reward-ing inputs versus rewarding outcomes Lazear [2000], we propose outcome-max , which rewards independently verified task completion per unit cost. Under this objective, further computation is justified when its expected marginal return exceeds the return achieved so far.

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed objective, we develop a three-level simulation framework spanning single-task execution, textual-memory adaptation, and employee selection. 3. Framework: From Token-Max to Outcome-Max 

In this section, we aim to formalize AI supervision around interaction volume or verified output per cost, then simulate the corresponding supervisory behaviors across execution, adaptation, and selection (Figure 2). 

3.1. Notation and observable outcomes

4. Mechanism: OutcomeShare 

In this section, we aim to specify conditions for aligning provider, employee, and firm incentives through OutcomeShare: payments for verified work, liability for defects, and shared value. 

4.1. From an objective to an implementable contract 

The intuition behind OutcomeShare is to share the value of verified outcomes among the provider, employee, and firm, thereby rewarding productive work rather than unnecessary token consump-tion. At the same time, liability for defective delivery discourages employees from approving or misrepresenting unsuccessful work as successful. The outcome-sharing and liability terms are fixed before production, and participation constraints ensure that each stakeholder benefits under the specified valuation and cost assumptions.

4.3. Theoretical analysis 

The results below characterize objective properties and verification losses (Appendix B), not LLM optimization or contract adoption. 

Theorem 1 (Objective response to extra computation) . Let π, π′ be feasible policies on the same task distribution with positive expected cost. If π′ increases expected tokens and cost while leaving positive expected verified output unchanged, then J tok (π′) > Jtok (π) and J out (π′) < Jout (π). More generally, for baseline output Y, cost C > 0, and changes ∆Y and ∆C > 0, outcome-max improves if and only if 

∆Y/∆C > Y/C. 

Matching expected defect costs aligns private and social verification effort. 

Theorem 2 (Pigouvian verification alignment) . Let verification effort e ∈ E cost c (e) and induce shipped-defect probability δ(e), with external damage κV per defect. Assume a risk-neutral employee, a fixed effective audit probability p > 0, a collectible fine F, and all other private and social payoff terms constant in e. If E is nonempty and both losses attain their minima, then pF = κV implies that private loss c (e) + pF δ(e) and social loss c (e) + κVδ(e) have identical minimizers. 

Implications. Theorem 1 favors continuation when its marginal verified gain per dollar exceeds the current average. Hard-task losses therefore motivate better verification signals, rather than uniformly shorter work. Theorem 2 aligns verification by matching expected liability to defect damage. 

4.4. From efficiency to shared gains 

Efficient production creates surplus; fees and bonuses divide it. Shared gains require value sufficient to cover production, supervision, verification, and defects while satisfying participation constraints (Eq. (5)). Thus efficiency creates the opportunity, while quality control and allocation determine whether participants benefit.

5.1. Experimental setting 

Tasks and external evaluation. We evaluate externally verifiable work across software engi-neering, program synthesis, and mathematics. SWE-bench Verified requires repairing repository issues, with correctness determined by containerized gold tests [Jimenez et al., 2024, OpenAI, 2024]. HumanEval+ evaluates generated programs with extended tests [Liu et al., 2023]. GSM8K and MATH-500 evaluate extracted final answers through normalized comparison [Cobbe et al., 2021, Lightman et al., 2024]. SWE-bench supports execution, adaptation, and workforce-selection experi-ments; the other benchmarks test whether the execution-level contrast extends beyond repository repair. Neither the employee nor the production agent sees the gold evaluation outcome during an interaction. An employee’s decision to accept therefore remains distinct from whether the submitted artifact is correct. Difficulty groups are defined from benchmark metadata or reference-solution structure, rather than from the observed success of either objective. Software repair uses estimated human repair time, competition mathematics uses benchmark difficulty labels, and the remaining tasks use reference-solution complexity proxies. These proxies describe task structure and need not rank model success monotonically. The mathematics stratification also relies on reconstructed task ordering. Appendix A.1 provides the grouping rules and their limitations. 

Models, personas, and the objective contrast. The production agent performs the task, while a separate employee model requests work, reviews the response, and decides whether to continue. Employee personas vary how clearly they describe the task and how actively they scrutinize the result. Profiles cover broad guidance and checking styles as well as individual behaviors such as asking for examples, clarifying goals, and requesting revisions. The behavioral dimensions and their prompt encoding are given in Table S1 and Appendix D. Within each paired comparison, the task, persona, model assignment, evaluator, and resource limits are held fixed. The main treatment changes the supervisory instructions: token-max encour-ages continued interaction, whereas outcome-max emphasizes a correct result with economical resource use. This compares specified policy packages; it does not establish that a payment objective alone causes those behaviors to emerge. Objective-only prompts provide a separate, narrower check of that distinction. Model assignments also differ between the static, adaptation, and cross-task cohorts, so absolute success differences across these cohorts do not isolate learning or model capability. 

Execution, adaptation, and selection protocols. The execution experiment crosses a common task roster with fixed employee profiles under both objectives. Interaction ends when the employee accepts or a shared resource limit is reached; final artifacts are evaluated externally regardless of acceptance. This separates the consequences of supervision within a task from changes in policy across tasks. The adaptation experiment adds reusable employee and agent playbooks. After training, reflection receives outcome summaries and the current playbook, but not task text, artifacts, or transcripts. It updates the employee playbook, the agent playbook, or both, depending on the ablation. Employee reflection retains the assigned supervisory objective, while agent reflection targets successful production with lower resource use. Model weights remain fixed. Updated playbooks are frozen before evaluation on disjoint held-out tasks, and held-out outcomes are never passed back to reflection. Thus the comparison tests transfer of learned routines rather than reuse of test-task solutions; Algorithm 1 gives the update procedure.

5.3. Evaluating OutcomeShare 

5.3.1. RQ4: How do heuristic contracts affect delivery and payoffs? 

In this section, we aim to evaluate delivery and payoffs when OutcomeShare is applied to fixed production under specified inspection assumptions. Table 3 compares token billing with outcome-based payments and damage-matched liability under a common task-value and salary scenario. 

Delivery and accounting design. We apply alternative submission rules to already generated artifacts, keeping their correctness and production costs fixed. Weekly attempted work is limited by both agent throughput and employee review capacity; rejecting an artifact does not recover that capacity. A hypothetical inspection signal yields a calibrated correctness probability. Under liability, the employee submits only when the expected bonus outweighs the expected fine. Token-billing baselines deliver all artifacts; alternative rules use confidence thresholds or random screening. The signal is a stylized Gaussian observation model with the observed arm-level success rate as its

prior, not an empirically trained verifier. Exact calibration and accounting assumptions remain in Appendix C.3.4. Provider fees and employee bonuses are shares of verified delivered value, with employee salary paid separately. Defective deliveries incur losses to the firm, while collectible fines transfer value from the employee to the firm. Those transfers cancel in aggregate; defect losses do not. Provider accounts measure revenue before serving costs. This separates producing more correct work, controlling defective shipments, and distributing the resulting value among participants. 

Delivery tradeoffs and shared gains. Under token billing, outcome-max increases correct produc-tion but also ships more defects, leaving the firm unprofitable. OutcomeShare makes delivery more selective: it sacrifices some correct outputs while removing a much larger share of defects. Relative to token-max, it delivers more correct work with fewer defects and improves provider revenue, employee income, and firm profit. The employee-heavy allocation gives the highest firm-profit estimate among the tested OutcomeShare rules (Appendix C.3.4). The employee-heavy allocation is selected from the tested contracts by its firm-profit point estimate; it is not an independently validated optimum. Its higher bonus changes the employee’s willingness to deliver, so allocation and delivery must be assessed jointly rather than treating sharing as a transfer applied after a fixed submission decision. Overall, OutcomeShare combines efficient production with defect control and shared gains in this scenario. Its advantage depends on selective delivery and sufficient task value, rather than maximizing shipment volume alone. Valuation sensitivity shows that the same delivery behavior can leave the firm profitable or loss-making under different task values. Conditional task-cluster intervals also include firm losses at the main valuation. The result illustrates a feasible scenario for shared gains, not robust profitability or observed adoption of the complete contract.

Does verification improve delivered quality? On matched attempts, checking and liability re-duce both defective and correct deliveries (Figure 7c; Appendix C.2.2). 6. Conclusion 

We simulated token-max and outcome-max behaviors across execution, memory adaptation, and workforce selection. Outcome-max yields more verified work per agent token under these settings, with quality costs on some hard tasks. OutcomeShare identifies scenarios in which selective delivery and value sharing benefit all three participants. The main comparison specifies two behaviors rather than testing whether objective optimization produces them. The small directional gain under objective-only prompts suggests a possible role for objective framing. Simulated participants and benchmark tasks limit external validity, while financial gains depend on verification, valuation, and collectible liability. Future experiments should separate reward from behavior instructions and test real-user participation and performance before drawing conclusions about workplace adoption.

A.5.2. Outcome-based pricing and the three-party model 

In this section, we aim to connect verified production with the payoffs received by each participant. Throughout, the three parties are the LLM provider, firm, and employee. The employee supervises production; the production agent is the LLM system. 

Production and review capacity. Weekly production is limited by whichever resource runs out first: agent computation or employee review time. If their capacities are μA and μH tasks per week, attempted output is M = min (μA, μH ) and verified output is Q = M ¯y. Agent capacity divides its weekly output-token budget by output tokens per task; review capacity divides available minutes by review minutes per task. With different task values, weekly verified value is Z = M Vy : we value each successful task before averaging. 

Allocating verified value. The outcome fee ϕ transfers a share of Z to the provider, and the bonus rate BE transfers another share to the employee. The firm retains what remains after salary. Thus provider revenue is ϕZ, employee income is salary plus BE Z, and firm profit is (1 − ϕ − BE)Z minus salary. These accounts hold observed production fixed as the fee changes. They do not assume that a higher fee improves behavior. Provider revenue is reported before its compute costs, so it does not measure provider profit. 

Pigouvian audit-fine. A shipped defect is detected with effective probability p (audit sampling probability times audit sensitivity), and a fine F is levied on its approver, with p · F = κV so the expected fine equals the social damage κV (rework cost per unit value). The equality internalizes expected defect damage in the reduced effort model below; it does not by itself establish a particular fee optimum or a joint improvement in all three parties’ payoffs. The observed outcome and audit-fine comparison is reported in Table S11b, separately from the fixed-skill fine sweep in Table S22. In the behavioral experiments, liability is communicated through the simulated employee’s objective and, for learned policies, its reflection instructions. The audit-fine prompt describes a large penalty for approving an incorrect fix. The fixed-policy sweep instead states expected penalties of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, or 2 times the value of a correct solve. These runs evaluate acceptance and correctness; they do not separately sample audit events or execute monetary deductions. Thus they test responses to stated liability, while the contract equation specifies how an implemented audit and fine would produce that expected liability. Identifying the sweep’s multiplier with λ = pF /(κV)

uses the explicit normalization κ = 1: defect damage is valued at one correct solve. This calibration is an accounting assumption, not an estimated damage level.

Inspection and submission. Signals follow unit-variance normal distributions with means ±d/2 for correct and incorrect artifacts, where d = 2Φ−1(s) and s denotes balanced-prior signal accuracy. This assumes ideal population calibration, not a trained verifier; gold labels generate and score signals but remain hidden from employees.
