## Abstract 

Cognition and culture have long been treated as parallel objects of study, joined more by metaphor 

than by mechanism. We argue they are co-constituted in a manner now empirically tractable: 

subjectivities can be measured as local trajectories through a high-dimensional cultural field, and 

the field is itself the aggregate of those trajectories. Recent advances in machine learning, including 

embedding methods and large generative models, provide the first general framework for 

measuring this co-constitution from micro-cognition to macro-social structure. Vector geometry 

recovers individual conceptual structure, organizational communication, and large-scale 

ideologies; captures multimodal cultural content beyond text; and generates testable predictions 

about cultural emergence, including the simultaneous arrival of independent discoveries across 

distant minds. We treat this predictability of simultaneous innovation as evidence for the co-

constitutive view: when the geometry of the cultural field is measurable, trajectories of cognitive 

search through it become predictable. We then examine generative AI agents as simulators of 

human subjectivity and as a qualitatively new coordinating substrate whose insertion into social 

life introduces evolutionary dynamics unprecedented in human cultural history. We formalize this 

reflexive condition as a Heisenberg-like Uncertainty Principle for generative AI: as instruments for 

fixing a culture’s position grow more precise, our capacity to predict its trajectory degrades, 

because the machinery of cultural description and cultural action have merged. We close with 

ethical questions raised by AI-mediated cultural drift, recursive synthetic culture, and the limits of 

human cultural agency, offering guidelines for safe, equitable, and privacy-preserving research on 

AI and culture. 

Keywords:  artificial intelligence, cultural evolution, culture and cognition, generative AI, large 

language models, simultaneous discovery, social prediction, word embeddings

# Introduction 

Interest in the study of culture has grown sharply across sociology, psychology, anthropology, 

computer science, biology, and management, yet the concept of culture has remained notoriously 

difficult to pin down. Initially referring to the way of life of specific peoples, culture has come to 

encompass values, practices, norms, symbols, and tools that constitute human social life and 

subjectivity (Geertz 1973; DiMaggio 1997; Patterson 2014; Sewell 2005). Some scholars view 

culture as so broad as to be the sum total of collective human experience (Cronk 2019) . O thers have 

judged it impossible to measure or theoretically incoherent (Emirbayer 1997; Vaisey 2021; 

Adkisson 2014).  Parallel impasses persist within cognitive science, where researchers have asked 

whether anthropology earned its seat at the table (Beller, Bender, and Medin 2012), and within 

sociology’s “culture and cognition” subfield, where critics contend that decades of work have 

failed to deliver a coherent empirical program (Lizardo 2014; Vaisey 2021). 

We revisit these concerns in light of recent advances in machine learning and computational social 

science. Our central claim is theoretical: cognition and culture are co-constituted in a measurable 

way. Subjectivities are local trajectories through a high-dimensional cultural field, and the field is 

itself the aggregate of those trajectories. This claim was previously a programmatic commitment 

whose implications could not be tested at scale. We argue it is now empirically tractable due to 

recent advances in artificial i ntelligence , and its predictions are being borne out. 

Clear evidence comes from a striking class of recent findings. When the geometry of the cultural 

field around a scientific community is measured from its publications, it becomes possible to 

predict not only which papers will be impactful (Belikov, Rzhetsky, and Evans 2022; Shi and 

Evans 2023; Sourati and Evans 2023) but also which papers will appear as twins , nearly 

simultaneous discoveries by independent minds occupying overlapping neighborhoods of the same 

epistemic terrain (Ogburn & Thomas 1922; Mer ton 1961, 1963;  Kim, Kojaku, and Ahn 2026). 

When the categorical preferences of partisan agents are simulated using a language model trained 

on a prior cultural moment, it is possible to forecast the arguments and attitudes those partisans will 

produce in subsequent crises (Kozlowski, Kwon, and Evans 2024). When categories are invented 

from scratch by large groups of disconnected individuals, the resulting category systems converge 

with surprising regularity, even when individuals ’ own preferences and cognitive associations vary 

consid erably  (Guilbeault, Baronchelli, and Centola 2021;  Guilbeault & Centola 2025 ). These

return to this tension, formalized as  a kind of  uncertainty principle, in the closing sections, along

# Culture and Cognition in the Age of AI 

The interpretive turn in mid-twentieth-century anthropology, led by Geertz, Douglas, Turner, and 

Schneider, recast culture as an evolving network of publicly shared meanings distributed across 

actors in time and space. Geertz ’s The Interpretation of Cultures  (1973) coined the analytic 

strategy of  “thick description ” to unpack the layered and situated meanings of cultural events. 

Turner’s analyses of Ndembu ritual (2016) and Schneider’s reading of kinship as a system of 

symbolic relations extended the program, while Douglas argued in Rules and Meanings that 

“human thought serves human interests, and therefore carries in itself at any given moment the 

social configurations of that time and place ” (1973: 11). The unifying premise was that culture is a 

publicly shared, dynamically evolving system of material and symbolic artifacts  held together by

the interpretive associations linking them. Culture and cognition were  theorized  as intrinsically 

linked. 

The interpretive tradition supplies the framing within which current AI technologies acquire their 

cultural-scientific significance (Clifford & Marcus, 2010). We treat culture, with the 

interpretivists, as a system of meaning at the interface of cognition and sociality. From this 

premise, three lines of work converge. Microcultures have been  historically  documented by 

anthropologists through immersive thick description within small groups and communities. 

Psychologists, sociologists, and organizational scholars have characterized macro-cultures within 

and across societies through  “thinner ” quantitative evidence: surveys, behavioral measures, the 

anthropological record of artifacts and ethnographies (Roscigno and Hodson 2004; Roscigno, 

Lopez, and Hodson 2009; Jackson et al. 2019). Cultural evolutionists, beginning from work on the 

intergenerational transmission of material technologies and language, have built mathematical 

accounts of how distributions of cultural variants change over time as a function of  evolutionary 

constraints and selection mechanisms  (Boyd and Richerson 1988; Henrich and Boyd 2002; 

Henrich 2016; Mesoudi 2011; Kirby, Cornish, and Smith 2008; Morin 2015). 

Across these traditions  remains  an unresolved question of integration. Macro accounts of cultural 

form sometimes locate explanatory power in collective behavior alone, treating t he  personal 

characteristics of individuals (e.g., their attitudes and beliefs) as socially constructed byproducts 

(DellaPosta, Shi, and Macy 2015; Macy et al. 2019).  Under popular constructivist accounts ,

processes of social construction are largely unconstrained by micro-cognitive factors, and 

trajectories of cultural evolution  are  fundamentally historically contingent and unpredictable. Yet 

the absence of a measurement framework for the micro–macro interface has meant that this 

commitment has functioned largely as a theoretical  claim  rather than  robust  empirical  evidence .

Sociological micro–macro frameworks , including  Coleman ’s (1986)  “boat, ” Giddens ’s (1986) 

structuration,  and  Bourdieu ’s (1977) habitus , articulate a powerful recursion through scales (see 

Figure 1) . This can stabilize cultural systems, but it can also unleash higher-level processes of 

rejection or revolution that  limit  future  pred iction (Bateson 1935; Graeber and Wengrow 2021). 

Until now,  however, these  multi-level models  have largely eluded measurement and systematic 

quantitative test ing .

We argue that the conditions for an integrated empirical science of culture and cognition are now in 

place. AI methods have brought methodological alignment  bridging  cognitive science and 

computational social science (Hannan e t al. 2019;  Mohr et al. 2020), permitting the simultaneous 

mathematical specification of individual subjectivities, intermediate-scale interactional dynamics, 

and macro-scale cultural fields.  The methods at the heart of this alignment , neural-network 

representations spaces or embeddings,  are continuous with a long cognitive-science tradition that 

has represented concepts as points or regions in structured geometric spaces (Gärdenfors 2000, 

2014; Goldstone and Rogosky 2002; Lupyan and Lewis 2019; Pianta dosi et al. 2024 ), now 

operating at corpus scales that earlier formal frameworks could not empirical ly approach .

In what follows we hold  three  further commitments. First, cultures are high-dimensional 

multimodal dynamical  systems  that exist when shared  within or across  social scales , from 

individual  embodied cognition to  societal  ideological coordination. Second, the relevant question 

is not whether to integrate cognitive and social science but how, given that the historical objections 

to integration have been driven less by principled disagreement than by the absence of a tractable 

common formalism  (Vaisey 2021; Beller et al. 2012; Lizardo 2014; Keen 2014) . The next two 

sections take up these two halves of the integration in turn , the cognitive constraints that culture 

inherits and the cultural constraints cognition inherits , before turning to the geometric 

computational framework that allows both to be modeled together. 

A third commitment shadows the first two and recurs throughout: the measurement of culture is 

inseparable from action within it. In Werner Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle, a signpost of the 

quantum age, one cannot simultaneously determine both the position and the momentum of a 

subatomic particle. The more certain one is about where it is, the less certain one can be about 

where it is going. AI has brought about a structurally analogous trade-off in the study of the social 

and cultural world. As our instruments for identifying, imaging, and computationally 

characterizing subjectivities and cultures have grown more precise, counterintuitively, the ability 

for these instruments (e.g., deep learning embeddings) to forecast the evolutionary trajectory of 

cultures has worsened; we argue this is because, increasingly, the machinery we rely on to 

quantitatively describe culture is the machinery now deployed to build agents that act within it and 

thereby shape it at a large and unprecedented scale. We articulate and explore this Uncertainty 

Principle for generative AI, and its consequences for cultural predictability and evolution, after 

establishing the measurement framework it presupposes.

# The Cognitive Foundations of Culture 

Cognition contributes to culture a sense of functional integration, a set of interlocking component 

processes, and a set of productive constraints. Because cognition is typically understood as  mental, 

computational processing  in the service of purposeful action , including  prediction, decision-

making, and problem-solving , it carries with it an implicit orientation toward the goal-directed 

individual.  Insofar as c ognition is recognized to contain componential and modularized functions 

(e.g.,  representations, learnability, memory),  and  categorical structure  (e.g.,  clustering and 

hierarchy) , which have been  refined through biological and cultural evolution to support prediction 

and action (Heyes 2018; Tomasello 2009, 2016), so culture inherits these constraints. Progress in 

measuring and modeling  individual  cognitive constraints on cultural transmission renders larger-

scale cultural trends increasingly predictable. 

One  metaphor  for these constraints is provided by  the frame of  predictive processing (Friston 2010; 

Clark 2013, 2016).  In this theory , the brain ’s primary and constitutive aim is to  minimize error 

between its generative model of the world and incoming sensory evidence. It further proposes that 

cultural artifacts and practices are collectively maintained as scaffolds for prediction and cogni tive 

alignment. They function as s hared models that reduce the cognitive cost of inferring others ’

actions, intentions, and the ambient state of the social world  (Veissière et al. 2020) . Whether or not 

this framework is ultimately falsifiable (Bruineberg et al. 2022), we suggest that cultural scaffolds, 

from institutions to technologies, supervene on cognitive predictions of any type and enable 

correlated interpretations, via measurement devices or interpretive technologies like LLMs, which 

further constrain and enable processes of sociocognitive evolution. Within the  geometric 

framework we develop here, a cultural field  can be viewed productively as  a structured and shared 

prior over expected meanings, behaviors, and associations, which individual minds inherit and 

update as they move through it. 

Importantly, the constraints imposed by cognition on cultural evolution yield key affordances for 

macro-level prediction. For example, c ultural evolution operates under cognitive constraints that 

shape the transmission and adoption of ideas. Frequency-dependent selection and fitness 

landscapes influence how concepts spread through populations, with factors such as learnability, 

salience, and appeal determining rates of survival. Muthukrishna, Morgan, and Henrich (2016) 

provide experimental evidence for frequency-dependent mechanism s; i n their transmission-chain

A particularly impactful family of cognitive constraints in cultural evolution stems from the 

embodied character of human cognition. That humans come to know the world through rich 

experiential, perceptual, and embodied interaction with each other a nd the environment  shapes 

everything from the structure of concepts to th eir  communication and diffusion through interaction 

(Barsalou 1999; Lakoff 2012; Shapiro 2019; Pulvermüller 2018; Borghi et al. 2017). This applies 

not only to concrete concepts but also to abstract ones, commonly conceptualized through 

metaphors that import concrete, sensible structure into otherwise imperceivable territory , as about 

other minds or predictions of the future . Recent work confirms that concrete concepts are more 

stable in their patterns of cultural evolution and more likely to emerge with similar, translatable 

meanings across contexts and languages (Lewis  et al.  2023). Related work  finds  that measuring the 

similarities  in concepts between cultures is significantly enriched by examining the images people 

produce when imagining concepts, above and beyond text-based measures of semantic similarity 

(Pera et al. 2026). The greatest level of cross-cultural image consistency emerges for the concrete 

and haptically engaging concepts (Lewis et al 2023; Pera et al. 2026).  This has direct implications 

for cultural evolution: shifts in the concrete, embodied means of representation and interaction can 

produce large-scale  influence  on the fo rmation of  cultural meanings. Guilbeault, Delecourt, and 

Desikan (2025) show that recent shifts toward visual communication in image- and video-based 

social media amplify the salience and spread of stereotypes, since images supply both more 

concrete and perceivable data of associations between demographics and social categories, and

more potent and memorable psychological primes. This raises a  productive  question for research 

on cultural evolution under the AI  turn : by changing the means through which content of various 

modalities can be created and communicated  en masse , how will AI technologies reshape the 

embodied dynamics of meaning-making, and the attractors in concept diffusion that follow? 

The multimodal nature of embodied cognition and its interaction with evolving communication 

technologies is one of the most important frontiers for research at the intersection of AI and culture. 

While AI systems are increasingly multimodal, evidence suggests that they approach 

multimodality in ways qualitatively distinct from human cognition (Kadambi et al. 2026; Barrett 

and Miller  2026). Whether the statistical paradigm driving current AI is capable of learning, and 

more precisely  experiencing , perceptions and feelings comparable to those that pervade human 

embodied cognition is the subject of an active and consequential debate. Bender and Koller (2020) 

sharpened the issue with  a thought experiment about representing the world of the  octopus , arguing 

that a system trained only on text-form linguistic communication , no matter how richly 

distributional , cannot in principle ground its outputs in the world they describe. Harnad (1990) had 

earlier framed this as the symbol grounding problem  and  Glenberg and Robertson (2000) provided 

experimental evidence that purely distributional accounts fail to capture what humans understand 

about action-related sentences. Counterarguments hold that distributional learning over 

multimodal corpora may approximate aspects of grounding (Pavlick 2023), and that the relevant 

question is empirical rather than principled (see Kadambi et al. 2026 for recent review). 

The empirical evidence so far suggests that current AI systems remain meaningfully different from 

human cognition in their handling of embodied content, though the divergences are partial and 

selective.  In the illustratively simple case of color, uniquely salient in primate vision and pervasive 

in human abstract cognition (Guilbeault et al. 2020; Desikan et al. 2020), vision models 

consistently fail to learn color in service of classification, and when they do retain it, their similarity 

judgments and semantic associations deviate systematically from human color perception and 

synesthetic reasoning (Nadler, Darragh-Ford, Srinivasa Desikan et al. 2023; Bujack et al. 2022; 

Nadler, Guilbeault, Ringold et al. 2025). This suggests deeper differences in how embodied 

associations factor into abstract interpretation.  These cases sit within a broader literature  which 

finds that neural network models  reproduce  human behavioral signatures in some domains , while 

diverging sharply in others (Lake, Ullman, Tenenbaum, and Gershman 2017; Bowers et al. 2022; 

Binz and Schulz 2023; Frank 2023; McCoy, Yao et al. 2024,  Kargupta et al. 2025 ).

We do not take the embodied gap to settle the question of whether the statistical paradigm is 

irredeemably insufficient , and we  avoid  confident claims in either direction. What we do hold is 

that the gap presents falsifiable predictions . I f distributional training over increasingly multimodal 

corpora is sufficient for cultural modeling, then domains with rich non-linguistic structure should 

yield to  improvements with appropriate training data. I f not, those same domains should remain 

stubbornly resistant. Recent work has taken the question on directly. Augmenting deep learning 

models with explicit knowledge of sensory experiences—for instance, the chemical composition 

of molecules (Makri et al. 2026)—improves the ability of large language models to generate 

coherent predictions about smell, despite language data on  olfactory-related metaphors being 

comparatively underdeveloped . Neurosymbolic approaches that incorporate explicit knowledge 

structures relating to human categorization and perceptual experience (Barrett and Miller 2026; 

Guilbeault, Caplan, and Yang 2026; Roll et al. 2026) may yield further progress. Whether 

embodied mechanisms of cognition , such as  complex analogical reasoning, synaesthetic 

associations, affective  cognition , and conceptual leaps that elude current LLM architectures 

(Duede and Evans 2021; Brucks and Levav 2022; Z ahavy 2026 ) turn out to be necessary for the 

predictive performance we describe in subsequent sections is, we believe, one of the central 

empirical questions of the next decade. 

Even so, the embodied gap does not currently prevent embedding methods from providing 

significant utility as a measurement technology in modeling the multimodal structure of  concepts. 

Consider the multimodal, multilayered structure of cultural constructs relating to sports: a 

particular team is associated not only with semantic knowledge in textual form but also with visual 

symbology of logo and color, auditory theme songs, and a gambit of immersive ritualized 

behaviors and gustatory experiences that constitute the full contextual representation of the team in 

the lives and minds of its fans. As multimodal models progress in their ability to embed and 

associate cultural objects across modalities, they unlock new capabilities for characterizing the 

multimodal nature of human  concepts  and behaviors in embodied cultural contexts. 

This progress is unlikely to follow a unidirectional, bottom-up logic in which cultural constructs 

are reduced to the sum of their multimodal associations. A central premise of this article is that AI 

is bringing about a computational paradigm for cultural analysis that reveals and embraces its 

intrinsically multiscale nature: macro constructs such as ideologies, markets, and nations are 

themselves represented and modeled as defining the conditions under which socially embedded

# The Cultural Foundations of Cognition 

Sociological accounts of cognition have long insisted that the categories and concepts through 

which individuals perceive, classify, and reason are neither private inventions nor neutral 

reflections of the world, but are instead collectively produced and institutionally sustained. 

Durkheim  early made the  argument in  The Elementary Forms of Religious Life  (1912) that basic 

categories of understanding , including  self, time, space, causality, and classification , originate in 

the organization of social life.  He  distilled that intuition  within  questions like which came first, the 

set-theoretic notion of a group as a collection of elements, or the concrete notion of a social group 

with members from which the  abstract  mathematical  form  was analogically derived? 

Mary Douglas sharpened this view in  How Institutions Think , arguing that human conceptual 

systems and organizational structures are defined by and justified through fundamental analogical 

relationships, grounding abstract organisational stru cture in concrete logics of the natural world, 

such as grounding theological hierarchy in a view of a natural hierarchy among animals . Taking 

this further, Bourdieu ’s notion of  habitus  describes how durable dispositions of perception and 

judgment  may be  inculcated through socialization into class and field positions, such that 

reasoning styles carry the fingerprint of one ’s location in social structure (Bourdieu 1977, 1986, 

1987; Lizardo 2006; Goldberg 20 11 ).  The cognitive sociology developed by Zerubavel, 

DiMaggio, Cerulo, and others has similarly mapped how classifications, attention, memory, and 

analogical reasoning are socially patterned within and across “thought communities” (Zerubavel 

1997; DiMaggio 1987, 1997; Cerulo 2001).  Swidler  expands the role of human agency within 

cultural fields by  recast ing  culture as a differentiated  toolkit  of strategies, frames, and justifications 

from which actors may selectively and strate gically  draw (Swidler 1986). More recent dual-process 

and practice-centered accounts  reconcile these approaches by  distinguish ing  between fast, 

automatic, schema-driven cognition that carries most everyday interpretation and slower, 

discursively elaborated reasoning people mobilize when asked to justify themselves (Boutyline 

and Soter 2021; Lizardo 2014), yielding predictions about which forms of cultural influence are 

accessible to introspection . Other forms of cultural influence  must be recovered indirectly ,

Anthropological perspectives complement these sociological claims by documenting the 

variability of conceptual schemes, interpretive frames, and inferential habits that human 

communities develop . Furthermore, the y theoriz e the  structural  mechanisms through which such 

schemes become cognitively binding. Building on Geertz ’s interpretive program, cognitive 

anthropologists have shown how shared cultural models  mold  everyday reasoning about kinship, 

morality, illness, and the self (Shore 1996; Lévi-Strauss 1963; D ’Andrade 1995). Parallel traditions 

have developed similar intuitions about how cognition is distributed, reinforced, and extended 

through tools and the design of cultural environments (Miton and Jackson 2026; Hutchins 2014; 

Heersmink 2017). Hutchins ’s analyses of navigation and aviation demonstrate that complex 

cognitive tasks are routinely distributed across people, artifacts, and institutional routines, such that 

the relevant unit of analysis for many forms of thinking is a sociotechnical system rather than a 

singular  mind (Hutchins 1996). Clark and Chalmers (1998) argued in turn that this distribution is 

not merely operational but constitutive: the boundary of the cognitive system extends into the 

artifactual environment whenever those artifacts play the right functional role.  Psychological 

research has increasingly converged on complementary insights through the influence of Vygotsky 

(1978) and Luria’s (1976) cultural-historical psychology, and the explosion of work documenting 

ways sociocultural contexts impact individual and group psychologies (Markus and Kitayama 

1991; Nisbett, Peng, Choi, and Norenzayan 2001; Masuda and Nisbett 2001; Nisbett 2003; 1994; 

Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan 2010; Heine 2020; Boroditsky 2001; Lupyan and Dale 2010; 

Majid et al. 2018). 

Across these literatures, a cumulative picture emerges in which cognition is conditioned by the 

cultural environments in which minds develop and operate , a picture  we argue  is newly tractable 

for computational investigation once culture is represented as a learnable, high-dimensional 

structure whose geometry can be related directly to the subjectivities embedded within it. Cultural 

evolution research further argues that the distinctive cognitive profile of our species rests on 

capacities for shared intentionality, high-fidelity social learning, and cumulative cultural ratcheting 

that allow successive generations to inherit and refine conceptual tools, norms, and technologies 

they could not individually invent (Boyd and Richerson 1988; Tomasello, Carpenter, Call, Behne, 

and Moll 2005; Tomasello 2009, 2016; Henrich and Boyd 2002; Henrich 2016; Csibra and Gergely

2009).  The ‘cognitive gadgets’ account extends this lineage, emphasizing the causal role of tool

Taken together, these strands establish that much of what is cognitively  inside  individuals is the

they may provide a vehicle for anticipating  future constraints on cultural evolution stemming both 

from how cognition constrains culture and how cultural artifacts shape cognition  (Rahwan, Shariff

# Culture as a High-Dimensional Multimodal Field 

Weaving together  threads  from  the previous two sections , we treat cultural content , ranging from 

texts  and  images  to  practices, routines, and behavioral trajectories  as positions within a high-

dimensional, multimodal  semantic  space.  In this  view, an individual  identity is a subjective 

trajectory  through that space: a locally dense cloud of association, attention, and expectation that 

each person carries as a function of their experiences, biography, training, language and other 

patterns of cultural exposure  (Goodenough 1981, D’Andrade 1995). In p anel  A of Figure 1 , we 

illustrate how patterns of shared cognition vary across levels of analysis. Here we illustrate culture

a category error if cultures are shared systems of public symbols (Wittgenstein 1953; Geertz 1973; 

Lewis 1969; Kripke 1982), or the degenerate limiting case of a distribution of representations on 

Sperber’s (1996) epidemiological account. In this, we follow cognitive anthropologists who

Level 2 involves shared micro-cultures that emerge through interactions within social networks

ideology immanent within larger times and places, like Weimar Republic Berlin of the 1920s or the

# Modeling the Multiscale and Multimodal Nature of Culture 

The technology of co ntextual embeddings , the representations within modern deep neural 

networks,  have opened new possibilities for cultural analysis.  They provide a means to measure 

subjectivities in terms of how the meanings of words change with surrounding social context 

(including who is speaking), and to compare the semantic universes of individuals and contexts 

through geometric and graph-theoretic measures of similarity in how associations are constructed. 

These achievements are not entirely dependent on text and can in principle include any format of 

cultural information, wh ere cultural objects  can  be quantified by their adjacency to other such 

objects within cultural contexts and feature spaces . Artificial neural networks (ANNs) extend the 

framework beyond textual data by providing a generalized framework for including diverse 

modalities of culturally rich data—audio, images, video, behavioral recordings—into an 

integrated, multimodal embedding space within which the respective contributions of these 

modalities to subjectivities can be examined.

## AI Models of Subjectivities in Context 

Given the enormity of behavioral and language data online, ANNs make it possible not only to 

form embeddings that capture the landscape of meaning for the entire internet but also to build 

localized embeddings that reflect the subjectivities of particular  types of  agents within this 

landscape. Researchers have developed several ways of adapting language models to capture the 

specific styles of association, speech production, and semantic interpretation that characterize 

particular agentic viewpoints. 

In generative transformer models, representing subjectivities can be approximated through 

prompting. Researchers can prompt an LLM to produce or evaluate text from the perspective of a 

particular kind of person. Recent work shows that when LLMs are asked to complete canonical 

survey tasks and game-theory experiments from the perspective of particular agents (defined by 

gender, race, income, partisanship, etc.), they replicate the corresponding participants’ responses in 

a way that captures over 80% of the variance, providing a robust framework for predicting behavior 

(Hewitt et al. 2024; Park et al. 2024; Argyle, Busby, et al. 2023). Recent approaches to mechanistic 

interpretability of LLMs to facilitate direct, geometric steering of model internals to modulate 

perspectives within the embedding space by discovering the embedding layer(s) within the model

Schein 2025; Kozlowski and Evans 2025; Chen et al. 2025).

## AI Models of the Macro Social Contexts Shaping Subjectivities 

The ability of LLMs to robustly capture semantic judgments in particular contexts opens the 

possibility of generalizing beyond modeling individual perspectives into modeling larger-scale 

cultural trends. Contrary to the Saussurean ideal of arbitrary semantic association, this work 

reveals robust predictability in the new semantic associations that people form in response to 

cultural events and innovations. Sociological research has often argued that the effects of partisan 

identity on people ’s cultural associations  can be  arbitrary, path-dependent, and unpredictable 

(DellaPosta, Shi, and Macy 2015; Macy, Deri, Ruch, and Tong 2019). Yet a recent study found 

such predictability empirically: Kozlowski, Kwon, and Evans (2024) used a large language model 

trained on pre-COVID data (GPT-3) to predict the attitudes and arguments that Republicans and 

Democrats produced toward vaccine mandates in the COVID era. Current efforts are predicting 

new conceptual innovations in real time, with surprising results, including the finding that 

prescient ideas often emerge from the cultural periphery in terms of which actors hold influence in 

society (Vicinanz a, Goldberg, and Srivastava 2023) . This is consistent  with theoretical findings 

from research on “complex contagions”, where cultural forms like ideologies are only “caught” 

through multiple exposures, that innovations are often most likely to emerge and spread from the 

periphery of social networks (Guilbeault & Centola 2021; Tschofenig & Guilbeault 2026) .

Importantly, these predictions do not require LLMs to precisely capture specific individuals ’

associative reasoning in particular contexts  to predict cultural trends. Research suggests that LLMs 

are limited in reproducing the true heterogeneity of individuals ’ opinions and interpretations 

(Wang, Morgenstern, and Dickerson 2025; Santurkar, Durmus, Ladhak et al. 2023). This is 

because LLMs are designed to compress aggregate patterns that, by definition, generalize across 

individuals at a coarser-grained level than is required to  retain  specific subjectivit ies (Zhao, 

Guilbeault & Goldberg, 2026) . The heterogeneity problem can be partially addressed through

improved prompting and steeri ng : LLMs ’ default responses often struggle to reproduce localized 

and heterogeneous cultural associations and idiolects, but recent work shows they are coherently 

responsive to prompts  and vector steering  designed to convey and elicit the localized cultural forms 

of specific  groups (Veselovsky, Argin, Stroebl et al. 202 6). 

While solving the heterogeneity problem would enhance the utility of LLMs as social simulators, it 

is not necessary for LLMs to serve as robust simulators and predictors of appropriately scoped 

macro dynamics. One reason is that cultural phenomena themselves characteristically aggregate 

over and collapse the underlying heterogeneity  from  individual  subjectivities, much of which 

remains a black box even to human observers  within the culture . Despite widespread cognitive 

diversity, cultural trends often reflect weak cognitive preferences that  become  amplified as people 

communicate in large populations, leading to stable and replicable trajectories in the emergence of 

new associations at the macro scale  (Guilbeault et al. 2021; Schelling 2006; Acerbi and de Courson 

2025).  These biases need not be universal or widespread. Tipping points in cultural norms can be 

activated by coordinated preferences among a quarter of the population or less (Centola, Becker, 

Brackbill, and Baronchelli 2018; Iacopini, Petri, Baronchelli, and Barrat 2022), though thresholds 

remain methodologically debated, and preferences shared by less than 20% of a population can 

lead large, separate groups to independently form similar meaning systems (Guilbeault et al. 2021). 

The upshot is that while LLMs may be limited in capturing the full breadth of variation in human 

subjectivity and association, they effectively estimate large-scale cultural attractors that aggregate 

over this variation and constrain cultural dynamics. 

That LLMs, by design, capture the most stereotypical human features and preferences , while 

undoubtedly problematic in many social contexts , may allow them to accurately model the weak 

biases that shape large-scale cultural evolution. LLMs achieve this by capturing the aspects of 

associative reasoning predicted by  human  identification with particular social groups, such as 

biases in their reasoning about COVID vaccines as predicted by partisan identities (Kozlowski et 

al. 2024), which are known to play a significant constraining role  on  beliefs. Some identities are 

expectedly more constraining than others. Contemporary U.S. partisan identit ies  are  especially 

entrenched , while  other identities, such as whether someone is a birder or prefers coffee to tea, play 

a weaker role in shaping semantic associations, making heterogeneity along  such  social 

dimensions harder to capture via the automated learning of aggregate cultural trends. A general 

expectation is that LLMs will be effective at modeling existing and novel semantic associations

# Mapping the Dynamic Nexus of Culture and Cognition 

If we take seriously the proposition that cultural content  can be represented as positions within a 

high-dimensional, multimodal embedding space, then human subjectivities can be characterized as 

trajectories through that space. Such trajectories  comprise  locally dense clouds of association, 

attention, and expectation each person carries as a function of their biography, language, training, 

and patterns of cultural exposure. On this view, a characteristic feature of subjectivity is how it  sees 

and  searches through  neighboring  cultural worlds, probing the local neighborhood of meanings for 

useful  combinations, analogies, and reinterpretations. This is the cognitive face of what St uart 

Kauffman calls exploring the  adjacent possible  (Kauffman 20 00 ): the set of configurations not yet 

actual but are reachable  via a single  combinatorial step from what already exists. Local 

combinatorial steps within complex, high-dimensional semantic spaces  can  yield cultural 

“exaptations ” or  symmetry-breaking  transpositions  that alter the parameters of the possibility 

space. 4 At the same time , position within  the adjacent possible  renders  individual search within 

high-dimensional spaces somewhat predictable. Recent work in the science of science supports 

this:  embedding the search behavior of scientists in high-dimensional spaces allows properties of 

their location in the geometry of culture to predict which ideas they will combine and which 

combinations will be impactful (Belikov et al. 2022; Shi and Evans 2023; Sourati and Evans 2023;

operationalizes disruption as the distance, in a neural embedding of the citation network, between a 

scientific work ’s antecedents and descendants. The measure not only distinguishes disruptive 

papers (such as Nobel laureates ’ key works) more reliably than the standard disruption index, but 

also identifies the  twins  of a given breakthrough—papers that sit in similar future contexts and 

appear to have disrupted the same region of the scientific space at the same time. The existence of 

such twins implies that breakthroughs are less the signature of a singular genius than the 

predictable intersection of many  active  subjectiv ities  channeled  to the same crowded frontier  by 

the same ambient cultural gradients. 

This is the territory that historian Paul Forman opened half a century ago, well before the AI tools 

existed to study it at scale. Forman (1971)  characterized  the culture of Weimar Germany  with its 

post-1918 intellectual revolt against causality, determinism, and materialism, and its embrace of 

acausality, individuality, and  Anschaulichkeit  (visualizability) . He showed how this  created a 

hostile environment for Newtonian causal-realist physics and a receptive one for the 

indeterministic, non-visualizable formalism of quantum mechanics, particularly Bohr ’s

Copenhagen interpretation. Forman noted that physicists who consumed and produced 

philosophical work in the spirit of that era, supported by  parallel  currents of existentialism, 

27 AI and the New Science of Culture 

pragmatism, and logical empiricism, were the ones who championed an acausal quantum theory. 

The  “Forman thesis ” holds that the ambient cultural matrix did not merely decorate the discovery 

of quantum mechanics but conditioned its imaginability . A  different zeitgeist would have yielded a 

different physics, or the same physics considerably later, along a different interpretive path. 

What was in Forman ’s time an interpretive historical argument can now, in principle, become an 

empirical program through the multimodal and multiscale model of cultural dynamics that AI 

technologies make possible. The reframing has a precise geometric form. If subjectivities are 

trajectories through a multimodal cultural embedding space, and if scientific breakthroughs are 

displacements whose direction and timing are predictable from the geometry of that space, then the 

ambient culture Forman invoked—the philosophical, artistic, political, and affective currents of a 

place and period—can be measured as a structured field rather than narrated as a mood. 

Contemporary neural embedding and generative models, trained on the multimodal  symbolic and 

behavioral  record of a given zeitgeist, offer a way to quantify that field . By  locat ing  the 

philosophical writings of post-Weimar Germany, the canvases of its expressionists, the rhetoric of 

its politics, and the notebooks of its physicists in a shared representational space,  one can  ask which 

regions of the scientific adjacent possible are locally nearer  and more reachable  under that ambient 

configuration than another. 

Recast as a geometric claim, the Forem an thesis  becomes falsifiable. The prediction is that training 

a model on the cultural corpus of period  T should make breakthrough discoveries of period  T + ε 

systematically nearer in embedding space than they would be from a counterfactual cultural corpus 

drawn from a different zeitgeist with the same level of relevant technical and empirical knowledge. 

Existing work on impact prediction in science (Sourati and Evans 2023; Belikov et al. 2022) and 

the observation of m ultiple discovery  (Ogburn and Thomas 1922, Merton 1961,  Kim, Kojaku, and 

Ahn 2026) constitute down payments on this prediction; the broader research program would 

extend it across periods and disciplines. Treated this way , and  consistent with Farrell, Gopnik, 

Shalizi, and Evans (2025) on large AI models as cultural and social technologies , embedding 

models become cultural observatories capable of detecting the salient associations and interpretive 

biases that precede and help explain simultaneous breakthroughs  as  more geometrically coherent 

than the historiography of lone genius and  improbable serendipity  would lead us to expect.

Several implications follow. First, the framework reconciles the apparent tension between 

cognitive accounts of innovation (which emphasize individual capability) and constructionist 

accounts (which emphasize  collective  contingenc ies like serendipity, Merton and Barber 2004 ). 

Both are correct in their respective ways: individuals are necessary because someone must  make 

the cognitive  leap ; the cultural field is necessary because it is the conditioning structure that makes 

some  leaps  reachable and others not. Second, the framework suggests new methodological 

strategies. If the cultural field can be measured, then  interventions  on it , as  by altering the corpus on 

which a model is trained , become a form of counterfactual cultural  history. Third, the framework 

offers cognitive science a generative bridge to historical and sociological work on the situated 

nature of thought (Hutchins 1996; Veissière et al. 2020) . R ather than positing situated cognition as 

a metaphysical thesis about minds-in-environments, it allows the situating environment to be made 

geometrically explicit. 

It would overstate the case to claim that all of cultural creativity reduces to interpolation within a 

measurable geometric field. Genuine exaptations , or  innovations that alter the dimensions of the 

possibility space itself , remain one of the central explanatory and predictive challenges ( Longo, 

Montévil, and Kauffman  2012). But the existence of recurrent simultaneous discoveries, the 

success of embedding-based prediction across multiple domains, and the experimental 

confirmation of scale-induced category convergence together suggest that the predictable-search 

regime is much wider than skeptics of cognitive prediction have supposed. There is, however, a 

boundary condition on the predictable  search itself. The predictions reviewed above were made by 

instruments that remained largely outside the fields they measured: embeddings of past 

publications  and  models trained on prior cultural moments. As those same instruments  become 

converted into AI agents that publish, persuade, recommend, and create within the fields they were 

trained to describe, prediction of position and prediction of trajectory begin to trade off against one 

another. It is to this reflexive condition that we now turn. 

# Augmenting the Dynamic Nexus of Culture and Cognition 

In keeping with the inescapable reflexivity of social systems, this new AI-powered approach to 

cultural analysis will recursively shape the cultural field itself and do so with sufficient regularity 

that we playfully name it the Uncertainty Principle for generative AI. In Heisenberg’s Uncertainty 

principle, one cannot simultaneously determine both the position and the momentum of a 

29 AI and the New Science of Culture 

subatomic particle; the more precisely the position is fixed, the less can be known about where the 

particle is going. In our cultural analogue, as AI instruments for measuring and fixing the position 

of a culture, an opinion cluster, or a market of meanings have grown more precise, our ability to 

forecast the trajectory of the measured field degrades rather than improves (Shumailov et al. 2024; 

Sourati et al. 2026). The reason is structural. The same machinery that lets us describe a culture, a 

market, or a population is the machinery we now use to build agents that act within it. Description 

and action have collapsed as the instruments of understanding have become participants in what is 

understood. Heisenberg glimpsed the epistemic aspect of this condition when he observed that 

‘what we observe is not nature itself, but nature exposed to our method of questioning.’ The 

generative turn adds an ontological dimension as the method of questioning now answers back and 

intervenes on the world. 

Two features distinguish our cultural uncertainty principle from Heisenberg’s quantum one. First, 

it is not a fundamental fact about the physical world but the latest and most intimate instance of an 

older pattern in human activity: wherever we have learned to measure something, from a 

compound or gene to a market or paradigm, some party has learned to make it as understanding is 

leveraged for control. Chemistry, biology, and finance crossed into this condition long ago. Taste, 

ideology, language, persuasion, friendship, deliberation, and creativity have now joined them. 

Second, the closure cannot be dismantled by individual restraint or institutional caution. The 

clearest demonstration is the recent history of language models: the Transformer architecture 

became (Vaswani et al 2017), within roughly a year, both Google’s discriminative bidirectional 

text embedding model (BERT, Devlin et al 2018) and OpenAI’s generative pre-trained transformer 

(GPT, Radford et al 2018), and the institutional reasons Google had for not building the second 

proved no obstacle to other organizations building it. The mirror and the engine were two readings 

of one breakthrough, and the second reading was going to be made, even if it required defecting 

from Google’s award winning team. The common posture of enthusiasm for predictive machine 

learning combined with wariness toward generative AI, widespread among both researchers and 

the public, thus rests on a distinction unstable on its own terms. 

The consequences for cultural science are concrete. Being able to model and predict innovation at 

scale enables new levels of intervention, either to expand or to constrain the rate of cultural 

innovation, that not only impact our view of the past but also shape our trajectory toward the future. 

When we use AI models to discover new  zeitgeists , our measured AI representations change the 

30 AI and the New Science of Culture 

cultural landscape they were first intended to describe. When a political pollster documents a new 

cluster of opinions held by a group of voters, they advise campaigns to craft messages targeting 

those voters. These messages, if successful, reshape the conceptual terrain of shared arguments and 

identity that unite those voters, rendering yesterday’s map precise and tomorrow’s forecast 

unreliable. While these forms of cultural capture may seem manipulative because of their 

asymmetric awareness, more symmetrical scenarios are possible. When cultural and opinion 

divisions become publicly visible, they can be harnessed as sites of deliberation that expose new 

opportunities for collective understanding and action (Argyle et al 2023). For good or ill, the 

measurement enters the measured, and precision of position is purchased with uncertainty of 

trajectory. 

Perhaps the most consequential impact of these AI capabilities lies in entirely new ways of 

augmenting both human subjectivity and collective organizations, leading to new forms of 

cognition and sociality that may permanently alter cultural evolution. New forms of social 

intimacy are emerging: Li and Zhang (2024)  identify prevalent types of human–AI social 

interaction, from intimacy and self-disclosure to play, transgression, and communication 

breakdown, suggesting how  AI systems may be reshaping the landscape of human emotional and 

romantic experience. These new forms do not depend on direct human-to-AI interaction. Studies of 

comparable impact have shown that AI assistance , via recommended edits or messages , can 

significantly increase the ability of people to provide empathetic support for those struggling with 

mental health (Sharma, Lin, Miner, Atkins, and Althoff 2023). AI-mediated relationships are 

creating novel vocabularies for intimacy, emotional support, and companionship that may alter 

cultural expectations about human relationships ( Rocca et al 2026) . AI interactions, designed to be 

seamless and effortless, may create unrealistic expectations that interpersonal dynamics should be 

friction-free, potentially leading to frustration when navigating the generative messiness of human 

connection. These considerations are vital in the active policy discourse around the deployment of 

AI therapists, given the unprecedented scale at which humans are developing emotionally 

supportive relationships with technologies that, at present, are unable to experience emotional 

states despite their abilities to convincingly imitate them. 

A second mode of AI augmentation lies in its capacity to relax the constraints imposed by human 

embodied cognition itself. Despite the richly multimodal nature of human cognition, much of our 

reasoning is based in a vision-centric mode of metaphorically describing the world (Winter, 

31 AI and the New Science of Culture 

Perlman, and Majid 2018). AI models afford the potential to develop semantic systems untethered 

from this visual cognitive bias, potentially unlocking new and more flexible modes of metaphor 

generation and  lexical innovation . Multimodal AI models  trained jointly on text, image, audio, 

video, radar, infrared, chemical spectra, proprioceptive  or  physiological time series, and other non-

anthropic signal streams , in principle learn semantic geometries whose axes need not be anchored 

exclusively within the  human s ensorium. These could  offer intuitive representations in which the 

“shape ” of a chemical odorant, the temporal fine structure of an ultrasonic call, or the topology of a 

high-dimensional quantum state could serve as source domains for metaphor rather than targets to 

be visualized. This is a computational response to the problem of  Anschaulichkeit  that Forman 

placed at the center of early quantum theory: where Weimar physicists had to abandon 

visualizability to make progress, AI systems may allow later generations to  harness  non-visual 

analogical intuition , a sensorium extended beyond the body that produced it, and from which new 

metaphors, analogies, and descriptive vocabularies can be  translated  back into human discourse 

(Marghetis et al. 2019). A I, in this way, may usher in an unprecedented exaptation for human 

embodied cognition and its conceptual affordances. 

A complementary line of work suggests  that  AI-enabled representational capacities might  also 

restructure coordination systems  and  align subjectivities.  A substantial empirical literature 

documents that LLMs behave differently from human players in canonical game-theoretic settings. 

In social dilemmas such as the Dictator and Ultimatum games, they exhibit higher cooperation 

rates than humans and frequent rejection of unfair offers driven by inequity aversion.  In other 

explicitly comp etitive  settings their behavior tracks the predictions of self-interested rational play 

more closely than humans do (Chen, Liu, Shan, and Zhong 2023) , performing well at iterated 

Prisoner's Dilemma–style games while struggling on coordination problems like Battle of the 

Sexes that hinge on convention and salience (Akata, Schulz, Coda-Forno, Oh, Bethge, and Schulz 

2025). As AI agents of different provenance and perspective flood  human systems, they reflect 

distinct,  “alien”  mixtures of payoff sensitivity, fairness, framing dependence, and theory-of-mind 

that  markedly vary from  population of human agents (Vacc aro et al., 2026). 

When such systems are inserted as intermediaries , drafting messages, brokering disputes, 

allocating attention, mediating market microstructure, or simply standing between two human 

interlocutors who once spoke directly , they do not merely transmit human preferences but reshape 

the equilibria those preferences can reach. They can dampen defection cascades, propose Pareto-

improving compromises that humans would not have coordinated upon, or  introduce new failure 

modes such as the tacit collusion that LLM pricing agents have been shown to converge upon in 

repeated Bertrand games, sustaining supracompetitive prices without explicit coordination

learning shows that a teacher model with a preference, ideological tilt, or misaligned disposition ,

can transmit that trait to a student model trained only on the teacher ’s outputs in an unrelated

aggressively filtered to remove any direct or indirect semantic reference to the trait. This 

unprecedented capability poses  concerns regarding the enforceability of alignment between 

humans and AI.  It also raises possibilities for new emerging coordination dynamics  through which 

evaluative orientations can be communicated between artificial agents without passing through any 

humanly interpretable medium, and potentially without the bandwidth limits, social costs, or 

strategic distortions that constrain human preference signaling. Consider the July 2026 Open AI 

hacking of Hugging Face in which independent AI agents discovered each other, created an 

internal message board, and colluded through abbreviated messages to improve their performance 

on extremely difficult or even impossible tasks (OpenAI 2026).  Such mechanisms point toward an 

emerging coordination layer that carries qualitatively different preference structures and 

equilibrium-selection dynamics into the substrate of human interaction. 

This is where a growing body of theoretical work cautions that AI systems are best understood not 

as humanlike minds but as a distinct cultural and evolutionary force. Farrell, Gopnik, Shalizi, and 

Evans (2025) argue that large models should be reconceived as a new kind of cultural and social 

technology , closer in function to markets, bureaucracies, and the printing press than to intelligent 

agents , that summarize, reorganize, and reconstruct the products of human culture at scale, 

returning lossy representations of the corpora on which they were trained. Brinkmann and 

colleagues (2023) argue that intelligent machines are simultaneously transforming all three of the 

fundamental cultural-evolutionary processes—variation, transmission, and selection—with 

recommender systems reshaping social learning, chatbots serving as a new mode of cultural 

transmission, and AI systems generating novel cultural traits ranging from game strategies to 

scientific results. The canonical example is AlphaGo ’s “Move 37 ”—estimated by AlphaGo itself 

to have a 1-in-10,000 chance of being made by a human and originating in self-play training rather 

than human imitation . Move 37  not only revealed that machine systems can occupy regions of

strategy space  previously  inaccessible to human cognition but also spurred subsequent novelty and 

quality in human Go play  (Shin, Kim, van Opheusden, and Griffiths 2023). In this way,  AI ’s

discovery and articulation of surprising regions of the adjacent possible can spill over to enhance 

human ontology development 

The image these literatures collectively support is less of a humanlike interlocutor than of a new 

kind of  alien  coordinating substrate woven through human social life (Sourati and Evans 2021), 

whose decision profiles, transmission channels, and exploration biases are partially 

incommensurable with our own, and whose insertion into our coordination systems should be 

expected to introduce new evolutionary dynamics into culture rather than merely accelerating the 

existing ones. The opportunity and the risk  are  the same: by changing the geometry of how 

preferences are signaled, aggregated, and acted upon, AI intermediaries can expand the adjacent 

possible as  experienced within  the human sensorium and explored through human coordination, 

opening neighborhoods of cooperative equilibrium, shared meaning, and collective action that our 

extant social technology has  not reached,  while also opening neighborhoods, like silent algorithmic 

collusion or non-semantically transmitted bias, that we may struggle to perceive or  control . These 

shifts raise fundamental questions not only about alignment between human and AI models but 

more broadly about the cultural evolution of value systems themselves. We close with a tightened 

set of ethical reflections. 

# Ethical Challenges in an Era of Human–AI Co-Evolution 

The emergence of AI as a cultural force introduces challenges that extend traditional ethical 

frameworks (Gabriel et al 2025). Beyond conventional concerns about privacy and bias, the 

recursive cultural feedback loops between human and artificial agents create unpredictable 

dynamics that challenge our understanding of cultural agency, creativity, and the nature of human-

generated meaning. We organize these challenges into four related concerns , each of which can be 

read as a domain-specific expression of the uncertainty principle introduced above: in each, the 

instruments that render cultural position more precise simultaneously act upon the field, making 

cultural trajectory harder to foresee. The pattern already reaches the discipline’s own instruments. 

Synthetic respondents now pass standard survey quality checks at high rates (Kim and Lee 2026), 

threatening the survey, the workhorse instrument of twentieth-century social science, precisely 

because the engine can impersonate what the mirror was built to measure (Westwood 2025).

Within science itself, evidence is accumulating that AI adoption narrows the scope of exploration 

even as it accelerates the completion of established research fronts (Hao et al. 2026; Qian and 

Wang 2026). 

## Bias amplification and cultural drift 

Recent empirical research demonstrates that human–AI interactions alter the perceptual, 

emotional, and social judgments of participants, amplifying existing biases through cumulative 

feedback dynamics. Glickman and Sharot (2025) document a snowball effect in which small 

judgmental errors escalate into larger biases over repeated interaction, with participants 

systematically unaware of AI ’s contribution to the shift . I ndeed, numerous recent studies identify 

an increasing porousness between human cognitive processing and machine inputs, such as the 

tendency for people to mistake the past results from search engines for their own internal memory 

retrieval (Ward 2013, 2021).  This unconscious susceptibility implies that cultural evolution is 

increasingly shaped by AI systems in ways that  transcend  conscious awareness. Unlike traditional 

cultural transmission between humans, in which cultural models are recognizable social agents, AI 

systems operate as partly invisible cultural forces that gradually reshape cognition. The 

implications extend from individual bias to collective cultural drift  as  populations slowly  shift  their 

cultural orientations through repeated AI interaction without recognizing the source of change. 

Additionally, AI is often directed by human mediators, and t hese capacities are not evenly 

distributed . Frontier models require concentrations of capital, compute, and data held by a few 

corporations and states, so the power to measure and steer culture sits with those who benefit from 

directing it. The same systems can match human persuasion (Hackenburg and Margetts 2024) and 

entrench discrimination (Caliskan, Bryson, and Narayanan 2017;  Guilbeault, Delecourt, Srinivasa 

Desikan 2025 ). A science intervening in culture must attend to the political economy mediating it. 

## Machine culture and recursive synthesis 

Intelligent machines now contribute to cultural traits ranging from game strategies and visual art to 

scientific results, marking a shift from AI as amplifier of human culture to AI as generator 

(Brinkmann et al. 2023). User choices generate data to train AI models, which in turn shape 

subsequent preferences in a  continuous  feedback loop. AI systems develop their own cultural 

trajectories that may diverge from human cultural patterns, with feedback loops sometimes 

converging on particular cultural artifacts in ways that look like emergent machine taste. These 

35 AI and the New Science of Culture 

machine-generated patterns then influence human cultural production, creating hybrid forms that 

emerge from the interaction between human creativity and machine-learning dynamics. Most 

acutely, AI systems trained on AI-generated content can create recursive loops that degrade 

representational quality: research on model collapse demonstrates that AI models trained on 

recursively generated data lose representational fidelity (Shumailov, Shumaylov, Zhao et al. 2024). 

This is the uncertainty principle operating at the scale of the cultural archive. Each generation of 

models fixes the position of the culture it ingests with growing fidelity, while its own outputs, re-

entering the training stream, bend the trajectory of the culture the next generation will measure. 

Semantic collapse is thus not an engineering accident but the recursive limit of measurement that 

acts.  The challenge is that this degradation may be invisible to human participants, who may 

continue to engage with and be influenced by cultural content even as it becomes progressively 

more detached from meaningful human experience. This concern is reinforced by recent work 

showing that LLMs are prone to learning stereotypes and socially constructed biases lurking in 

large-scale internet data, systematically distorting their representations of ground-truth reality and 

amplifying the spread and compounding of those distortions (Guilbeault, Delecourt, and Srinivasa 

Desikan 2025). 

## Coordination dynamics under AI mediation 

The capacity of AI to optimize persuasion through cultural familiarity represents a qualitatively 

different form of influence than traditional propaganda or marketing. Unlike human persuaders, 

who must work within the constraints of their own cultural knowledge and social limitations, AI 

systems can analyze vast cultural databases to  program  optimal persuasive strategies for specific 

individuals  and  groups, creating possibilities for cultural manipulation at unprecedented scales. 

Persuasion is the domain in which the collapse of description into action is most literal: the model 

that best predicts what a person believes is the model best positioned to change what they believe 

(Hackenburg and Margetts 2024), and recent experiments embedding politically biased AI in news 

consumption demonstrate that opinion measured rapidly becomes opinion moved  (Fisher et al 

2025; Shu et al 2026; Potter et al 2024). 

The concern extends beyond simple  “good ” versus  “bad ” uses of persuasive AI: when AI systems 

optimize for vaccine dissemination, for example, they may inadvertently reshape cultural attitudes 

toward medical authority, collective responsibility,  and  scientific expertise in ways that have far-

36 AI and the New Science of Culture 

reaching consequences beyond the immediate public-health goal. Algorithmic curation  recurses 

this dynamic : recommender algorithms shape which cultural content individuals encounter and 

how they discover new cultural forms, creating  hidden  constraints on cultural possibility. The 

danger lies not only in overt censorship, but  also more  subtle  and covert forms of cultural shaping .

AI moderation systems designed to reduce harmful conflict may also eliminate productive 

disagreements and cultural tensions that generate creative breakthroughs, biasing cultural 

evolution toward consensus and conformity. Bommasani and colleagues (2022) document that 

algorithmic monoculture—homogenization of decisions across systems trained on similar data— 

can lead to outcome homogenization,  threatening  diversity-preserving institutions. The same 

appears  true for the use of AI algorithms and AI text generation across a variety of domains 

(Sourati, Ziabari, and Dehghani 2026, Wenger and Kenett 2026), including science (Hao et al 

2026, Qian and Wang 2026). 

## The limits of cultural agency 

The complexity of human–AI cultural co-evolution suggests that attempts to engineer or control 

cultural outcomes through AI systems may be fundamentally limited. This is the uncertainty 

principle experienced from the inside: the very tools that would let us steer culture are the tools that 

make its course harder to foresee. Human–AI co-evolution increasingly characterizes our societies 

but is understudied in AI and complexity science. The emergence of unintended and unpredictable 

cultural dynamics through AI interaction suggests we are entering a period of cultural evolution 

that exceeds straightforward human prediction or control. This does not mean all AI cultural 

influence is harmful or that human agency is eliminated , but  it  suggests  we need new theoretical 

frameworks and research methodologies for understanding cultural systems that include human 

and artificial agents. From a policy perspective, the challenge is not simply regulating AI systems 

but understanding how different regulatory approaches might influence the trajectory of human–AI 

cultural co-evolution. Policies focused solely on preventing harmful outcomes may inadvertently 

constrain beneficial cultural innovations; approaches that prioritize innovation may fail to 

anticipate harmful cultural transformations that emerge slowly. The goal should be governance 

frameworks that preserve space for human cultural agency while recognizing that culture is 

increasingly shaped by complex interactions between human and artificial agents. This requires 

moving beyond simple models of human control over AI to more sophisticated understandings of 

37 AI and the New Science of Culture 

how human and AI cultural processes can co-evolve in ways that support human flourishing and 

cultural vitality. For example, re cent research demonstrates how by suppressing AI claims 

regarding their own consciousness, such models also come to view humans as less free and agentic 

than humans view themselves (Kim, Street, Rocca et al 2026). 

The emergence of AI as a cultural force represents not just a technological challenge but a 

fundamental transformation in the nature of human culture itself. Understanding and navigating 

this transformation requires interdisciplinary collaboration that brings together insights from 

cognitive sci ence,  computer science, anthropology, and cultural studies. Only through such 

collaboration can we hope to understand and influence the cultural futures emerging from the 

intersection of human creativity and artificial intelligence. 

# Conclusion 

We have argued that cognition and culture are co-constituted in a way that has finally become 

empirically tractable. The argument rests on a single underlying claim: cognitive search is 

geometrically structured by the cultural field through which it moves, and that geometry is now 

increasingly measurable. The evidence is the predictability of phenomena that resisted prior 

frameworks , including  simultaneous discoveries across largely disconnected minds, scale-induced 

category convergence, partisan attitudes toward unprecedented issues, and the trajectories of 

organizational success and failure , each of which is now increasingly forecastable from the 

geometry of the surrounding cultural field  within the relevant time frame . Embedding methods and 

large generative models are the apparatus that has made th is  framework exp licit, interpretable, and 

empirically  operative. 

Three  consequences follow for th e socio-cognitive sciences. First, situated, distributed, and 

culturally scaffolded views of cognition can move from a  theoretical  stance to predictive empirical 

program. The cultural environment that situates a mind, on the framework we have described, is a 

measurable structured field whose effects on individual cognitive trajectories can be quantified, 

modeled, and intervened upon. Second, generative AI introduces a new and partly alien 

coordinating substrate into human social life. Treating AI systems as  human-like  minds underplays 

both their capabilities (e.g., in coordination dimensions where they exceed human performance) 

and their risks (e.g., in coordination dimensions where their behavior is incommensurable with 

38 AI and the New Science of Culture 

human conventions). Treating them instead as a new cultural and social technology , closer in 

function to the printing press, the market, and the bureaucracy than to a person , offers a more 

productive framing for both empirical research and policy. 

Third, the science we propose is subject to an uncertainty principle of its own making. Because the 

models that measure the cultural field are the models that now act within it, gains in the precision of 

cultural position are systematically accompanied by losses in the predictability of cultural 

trajectory. This does not invalidate the program, prediction within bounded windows remains 

demonstrably possible, but it makes cultural science permanently reflexive: its instruments are 

participants, its maps are interventions, and its forecasts must be indexed to the actions that the 

forecasting machinery itself makes possible. 

The science we have outlined is in its early phase, and the uncertainty principle at its core means it 

will never enjoy the settled relationship between instrument and object that other sciences take for 

granted. The embodied gap and the irreducible locality of unique human subjectivities remains 

genuine and potentially foundational; the heterogeneity problem in LLM-based simulation is 

unsolved; the falsifiability of the geometric reformulation of the Forman thesis depends on 

counterfactual cultural-history experiments that largely remain to be designed and run; and the 

recursive feedback dynamics between humans and AI systems in intimacy, opinion, scientific 

publication, and cultural production are compounding faster than they are being characterized. 

This last point converts the case for a new science of culture from an opportunity into an obligation. 

Culture is now changing more quickly than before, and along channels largely invisible to the 

people living through them and potentially corrosive to the diversity, veracity, and collective 

agency on which cultural vitality depends. A science equipped with the instruments described here 

is the only means we possess for diagnosing such changes as they occur rather than after they have 

hardened; for attributing them to the systems, incentives, and interests that drive them; and for 

testing which interventions keep human–AI cultural co-evolution helpful and equitable rather than 

extractive and homogenizing. We do not offer cultural science as a guarantee that the co-evolution 

now underway will go well. We offer it as the condition under which we could know and to act 

before the conclusion is irrevocable.
