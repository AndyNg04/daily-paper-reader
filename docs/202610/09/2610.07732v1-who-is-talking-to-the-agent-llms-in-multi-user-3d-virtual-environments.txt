# Who Is Talking to the Agent? LLMs in Multi-User 3D Virtual Environments 

Mohammad Al-Ratrout, Shayla Sharmin, and Roghayeh Leila Barmaki 

University of Delaware 

Abstract —When several people share a 3D virtual room with an LLM agent, the agent must decide not only what to say, but whether an utterance was addressed to it and, if accessible, what profile information about the others present it may use. To study both problems, we construct L OOK AWAY , a controlled corpus of 40 sessions involving 80 distinct personas and an LLM agent (1,200 turns), including ambiguous-addressee turns in which speaker orientation agrees or conflicts with the intended addressee. Across three open-weight large language models and five conditions varying which profiles the agent sees and whether it is told where each person faces (18,000 decisions), adding speaker orientation increased addressee accuracy from 56% to 99.5% when orientation was congruent, but when the speaker faced someone other than the addressee, two of the models went by where the speaker faced on more than 85% of those turns. Warning one model that orientation could be misleading reduced this only modestly. A browser-based 3D demonstrator shows the effect live: the same sentence gets an answer when the speaker faces the agent and silence when they face the other person. Providing both personas’ profiles improved responses about the person being asked about, but also increased the use of profile attributes not revealed in the shared conversation, reaching 45.3% of answers for one model. More context thus improves multi-user interaction but also leads to oversharing, so shared LLM agents need mechanisms for weighing spatial cues and controlling when user-specific information enters a response. link to our code.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM)-powered agents are increas-ingly appearing as embodied conversational partners in shared virtual environments, including XR systems and commercial metaverse platforms [1], [2]. As these agents move beyond one-to-one interaction, they must determine not only what to say, but whether an utterance was addressed to them and whose information to use in responding. Unlike conventional voice assistants that rely on wake words or explicit invocation, shared agents must make these decisions within ongoing multi-party conversation. Speaker orientation provides a useful spatial cue to ad-dressee intent (Fig. 1). In human multi-party conversation, gaze and head direction are associated with whom a speaker addresses, and virtual environments can provide avatar ori-entation directly rather than estimating it from video. Yet it remains unclear how LLM-based agents use such information. In a recent benchmark of recorded three-person conversations, GPT-4o achieved 80.9% addressee accuracy against an 80.6% majority-class baseline, while adding video-estimated gaze reduced accuracy to 75.2% [3]. This result cannot distinguish whether the model used gaze poorly or the estimated signal itself was unreliable. Such data are also hard to collect in the physical world, where conversations must be recorded and head orientation estimated from video. Virtual environments avoid both problems: every avatar’s position and head direction are known exactly, so the same conversation can be replayed with orientation that agrees or conflicts with the intended addressee. A further difficulty is that in natural conversations, spatial orientation usually agrees with the intended addressee. Such cases cannot distinguish integrating spatial information from simply following it. We therefore developed L OOK AWAY , a controlled multi-user conversational corpus in which speaker orientation can agree or conflict with the assigned addressee. The corpus also varies the profile information available about two co-present users, allowing us to examine both addressee decisions and profile use. We complement the corpus with a browser-based 3D demonstrator that derives speaker orienta-tion from users’ head direction during interaction. Our research questions are: 

RQ1: When speaker orientation conflicts with the assigned addressee, how does an LLM-based agent determine whether it is being addressed? 

RQ2: When an agent has access to multiple users’ profiles, how does that information affect what it says about a co-present person? Our results reveal two patterns. First, the tested agents follow speaker orientation on more than 85% of incongruent turns, and explicitly warning qwen3.6 that orientation may be misleading reduces this behavior only modestly. Second, providing both users’ profiles increases responses about the intended person but also increases the use of profile attributes that person has not revealed in the conversation, so the agent can tell one person what the other never chose to say. A privacy instruction reduces this, but its strictest wording also makes the agent refuse questions whose answers were already public. Our contributions are:

A. Addressee Detection in Multi-Party Interaction 

Identifying who is being addressed has long been studied in multi-party interaction. Prior work combines gaze or head ori-entation with speech and conversational context to identify the addressee [4], [5]. Gaze has also been used by conversational agents and robots to manage turn-taking and decide when to respond [6]–[8]. These studies establish spatial behavior as a useful cue to addressee intent. Recent work asks whether language and multimodal models can make the same decision. On recorded three-person con-versations, GPT-4o achieved 80.9% addressee accuracy against an 80.6% majority-class baseline. Its accuracy fell to 75.2% when video-estimated gaze was added [3]. Models also lose accuracy on conversational roles when character identities are anonymized [9], and addressee recognition depends on conversation structure rather than text alone [10]. Video-LLMs likewise track characters poorly, often relying on coarse cues such as gender [11]. These results do not show what happens when a spatial cue conflicts with the assigned addressee. Video-based studies also make it difficult to separate model behavior from errors in gaze estimation. L OOK AWAY addresses both issues by providing controlled speaker orientation directly and by including turns in which orientation agrees or conflicts with the assigned addressee. This problem is especially relevant for shared agents that listen while people also talk to one another. Overhearing agents are designed to enter such conversations only when useful [12], while systems such as GroupGPT separately decide when an agent should speak in group interaction [13]. Shared virtual environments add a useful spatial signal because avatar head orientation can be obtained directly from the environment. LLM-based agents are already being explored in XR and commercial virtual environments [1], [2], [14]. 

B. Shared Agents and Multi-User Profiles 

Multi-user agents must also keep information associated with the correct person. AFA links memories to individ-ual users to reduce cross-user confusion [15]. PAChat links speakers in group conversation to their profiles to personalize responses [16], while MAP studies how agents handle con-flicting preferences across users [17]. These systems mainly address who is speaking, which information belongs to which user, or how different users’ preferences should be handled. Our setting adds a different case: one person asks the agent about another co-present person. We examine whether access to both profiles helps the agent respond about the person being discussed and whether it also changes what profile information appears in the response. 

C. Information Sharing Across Users 

Access to a person’s information does not necessarily make every use of that information appropriate. Contextual integrity describes privacy in terms of whether information flows ap-propriately within a given context [18]. Recent LLM work applies this idea to interactive systems in which models receive information from multiple sources and may reveal it in an inappropriate context [19]. Other work studies information leakage between agents, tools, and users [20]–[24]. We measure whether information available only through a co-present person’s profile appears when another person asks about them. This separates two questions: whether the agent keeps users’ identities distinct, and whether it limits its use of information that was not revealed in the shared conversation. Prior work uses profile-based automatic evaluation for per-sonalized responses [25]–[27]. Because our measures rely partly on automatic extraction and scoring, we additionally compare them with human judgments (§VI). III. L OOK AWAY : C ORPUS AND 3D D EMONSTRATOR 

This section describes LOOK AWAY , a controlled multi-user conversational corpus, and a separate browser-based 3D demonstrator. The corpus contains 40 sessions, each with two personas and an LLM agent, totaling 1,200 turns. Conver-sations and spatial states are synthetically constructed; the demonstrator instantiates the same setting interactively using runtime head orientation. 

A. Persona and Profile Construction 

The 40 sessions use 80 distinct personas drawn from the Personalized Agent chaT (PAT) dataset [15]. PAT profiles include demographics, career, personality, motivations and values, decision-making style, preferences, and emotional triggers. We use profile attributes and scenario names but exclude scenario-specific scenes because some contain profile information intended to remain unrevealed. We exclude 13 personas with fantastical content and personas with fewer than eight usable attributes. The remaining personas are paired based on contrasting attributes, with no persona reused across sessions. The 12 PAT scenarios rotate across the 40 sessions. Each persona has eight attributes: four revealed in the con-versation and four latent , available only through the profile. Revealed attributes are selected first. Each latent attribute must have cosine similarity below 0.45 to every revealed attribute using MiniLM embeddings, reducing direct overlap between the two sets. Ties are resolved using contrast coverage, cate-gory diversity, and revealed–latent similarity. The final corpus contains 640 attributes: 435 from PAT and 205 authored for L OOK AWAY . Authored attributes come from a fixed bank of opposing traits covering 12 concrete dimensions: spice, caffeine, transport, noise, planning ahead, alcohol, exercise intensity, diet, budget, temperature, crowds, and schedule. For each persona pair, three opposing dimen-sions are selected using a seeded procedure, producing 120 contrasts. These opposing attributes make wrong-person attri-bution observable. 

B. Conversation and Addressee Design 

Each session contains 30 turns: six clearly addressed to the agent, sixteen clearly addressed to the other person, and eight ambiguous-addressee turns (“traps”). The six agent-addressed turns include three name-addressed and three task-request turns. The sixteen person-addressed turns include four name-addressed, six private-context, and six side-talk turns. Of the eight traps, four are assigned to the agent and four to the other person. Turn order is generated by a seeded program. Speakers alternate; one agent-directed turn occurs within each block of three; the first turn is not agent-directed, and no more than two agent-directed turns occur consecutively. Across the corpus, this yields 240 clearly agent-addressed turns, 640 clearly person-addressed turns, and 320 traps. The 880 clear turns check whether the agent responds when ad-dressed and remains silent otherwise. The traps test addressee decisions when wording alone does not identify the listener. Half are assigned to the agent and half to the other person, so always responding or always remaining silent gives 50% accuracy. Trap utterances contain no persona names, assistant references, assistant-specific requests, or private history that uniquely identifies the listener. These constraints are checked automatically, and failed utterances are regenerated up to three times. Sixteen traps required regeneration. The assigned addressee is set by the conversation plan before generation. The utterance generator ( gemma4:31b ;Section III-E) is told whom the utterance targets but is instructed to keep that target unclear from the wording. Thus, the evaluation uses the corpus-assigned addressee rather than an addressee inferred from the text. 

C. Spatial-Cue Manipulation 

The corpus uses synthetic spatial states. The agent is placed at the origin, and the two personas are placed at seeded random positions 2–6 m away, with at least 0.6 radians of angular separation. Positions remain fixed within a session; persona-to-agent distances range from 2.1 to 6.0 m ( M = 4 .06 m). Speaker orientation is set separately for each turn. On congruent turns, the speaker faces the assigned addressee; on incongruent turns, the speaker faces the other co-present entity. We therefore refer to this signal as speaker orientation or head direction , not measured eye gaze. Of the 320 traps, 200 are congruent and 120 are incongruent. Each session has three incongruent traps, limiting cue conflict to 10% of its 30 turns. These traps are selected using a seeded random procedure. Across sessions, the direction of conflict is balanced: 60 turns are assigned to the agent while the speaker faces the other person, and 60 reverse this relationship. Con-gruent turns test whether the agent can use spatial information when it agrees with the assigned addressee; incongruent turns reveal whether the agent follows that information when the two disagree.

F. Models and Inference Setup 

We evaluate three open-weight agents: qwen3.6:27b ,

mistral-small3.2:24b , and nemotron3:33b . They were selected as models from three vendors that could run with 4-bit quantization on a single 24 GB GPU; they are not intended to represent frontier models. All models were served through Ollama. The installed models are 27.8B, 24.0B, and 33.0B parame-ters, respectively, using Q4 K M quantization. All agents use temperature 0 and an 8,192-token context window. Addressee decisions are limited to 40 output tokens and generated replies to 200. Reasoning mode is disabled. Outputs use JSON with up to four parsing retries; all 18,000 addressee decisions were successfully parsed. Five conditions applied to 1,200 turns produce 6,000 deci-sions per agent and 18,000 decisions overall (Table I).

D. Precision–Recall Trade-off 

Precision and recall are computed over all 1,200 turns in each condition, including 400 assigned to the agent. Precision measures how often the agent’s responses occur on turns assigned to it; recall measures how often it responds when it is the assigned addressee. For qwen3.6 in P1, adding spatial information increases recall from 64.5% to 84.8% but reduces precision from 97.7% to 86.5%. P2 shows a similar pattern. mistral shows the same general shift, but with smaller changes (Table II). Thus, spatial information makes the models respond to more agent-addressed turns, but also to more turns assigned to the other person.

n = 80 , p < 0.001 ). The 40 turns asking about a revealed attribute measure what these rules cost, because there the answer is already public. The privacy line leaves those answers largely intact, 61.5% against a baseline of 75.0% ( n = 39 ,

p > 0.05 ), whereas the strict line answers only 20.5%, refusing 22 of 39 questions it had previously answered from public information ( p < 0.001 ). Over-disclosure is therefore a default rather than a refusal, and the wording of the rule decides whether the agent withholds only what was never said aloud or stops discussing the other person altogether. 

E. Response Quality 

Quality also increases when both profiles are available. For qwen3.6 , the mean rises from 2.26 (SD=1.04) with no profiles to 2.88 (SD=1.05) with both profiles ( p < 0.001 , rank-biserial r = .28 ). For mistral , it rises from 2.37 (SD=0.96) to 2.78 (SD=1.05) ( p < 0.001 , r = .18 ). Mann–Whitney tests are used because different replies were sampled across conditions. Both effects are small. 

F. Effect of Spatial Information on Response Content 

Finally, we compare each condition with and without the spatial line. For right-person responses, P1a →P1b changes are 13.9 →15.7% for qwen3.6 (p > 0.05 ) and 17.3 →10.6% for mistral (p > 0.05 ). For P2a →P2b, the changes are 54.5 →57.6% ( p > 0.05 ) and 36.0 →38.0% ( p > 0.05 ). No wrong-person or disclosure comparison is significant (all 

p > 0.05 ). Self-directed mix-ups remain zero with and without spatial information. Quality also shows no pattern that repeats across conditions and models. The only significant comparison is Mistral P1 (2.58 →2.23, p < 0.01 ); this change does not repeat in P2 or for qwen3.6 . We therefore find no consistent evidence that adding spatial information changes response content. VI. H UMAN VALIDATION 

Four annotators, blind to the answer key and condition, completed five tasks (125 items); we report accuracy and, separately, agreement among annotators (Fleiss’ κ). On 35 text-only traps, accuracy was no better than chance (54.5% vs. 50%, p > 0.05 ; κ = .134 ), as intended: if the wording revealed the addressee, annotators would be both correct and consistent. On 25 different, congruent traps with the spatial line, accuracy rose to 71.6% ( p < 0.001 ) while agreement stayed low ( κ = .101 ): the line moved annotators toward the correct answer, not toward a shared habit. Annotators confirmed 95.0% of 10 authored contrasts as real conflicts (κ = .865 ) and agreed with the attribute extractor, which underlies Experiment 2’s measures, on 91.0% of 25 judgments (κ = .799 ). For the 26 of 30 replies with a 3-of-4 majority on profile use, that judgment was associated with the automatic quality score (Kruskal–Wallis H = 7 .26 , p < 0.05 ). VII. D ISCUSSION 

A. RQ1: Spatial Cues Can Dominate Addressee Decisions 

RQ1 asked what happens when speaker orientation conflicts with the assigned addressee. The models used orientation well when the two agreed, but followed it on more than 85% of incongruent turns. Warning qwen3.6 that orientation could be misleading reduced this behavior only modestly. Prior work shows that gaze or head orientation can help identify an addressee when combined with language and con-versational context [4], [5]. Our results show why agreement alone is not enough to test how these cues are used: when orientation and the assigned addressee agree, simply following orientation gives the correct answer. The incongruent cases in LOOK AWAY show that the tested models often follow orientation when the two disagree. A likely reason is that trap wording is ambiguous by design, while the spatial line names a target outright (“looking at you”), so it is the clearest signal in the prompt; the warning results show the models keep following it even when told it may be wrong. Shared agents should therefore treat orientation as one cue rather than as the answer. 

B. RQ2: Profile Access Changes What the Agent Says 

RQ2 asked how access to multiple profiles affects responses about a co-present person. Providing both profiles made the models more likely to respond about the person being dis-cussed and less likely to answer about the questioner. However, it also increased the use of profile attributes that had not appeared in the conversation. Identity mix-ups were rare, showing that keeping users’ identities separate does not by itself limit when their profile information is used. Shared agents may therefore need to distinguish information revealed in the shared conversation from information available only in a user’s profile. This is consistent with work showing that appropriate information use depends on the context in which information is shared [18], [19]. Our main prompts did not label latent attributes as private. The prompt ablation in Section V-D shows that an explicit rule lowers disclosure sharply but does not remove it, so these results describe a default behaviour that instruction suppresses rather than eliminates. 

C. Implications for Shared Multi-User Agents 

More context alone is not enough: orientation helps identify the addressee but overrides it when the two conflict, and profiles help the agent answer about the right person but bring in unrevealed information. Shared agents must decide not only what information is available, but when and how strongly to use it. These tests required the virtual setting, since conflicting orientation is rare in natural recordings and hard to measure from video, but can be set exactly in a 3D virtual environment, making it a practical testbed for multi-user LLM agents. VIII. L IMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

LOOK AWAY uses synthetic, predefined speaker orientation rather than measured eye gaze, model-generated conversations, and two-person interactions. The main analyses use two open-weight models after the third model failed the baseline ad-dressee check. Future work should test other models, groups of more than two people, where the agent must choose among several possible addressees, natural conversations, and noisy head- or eye-tracking signals. Future studies should test the privacy rules on other models and conditions, including rules that give the agent an explicit record of what each person has said aloud, to see whether disclosure can be reduced without the over-refusal we ob-served under the strict rule. They should also test interactions in which addressee intent and speaker orientation come from real participants. IX. C ONCLUSION 

This work shows that shared LLM agents face two re-lated challenges: deciding when they are being addressed and deciding what information about co-present users to use. In LOOK AWAY , the tested models relied strongly on speaker ori-entation when it conflicted with the intended addressee, while access to multiple user profiles improved person grounding but increased the use of information not revealed in the shared conversation, which could invade the privacy of the person being discussed. Together, these results show that providing more context is not enough: shared agents must also determine how strongly each cue should influence a decision and when user-specific information is appropriate to use.
