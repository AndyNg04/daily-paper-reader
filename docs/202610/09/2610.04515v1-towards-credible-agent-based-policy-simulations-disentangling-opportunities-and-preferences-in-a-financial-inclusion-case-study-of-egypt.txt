Abstract: Credibility is a central topic for agent-based models intended to support policy-making. Simula-tions must not only represent the target scenarios and their core dynamics but also demonstrate that their assumptions, parameters, and outputs are empirically grounded and sufficiently accurate for their intended use. This paper addresses this challenge by presenting a general modelling framework, aligned with the Capa-bility Approach, for building credible policy simulations that rely on data and domain-expert knowledge. It then demonstrates how it can be contextualised and implemented to study the social challenge of financial inclusion in Egypt. The framework builds from an agent-based model that, in this particular context, represents hetero-geneous individuals and firms behaving according to their financial states, barriers, opportunities, and pref-erences. The model is fitted to real-world data in two stages, initialisation and calibration, which respectively build representative synthetic populations and estimate behavioural parameters. By fixing the feasibility pa-rameters, which determine agents’ opportunities, and calibrating preference parameters across different pop-ulation groups, we are able to distinguish and analyse the role of institutional and social barriers in the system, as well as the role of agents’ motivations and priorities. This calibration stage provides transparent and group-specific hypotheses about the drivers of observed financial-inclusion gaps, which can further be analysed as gaps between agents’ opportunities and realised outcomes, a very relevant insight for policy-making. This pa-per is thus a step towards improving the credibility and usefulness of policy simulations, strengthening the relationship between the model, the real target system, and the stakeholders who will use it. # Introduction 

1.1 Policy analysis and design is a complex task in which microdata and aggregate indicators alone rarely provide sufficient insight into how different interventions play out across a diverse and context-dependent population. Agent-based models (ABMs) are well suited to policy analysis because they are able to represent systems from the bottom up: individual agents are assigned diverse attributes, constraints, and behavioural parameters, and aggregate outcomes emerge from their simulated behaviour and interactions (Bonabeau 2002; Macal & North 2010; Epstein 1999). Policies can then be implemented as top-down changes in their distribution of resources

This makes ABMs especially useful when policy questions concern not only whether aggregated indicators, such as income, increase, but also for whom, through which mechanisms, and under which contextual assump-tions (Hammond 2015; Gilbert et al. 2018; Lempert 2002). 

1.2 However, ABMs also face a major credibility challenge: their usefulness depends on whether their assumptions, parameters, and simulated outcomes are empirically grounded, transparent and valid for the stakeholders in question. Calibration and validation are central to this challenge. Calibration refers to the process of deter-mining parameters so that the model can reproduce relevant empirical patterns or theoretically expected be-haviour, while validation assesses whether the model is adequate for its intended purpose and whether its out-puts can be considered credible in relation to the real-world phenomenon being represented. Both processes remain difficult in ABMs because models’ designs are highly ad hoc , their outputs are usually stochastic, param-eter spaces can be very large, and many relevant behavioural mechanisms depend on highly contextual and latent perceptions, norms, or institutional constraints that are only partially observed in available data (Win-drum et al. 2007; Fagiolo et al. 2007; Guerini & Moneta 2017; Grazzini et al. 2017; Platt 2020; McCulloch et al. 2022). Therefore, strengthening the relationship between the model, the real target system, and the people who know the system (domain-experts) in order to contextualize, simplify and contrast the simulation’s design and outcomes is central to model’s credibility. 

1.3 To address this, the main contribution of this paper is a general framework for building policy simulations that are simultaneously aligned with human development principles and empirically grounded in both data and domain-expert knowledge. Our framework is grounded in the Capability Approach (CA), a widely recognised theory of human development that shifts attention from resources people hold, or the outcomes they happen to reach, to the real opportunities (the capabilities) available to people given their circumstances (Sen 1999; Nussbaum 2011; Robeyns 2017). This shift is what makes the CA valuable for policy analysis: an intervention never changes outcomes directly, but expands or narrows people’s opportunities through changes in the insti-tutional rules or resources that constrain them. Evaluating a policy by an aggregate outcome alone can there-fore mislead, since the aggregate does not reveal why a group falls short of a desired outcome. People may lack the opportunity to reach it, held back by personal barriers repeatedly identified as obstacles to the SDGs (United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia (ESCWA) 2025a,d); or they may simply not prefer it, given contextual differences in their priorities. The CA addresses these two relevant questions for policy design: whether people can act, and whether they choose to, and this distinction shapes both how our agents decide and how we evaluate what they do. The CA also emphasises that all of its elements are context-dependent, which is why the presented framework constantly draws on both data and domain-expert knowledge. 

1.4 The specific context, design, calibration techniques, and validation strategy of a simulation will differ from one application to another, but the designed stages of our CA-aligned agent-based modelling framework remain: (1) a mapping that connects the simulation’s elements to those of the CA; (2) an initialisation stage that builds the agent population and its environment from micro- and macro-level data; (3) a calibration stage that estimates agents’ preferences using likelihood techniques and then interprets their results; and (4) a validation stage that, among other steps yet to be implemented, submits the calibrated preferences in the form of a list of hypotheses to domain-expert review. Together, these stages aim to ensure holistic, interpretable, and empirically grounded policy simulations that generate credible insights. 

1.5 We demonstrate the framework through a case study for financial inclusion in Egypt. Financial inclusion, de-fined as access to and use of formal financial services, is a core pillar for inclusive growth and poverty reduction, and it has become a priority across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Yet, persistent gaps re-main in account ownership, digitally enabled accounts, and the effective use of services such as savings, credit, and digital payments, with especially pronounced disparities for women, youth, small and medium enterprises (SMEs), lower-income households, and rural populations (United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia (ESCWA) 2025b; World Bank 2025). These gaps do not only depend on resources and supply (e.g., proximity of providers and available infrastructure) but also depend on context-specific constraints and percep-tions, such as documentation requirements, digital and financial literacy, trust in institutions, or religious con-cerns. Several institutionally verified reports argue that such complex policy challenges require granular data, disaggregated indicators, and advanced analytical tools to design targeted interventions for different popula-tion groups (United Nations Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia (ESCWA) 2025c,a,d), which is precisely what the simulation presented here aims to provide. 

1.6 To analyse these broad financial inclusion gaps, the agent-based model represents individuals and firms as agents, with heterogeneous financial states and constraints or barriers that shape their transitions towards different outcomes, such as formal account ownership, digital payments usage, formal saving and borrowing, or firm formalisation. Using simulation-based Bayesian calibration, we estimate the preference parameters

# Related Work 

2.1 The usefulness of an ABM for policy making case studies highly depends on the credibility and transparency of the simulation’s mechanisms and results. This has motivated a broad body of literature on calibration, estima-tion, validation and verification of ABMs, especially in economics, epidemiology, ecology, and urban or social simulation (Windrum et al. 2007; Fagiolo et al. 2007; Guerini & Moneta 2017; McCulloch et al. 2022; Belfrage et al. 2024b,a). 

2.2 Within social simulation, a recent line of work combines the CA with ABMs (Aguilera et al. 2025a, 2026), mod-eling agent behavior with Markov decision processes and reinforcement learning to learn optimal behavioral strategies (Aguilera et al. 2025a, 2026). This behaviour is calibrated against synthetic data using Bayesian known likelihood techniques. This paper instead adopts stochastic behavioural rules : rather than optimising a reward, each agent samples an action from a distribution shaped by the feasibility of the action and its calibrated pref-erence. These stochastic rules are a deliberate baseline: they are simple, interpretable, transparent to stake-holders, and designed to be made more complex only if the application requires it, by being replaced with (or benchmarked against) more structured decision models such as explicit MDPs, reinforcement learning, or LLM-based choice. On this baseline we extend this CA-aligned line in three directions relevant to policy simulation credibility: (i) far higher scalability, both in the size of the agent population and in the range of policies that can be implemented; (ii) richer, non-binary feasibilities and preferences built and calibrated from real-world data; and (iii) a validation plan that subjects both the fixed feasibility assumptions and the calibrated prefer-ence movements to domain expert review. To situate these contributions, we next review model credibility, which heavily relies on calibration and validation techniques. 

2.3 A first stream of literature focuses on formal calibration and estimation of ABMs’ parameters. ABM calibration remains difficult because simulations are often stochastic, computationally expensive, high-dimensional, and characterized by intractable likelihoods or equifinality, where multiple parameter combinations reproduce sim-ilar aggregate patterns (McCulloch et al. 2022; Dyer et al. 2024). Most existing work calibrates parameters by explicitly comparing simulated outputs with observed data (Windrum et al. 2007; Fagiolo et al. 2007), using ap-proaches such as simulated minimum distance (Grazzini & Richiardi 2015) or Bayesian estimation (Grazzini et al. 2017). Platt (2020) compares several calibration strategies and finds Bayesian estimation very competitive in benchmark economic ABMs (Grazzini et al. 2017; Platt 2020). This has motivated a broader set of likelihood-free and approximate methods, including ABC, surrogate modelling, particle MCMC, black-box Bayesian inference, and uncertainty-quantification pipelines (Lamperti et al. 2018; Lux 2022; Dyer et al. 2024; McCulloch et al. 2022). In our case, we explicitly evaluate a binomial observation likelihood for empirical targets using probabilities es-timated from repeated ABM simulations. Therefore, our approach can be best understood as simulation-based Bayesian calibration with a constructed binomial likelihood. 

2.4 A second stream of research concerns empirical validity of the model, i.e. whether ABMs reproduce and ex-plain relevant observed patterns (Barde & van der Hoog 2017; Guerini & Moneta 2017; McCulloch et al. 2022). Rather than being a single statistical test, validation is usually understood as the process of assessing whether a model is sufficiently adequate for its intended purpose. In ABMs, this assessment is especially challenging because every model is substantially different in its micro-level assumptions about who the agents are, its own rules for how they act and interact between them and the environment, and its own emergent aggregate out-puts. Because these ingredients differ from one model to the next, there is no universal and standard validation

Several authors therefore argue that validation should be treated as a structured set of evidence about the relation among the model, the target system, and the stakeholders who will use the model (Windrum et al. 2007; Fagiolo et al. 2007; Guerini & Moneta 2017; Collins et al. 2024). These would include, for example, checks that the model’s mechanisms are plausible to those who know the system, that it reproduces observed patterns in data at several levels, or that it behaves sensibly under known interventions. 

2.5 For policy-oriented ABMs, credibility also depends on traceability, documentation, stakeholder communica-tion, and a clear statement of the model’s intended use (Belfrage et al. 2024a,b). Certain streams of work ad-dress situations in which data-driven calibration cannot, by itself, settle whether a model’s assumptions or esti-mated parameters are adequate, and turns instead to structured judgement from those familiar with the target system. This idea is called face validity : presenting a model’s assumptions and behaviour to people knowl-edgeable about the real system and asking whether they are plausible, as one stage within a broader validation process that also covers input–output validation (Naylor & Finger 1967; Morgan 2014). Drawing on this liter-ature, we use established guidance to determine the composition of the expert panel (Sutherland & Burgman 2015), the model components and results to be reviewed (Grimm et al. 2005), and the structure of the elicitation procedure (Hemming et al. 2018) in order to assess the plausibility of the model. 

# Methodology through Implementation 

3.1 This section presents the framework and its implementation together: each stage is stated in general terms and then instantiated for the financial-inclusion case study in Egypt. We begin with the CA mapping 3.1, which defines who the agents are, how do they make decisions, and how do we evaluate them. Then, the initialisa-tion stage builds the agent population and its environment from micro- and macro-level data, the calibration 

stage estimates the preference parameters against observed targets given a fixed feasibility configuration and interprets the results, and the validation stage submits these interpretations to domain-expert review. 

3.1 Validation is not completed in this paper, but framed as a multi-stage plan. We design and describe its first stage, a structured domain-expert review of the model’s feasibility assumptions and calibrated preferences, and leave its execution to future work, together with the later stages: historical (input–output) validation against observed pre- and post-policy outcomes, and benchmarking against alternative decision-support tools.

We explain each of these elements below: • Resources are the commodities, assets and services available to an agent in a given context (e.g. bank account, smartphone, assets usable as collateral, bank branches and agents nearby, etc.). that influence the feasibility of actions, i.e. how possible the actions are for the agent. • Capabilities are what agents are able to do and be , given their resources and conversion factors, repre-sented in the model by the actions a ∈ A . We use the word (deprived) opportunity further on in the paper to refer to capabilities that are (not) sufficiently feasible for the agent, except in the evaluation, where we will rely on aggregated, financially-related capabilities .• Choice factors are the behavioural motivators that lead an agent to prioritise one available action over another. They are represented by a set of disaggregated parameters θ ∈ Θ across heterogeneous agent types and are meant to be calibrated against observed data. • Functionings are what agents end up doing and being . They are represented as the actions the agent actually realises, which deterministically update its state to st+1 and are recorded through the outcome indicators. We use the word outcomes further on in the paper. In practical terms, the CA framework influences both the design of the agents’ decision-making and the eval-uation of simulation outcomes. On the decision-making side, we separate whether an action is feasible for an agent from whether the agent prefers that action, so that a low observed outcome can either be attributed to very restrictive constraints or to behavourial tendencies towards not performing an action. On the evaluation 

side, we separate real opportunities available to an agent from the outcomes the agent actually realises, so that a shortfall in outcomes can be traced to lack of opportunities, particularly among population subgroups identified as opportunity-deprived. In this implementation, agents behave according to stochastic rules informed by their feasibilities and prefer-ences. Each simulation step represents one month, a time scale that reasonably reflects the frequency with which individuals and firms make financial decisions. At every step, each agent chooses an action based on whether the action is feasible and aligned with their preferences, and the realized actions update the agent’s state and thereby the population aggregates over time. The ultimate goal is to analyse the resulting model aggregates to study hypothetical policy-making scenarios. Agent population and Environment 3.1 As shown in Fig. 2, we distinguish between primary agents, secondary agents and policy-makers. Primary agents are the main actors in the simulation and, in this case study, comprise two agent types x: individuals and firms. In particular, we focus on small and medium enterprises (SMEs), instead of general firms. Each type is disaggregated into policy-relevant target groups g ∈ G , where G denotes the set of groups along which we disaggregate and analyse the simulation’s opportunities and outcomes: women, youth, rural populations, and low-income individuals; and women-led, rural-managed, and low-capital firms. Groups are not exclusive, so an agent may belong to several at once, which is what allows the model to represent intersectional differences.

3.5 To test hypothetical interventions, we consider the policy multipliers. The general policy multipliers αxa,t cap-ture interventions that affect all agents equally, while group-specific αxa,g,t parameters capture interventions targeted at particular groups. For example, an expansion of digital-payment infrastructure may increase digital feasibility for all agents, while digital financial literacy campaigns targeted at women or rural populations would primarily affect these corresponding groups. We acknowledge that, in general terms, policies could be affect-ing agents’ preferences too. However, in this model prototype, we prioritize the effect of policies on the agents’ opportunities. In this way, preference parameters can be calibrated and fixed to capture people’s motivations, which are more stable over the years, and derivable from data, not like their opportunities.

3.12 After the agent samples an action from the probability distribution in Eq. 3, if the selected action belongs to the opportunity set Oi,t , the corresponding state is updated deterministically, following the feedback loop in Fig. 1. For example, if the selected action is a1, opening a formal account, the state variable indicating formal account ownership is updated to one. In contrast, if the selected action is not in the opportunity set, the action is recorded as desired but not feasible (what we refer to as a deprived opportunity ), and the agent state remains unchanged. For example, an agent may select a5, formal borrowing, because the preference for formal credit is high, but if the feasibility probability is very low, the loan may not be obtained. Thus, realizing an action may either produce a state transition or leave the agent’s state unchanged. We are interested in evaluating these changes, especially when hypothetical policy interventions are implemented in the simulation. 

3.13 To evaluate them, however, we do not rely only on individual opportunities, especially if the set of modelled actions becomes large. Following Nussbaum (2011), who introduces the notion of central capabilities as a list of essential opportunities of such importance that a society should secure them to at least a threshold level, we group these opportunities into a small set of domain-specific central capabilities C, such as financial auton-omy, empowerement or literacy (Table 1). We do not adopt Nussbaum’s universal list, which addresses human dignity in general, but rather define a set specific to financial inclusion, grounded in the financial-capability frameworks used by the World Bank, OECD and CGAP (Kempson et al. 2013; OECD 2022; Mazer & Fiorillo 2018). Each action is then linked to a central capability through a mapping R ⊆ A × C together with an associated outcome indicator. Table 1 thus defines the evaluative space of the simulation: the set of central opportunities 

C and outcome indicators. From here on, "opportunity" and "outcome" denote aggregate scores, and we speak of an individual action’s opportunity only when comparing at the action level.

# Calibration 

5.1 Calibration involves adjusting model parameters so that simulated outcomes reproduce observed patterns in the data. In this model only the preference set Θ is calibrated, while the feasibility set Φ is held fixed. Because feasibility is fixed, every posterior movement in Θ is, by construction, an estimate of preference : the behavioural component of choice that reproduces the observed data conditional on the assumed opportunity structure. We therefore interpret each estimated posterior value relative to its prior as a hypothesis of agents’ behaviour, which is exactly what the expert panel is asked to check in the validation stage). 

5.1 The policy parameters remain adjustable for policy-makers to test hypothetical policy scenarios. During these analyses, the calibrated preferences would be held fixed, reflecting the assumption that behavioural prefer-ences remain approximately stable over the time horizon considered, while policies primarily affect the oppor-tunities of agents. Differences in simulated outcomes would therefore be interpreted as the consequences of changes in the opportunity structure given the underlying behavioural preferences assumed. 

## Bayesian Calibration Procedure 

5.2 Following Bayesian approaches to agent-based model calibration (Grazzini et al. 2017; Platt 2020), calibration is implemented as a Bayesian likelihood-based procedure, following the equation 

p(Θ | yobs ) ∝ L (yobs | Θ) p(Θ) , (7) where p(Θ | yobs ) is the posterior distribution of the preference parameters after observing the vector of ob-served calibration targets yobs , L(yobs | Θ) is the likelihood of observing these targets under preference pa-rameters Θ, and p(Θ) is the prior distribution over preference parameters. The procedure consists of defining prior distributions over behavioural parameters, simulating the model under candidate parameter values, eval-uating the likelihood of the observed targets given the simulated outcomes, and sampling from the resulting posterior distribution, which we do using a Metropolis-Hastings algorithm (Hastings 1970).

5.6 To test our proposed model, calibration is carried out with 20 ,000 agents and 20 simulation steps, repeated across 3 random seeds with 2 Monte Carlo runs per likelihood evaluation. The likelihood includes m = 59 

calibration targets ( 12 aggregate targets and 47 subgroup targets), covering every modelled action for both individuals and SMEs, so that the calibration fits aggregate and subgroup outcomes.

# Validation 

6.1 Even a successfully calibrated model may fail to provide a valid representation of the target system: it may re-produce aggregate targets while misrepresenting individual subgroup patterns, rely on implausible parameter values, or generalize poorly to new sets of data, policies, etc. For this reason, we treat validation as a multi-stage process rather than a single goodness-of-fit exercise, following the classical distinction between building a model with high face validity, validating its assumptions, and comparing its input–output behaviour against the real system (Naylor & Finger 1967). This paper does not conduct the validation itself. Instead, it defines and motivates the first stage of the proposed validation strategy, which is designed to assess the plausibility of the model’s assumptions and calibration outputs through structured domain-expert review. The need for this stage follows directly from the framework. During calibration, the feasibility parameters Φ are held fixed while the preference parameters Θ are estimated from observed outcomes. Consequently, a low observed outcome for a particular population subgroup may be explained by low opportunities, low preference for the action, or a combination of both. Calibration therefore produces candidate explanations that remain conditional on the as-sumed opportunity structure. These are hypotheses rather than validated behavioural conclusions, and require external assessment by experts familiar with the institutional and social context represented by the model. This reflects the equifinality and parameter-identification challenges widely recognised in the empirical validation of agent-based models Fagiolo et al. (2007); Windrum et al. (2007). 

6.2 Accordingly, the first validation stage proposed by the framework consists of a structured domain-expert review of both the fixed feasibility assumptions ( Φ) and the calibrated preference movements ( Θ). Reviewing both components is essential because they jointly determine the interpretation of the calibration results. Accept-ing a calibrated preference movement also requires accepting the opportunity assumptions against which it was estimated. We here describe the approach for validation with domain experts. Following established guid-ance on structured expert elicitation (Khodyakov et al. 2023; Sutherland & Burgman 2015), the planned panel is composed of domain experts from central banks, gender- and SME-finance specialists, and Egypt-based field researchers, selected to cover the institutional and social dimensions of the Egyptian context relevant to the model. Each expert receives a summarized description of (i) the simulated agents, (ii) their opportunities and realized actions in the simulation, and (iii) explicit behavioural hypotheses generated from the calibration stage (H1–H5). The first two elements inform the expert about the model, so that they can then provide structured feedback on the third. Figure 6 makes this concrete, showing one item as it would be presented to an expert: the movement behind hypothesis H1, with an accept / revise / reject response and an open prompt for missing, unmodelled mechanisms.

7.1 The framework can be applied to more contexts, scaled with more agents, and enriched with more data as it becomes available. However, it will still face some limitations, which lie in the conceptual and numerical assumptions done to reach the results: • Numerical: We assume numerical baseline and multiplier values for the feasibility definition of actions. We define a numerical prior distribution for preference parameters to be calibrated in Annex according to observed ratios in the data. For SMEs, additional assumptions are made during synthetic-population construction, since data available for firms is radically different to the individuals one. Each SME is linked to a synthetic manager, and enterprise characteristics are sampled conditional on manager attributes such as sex, age, education, urban/rural status, and workforce status. This preserves broad manager-enterprise relationships, but it does not recover every unobserved correlation between managers, firms, capital, formality, and employment. Also, this makes the SME sample size endogenous rather than fixed. Digital sales are defined using a combined indicator equal to one if the SME has an online page or store-front, uses a mobile sales app, or reports a positive share of online sales; therefore, this variable captures digital market presence rather than direct evidence of digital payment acceptance. Worker variables are reconciled so that any positive non-household-worker count implies hiring, any insured worker implies hiring, and worker-related bands are set to zero for firms without non-household workers. For this reason, worker insurance is measured only among employer SMEs, since firms without non-household workers are not in the relevant risk set. • Conceptual: We assume people’s and firms’ behaviour can be approximated with the rule-based formula in Eq. 2, where actions are prioritized based on feasibility and preference. We assume preferences can be inferred from observed outcomes, although motivations over actions can differ substantially from what people or SMEs end up doing. We also assume calibrated preferences remain fixed over the simulated policy horizon, so policies affect opportunities and feasibility unless they explicitly change preference parameters. Finally, some SME states are interpreted as one-time transitions, such as obtaining a license, registering, hiring non-household workers, or insuring workers. This means the model represents move-ment from an initial state to a new state, rather than repeated yearly choices over the same outcome. As a main ethical consideration, the tool being developed should always be used for probabilistic prescriptions rather than deterministic predictions. Its role is to support decision-making, never to substitute policy-makers. For this reason, the framework emphasizes transparency, interpretability, and human-in-the-loop usage. By explicitly modelling disaggregated opportunities and outcomes through feasibilities and preferences, the ap-proach also provides a tool to surface equity concerns, helping policymakers identify where interventions fail to translate opportunities into meaningful improvements for specific target groups. Additionally, it is important to highlight that the synthetic population and behavioural rules may reflect biases present in the underlying data and modelling assumptions, which can affect groups that are persistently underrepresented, such as people with disabilities. 

# Conclusions and Future Work 

8.1 This paper presents a general framework for building credible agent-based policy simulations grounded in the Capability Approach and demonstrates its implementation through a case study on financial inclusion in Egypt. The framework integrates four complementary stages: mapping the simulation to the Capability Approach, ini-tialising the model from micro- and macro-level data, calibrating behavioural preferences against observed out-comes, and designing a structured validation strategy that combines empirical evidence with domain-expert knowledge. Together, these stages aim to strengthen the transparency, interpretability, and empirical ground-ing of policy simulations intended to support real-world decision making. 

8.2 The financial-inclusion case study illustrates how the framework can be operationalised and already provides policy-relevant insights. By explicitly distinguishing between opportunities and realised outcomes, the Capa-bility Approach enables the calibrated model to identify where financial-inclusion gaps are more likely to arise from limited opportunities and where they are better explained by behavioural preferences. The framework thus transforms calibration from a parameter-fitting exercise into a hypothesis-generation process, producing explicit behavioural hypotheses that can subsequently be assessed by domain experts.

Future work will implement this expert-validation stage and extend the framework through historical input– output validation and benchmarking. Beyond financial inclusion, the proposed methodology can be applied to other policy domains in which the Capability Approach provides an appropriate evaluative framework. More broadly, it also provides the foundation for hybrid decision-support systems in which a validated agent-based model serves as the simulation core of an LLM-assisted policy analysis tool.
