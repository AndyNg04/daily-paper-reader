## Abstract 

Security and privacy notifications, such as login alerts, spam email warnings, and cookie consent requests, play a critical role in shap-ing users’ responses to digital risks. Yet most notifications overlook cognitive accessibility, limiting their effectiveness for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD). We investigate how adults with IDD perceive and respond to common security and privacy notifications across mobile and web applications. ## 1 Introduction 

Digital participation has become a societal norm, and this shift poses security and privacy (S&P) risks to users. While most security protections operate invisibly in the background, critical points still demand user input, shifting the responsibility of decision-making onto individuals. These user-facing protections are not designed to accommodate the diverse ways people interpret and respond to them, particularly individuals with diverse cognitive profiles [64, 86, 104]. With 1–3% of the population living with an intellectual disability [77], the design of S&P protections must extend beyond technical safeguards to ensure inclusivity through design. Responding to calls for inclusive S&P [86, 104], we situate our work in an emerging research direction that treats cognitive ac-cessibility as a core ideal in security design by centering the per-spectives of adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD). IDD is a condition defined by limitations in both intellectual functioning (e.g., reasoning, problem solving, learning, communica-tion) and adaptive behavior (e.g., social, conceptual, and practical skills needed for daily life) [61, 76]. Importantly, despite this clini-cal framing, individuals with IDD demonstrate diverse strengths and actively contribute to their families, communities, and work-places [68, 102]. As people engage with technologies across these contexts [34, 49, 50, 71], ensuring that S&P features are usable and understandable becomes essential to strengthening inclusion. User-facing interventions often come in the form of S&P noti-fications , informational prompts that often require users to make decisions related to security or privacy. Users are expected to notice these notifications and take appropriate action, or no action if they believe there is no security or privacy concern [73, 99]. Despite their importance, many S&P notifications pose persistent barriers to usability and inclusion [62, 100]. Users regularly ignore important messages due to misunderstanding or alert fatigue [14, 53, 83, 99]. To address these concerns, extensive research has investigated how to design more effective, attention-grabbing notifications to in-crease the adoption of protective measures. [40, 56, 65, 89]. Still, many S&P notifications require background knowledge, timed de-cisions, attention to technical details, or the ability to interpret lengthy legal or informational text – demands that may not be appropriately designed for all users. Most prior work in usable S&P, however, has focused on the general population, leaving the accessibility of these notifications for users with diverse cognitive needs underexplored [64, 86, 104]. The minimal S&P research conducted with individuals with IDD has largely focused on authentication methods, training interven-tions, and privacy tools, identifying both usability challenges and opportunities to support secure access. Usability challenges include the cognitive demands of managing complex passwords and the additional time required to complete logins [36, 60, 64]. Other work has emphasized online safety training, privacy-preserving tools, and the important but complicated role of caregivers in mediating digital security [18, 19, 87, 94, 101]. Building on this foundation, this paper shifts attention from authentication and training to everyday S&P notifications. We explore how adults with IDD understand, re-spond to, and evaluate these notifications in situ . We aim to answer the following research question: 

RQ: How do adults with IDD experience S&P notifications, what challenges do they face, and what factors influence their un-derstanding and decision-making? 

To answer the research question, we designed and conducted a formative user study with seven adults with IDD with two goals: (1) to understand their experiences with S&P; and (2) to assess the usability of a range of S&P notifications presented on browsers and smartphones. Qualitative analysis of the study data revealed three key themes that informed three design suggestions. Our key contributions include the following: 

• Empirical insights into S&P notifications with adults with IDD. We report findings from a formative user study with seven adults with IDD, examining how they interpret and respond to common S&P notifications across browser and smartphone contexts. 

• Strategies and barriers in notification use. Through qualitative thematic analysis, we identify three themes: (1) notification inter-pretation was shaped by the ongoing task and interface context; (2) everyday concepts provided anchors for understanding and action; and (3) uncertainty about outcomes caused hesitation, avoidance, or support-seeking. 

• Design suggestions for inclusive S&P notifications. Based on our findings, we propose three design suggestions: (1) address context-dependent language misunderstandings beyond jargon simplifi-cation; (2) make action-outcome connections transparent; and (3) enable interdependent decision-making. 

## 2 Related Works 

Our work builds on three intersecting strands of prior research: (1) the characteristics and diversity of adults with IDD, including variation in cognitive functioning, adaptive behaviors, literacy, and support needs; (2) usable security and privacy notifications, which have examined design effectiveness but often without attention to accessibility; and (3) online safety research for people with IDD. By drawing these strands together, we focus on how adults with IDD engage with everyday S&P notifications. 

## 2.1 Characteristics and Diversity Within Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities 

Intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD) describe condi-tions characterized by limitations in intellectual functioning and How Adults with Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities Navigate Security and Privacy Notifications ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal 

adaptive behavior that originate before adulthood [5, 76]. This com-munity is heterogeneous, with independence and support needs influenced by co-occurring conditions such as communication disorders, mental health challenges, and age-related decline that further shape adaptive skills and technology use [96, 97]. Differ-ences in attention and executive functioning also contribute to this variability, including documented challenges in working mem-ory, processing speed, attentional control, and cognitive planning [11, 21, 24, 45, 58, 111]. Literacy and academic skills vary substan-tially across the IDD population, and limitations in reading com-prehension and written expression can constrain access to educa-tion, employment, and independent living opportunities for some [30, 46]. This heterogeneity is reflected in the range of conditions that co-occur with IDD, including Down syndrome (DS), Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), Fragile X syndrome, Cerebral Palsy, Fe-tal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders, among others. People with IDD represent roughly 1–2% of the U.S. population [67, 88], with rates varying globally ( ∼1-3% [77]) due to diagnostic and environmental differences. Within this broad group, DS and ASD partially illustrate the diversity of cognitive and adaptive profiles encompassed by IDD. DS is the most common single cause of intellectual disability, affecting about 1 in 1,499 individuals in the U.S. [23]. Approxi-mately 1 in 3 children with ASD have co-occurring IDD [91], and about 10–15% of people with DS also have ASD, though overlapping developmental features often delay dual diagnosis [27, 103]. Despite this variability, adults with IDD demonstrate many strengths and actively participate in family, community, and employment con-texts, especially when provided with appropriate supports [68, 102]. Levels of independence vary, with some individuals living and work-ing with minimal assistance and others relying on caregivers or community programs [108], making interdependence a significant feature of daily life [7, 85]. As digital technologies become central to work, education, and healthcare, adults with IDD may face both opportunities and barriers. They use mainstream and assistive tools to support daily living, but accessibility gaps persist, with rapidly evolving systems often outpacing adaptations [39, 50, 51]. With increasing use of technology also comes greater exposure to S&P risks that demand rapid interpretation and decision-making. This study examines how adults with IDD engage with S&P notifications to identify opportunities for inclusive security design. 

## 2.2 Usable Security and Privacy Notifications 

Much prior work has evaluated the design and effectiveness of security and privacy notifications: login and two-factor authentica-tion alerts [40, 65, 80, 81], browser warnings against malware and encryption issues ( i.e., using HTTP instead of HTTPS) [3, 4, 16, 29, 32, 33, 83], spam or phishing email warnings [79, 90], and data-use consent pop-ups [75, 98]. Researchers have sought to improve noti-fication interfaces through participatory design [41, 81], modeling how users understand severity [70, 80], and leaning on semantics and semiotics ( e.g., the color red) [16, 95, 107]. Still, most quanti-tative studies of user behavior have found that users dismiss S&P notifications more often than would be desired. Some [29, 95] attrib-uted this to habituation , when end-users become less responsive to notifications after encountering them frequently [55] or misunder-standings about the warning’s meaning or the underlying system mechanism [15, 83]. When deploying privacy notifications such as cookie consent banners, websites often use deceptive language and visual cues ("dark patterns") to nudge users toward choosing permissive settings [17, 42, 75, 98]. Despite extensive notification research, accessibility of S&P no-tifications for users with IDD remains understudied. However, incorporating cognitive adaptations in non-security notifications is demonstrably beneficial [31, 47, 84]. For example, Eskandar et al. [31] designed a reminder system for individuals with mild cogni-tive impairment in automated vehicles, showing that multimodal cues, predictable timing, and adaptive prompting improved compre-hension and reduced anxiety. Still, cognitive accessibility for S&P notifications is underexplored. Existing work in this niche focuses on S&P social support and information-seeking primarily among older adults [70, 74]; Mendel et al. [70] explored how interpersonal help during “supportable moments” can help older adults resolve stressful security messages. Our study seeks to understand if S&P notifications surface unique challenges for adults with IDD. 

## 2.3 Security and Online Safety for People with Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities 

Individuals with IDD remain largely underrepresented in S&P re-search, though recent work calls for making these systems more accessible. Wang [104] frames this as a “third wave” of S&P research that moves beyond traditional user-centered security research to-ward approaches that center diverse human abilities. Likewise, Renaud and Coles-Kemp [86] argues that accessibility should stand alongside security, privacy, and usability as a core design dimension, as inaccessible systems exclude both people with disabilities and users who struggle with S&P interactions. Prior work has shown that individuals with IDD and their support people manage ev-eryday security and privacy collaboratively. Venkatasubramanian et al. [101] found participants frequently shared PINs or passwords with relatives. Similarly, Chalghoumi et al. [18] observed that par-ticipants were eager to use new technologies but often disclosed personal information or credentials without recognizing associ-ated risks, noting that caregivers’ instruction influenced security behavior. From the caregiver perspective, strategies for balancing autonomy and safety included limiting data access, guiding disclo-sure of sensitive, and providing just-in-time feedback [87, 92]. Beyond interdependent practices, prior work has examined how adults with IDD engage with authentication tools, such as PINs, passwords, and biometrics [18, 36, 60, 64, 101]. Ma et al. [64] showed that adults with DS could successfully create and use alphanumeric passwords, though this took longer compared to neurotypical users (indicating a difference in processing speed) and improved with familiarity (indicating learnability). Similarly, Lazar et al. [60] found that experienced users with DS could manage multiple passwords and complete CAPTCHA tests. Despite these successes, password systems remain cognitively demanding [36, 37], and empirical stud-ies with adults with DS report longer task times, higher cognitive load, and difficulty with multi-step interactions [60, 64]. As de-scribed in Section 2.1, these patterns are consistent with differences in processing speed, working memory, and attentional control in IDD populations. ## 3 Method 

This study examined how adults with IDD experience and interact with everyday S&P notifications. We conducted a formative user study with seven adults with IDD, who encountered realistic notifi-cations integrated with semi-structured interviews. Because S&P notifications often appear as secondary tasks, each study task was designed around a primary activity ( e.g., checking email, brows-ing a social media website, using a mobile app) with embedded notifications that requested a decision or response. This let us cap-ture natural reactions to notifications without making security the explicit focus of the task. In this section, we explain (1) how we selected security and privacy notifications to evaluate; (2) partici-pant demographics; (3) outline the study protocol; (4) our inclusive and ethical study design choices; and (5) the data collection and analysis procedures. 

## 3.1 Selecting Notifications 

McCrickard et al. [69] define notification systems as interfaces “at-tempting to deliver current, valued information through a variety of platforms and modes” such as operating systems, browsers, and mobile applications. Security and privacy (S&P) notifications deliver security- or privacy-related information, and they often addition-ally require implicit or explicit decision-making [70]. Privacy noti-fications include cookie consent banners, advertising or tracking notices, and data-sharing requests. Security notifications include browser warnings ( e.g., when using HTTP instead of HTTPS), un-familiar sign-in alerts, and software update reminders. To ensure users encountered a diverse set of S&P notifications, we selected multiple from the “interruption” categories identified by Addas and Pinsonneault [2]. 1 Their taxonomy has two dimensions: 

Content relevance describes whether the notification is relevant to the user’s primary task ( intervention ) or interrupts the primary task with other information ( intrusion ). Content structure describes whether a notification is informational or actionable . In this context, we take actionable to mean it requires the user to take security- or privacy-related action to proceed with their task ( e.g., approving a login request). ## 3.3 Study Protocol 

This qualitative study integrated semi-structured interviews and behavioral analysis from usability testing to explore how adults with IDD experience and interpret S&P notifications. Regardless of the presence of support people, participants with IDD were encouraged to be the primary decision-makers and to complete tasks as independently as possible. The Institutional Review Board at the authors’ university approved the study. The study consisted of four main phases: (1) online or in-person screening and consent ; (2) semi-structured interview to assess general technology and computer security experience ; (3) usability testing through three tasks using a lab-provided computer and smartphone ;and (4) post-study interview and payment . The initial screening included eligibility questions, rapport-building icebreakers, and a walkthrough of an accessible consent form segmented into short sections with supportive visuals. Once consent was obtained, partic-ipants scheduled an in-person study session. Screening and consent took 15-30 minutes, and the in-person study lasted 1-1.25 hours. If participants invited support persons and they were present, they were asked to complete a separate consent process. Study sessions were held at the authors’ university or in alternative accessible lo-cations ( e.g., community centers, libraries) at participants’ request. Breaks were offered at any time. At the conclusion of all tasks, a short post-study interview was conducted to reflect on the tasks, interface designs, and broader preferences for online safety tools. Participants were compensated $20 an hour for their time. Study materials are available on OSF. 2

3.3.1 Task Design. Participants completed three tasks using a smart-phone and a computer provided for the duration of the study. De-pending on the participant’s preferred operating system, they used an iPhone 7 (iOS 15) or a Samsung Galaxy S20 (Android 13), and a 2020 MacBook Air (macOS Monterey) or a Lenovo ThinkPad T490 (Windows 11). Participants accessed the computer with an external monitor, keyboard, and mouse, and interviewers adjusted the text

A cookie consent prompt (CC) appeared on entry, and users logged in using a saved username and password. The interviewers then prompted participants to differentiate between the two emails and explain how they identified potential risks. 

Task 3: Weather App Permissions (Smartphone). In this task, par-ticipants used AccuWeather [1] to identify the current temperature. Upon opening the app, which had been freshly reinstalled before each study, typical mobile notifications were triggered, such as terms of service agreements and permission request pop-ups (TP, LP, and NP). The researchers had additionally initiated screen and audio recording in the background, which caused indicators (SRI, MUI; typically small dots in the top corner of the screen) to appear. 

## 3.4 Inclusive Methods and Ethical Study Design 

3.4.1 Inclusive Methods. We incorporated a range of practices drawn from prior literature on inclusive study environments [57]. Participants could include a support person for comfort, though we directed questions only to the participant with IDD to preserve their role as primary decision-maker. Study materials were personalized to participants’ interests based on the icebreakers: inbox messages came from familiar organizations, Pinterest-style images matched their hobbies, and participants chose their preferred device (Mac vs. Windows, iPhone vs. Android). When participants showed avoidant behaviors, researchers offered guiding questions (“What do you think?”), reassurance (nods, smiles), or hints (“You could open the email and look if you want.”), always letting participants act first and using prompts to encourage exploration rather than signal a “correct” choice. Visual aids were used to support comprehension and reflection, including printed copies of each notification and an adapted consent form split into short, illustrated sections. Partici-pants could also choose their study location ( e.g., our university or a local library), and breaks were offered between tasks. 3.4.2 Ethical Study Design Choices. This study exposed users to simulated cybersecurity risks ( e.g., phishing emails, malicious web-sites) and prompted them to make choices when faced with such risks. Moreover, individuals with IDD are often considered at-risk users because unmet accessibility needs can increase their vulnera-bility to cyberattacks or result in disproportionate harm [8, 66, 105]. Although participants did not use their personal devices, accounts, or information for the tasks, we took additional steps to prevent exposure to real malicious content, emotional distress, and negative training effects [8, 26]. While we used genuine marketing emails from companies and organizations in Task 1, we blocked naviga-tion from these emails to external links. We designed the phishing email to trigger Gmail’s spam filter through known signatures [25] without malicious content. Similarly, Chrome’s website warnings in Task 2, while authentic, were triggered by benign security-testing websites. 3

Only one participant made an explicitly unsafe choice by clicking a link in the simulated phishing email. To avoid negative training effects, the interviewers reviewed the participant’s choice with their support person. They had a post-task follow-up conversation about how to mitigate this risk going forward. 

## 3.5 Data Collection and Analysis 

3.5.1 Data Collection. Data were collected through a combination of audio and video recording, researcher observations, eye-tracking, and system logging, supported by Hollomotz [44]. Semi-structured interviews were conducted before, between, and after tasks to cap-ture participants’ perspectives on technology use, experiences with S&P in daily life, their understanding of notification purposes, and the perceived importance of different cues. These interviews also explored the role of interdependence [9] in online safety. Field notes, taken primarily by the second author, supplemented inter-views and recordings by documenting participant behaviors, navi-gation choices, and task sequences. Researchers additionally noted the amount of assistance required and visible signs of confidence,

3.5.2 Data Analysis. We employed Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) [12, 13] to explore how adults with IDD interact with S&P notifications. RTA supports inductive pattern-finding that moves from semantic (explicit, observable) to latent (implicit, underly-ing) meaning, treating the researcher’s interpretation as central to theme development [13]. Our process followed five stages: (1) conducting the study and collecting data; (2) transcribing record-ings and assembling composite videos that synchronized room footage, participant audio, and screen recordings with click and eye-tracking visualizations when available; (3) one researcher com-pleting an initial round of semantic coding of participant behaviors (e.g., interaction sequences, verbal statements, eye-tracking, etc. )followed by a second researcher coding random subsets of each participant’s transcript and composite video to develop shared an-alytic familiarity with the dataset; (4) jointly developing initial themes through reflexive engagement with qualitative codes, field notes, and researcher memos recorded throughout data collection and analysis; and (5) repeating coding and theme development in two subsequent iterations. Researchers coded transcripts alongside composite recordings, allowing behavioral observations to supple-ment or occasionally contradict participant self-reports. Click and eye-tracking data were used as qualitative rather than quantitative evidence since they were not uniformly available (see Section 3.6). Theme development drew on latent patterns inferred from behav-ioral sequences, verbal reasoning, and the researcher’s observations. Across three collaborative sessions, the two researchers developed and refined emerging themes. The analysis process (steps 2–5) took roughly 50 hours of work across both researchers. ## 3.6 Limitations 

This study’s small sample (seven participants) and narrow diagnos-tic range (five primary diagnoses of DS, one IDD, one ASD; see Section 3.2) only partially covers the broader community [5, 76]. Small samples are common in qualitative research with underrep-resented groups [59], but limit generalizability; future work should pursue a more diagnostically diverse population. Five aspects of our data collection may have shaped the behav-iors and reports we captured. (1) Eye-tracking calibration succeeded for only five of seven participants (three with high accuracy) due to vision challenges and attention difficulties, limiting our ability to verify gaze; we therefore used gaze data qualitatively to contex-tualize attention patterns rather than quantitatively. (2) We primed participants about the S&P focus. We used researcher-provided accounts and devices for procedural and ethical reasons (Section 3.4.2), unlike S&P usability studies that use deception or personal devices for realism [95]. This may have altered natural interac-tions, personal stakes, and help-seeking tendencies; to mitigate this, we matched each participant’s preferred operating system and customized study tasks (see Section 3.4.1). (3) We asked partici-pants whether they were already familiar with the notifications ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal Johnson et al. 

encountered; self-reported data is helpful but sometimes unreliable, and given participants’ considerable technology experience (see Table 4), retrospective recall may be a limitation here. (4) We con-sidered only visual notifications on phones, tablets, and laptops; future research should explore accessibility requirements for other modalities, such as auditory or haptic cues. (5) Researchers offered gentle prompts during moments of avoidance, which may have reduced ecological validity by encouraging persistence with tasks participants might otherwise have abandoned. Our analysis also does not fully trace evidence to its source within the participant–support person dyad: although prompts targeted participants with IDD (Section 3.5.1), support persons sometimes volunteered comments or confirmed routines that we incorporated alongside participant and researcher observations. Since our coding did not consistently attribute evidence by source, findings on interdependence, help-seeking, and household practices may reflect a mix of participant, researcher, and caregiver perspec-tives. The study also was not designed to examine dyadic or power dynamics, so we cannot assess how support persons influenced participants’ stated preferences or hesitancy; future work should attribute evidence by source and examine these dynamics.

## 4.1 Technology and Security Experience 

4.1.1 Technology Experience. All seven participants were active technology users with experience with multiple types of devices, shown in Table 4. Tablets were widely used for entertainment, such as YouTube, streaming, and gaming (P3–P7), with some also relying on them for video calls via FaceTime or Zoom (P3, P4). Smartphones were equally common (P1–P5, P7), supporting messaging (P1, P2, P4, P5, P7), social media (P1, P2, P4, P7), work coordination (P2), and mobile banking (P1, P2, P7). Only P7 reported active use of Pinterest. All had experience with Apple smartphones, either their own or their family members’. Four participants routinely used a Windows (P1, P3) or Mac (P2, P4) computer, but it was rarely their primary device. P3 mainly used an iPad but occasionally accessed their mother’s computer for scrapbooking or job training, and P2 shared a Mac with their father. P4 had prior experience with a Chromebook but preferred their iPad. Technology use often involved family support, such as managing email (P3, P6) or online shopping (P4, P6). At the same time, participants independently used email and calendars to track birthdays, rehearsals, and community events (P1, P2, P4, P5, P7). Checking the weather online was a very popular activity among participants (P1–P5, P7), with none reporting active use of AccuWeather. 

4.1.2 Security Experience. Participants reported varied approaches to securing their devices and accounts. Device PINs were widely used on smartphones and tablets (P1–P7). Four paired this with biometric methods such as Apple’s FaceID or TouchID (P2: “ I like the face better because I don’t remember my passcode, ” P3, P5, P7). Account password practices showed differing levels of indepen-dence. P1 and P4 relied on password managers built into iCloud and browsers, while P3 and P7 preferred to remember passwords, occasionally writing them down. In contrast, P2 depended on their father to store and update all passwords, and P5 also described interdependence in managing theirs: “ I ask my mom and dad if I can get help [with passwords and account management], and she could, yeah, a lot, helps out. ”Experiences with account and device lockout were common, and issues were typically resolved with familial support. P1 had been locked out after forgetting passwords for shared family accounts, typically regaining access after calling SP1. P2 described an incident they believed was a Facebook “ hack, ” though it was an account lockout; their father helped reset the account. P3 relied on parents to remember updated passwords: “ He’s trying to use old passcode, and it wouldn’t work. So then he came up to find Dad ” (SP3). P4 frequently became locked out of their tablet due to PIN errors and would wait or ask their parents for help. In contrast, P7 reported how they would “ change my password, if I don’t remember it, ... or I call, the– whoever I need to call and they get me back in . ” Participants also described broader security awareness of online scams and fraud. P1 recounted a social engineering attempt on Instagram in which an impostor requested their password, which they refused to share: “ I was talking to someone that I know with a friend. Whoops, I’m sorry, it was not my friend. There was a friend from college, but it turns out it’s not that person. [They messaged me] something like, ‘what’s your password?’ ” P2, P3, and P7 reported receiving scam emails and texts, which they deleted immediately if not from known contacts. For finances, P1 described freezing or locking their debit card if it were misplaced, P7 described having to call customer service to cancel their debit card after seeing sus-picious charges, and P3 reviewed banking statements handled by their parents. 

## 4.2 Experiences, Challenges, and Interaction Decisions with S&P Notifications 

In this section, we answer RQ: How do adults with IDD experience S&P notifications, what challenges do they face, and what factors influence their understanding and decision-making? Participants encountered a range of S&P notifications embedded in everyday tasks, providing insight into their decision-making processes. Chal-lenges arose less from isolated design features than from the ways notification content, context, and task goals combined to shape understanding and choice. Three themes capture these challenges and the factors influencing decision-making: (1) notification was interpreted through the ongoing task and interface context; (2) everyday concepts provided anchors for interpreting unfamiliar terminology; and (3) exploratory and avoidant actions emerged when participants were unsure of the action outcomes. 4.2.2 Theme 2: Everyday Concepts Provided Anchors for Under-standing and Action. When S&P notification semantics or semiotics resembled something participants already understood, those fa-miliar associations framed their interpretation of the notifications. For example, cookies (CC) were read through the food concept re-gardless of the banner’s actual content ( ex., Table 5). The cross-app tracking permission request (TP) and notification requests (NP) were interpreted through everyday forms of social sharing (P1, P2, P5) with P2 grounding terminology, “across other companies,” in their family’s real Spotify-sharing practice, extending the idea to shared devices and even “your next-door neighbor.” 

Heuristics learned from family or work shaped how participants assessed S&P notifications (P1–P7). P1 consistently said the color made no difference to him and looked to the sender or email content instead. Existing accessibility and usable security guidance emphasizes avoiding jargon and simplifying wording [19, 20, 38, 93, 109]. Our findings extend this work by showing that participants grounded unfamiliar language in experience-based anchors. These anchors helped participants reason about unfamiliar security concepts, even when the analogy was misleading ( e.g., TP is not related to your personal location; interacting with DD will not “block you out, the entire computer... open up different viruses” (P2), etc. ).

## 5 Discussion 

While designing and conducting the study, additional tensions and accessibility challenges within S&P notifications became clear, be-yond our three themes. First, participants often expressed a desire for safety ( e.g., “I’m trying not to get hacked ”), but this often produced cautious inaction or exploratory clicking rather than the intended secure action. Most avoided perceived unsafe actions by dismissing or ignoring a notifi-cation, with one exception: P6, who clicked through to a simulated phishing link. However, this caution is not uniformly protective. For new sign-in alerts (NSI), where the secure response requires acting rather than avoiding, the same hesitation could leave participants exposed, echoing prior work showing inaction toward warnings can itself undermine security [14]. That work attributes inaction to habituation or warning fatigue [15, 83]. Our findings suggest a related but distinct pathway for participants with IDD. Avoidance may be rooted not only in fatigue, but in genuine uncertainty about a notification’s meaning or an action’s outcome. This distinction may call for different interventions: novelty or interruption for fatigue, and decision support for uncertainty. Our findings expose potential limitations in how existing acces-sibility standards, such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) [109], address S&P notifications. WCAG emphasizes that interfaces should be perceivable, operable, and understandable, but we observed breakdowns in all three. Subtle recording indicators were imperceptible, hyperlinks with actions like “Report this” were overlooked (operability), and abstract terms such as “verification” compromised understandability. Because notifications are brief, secondary, and time-sensitive, they may require tailored accessi-bility principles distinct from the large-scale systems WCAG was designed around. As prior accessible web content design has shown [52], linguistic simplicity alone does not guarantee comprehension. Our findings point to the value of situated semantic design, where meaning stays consistent across shifting visual and task contexts. We also observed patterns consistent with existing IDD accessibility guidance – large, clearly labeled elements and minimal interactive choices [19] – but participants still struggled when notifications failed to separate from the ongoing task. Although animations are often recommended [94], the abstract 2FA animation was frequently ignored, suggesting animations may need to be realistic and action-oriented. Future research could investigate whether audio or haptic options would be beneficial as well. S&P notifications, though considered together here, often have competing design goals. Most security notifications nudged users to-ward protective choices, whereas many privacy notifications serve a business interest in permissive settings by exploiting cognitive biases or misconceptions [75]. Users often accept cookie banners believing this is required for the site to function [98]; two partic-ipants feared denying cookies would log them out or break the site, citing notification phrasing that reinforced the misread. Yet even in these privacy contexts, accessibility and commercial in-centives are not entirely irreconcilable. As accessibility becomes a regulatory requirement ( e.g., WCAG 2.2, EU Digital Services Act, Section 508), compliance provides a rationale for clearer designs, and strengthening consumer trust and retention may offset short-term data losses. While accessibility cannot resolve the incentives for dark patterns [17], it can limit their most harmful forms and set a normative baseline for transparency and accountability. 

## 5.1 Design Implications 

By examining participants’ interactions with a range of S&P notifi-cations (Appendix C and B) and identifying challenges and factors that influenced their engagement (Section 4.2), we derived three design implications for more inclusive security and privacy notifi-cations (Table 6). 

5.1.1 Implication #1: Address Context-Dependent Language Misunderstandings Beyond Jargon Simplification . Security and privacy notifications should go beyond avoiding technical jargon to account for how context shapes meaning. Participants’ misunder-standings, such as equating “cookies” with food or interpreting “deny” as “log out,” did not arise purely from vocabulary deficits, but from contextual blending between task, prior experience, and interface, described in Themes 1 and 2. Existing guidance empha-sizes simplifying language [6, 19, 109], but our findings suggest this alone may be insufficient. Designers should therefore treat clarity as context calibration by explicitly identifying what is being acted upon (“your phone’s location,” “this laptop”) and using progres-sive disclosure when additional clarification is needed. Visual and multimodal cues could further reinforce this meaning by distin-guishing S&P notifications notifications from surrounding interface content ( e.g., a temporary boundary highlight or audio cue could distinguish notifications from surrounding interface content). By communicating meaning through language, visuals, and interac-tion, notifications may reduce context-driven misunderstandings that simplified language alone cannot resolve. 

5.1.2 Implication #2: Make Action-Outcome Connections Transparent . Notifications should clearly connect the security ac-tions a user can take to the actions’ outcomes. Participants’ uncer-tainty revealed that action verbs alone ( e.g., “Secure your account” or “Learn more”) do not foster confidence when outcomes are opaque. This need emerged across Themes 1 and 3 (Table 6), where uncertainty about action outcomes led participants to hesitate, avoid acting, or seek help. Existing guidance recommends concise, command-style options [6], while prior work on notification design for cognitively diverse users emphasizes timing, modality, and sit-uational framing to support comprehension and trust [31, 47, 84]; ASSETS ’26, October 25–28, 2026, Vila Nova de Gaia, Portugal Johnson et al.

5.1.3 Implication #3: Enable Interdependent Decision-Making .

S&P mechanisms should be designed to allow users to rely on trusted others and to alleviate time and uncertainty pressures. Adoption of S&P features and responses to security concerns are deeply social and interpersonal experiences for many users [22, 70, 80, 82]. This became clear during our study as well. Participants already had established ways of handling uncertainty by turning to a trusted person, whether through a standing household practice (Theme 2 and Section 4.1) or a request for help in the moment (Theme 3). Participants often drew on heuristics learned from caregivers or trusted individuals ( e.g., spam = scam, never open marketing emails), paused to ask what their support person would do, or sought ex-plicit affirmation before performing an action. This is consistent with prior work on how adults with IDD make S&P decisions with caregivers [18, 19, 87, 94, 101] Crucially, in our study, participants flipped this dynamic, routinely helping their parents or peers with tech-related tasks (P1–P4). We view this phenomenon through the frame of interdependence for assistive technology (AT) intro-duced by Bennett et al., which posits that “independence” is not 

the only form of autonomy for users with disabilities, and that giving/seeking support are important expressions of agency. Partic-ipants often described working with others when navigating S&P decisions, particularly around authentication and consent (P1–P6). For example, when asked what they would do if locked out of an account, P1 replied, ‘call for assistance... family, of course... we all need to rely on someone.” One participant (P7) more often described resolving authentication challenges independently ( e.g., “I call the [customer service]– whoever I need to call and they get me back in.” ), suggesting that interdependent security practices were common, but not universal, among our participants. We argue that S&P notifications can and should flexibly support interdependence when users are faced with decisions ( e.g., whether a button is safe to click, which privacy settings make sense for them). This does not involve taking away control from the user, but rather reducing friction, confusion, and stigma if and when users give/seek support. For example, time-sensitive notifications, such as 2FA, should clearly signal that exiting the flow is safe and the user can try again later after they have received support. Designing for such flexible stewardship ( e.g., asking the primary user to designate a collaborator during application setup, user-initiated controls, time-based access, etc. ) could balance independence with the reality of interdependent security practices. However, care must be taken in the design of such a multi-user system to avoid creating new avenues for technology abuse [48, 72, 78]. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We conducted a formative user study with seven adults with IDD to understand how they perceive and interact with common security and privacy notifications in email, social media, and mobile apps. Thematic analysis revealed that participants interpreted notifica-tions through the task and interface context, mapped both technical and non-technical terms to everyday concepts, and uncertainty about meaning and consequences drove hesitation, avoidance, and support-seeking. We present three design recommendations: (1) address context-dependent language misunderstandings beyond jargon simplification; (2) make action-outcome connections trans-parent; and (3) enable interdependent decision-making. We call on researchers and developers to adopt these principles to make security and privacy notifications accessible for adults with IDD.
