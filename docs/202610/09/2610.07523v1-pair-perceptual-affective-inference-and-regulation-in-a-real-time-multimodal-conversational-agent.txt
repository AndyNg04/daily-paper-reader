# Abstract 

Sustained emotional support requires generative agents to connect momentary emotion inference and regulation with continuity across encounters. We present PAIR (Perceptual Affective Inference and Regulation), a real-time multimodal agent that reconstructs how an event is appraised into an emotional state. Ap-praisal scaffolds produce a valence-arousal-dominance estimate and select regu-lation guidance, delivered through conversation with coordinated speech, color, and avatar cues. Rolling memory carries context across sessions, and the scaf-fold re-runs after guidance. In a 14-day deployment with 19 participants, 1,093 sessions paired initial and post-guidance estimates with unanchored self-reports. Initial valence reached MAE 1.20 on the 9-point SAM scale ( r=.68 ), dominance reached MAE 1.30, and arousal showed weak agreement even after coarsening. Self-reported emotional change varied with initial state, with the largest valence increases in sessions that began at negative valence. Perceived understanding was associated with greater valence increase and showed little correspondence with numerical prediction error. Over two weeks, helpfulness increased while input shortened; interviews traced personalization and companionship to relevant recall, context updates, and familiar dialogue. These findings connect inference accuracy to conversational and temporal patterns of support through per-event, first-person evaluation. 

# 1 Introduction 

Real-time conversational agents are increasingly deployed in emotionally charged settings such as companionship, coaching, and mental health support [7, 17, 21]. Support requires understanding what the user feels and why, selecting a response, and retaining relevant context. Few systems verify the interpretation behind that response against the user’s own experience. Connecting emotion recognition to supportive responses requires reasoning about how an event produces emotion and which response fits. Multimodal models recognize emotion from acoustic, visual, and textual signals [3], benchmarks probe the emotion competence of LLMs [10, 27], and empathetic generators produce supportive replies across modalities [6, 33]. Offline benchmarks, third-party judgments, and simulated users [10, 28, 31] characterize model responses; everyday support additionally requires first-person evaluation across conversations. We present PAIR (Perceptual Affective Inference and Regulation), a real-time multimodal conver-sational agent that reconstructs how emotion forms in response to a specific event. A lightweight router dispatches each narration to one of two appraisal-theoretic reasoning scaffolds [13, 25], se-lected through formative human evaluation. RQ2 (Regulation Outcomes): How do initial emotional state, regulation strategy, and guidance fit relate to momentary regulation and guidance uptake? 3. RQ3 (Long-Term Companion): How do conversational familiarity, cross-session memory, and accumulated personal context shape personalization and companionship across a 14-day deploy-ment? Our contributions are 1) an integrated architecture linking appraisal reasoning, contextual regulation, and multimodal delivery; 2) an in-situ protocol pairing initial and post-guidance estimates with first-person reports across everyday encounters; and 3) findings on dimension-specific inference accuracy, state-dependent emotional change, and the conversational and memory processes shaping support over two weeks. 

# 2 Related Work 

Emotion reasoning in conversational agents. Emotion reasoning research connects affec-tive judgments to multimodal context, explicit appraisal, and personal information. Emotion-LLaMA integrates multimodal recognition and reasoning [3], while EmotionBench, EmoBench, and CAREBench examine how model judgments align with human affective assessments [10, 24, 27]. Explicit appraisal dimensions ground generated cognitive reappraisals [32]. PersonaAgent further personalizes agents through test-time persona adaptation [34]. PAIR uses appraisal output for strat-egy selection and accumulated feedback for subsequent context. 

Multimodal generation for conversational agents. Empathetic generation extends emotional expression across text, speech, and avatars. EmpathyEar generates avatar, speech, and text re-sponses [6]; AvaMERG studies avatar-based empathetic generation [33]; and WeSCon provides 2Figure 2: Prompt evaluation pipeline. (a) Four scaffolds and (b) 30 persona–event scenarios enter (c) blind pairwise tournaments with (d) three rating dimensions, (e) yielding COG and COMBINED for routing. word-level emotional control of speech [30]. PAIR coordinates these expressive channels through the emotional direction targeted by regulation. 

Evaluation of live systems. Participant annotations and field deployments extend evaluation to experienced support within and across conversations. AttuneBench collects participant annotations across genuine dialogues [16], and HEART compares human and LLM responses in support con-versations [11]. Field deployments include a seven-user health coach [22], MindScape’s eight-week journaling study with 20 students [21], and a four-week study of affective chatbot use with 981 participants [5]. Our evaluation connects event-level estimates and self-reports to guidance, person-alization, and companionship across encounters. 

# 3 System Architecture 

PAIR links emotion inference to regulation guidance and multimodal delivery (Figure 1). Claude Opus 4.8 performs emotion reasoning, while GPT-5-mini handles routing and memory summariza-tion. Both typed narration and browser-transcribed speech are processed as text for inference. 

3.1 The Emotion Inference Path Scaffold design and event-adapted routing. A blind comparison of four emotion-prediction prompts identified complementary scaffolds for single- and multi-component events (Figure 2). The tournament used 30 persona–event scenarios, three trained raters, and 1,080 pairwise judg-ments (Appendix A). The cognitive-appraisal scaffold ( COG ) follows a five-stage appraisal chain [13, 25]. COMBINED extends this scaffold with bottom-up neural reasoning for events involving interacting bodily and cognitive components [14, 23]. The raters compared anonymized outputs from the same model on outcome plausibility, reasoning quality, and overall preference. Separately, two researchers coded each scenario as single- or multi-component without consulting the rankings. COG and COMBINED each won 15 scenarios, and event structure matched the preferred scaffold in 26 of 30 scenarios (87%). The router therefore directs single-component events to COG and multi-component events to COMBINED. 

Prediction output and per-user grounding. Each prediction is grounded in the user’s onboarding persona, a calibration event paired with self-reported SAM ratings, and rolling memory from prior encounters. Both scaffolds output continuous PAD estimates, predicted SAM ratings on the user’s 1–9 scale, emotion labels and intensity, controllability, and temporal phase. After each evening session, a summarizer updates this memory by compressing recurring themes, prediction discrepan-cies, recent corrections, regulation outcomes, and the previous four days of context into at most 150 words (Appendix B). 

3.2 The Regulation Path Strategy selection and guided conversation. The appraisal call selects among four regulation strategies: situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modula-tion [8]. Strategy selection considers emotional intensity, which guides the balance between imme-3diate relief and reappraisal [26], and controllability, which guides whether to modify the situation or its interpretation [9, 29]. Temporal phase, touchpoint, and personal context further refine the selec-tion (Appendix C). The system then generates an internal plan specifying the regulatory move and a concrete first step. The conversation follows an Exploration–Comforting–Action (ECA) arc [15], first eliciting context, then acknowledging the participant’s emotion, and finally introducing guid-ance within a ten-turn budget. 

Post-guidance estimation. After conversation, the selected appraisal scaffold runs again using the original narration, initial estimate, and conversation transcript to estimate the participant’s post-guidance state. This estimate is evaluated against the participant’s post-guidance SAM report. 

3.3 Coordinated Multimodal Delivery 

A shared regulation target coordinates speech prosody, ambient color, and avatar pulse to convey the emotional direction of guidance. GPT-4o-mini-TTS renders speech using directions for pace, energy, pitch, and pauses; ambient hue follows emotion–color associations, with brightness and sat-uration varying with target valence and arousal; and an avatar pulse slows for lower-arousal targets. A lightweight expression selector also reads the accumulating transcript and updates the avatar’s face during interaction. Users can toggle speech output. 

# 4 Evaluation: 14-Day Field Deployment 

Participants and protocol. We recruited 24 U.S. adults through university and social-media chan-nels; five withdrew and 19 completed the study (13 women, 6 men; age M =25 .1, SD =5 .1, range 18–33). High AI familiarity was reported by 68%, and prior emotional-support use by 42%. Eligible participants were not receiving psychological or psychiatric treatment. The study received univer-sity IRB approval, and compensation was up to $80, prorated by completion. Consent explained the system’s non-clinical role and provided mental-health resources. Each session paired narration and guided conversation with before/after SAM reports, four feedback items, and an understanding check. Day-0 onboarding covered consent, surveys, a persona interview, and calibration. Over 14 days, participants completed morning check-ins, self-initiated just-in-time logs, and evening reflections. Numerical estimates were withheld; understanding ratings at or below 4 prompted corrections. Alternating evenings included a rotating probe. All completers answered a 13-item exit questionnaire and a semi-structured interview. 

Measures and analysis. We evaluated inference, regulation, and cross-session experience by com-bining paired SAM reports, logs, and feedback from 1,093 completed post-onboarding sessions with interviews from all 19 completers. Accuracy measures were MAE, Pearson r, signed error, and coarse-grained Cohen’s κ. Initial valence was also compared with an unscaffolded same-model baseline and a five-fold cross-validated NRC-VAD lexicon estimator [19]. SAM change was sum-marized overall and by initial emotional state. Wilcoxon tests used participant mean changes, with Holm correction across the three dimensions; strategy comparisons used participant-level Friedman tests with permutation inference. Understanding correlations used whole-participant bootstrap in-tervals, and correction contrasts used paired participant means. Mixed-effects models estimated day and week contrasts, with false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction across ten measures per model. Strategy-feedback models controlled participant and participant-day clustering. Two researchers independently coded interviews and corrections and reconciled differences; 44 adoption-probe clas-sifications received manual review after LLM-assisted coding.

Just-in-time logs accounted for 601 sessions (55.0%), morning check-ins for 258 (23.6%), and evening reflections for 234 (21.4%). 5.2 RQ1: Appraisal Inference Recovers Valence More Closely Than Arousal 5.2.1 Appraisal-Guided Estimates Show Stronger Valence and Dominance Agreement 

Initial estimates tracked self-reported valence and dominance more closely than arousal, while par-ticipants valued interpretations that connected feelings to their causes. Valence reached r=.681 

(MAE 1.196) and dominance r=.538 (MAE 1.299; Tab. 1). In the separate valence comparison, MAE fell from 1.679 for NRC-VAD to 1.334 for the unscaffolded model and 1.196 with appraisal. The largest descriptive gain came from the model’s contextual reading; the scaffold additionally sup-plied the intensity and controllability used for regulation. Fourteen participants described cases in which the companion inferred feelings or their causes without explicit emotion words. P1 valued its ability to go “straight from my description to dissecting what was going on inside me,” and P10 val-ued identifying “what was responsible” for an emotion she could not name. Exit ratings were high-est for understanding feelings in the moment ( M =5 .63 , SD =.68 ), followed by post-conversation understanding (5.37, .90) and emotional nuance (5.21, .79). 

5.2.2 Text-Only Estimates Show Weak Arousal Agreement and Intensity Mismatches 

PAIR’s arousal estimates showed weak agreement with self-report, and participants described emo-tional direction being recognized while intensity was misjudged. Speech input was transcribed before inference, leaving prosody unavailable alongside facial and bodily cues. Its arousal estimates therefore reflected what participants said in the transcript without information from how they said it. Arousal correlated weakly with self-report ( r=.236 ) and was underestimated after guidance by 1.097 points on average. Coarsening to low/high ratings did little to resolve the mismatch ( κ=.154 ;Appendix F); no tested arousal cut point exceeded .21 , and NRC-VAD likewise showed little corre-spondence with self-report ( r=.016 ). Eight participants described intensity mismatches. P12 found her emotions taken “way too extreme,” while P3 distinguished venting from actually being angry. Participants sometimes compensated by softening wording (P12) or explicitly stating intensity (P18), linking this input boundary to how they communicated with the companion.

5.3.2 Contextual Exploration and Acknowledgment Shape Perceived Understanding 

Participants described feeling understood when the conversation clarified their situation and ac-knowledged their emotion before offering advice. Fourteen participants valued clarification and follow-up questions. P3 reported a good grasp after “going back and forth three, four times,” and P16 described a clarification question followed by an attempt to empathize. These exchanges gave participants room to refine the interpretation before guidance. Questions could also miss the is-sue, as P8 found they were not on “the right track.” Per-session understanding averaged 5.45 of 7 ( SD =.81 ) and showed little correspondence with numerical prediction error. Participants never saw the numerical estimates; their judgments concerned the understanding conveyed through con-versation. Corrections likewise extended beyond emotion labels to context, memory, and the desired support, with P2 correcting “the wrong suggestion.” Perceived understanding was positively associ-ated with valence gain ( r=.134 ), and corrected sessions showed lower mean gains than uncorrected sessions ( −.273 versus +.559 ; paired Wilcoxon p=.005 ). 

5.3.3 Contextual Strategy Selection Yielded Comparable Immediate Outcomes 

Selected strategies produced distinct regulatory actions with no detectable difference in participant-level valence gains (Friedman χ2(3)= .613 , permutation p=.897 ). Immediate feedback was also similar across families (Tab. 6; all pFDR =.395 ). Participants nevertheless recognized different moves, including starting with the easiest part of a task (situation modification, P1), reconsider-ing self-blame against completed work (cognitive change, P19), taking a snack break (attentional deployment, P12), and breathing before an exam (response modulation, P16). Six participants also described a recurring pool of suggestions, making contextual fit a continuing requirement across strategy families. 

5.3.4 Practical Feasibility and Support Needs Shape Guidance Uptake 

Adoption depended on available resources and the support wanted at that moment. Of 44 manually reviewed responses, 24 reported trying guidance with a positive result, five described partial or alternative enactment or mixed outcomes, and four reported actions already intended. 5.4 RQ3: Cross-Session Continuity Shapes Long-Term Personalization and Companionship 5.4.1 Conversational Familiarity Changes Disclosure and Support Expectations 

Participants continued using PAIR at a similar frequency while writing less and rating helpfulness more highly (Tab. 2). Narration length and user characters per turn decreased by 3.37% and 4.30% per day; helpfulness increased by .029 points per day. Unadjusted helpfulness rose from 4.94 in Week 1 to 5.14 in Week 2, with a model-estimated week contrast of +.216 (pFDR =.012 ). Ses-sion frequency, understanding, warmth, feasibility, and willingness showed no reliable day trends after correction. Nine participants described adapting their wording or conversational approach. P5 compressed openings to “use as few words as possible” while retaining context; P8 shortened openings to wait for follow-up questions; and P18 learned to state emotion explicitly. Shorter input also reflected withdrawal, as P16 shared less after repetitive advice. Three participants connected changing evaluations to their own goals, familiarity, or interaction skills. P5 increasingly sought practical suggestions, P8 grew more accepting of familiar responses, and P12 could not separate system improvement from improved interaction on her part. Shorter input thus encompassed both strategic adaptation and withdrawal. 

5.4.2 Relevant Recall and Persistent Corrections Shape Perceived Personalization 

Participants associated personalization with relevant memories, incorporated developments, and per-sistent corrections. Interviews and perceived-learning ratings captured this experience of personal-ization, while absolute valence error showed no significant decline across days ( pFDR =.087 ; Tab. 2). Perceived learning over time averaged 5.37 of 7 ( SD =1 .01 ), while personalized emotion interpreta-tions averaged 4.79 ( SD =1 .40 ). Ten participants described personalized responses, while fourteen also encountered generic ones. Recall became meaningful when it connected earlier experiences to later developments. P19 described the companion remembering a new after-work café routine and recognizing it as “a very good change.” P1 acknowledged retained information but wanted more than “a fairly linear answer.” Continuity weakened when context was missing or outdated. Twelve participants reported forgetting, and six reported temporal or contextual confusion. P10 had to “list it all over again” after an earlier same-day conversation; P2 encountered an event from three days earlier treated as current; and P15 found occasional onboarding activities turned into recurring as-sumptions. Six participants reported incorporated feedback, while nine described corrections that did not persist or eventually stopped correcting. P12 observed calmer language after correcting exaggerated intensity, whereas P4 saw an immediate clarification fail to carry forward. 75.4.3 Accumulated Records Enable Reflection and Regular Dialogue Builds Companionship 

Accumulated records provided material for reflection, while daily exchanges gave some participants a sense of companionship. Five participants valued logs for revisiting and organizing experiences, including people who did not ordinarily keep diaries. P15 described an “interactive diary,” com-bining a record with responses. Nine participants valued an available listener with fewer obligations to reciprocate support or manage another person’s reactions. P7 emphasized that it “will always answer you,” while P11 valued sharing ordinary updates without burdening someone else. Four par-ticipants described the companion as a friend, often linking this feeling to daily contact. The avatar supplied a visible counterpart (P2, P18), and familiar exchanges became something to anticipate. P10 reported, “On day 15, I actually missed talking to it.” Others described a listener or acquain-tance, sought people for more serious concerns or broader involvement (P3, P4), or disclosed private material to the companion that they would hesitate to share with others (P11, P12, P19). 

# 6 Discussion & Conclusion 

6.1 Ethical Considerations 

Our study raises four ethical considerations for emotional support companions. (1) Clinical bound-aries. Everyday emotional support should remain clearly separated from treatment and crisis re-sponse, given documented failures of LLMs in clinically sensitive situations [20]. Participants were informed of PAIR’s non-clinical role and provided mental-health resources, while higher-risk use requires dedicated safety mechanisms and access to human support. (2) Guidance quality and autonomy. Inaccurate emotion inference, misplaced reassurance, memory errors, and sycophantic responses can undermine support or reinforce questionable judgments [2]. Systems should preserve users’ ability to reject advice, correct interpretations, and express negative emotion without pressure toward positivity. (3) Relational attachment. Participants’ reluctance to end the study and reports of missing the companion illustrate the attachment risks documented for social chatbots [12]. Emo-tional companions should support deliberate closure, avoid exclusivity or guilt-based engagement, and reinforce existing human relationships. (4) Privacy and persistent memory. Intimate disclo-sure requires clear control over what is stored and retained, particularly when companion framing can obscure organizational data practices [4]. Systems should minimize stored information and sup-port transparent data use, memory inspection, correction, and deletion. We therefore withhold study conversations and diaries from public release. 

6.2 Limitations and Future Work 

Our single-group diary study deployment did not isolate the effects of guided conversation from natural emotional change, disclosure itself, regression to the mean, or other system components. Context-dependent strategy selection also prevents causal comparison across strategy families. Fu-ture studies should include unguided journaling, alternative selection policies, memory ablation, and text-only delivery as comparison conditions. Besides, self-reported affect provides a first-person reference but remains subject to individual differences in emotion awareness and scale use. The two-week deployment primarily captures early adaptation under scheduled check-ins and compen-sation. Longer studies with fewer required interactions are needed to assess sustained voluntary use, personalization, and attachment. Moreover, generalizability is also limited by the small, young, AI-familiar sample. Broader samples and longer-term outcome measures should test whether these findings extend across populations and persist over time. 

Conclusion. Across 1,093 sessions with 19 participants, PAIR connected appraisal-grounded emotion inference, regulation guidance, and post-guidance estimation. The fourteen-day deploy-ment showed dimension-specific inference accuracy and state-dependent emotional change, while participants linked their experience of support to conversational fit and updated memory. These findings frame sustained emotion regulation as a task of connecting momentary understanding with continuity across encounters. Per-event, first-person validation provides a practical basis for evalu-ating that connection in everyday use. 8References

# B Rolling Memory and Input Processing 

The 150-word memory block retains up to three recurring themes, the two most recent user cor-rections, adoption and outcome information for attempted regulation techniques, and one-line sum-maries of the previous four days. The block enters both inference and conversation calls. Spoken input is transcribed through the browser’s Web Speech API and can be edited before sub-mission. The study system stores transcripts rather than raw audio. Inference runs concurrently with the SAM interface, and the initial output is withheld until the participant submits the pre-guidance ratings. Post-guidance estimation uses the full transcript and runs in the background while the par-ticipant completes self-report and feedback.
