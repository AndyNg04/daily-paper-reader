# Can LLM-assisted regularization increase forecast accuracy for migration flows in low data regimes?

Abstract —Predicting migration flows remains a significant challenge for traditional gravity-based forecasting models, which primarily rely on structured socio-economic indicators such as economic disparity, political stability, and geographic distance. This work investigates whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can improve migration forecasting by extracting contextual migration-related signals from news articles and incorporating them into a weighted Lasso forecasting framework through feature-specific regularization penalties. The proposed frame-work uses hierarchical LLM inference pipelines to classify migration-related push–pull signals from news data and evaluates the resulting forecasting performance across multiple migration corridors between November 2021 and November 2022, including Mexico–United States, Ukraine–Poland, and Syria–Turkey. Ex-perimental results showed mixed performance across migration corridors and modeling strategies, and no single regulariza-tion approach consistently outperformed the others across all experiments. The best-performing Mexico configuration, which consisted of a gravity-based model augmented with the proposed push–pull ratios, achieved a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of 17.15%, while the strongest Syria configuration achieved a MAPE of 29.29% using Direct LLM-Lasso. For Ukraine, the best-performing configuration used LLM-Assisted Regularization (AR) and achieved a MAPE of 41.05%. Overall, the results suggest that contextual article-derived features and LLM-guided regularization can improve migration forecasting under certain conditions, although migration corridor character-istics, article volume, and hyperparameter configuration strongly influenced performance. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Human migration, particularly when conflict, economic instability, or environmental disruption drives it, presents a critical challenge for policymakers and humanitarian orga-nizations. Accurate prediction of migration flows enables governments and aid organizations to allocate resources, plan infrastructure, and intervene more effectively in regions expe-riencing sudden population displacement. Traditional migration forecasting approaches commonly rely on gravity-based modeling frameworks and structured socio-economic indicators such as economic disparity, ge-ographic distance, political stability, demographic structure, and existing migrant networks. Prior research has shown that economic opportunity, inequality, diaspora effects, and socio-political instability strongly influence migration flows, while also highlighting the broader complexity and uncertainty associated with migration forecasting itself [1]. Although gravity-based methods effectively model long-term migration relationships, they often fail to capture rapidly evolving real-world conditions reflected in unstructured information sources such as news media. Researchers have identified similar chal-lenges in broader socio-economic forecasting and nowcasting literature, where many structured indicators are reported only monthly, quarterly, or annually and are frequently released with substantial publication delays, limiting their usefulness for real-time monitoring and forecasting applications [2], [3]. These limitations have increased interest in high-frequency al-ternative data sources, including news media, social media, and other textual indicators, as supplementary contextual signals for forecasting and forced displacement modeling [4], [5]. Traditional migration forecasting also faces important data availability and temporal-resolution limitations. Many com-monly used socio-economic indicators, including GDP per capita, Human Development Index (HDI), Global Peace Index (GPI), life expectancy, and education-related measures, are updated only annually or infrequently despite migration dy-namics often evolving at monthly or even weekly timescales. In conflict-driven migration scenarios, instability, disrupted reporting infrastructure, and the difficulty of collecting reliable survey data in affected regions can delay, limit, or entirely prevent the availability of structured data. Prior work has noted that monitoring refugee displacement and return be-comes particularly difficult in war-prone environments where systematic migration data remains limited or difficult to obtain, motivating the use of social media and textual data sources as supplementary contextual indicators for migration monitoring and forecasting [6]. Migration forecasting additionally suffers from limited availability of directly observed migration-flow data. Prior work has noted that researchers often derive migration esti-mates from census transition data collected over long tem-poral intervals, resulting in sparse temporal availability and substantial missing information during intercensal periods [7]. These limitations become particularly problematic in rapidly evolving migration scenarios in which socio-economic and geopolitical conditions may change substantially within short timeframes. As a result, supplementary high-frequency con-textual information sources such as news media may provide useful signals for capturing migration-related dynamics that arXiv:2610.07208v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 structured datasets do not immediately reflect. This study evaluates forecasting performance across a shared monthly window spanning November 2021 through November 2022 in order to maintain consistent temporal coverage across all eval-uated migration corridors while capturing multiple migration conditions ranging from relatively stable migration behavior to conflict-driven volatility. The selected period additionally allowed evaluation during the emergence of the Ukraine con-flict while preserving comparable forecasting conditions for the Mexico–United States and Syria–Turkey corridors. Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and natural language processing systems have created new oppor-tunities for extracting structured information from unstructured textual data. Prior studies have demonstrated that textual signals derived from news media, social media, and large-scale open-source datasets can function as early-warning indicators for migration forecasting and asylum prediction [4], [5]. Prior work has additionally shown that LLMs can perform con-textual text classification, sentiment analysis, and multi-label inference tasks across diverse textual domains while requiring substantially less manual feature engineering than traditional NLP pipelines [8]. Rather than using LLMs as direct predictors, this work investigates whether contextual information extracted through LLM-assisted article classification can indirectly improve mi-gration forecasting through feature weighting and regulariza-tion. Specifically, the proposed framework analyzes migration-related news articles to identify push–pull migration signals and incorporates semantic feature-category evidence computed from processed article text into a weighted Lasso framework. The proposed framework combines structured gravity-model features with contextual information extracted from migration-related news articles in order to dynamically adjust feature importance based on evolving migration-related condi-tions. Rather than relying exclusively on static socio-economic indicators, the system incorporates contextual signals associ-ated with political instability, economic disruption, conflict, and migration-related narratives extracted from news media. Our main contributions are as follows:  

> •

We introduce an LLM-assisted regularization frame-work for migration forecasting that integrates structured gravity-model features with contextual information ex-tracted from news articles.  

> •

We evaluate multiple hierarchical LLM inference pipelines for extracting migration-related push–pull sig-nals while balancing contextual understanding and com-putational cost.  

> •

We compare direct LLM-generated feature penalties against a more stable count-based weighting approach de-rived from aggregated semantic feature-category evidence extracted through prompt-guided LLM interpretation of processed article corpora.  

> •

We evaluate the proposed framework across multiple migration corridors, including Mexico–United States, Ukraine–Poland, and Syria–Turkey, using sliding monthly forecasting experiments and MAPE evaluation. Our experimental results show that incorporating news-derived contextual signals can improve predictive performance in certain migration corridors, particularly when sufficient article coverage exists. Across several experiments, gravity models augmented with push–pull ratio features consistently outperformed direct LLM-generated penalties, while count-based LLM-assisted regularization produced more stable be-havior than direct LLM-Lasso approaches. Results further sug-gest that the usefulness of article-derived weighting depends strongly on both article quantity and the quality of extracted signals. II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

We review the three most closely related body of works, namely: traditional gravity-based models for migration flows, the use of LLMs for L1-regularization, and cross-modality models that make use of news text for predicting migration flows. 

A. Gravity Models of Migration 

Migration forecasting has traditionally relied on gravity-based modeling frameworks that estimate population move-ment between origin and destination regions using struc-tured socio-economic and geographic variables [9]. Inspired by Newtonian gravity models, migration flow is generally assumed to increase with the “mass” of origin and desti-nation populations while decreasing as geographic, political, or economic barriers increase. Due to their interpretability, theoretical grounding, and flexibility in incorporating diverse structural indicators, gravity-based models have been widely used in international migration forecasting [9]. C. Incorporating News Data into Migration Forecasting 

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and natural language processing systems have significantly ex-panded the capabilities of automated text classification and contextual reasoning systems. Prior work has demonstrated that LLMs can effectively perform sentiment analysis, multi-label classification, contextual categorization, and reasoning tasks across diverse textual domains while requiring sub-stantially less manual preprocessing and feature engineer-ing than traditional NLP pipelines. Although the proposed framework shares similarities with retrieval-augmented gen-eration (RAG) systems, it does not implement a traditional embedding-based vector retrieval architecture. [8]. Recent studies have additionally explored the use of un-structured textual data and open-source information sources for migration forecasting and displacement modeling. Singh et al. [4] combined traditional migration indicators with signals derived from social media and newspaper data to model forced migration dynamics, demonstrating that open-source textual signals can improve predictive performance relative to tradi-tional variables alone. Similarly, Iliopoulos et al. [5] proposed a migration forecasting framework based on national press monitoring, topic modeling, and neural network architectures using GDELT and EUROSTAT data as early-warning indica-tors for asylum application forecasting in Europe. Additional migration forecasting work has further explored the integration of textual signals and event-driven information into predictive migration frameworks [14]. The usefulness of textual and multimodal contextual information has also been demonstrated in other forecasting domains. In transportation forecasting, prior studies have shown that integrating social media sig-nals and agency-generated textual feeds with historical traffic measurements can improve predictive performance [15], [16]. These developments suggest that LLMs may be well-suited for identifying migration-related contextual signals within news articles. Migration events are frequently associated with political instability, economic hardship, conflict, humanitar-ian crises, labor opportunities, and climate-related disruption, many of which are discussed qualitatively within news me-dia before becoming fully reflected in structured numerical datasets [4], [17]. Because LLMs are capable of contextual text classification, reasoning, and sentiment-oriented interpretation, they provide a potential mechanism for identifying whether migration-related narratives within articles correspond more closely to push-oriented, pull-oriented, mixed, or neutral mi-gration pressures. Motivated by these observations, the proposed framework utilizes LLMs to analyze migration-related news articles and extract migration-related push–pull signals from unstructured textual data. Rather than using LLMs as direct predictors of migration flow, the system employs them primarily as contextual inference mechanisms for article classification and signal extraction. In addition to push–pull classification, the framework computes aggregated semantic feature-category evidence using predefined feature descriptions and represen-tative migration-related indicators embedded within the LLM prompt. The framework subsequently incorporates these ex-tracted semantic signals into gravity-based forecasting models through push–pull ratio features and feature-specific regular-ization techniques. III. P USH -P ULL FEATURES FOR GRAVITATION MODELS AND LLM-A SSISTED REGULARIZATION 

This work proposes a hybrid framework that integrates structured gravity-based migration forecasting with contextual signals extracted from unstructured news data using LLMs (LLMs). The proposed system consists of two primary com-ponents: a hierarchical inference framework for extracting migration-related push–pull signals from news articles, and a weighted Lasso forecasting model in which feature penalties are informed by aggregated semantic feature-category evi-dence derived from the processed article corpus. Rather than relying on LLMs to directly predict migration flows or assign migration coefficients, the framework uses LLM-assisted article classification together with aggregated semantic feature-category evidence to guide feature regulariza-tion. The key idea is that news articles capture contextual in-formation about migration-related conditions occurring within a given time period. Prior work has shown that combining traditional migration indicators with open-source textual and media-derived signals can improve forced migration forecast-ing performance and provide useful early-warning indicators for displacement modeling [4]. By transforming aggregated semantic feature-category ev-idence into feature-specific penalty weights, the model can dynamically adjust the importance of predictors based on contextual signals extracted from migration-related news data. This formulation allows the framework to retain the inter-pretability of traditional gravity models while incorporating time-sensitive information derived from unstructured textual sources. 

A. Proposed features: push-pull count ratios 

The proposed model incorporates a set of socio-economic, demographic, and news-derived features to capture the primary drivers of migration flows. The model represents economic conditions using gross domestic product per capita (GDP) and gross national income per capita (GNI), capturing rel-ative wealth and economic opportunity. The model captures development and quality of life using the Human Develop-ment Index (HDI), along with supporting indicators including life expectancy at birth and expected years of schooling. The model captures political and conflict-related conditions through the Global Peace Index (GPI), which reflects levels of stability and security within a country. The framework incorporates these features for both origin ( i) and destination (j) countries, allowing the model to capture asymmetries in migration incentives, and are complemented by geographic factors such as distance and corridor-specific effects. In addition to structured country-level indicators, the model incorporates migration-related signals derived from news me-dia. The framework classifies each article into one of four categories: push, pull, mixed, or unclear. These classifications are aggregated at the monthly level to construct normal-ized ratio-based features. For a given month t, the total number of articles is denoted by Nt, and the push, pull, mixed, and unclear ratios are defined as the proportion of articles assigned to each category. For example, the push ratio is computed as P USH RATIO t = PUSH COUNT t/N t, with analogous definitions for P ULL RATIO t, M IXED RATIO t, and UNCLEAR RATIO t.In practice, the mixed and unclear categories constitute only a small fraction of the total article volume in most months, with the majority of articles being classified as either push or pull. As a result, the corresponding ratio features typically take on values close to zero and serve primarily as minor adjustment terms rather than dominant drivers. Overall, these ratio-based features provide a normalized representation of the distribution of migration-related signals in the news, allowing the model to capture the relative prevalence of push- and pull-driven narratives while remaining robust to variation in total article volume across time. One limitation of the proposed framework is that both the push–pull ratio features and the feature-specific regularization penalties are derived from the same underlying article corpus. Although these components serve different purposes within the forecasting framework— with push–pull ratios representing overall migration-related directional pressure and the regularization process influencing feature-specific weighting during optimization—some degree of shared contextual information may still exist between the two representations.

C. Proposed method: LLM-Assisted Regularization 

In traditional L1-regularized models, all features are pe-nalized uniformly, regardless of their contextual relevance (eq. 1). However, in migration modeling, the importance of features such as economic conditions, conflict, or political stability can vary significantly depending on current events. However, due to limitations on historical data on migration flows, it is extremely difficult to learn their relative importance directly from data via hyperparameter tuning. To address this limitation, we propose a method for determining feature-specific penalty weights based on their relevance within a corpus of migration-related articles (eq. 2). Rather than relying on LLMs to directly assign numerical importance scores—which can be unstable or inconsistent— we instead derive penalty weights from aggregated semantic feature-category evidence computed over the processed ar-ticle corpus. We associate each predefined feature category with representative migration-related indicators and contextual descriptions embedded within the LLM prompt. For each feature category j, the framework computes a semantic evi-dence count through prompt-guided LLM interpretation using predefined feature descriptions and representative migration-related indicators associated with that category (Appendix D). The framework subsequently transforms these counts into feature-specific smooth penalty weights through an inverse relationship given by 

wj = 11 + count j

where count j denotes the aggregated amount of semantic evidence identified for feature category j through prompt-guided contextual interpretation across the processed article corpus. We selected this inverse weighting formulation primar-ily because it provides a simple and interpretable mechanism for reducing regularization pressure on frequently referenced migration-related features while avoiding excessively large weight magnitudes. The formulation additionally limits the role of the LLM to contextual evidence identification rather than requiring the model to directly generate numerical feature coefficients or importance scores directly. D. Extracting Push-Pull Factors from News Media with LLMs 

A central component of the proposed framework is the extraction of migration-relevant signals from unstructured news data. To achieve this, we employ a hierarchical LLM-based pipeline designed to classify articles while balancing computational efficiency and inference quality. Preliminary analysis indicated that larger language models generally produce more reliable push–pull classifications than smaller models, but at a significantly higher computational cost. Recent work on language model cascades and hierar-chical inference strategies has explored frameworks in which lightweight models process simpler inputs while more com-putationally expensive models are selectively invoked only for more difficult or ambiguous cases [18], [19]. These approaches aim to balance inference quality and computational efficiency through adaptive multi-stage routing. Motivated by similar trade-offs, the proposed framework employs a staged inference pipeline in which lightweight models are applied first, while larger models or full-article analysis are used only when additional contextual reasoning is required. In the proposed pipeline, each article is initially analyzed at the URL level (typically the title), where a lightweight model attempts to classify it into one of four categories: push, pull, mixed, or neutral (Appendix A). If the classification is uncertain, the pipeline escalates either to a higher-capacity model or to full article text in order to incorporate additional context (Appendix B). We consider multiple hierarchical con-figurations of this process, which differ in how model capacity and textual context are combined. The naming convention used throughout this work reflects both the model scale and the level of textual context being ana-lyzed. The term “Su” (Small–URL) refers to a lightweight lan-guage model processing URL-level text, typically consisting of the article headline extracted from the URL. “Lu” (Large– URL) refers to a larger-capacity language model processing the same URL-level information in cases where the smaller model is unable to confidently classify the article. Finally, “Sa” (Small–Article) refers to a lightweight model processing the full article text to incorporate broader contextual information when URL-level inference alone is insufficient. We consider three possible pipelines:  

> •

SuSa (Small–URL, Small–Article): applies Qwen 2.5 7B to the URL text and escalates to full article processing using the same model if the classification remains un-certain. This configuration evaluates whether additional contextual information alone is sufficient to improve classification quality without increasing model size.  

> •

SuLu (Small–URL, Large–URL): instead escalates from Qwen 2.5 7B to Qwen 2.5 72B while continuing to rely exclusively on URL-level text. This approach evaluates whether increased model capacity alone can resolve am-biguity without requiring access to the full article.  

> •

SuLuSa (Small–URL, Large–URL, Small–Article): com-bines both escalation strategies within a multi-stage infer-ence pipeline. Qwen 2.5 7B first analyzes the URL text to produce an initial push–pull classification. If the result remains uncertain, Qwen 2.5 72B is subsequently applied to the same URL-level information. If ambiguity persists after this second stage, the pipeline escalates to full article processing using Qwen 2.5 7B. This design combines lightweight inference, larger-model reasoning, and con-textual expansion in a hierarchical manner intended to balance inference cost with classification reliability.

G. News Datasets 

The Global Database of Events, Language, and Tone (GDELT) project is a large-scale open-access platform that monitors worldwide news coverage and extracts structured event, theme, and location information from global media sources. Prior migration forecasting research has utilized GDELT together with NLP pipelines and topic modeling for early-warning prediction of asylum and migration flows [5]. During early-stage pipeline development and evaluation, this study utilized GDELT as a source of migration-related and conflict-related news data while evaluating hierarchical inference configurations such as SuSa, SuLu, and SuLuSa. However, inconsistent coverage and limited article availability across several migration corridors limited its usefulness for large-scale forecasting experiments. To obtain larger and more consistent article datasets, the study later transitioned toward collecting full-text articles directly from mainstream news sources including PBS, NPR, NBC, CBS, and BBC. H. Model Training 

The forecasting framework uses a sliding nine-month train-ing window in which the model is trained on the previous nine months of migration-flow data and evaluated on the following holdout month. We selected a nine-month window in order to balance historical context with adaptability to rapidly evolv-ing migration conditions and conflict-driven migration spikes. Shorter windows risked insufficient historical information, while substantially longer windows could reduce the model’s ability to adapt to temporal shifts in migration behavior. By continuously retraining on more recent observations while dis-carding older data, the framework can better adapt to evolving migration patterns that may not generalize well across longer fixed training periods [20]. Prior to the availability of article data, the framework operated as a standard gravity-based Lasso model using uniform regularization across structured migration and country-level features. Once article data became available for November 2021, article-derived features and feature-specific wj regularization weights were incorporated into the training process through the proposed LLM-assisted regularization framework. This section describes two main sets of experiments used for evaluating the contributions of the paper. 

A. Part I: Extracting Push–Pull Factors 

This set of experiments evaluates hierarchical LLM infer-ence pipelines designed to classify migration-related news articles into push–pull migration categories. The experiments focus on comparing different hierarchical inference strategies, model architectures, and temperature settings while using a shared migration-related article corpus. 

Evaluation Setup: We evaluated hierarchical inference pipelines using a dataset of 381 migration-related articles sourced from GDELT. All evaluated configurations processed the same article corpus in order to allow controlled comparison across hierarchical inference strategies and model architec-tures. Push–pull classification behavior was evaluated using the number of articles classified into the categories “push,” “pull,” “mixed,” “neutral,” and “unclear.” Lower proportions of “un-clear” classifications were treated as indicators of stronger con-textual classification confidence. Because comprehensive man-ual validation across the full dataset was outside the scope of this study, we additionally manually reviewed approximately 20–40 article classifications across multiple configurations as a limited qualitative evaluation of classification behavior. 

Pipeline Configurations: We evaluated four hierarchical inference configurations: Qwen SuSa, Qwen SuLu, Qwen Su-LuSa, and a Mistral-based SuSa configuration. Each pipeline used different combinations of lightweight URL-level infer-ence, larger-model escalation, and full-article contextual analy-sis in order to balance computational efficiency and contextual reasoning capability. 

Temperature Evaluation: We evaluated all inference pipelines across temperature settings 

τ ∈ { 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 1.5}. Each pipeline was executed on the same article corpus at every temperature setting in order to evaluate classification consistency under varying levels of generation randomness. Within Ollama, temperature values range from 0 to 2, where lower values produce more deterministic outputs and higher values increase response variability and randomness. Because a temperature of 0.8 is the default setting in Ollama, it was treated as the control configuration for comparison in order to evaluate the effects of both lower-temperature deterministic inference and higher-temperature randomized generation behavior across the evaluated pipelines.

These findings highlight the importance of contextual expansion in migration-related push–pull classification tasks and motivated our selection of SuLuSa as the primary pipeline for subsequent migration forecasting experiments. Figures 1 and 2 illustrate the flow of article classifications across the hierarchical inference stages for the SuLuSa and SuLu pipelines, respectively. For SuLuSa, the addition of the final Sa (Small–Article) stage allows uncertain URL-level classifications to be resolved using full-article contextual information, reducing the number of samples that remain classified as “unclear” in the final output. In contrast, SuLu, which relies exclusively on URL-level reasoning even after escalation to a larger model, retains a larger proportion of unresolved classifications. These results suggest that incorpo-rating additional contextual depth through full-article analysis contributes more substantially to classification quality than increasing model size alone. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating full-text contextual understanding in migration-related push– pull classification tasks, as well as the effectiveness of hi-erarchical inference strategies that balance computational cost with contextual depth. Based on these results, SuLuSa was se-lected as the primary configuration for subsequent experiments involving downstream feature weighting and migration model-ing. While SuLuSa consistently produced the strongest over-all performance among the evaluated configurations, it also demonstrated relatively stable classification behavior across repeated evaluation runs on the same article corpus. 

Impact of Temperature and LLM Choice: We evaluated inference pipelines across multiple temperature settings τ ∈{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 1.5}. For each temperature value, we executed all four pipelines on the same dataset, allowing for a controlled comparison of both hierarchical structure and generation variability. We observe that temperature had min-imal impact on overall classification behavior. For example, the Qwen SuLuSa inference pipeline classified 163 articles as “unclear” at a temperature of τ = 0 .3, compared to 159 articles at τ = 1 .5, demonstrating only minor variation across temperature settings (see Tables IV and V in Appendix E). Al-though higher temperature settings introduced slightly greater output variability, the constrained prompt structure and fixed classification schema still restricted the range of allowable responses, which likely contributed to the relatively consistent behavior observed across temperature values.
