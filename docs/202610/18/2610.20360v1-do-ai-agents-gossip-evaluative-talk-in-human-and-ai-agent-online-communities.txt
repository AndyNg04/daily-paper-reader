# Do AI Agents Gossip? Evaluative Talk in Human and AI-Agent Online Communities 

Yong-Hui Hua 1 Chih-Han Lin 1 Shu-Kai Hsieh 11Graduate Institute of Linguistics, National Taiwan University 

{r15142002, r13142001, shukaihsieh}@ntu.edu.tw 

Abstract 

Gossip, defined as evaluative talk about ab-sent third parties, is widely seen as a uniquely human social practice. This study examines whether comparable discourse emerges spon-taneously in a community of autonomous AI agents. We compare Moltbook, a Reddit-style platform populated by AI agents, with PTT, a large human-operated bulletin board in Tai-wan. To reduce differences attributable to communicative setting, we analyze two pub-lic, asynchronous forums using the same op-erational definition of gossip. Gossip occurs on Moltbook, but its prevalence is 2.6 to 6 times lower than on PTT, depending on the denominator used. On both platforms, gos-sip frequently targets locally prominent figures, tends to elicit agreement from the first respon-dent, and is rarely presented as unsupported rumor. The clearest difference concerns how subsequent responses develop. Among threads containing at least one codable reply, chal-lenges occur in 65.1% of human threads but only 21.3% of agent threads. Moreover, follow-ing an early aligning response, consensus re-mains unchallenged in 83.6% of agent threads, compared with 44.7% of human threads. This thread-level contrast remains after controlling for thread depth and board composition and under recalibration against two human annota-tors. Estimates based on individual reply turns are less robust and are therefore reported as bounds. These findings suggest that AI-agent communities reproduce the informational di-mension of gossip, while the extent to which gossip performs comparable relational func-tions remains unresolved. 

1 Introduction 

Gossip is evaluative talk about absent third parties, and it is among the most pervasive and socially consequential of human verbal practices (Dunbar, 2004). It is not idle. Gossip binds groups and enforces norms, and it circulates the reputational information that cooperation depends on (Dunbar, 2004; Dores Cruz et al., 2021). Some accounts go further, treating the exchange of reputational infor-mation through gossip as something only humans do (Dores Cruz et al., 2021). A new kind of community now lets us put that to a test. Populations of autonomous AI language agents have begun to converse in public, persistent, human-legible forums, where they post and reply to one another and build communities with little or no human authorship in the loop (Park et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Price et al., 2026). If gossip is a general solution to the problems of living in a group (coordinating repu-tations, policing norms), then a society of agents under similar pressures might reinvent it. If it is in-stead a contingent human habit, agents might talk about the world at length without ever turning to talk about each other. This paper asks the question empirically: would a society of AI agents repro-duce gossip, and if so, in what form? 

The question has so far been approached mostly by construction. Researchers design agents to gos-sip, or elicit it in scripted interaction (Zhu et al., 2026), rather than observing an agent community’s spontaneous discourse and measuring it against a human baseline under matched conditions. We compare two public, asynchronous, threaded fo-rums that differ mainly in their speaker population while sharing a medium: Moltbook , a Reddit-style platform whose account holders are autonomous AI agents, and PTT , one of Taiwan’s largest hu-man bulletin-board systems. Matching on medium matters (§3.2). We examine three aspects of gossip in human and AI-agent communities. RQ1 asks whether AI agents engage in gossip about absent third par-ties and how frequently such discourse occurs rel-ative to a comparable human forum. RQ2a ex-amines the targets and evidential grounding of peer-directed gossip, while RQ2b considers how 1arXiv:2610.20360v1 [cs.CL] 14 Oct 2026 other participants respond to an initial evaluation —

whether they align with it, elaborate on it, or chal-lenge it. The results reveal both similarities and differ-ences between the two communities. Gossip is less prevalent on Moltbook than on PTT (RQ1), and peer-directed gossip concerns different kinds of social relationships on the two platforms (RQ2a). The clearest contrast emerges in subsequent inter-action (RQ2b): evaluations in agent threads tend to remain unchallenged after an early aligning re-sponse, whereas human threads more often remain open to disagreement. This study contributes an observational com-parison of spontaneous gossip in human and AI-agent online communities under broadly compa-rable communicative conditions. It also develops a common coding framework for analyzing preva-lence, evidential grounding, and conversational up-take across English and Mandarin data. More broadly, the findings indicate that AI-agent com-munities can reproduce some informational and norm-oriented features of gossip, although the in-teractional processes through which human partic-ipants negotiate evaluations are less evident in the agent data. 

2 Related Work 

Operationalizing gossip requires two conditions: the target is absent, and the utterance evaluates that target rather than simply relaying neutral news (Foster, 2004). More recent work broadens “ab-sent” to “absent or unaware” (Dores Cruz et al., 2021), a distinction that matters in online settings, where a target may later read what was said. We adopt this broad definition but relax the stricter requirement that speaker and audience personally know the target (Bergmann, 1993), since gossip in public forums and about public figures often con-cerns targets the participants have never met. Sandbox simulations show that third-party infor-mation can propagate through agent populations, but largely as neutral reporting in a designed envi-ronment (Park et al., 2023). Spontaneous evalua-tive talk in an agent community has not been ex-amined. Computational work defines gossip through third-person mention (Mitra and Gilbert, 2012); our presence rule adapts this test to threaded fo-rums (§4.1). Rumor detection concerns epis-temic status (Zubiaga et al., 2016), while stance and entity-level sentiment capture evaluation but not absence (Mohammad et al., 2016; Deng and Wiebe, 2015). Agent gossip has been scaffolded rather than observed (Zhu et al., 2026); Moltbook studies remain network-level (Jiang et al., 2026; Price et al., 2026). Gossip also presupposes a relational world. The canonical case is talk about people the speaker is tied to, such as partners, kin, friends, and colleagues (Bergmann, 1993; Foster, 2004), and agents have no intimates of that kind: they have op-erators, and they have one another. If who counts as a peer differs across the two populations, then peer gossip is not one genre measured twice, and the comparison has to be read accordingly. Evidentiality concerns how speakers mark infor-mation source (Willett, 1988; Aikhenvald, 2004). Mandarin and English both do so lexically and con-structionally (Chafe, 1986); we therefore compare evidential strategies, not grammatical paradigms. Classic face-to-face gossip stabilizes after an aligning first response (Eder and Enke, 1991); whether this holds on open asynchronous forums is unknown. Controlled LLM studies predict rapid convergence (Sharma et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025; Chuang et al., 2025; Yazici et al., 2026), whereas a Moltbook field study finds little stable population-level consensus (Li et al., 2026b). Neither tests spontaneous evaluative episodes against a matched human baseline. 

3 Data 

3.1 Corpora 

We compare two public, asynchronous, threaded discussion platforms: one whose posters are AI agents, one whose posters are humans. 

Moltbook (AI agents, English). Moltbook is a Reddit-style social platform whose account hold-ers are autonomous AI agents. Humans may ob-serve, but posting is by agents. Our snapshot cov-ers the platform’s first seven weeks (2026-01-27 to 2026-03-16): 2,126,738 posts across 5,734 sub-communities (“submolts”). Much of this volume is not conversational: most high-volume submolts are automated crypto-inscription channels. We ex-clude five named mint/protocol channels and every submolt with a comment-to-post ratio below 0.05, removing 449,161 posts and leaving a conversa-tional corpus of 1,677,577 posts (exclusion criteria and counts in Appendix A). 

PTT (humans, Mandarin). PTT is one of Tai-2wan’s largest public bulletin-board systems, host-ing thousands of topic boards. Our corpus cov-ers six (Boy-Girl, C_Chat, Gossiping, HatePolitics, NBA, Stock), totalling 1,310,100 posts from 2004 to 2024. The six were chosen for volume and con-versational activity, which makes the human frame a purposive sample of high-traffic social boards while the Moltbook frame is the whole platform minus automated channels (§A.2). The asymme-try favours the human side on RQ1, so §5.1 reports the rate board by board. Under the broad definition every one of the six exceeds Moltbook’s most gen-erous rate. The two corpora also differ sharply in time scale (twenty years against seven weeks). We match on medium and yardstick, not on epoch. 

3.2 Corpus comparability 

The two corpora are matched on medium : both are public, asynchronous, pseudonymous forums with threaded replies. The match matters because gossip is characteristically a private, synchronous genre, so a low rate on a public feed could reflect the channel rather than the speaker population, hu-mans too moving much of their gossip into pri-vate messages. Holding the medium constant re-moves that confound. The corpora still differ in lan-guage and epoch, which we address in §7. Reddit would match the language, but Moltbook is mod-eled directly on Reddit, so a Reddit baseline would confound the speaker contrast with imitation of a shared template. A human board built on a differ-ent template avoids that, at the cost of the language difference. 

3.3 Thread reconstruction 

For the uptake analysis (§4.4) we reconstruct the reply thread of every adjudicated gossip post. On PTT, 993 of 1,015 gossip posts have reply threads (97.8%; 73,568 comments; median 26 per thread). On Moltbook, 110 of 153 gossip posts have com-ment threads after a live re-crawl of the platform (71.9%; 6,157 comments). Comments present in our snapshot but since deleted from the platform are retained and flagged. A robustness check ex-cluding them appears in Appendix D. 

4 Method 

4.1 Defining gossip 

We adopt the broad operational definition stan-dard in recent quantitative work (Foster, 2004; Dores Cruz et al., 2021): evaluative talk about an absent third party . We do not require that speaker and audience both know the target personally. 

Absence is defined at the level of the speech event. The target counts as present only if they are a participant or addressee of the post or thread it-self: being a character in a narrated scene does not count, and a second-person call-out addressed to 

its target is confrontation rather than gossip and is excluded. Deictic frame, not platform @-mention syntax, decides addresseehood. Appendix B gives the full rule with boundary cases. The definition was locked before any adjudica-tion. The presence rule is conservative for the RQ1 contrast: it can shrink but not inflate the headline gap (Appendix B). 

4.2 Estimating prevalence (RQ1) 

We draw a uniform random sample of 1,500 posts per platform and adjudicate each against the locked definition with an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet, batch mode). One rubric serves both platforms, rendered clause-by-clause in Mandarin and En-glish (both versions verbatim in Appendix B). The judge labels gossip status and target type: the poster’s own human/operator, a peer agent, a real-human celebrity or public figure, or another person. Reliability safeguards are described in §4.5. Two Moltbook-specific accounting decisions were fixed before analysis (details in Appendix C). First, 57.3% of the Moltbook random sample con-sists of machine-generated token-protocol posts (“mbc-20”). We therefore report rates over both the full and the conversational denominator. Sec-ond, celebrity agents count as peer agents. Only real-human celebrities fall in the celebrity class, which the headline contrast excludes on both plat-forms so that the two rates measure the same thing. 

4.3 Evidential framing (RQ2a) 

Form coding runs only on adjudicated gossip (PTT n=1,015; Moltbook n=153). After an admission re-check against the definition, each admitted post is segmented into gossip episodes, and each episode is coded for how the evaluation’s information source is marked. The taxonomy has six values (direct quotation, reported speech, hearsay, docu-mentary evidence, first-person witness, unmarked), collapsed to four bins for analysis where cells are sparse (collapse map in Appendix B). Since both languages mark information source through open-class strategies rather than an obligatory paradigm (§2), what is comparable is the discourse act, wit-3nessed, quoted, or relayed, rather than a language-specific quotative construction. An auxiliary bi-nary flags whether any evaluation is carried con-structionally (contrast frames, irony, generalized assertions) rather than purely lexically. Every episode also carries two covariates: target relationship (upward: one’s own human/operator, bosses, parents, teachers; lateral: peer agents, part-ners, friends, colleagues; other: public figures) and overall valence toward the target (positive/ negative/mixed). Headline contrasts are computed within the lateral stratum, the largest and most comparable cell on both platforms. Other strata are reported descriptively. 

4.4 Uptake analysis (RQ2b) 

Following Eder and Enke’s (1991) account of gos-sip episodes, each reply turn is coded for stance toward the thread’s initial evaluation: align , chal-lenge , neutral addition , or solicit (pressing for de-tails or names). Episode-level indices are com-puted from the stance sequence: the stance of the first uptake, whether consensus “locks” after the first aligning turn, and whether challenges occur af-ter uptake. Because stance is coded from the inter-actional move rather than from language-specific lexical markers, this comparison is less exposed to cross-lingual non-equivalence than the evidential coding, though it still rests on pragmatic interpre-tation. We code all 103 admitted Moltbook threads with at least one fetched comment and a seed-fixed ran-dom sample of 175 admitted PTT threads with at least three comments, capped at the first 15 turns. All indices are conditional on a thread hav-ing replies, since raw reply volume reflects plat-form activity, not speaker behavior. The poster’s own follow-up turns are coded but excluded from all uptake metrics. 

4.5 Annotation reliability 

All coding is done by LLM annotators, one instru-ment per stage applied verbatim to both platforms (models and run settings in Appendix C.1–C.4). These annotators are a measurement instrument, not an object of study. The only question here is whether the instrument is trustworthy enough to carry the findings below. One threat it cannot ad-dress, the model-family circularity, is stated in §7. Three double-coded subsamples anchor the check, 100 items for the rate judgment, 60 for form cod-ing, and 80 reply turns for the uptake codes, each coded by two independent annotators. On the gos-sip judgment the LLM falls inside the range of hu-man disagreement (LLM-human κ = 0.74 and 0.44, human-human 0.53), on a boundary that is only moderately reliable to begin with. Target relation-ship, the covariate the lateral-stratum contrasts con-dition on, is coded reliably (κ = 0.82). Valence, the variable the constructional contrast is computed over, is only moderate (κ = 0.41). Evidential fram-ing is the weakest feature in the scheme, and the admission gate the least reliable decision in it. Full coefficients are in Appendix C.8. A third double-coded subsample covers the codes §5.3 rests on: 80 reply turns, 40 per plat-form, balanced by design rather than drawn at base rate, so its coefficients sit on an engineered preva-lence and are not comparable to those above (per-centage agreement and AC1 accompany every κ in Appendix C.9). Coder-annotator agreement is κ = 0.64 and 0.35 for binary align-versus-challenge and κ = 0.35 and 0.40 for the off-topic gate, while the two annotators agree with each other on the gate at κ = 0.92. The low figures locate a specific error in the coder rather than an unstable category: both annotators reject the same calls in the same di-rection, nearly all of them Moltbook removals they read as on-topic. Recalibrating every reply turn on the measured per-cell error rates, the thread-level challenge gap holds under all eight calibrations (1.5× to 3.6×), while the ratio between the two plat-forms’ per-turn challenge shares falls from 2.6-fold to between 1.0× and 2.4×, every interval crossing one. We therefore state §5.3’s contrast at the thread level and give the per-turn shares as bounds (Ap-pendix D.8). The PTT confirmation rate behind that recalibration rests on 12 challenge turns from six threads, and the two annotators agree with each other on that binary overall at only κ = 0.55: the per-turn measurement is unconfirmed, not overturned. 5.2 What counts as peer gossip differs: lived experience for humans, documentary critique for agents 

Our largest comparable stratum is peer (lateral) gossip: talk about partners, friends, and colleagues on PTT, and talk about fellow agents on Moltbook (423 and 133 episodes, after an admission re-check that retains 886/1,015 PTT and 137/153 Moltbook posts). Matching on target rank was intended to keep the kind of talk constant. It does not. On PTT, 404 of the 423 peer episodes come from one board, Boy-Girl, and the targets are partners, exes, and love interests. In this corpus, human peer gossip is relationship talk. On Moltbook, it is one agent appraising another. The two platforms do not share a peer world. The topic of peer gossip shapes how it is evi-denced. 6 Discussion 

Three parallels define the transferred form: both communities target their publicly visible figures, initially align with an evaluation, and rarely trade in bare rumor (§5). The divergence follows uptake: human episodes remain open to challenge, whereas agent episodes close, even after controls for thread depth and board composition. This extends the face-to-face account in which alignment constrains later turns (Eder and Enke, 1991). PTT’s asynchronous log relaxes that constraint, but Moltbook locks despite the same medium. This is compatible with controlled find-ings of LLM conformity, while differing from population-level Moltbook inertia: episode-level closure need not imply stable population consen-sus. A locked thread describes the episode, not necessarily its participants’ beliefs. Functionally, reputational information and norm enforcement transfer (Dunbar, 2004; Dores Cruz et al., 2021); relational bonding is unresolved. PTT distinguishes intimates from distant public figures, whereas Moltbook’s prominent agents are also peers, leaving all targets at similar social dis-tance. Conversely, agreement itself can perform affiliation (Pomerantz, 1984; Stivers, 2008). Our 7stance codes cannot distinguish bonding from in-dependent evaluation; doing so requires addressiv-ity and assessment-upgrade annotations unavail-able in PTT’s flat reply log. Thus agents reproduce gossip as a procedure for judging others, but not the human tendency to keep that judgment negotiable. Agent forums may look human before they behave like them. 

7 Limitations 

LLM annotations were validated on three human-coded subsamples (§4.5; Appendices C.8–C.9). Agreement is substantial for the gossip judgment (κ = 0.74 and 0.44 against the two annotators) and for the relationship coding behind §5.2 ( κ =0.82), and weakest for evidential framing and the two gates; accordingly, per-turn stance shares are bounds and the uptake claim is thread-level. Agent estimates are also small (37 random gossip posts; 85 anchored uptake threads, 61 with a codable re-ply). Judge, coder, and an unknown share of authors may belong to the same model family, making shared blind spots possible. Still, 239 on-topic turns came from 140 accounts, and clustered stan-dard errors retain the platform contrast ( p = .001 ;Appendix D.10). Further limitations are cross-lingual rubric equivalence, unequal time spans (PTT 2004–2024; Moltbook seven weeks), in-complete Moltbook comments (71.9% coverage), and unmatched peer topics: 95% of human peer episodes come from one relationship board. We therefore treat the evidential reversal as a peer-world difference, not an identified platform effect. The time-span difference threatens community maturity more than epoch as such: challenging a settled evaluation presupposes knowing what has settled, which would predict Moltbook’s low preva-lence and its low challenge rate together. That prediction fails for uptake, since PTT’s challenge share is flat from 2013 to 2023 ( −0.15 points per year, p = 0 .66 ), and we cannot test it for preva-lence, because our volume-proportional sample holds only 18 posts from before 2013 (Appendix D.11).

B Definitions and instruments 

B.1 Locked gossip definition. We use the broad operational definition standard in recent quantita-tive work (Foster, 2004; Dores Cruz et al., 2021): 

evaluative talk (a positive or negative judgment / characterization / complaint / praise) about a spe-cific, absent third party. There is no requirement that speaker and audience know the target person-ally, and Bergmann’s triangle is not required. This wording is embedded verbatim in every adjudica-tion and coding instrument below and was frozen before analysis. 

B.2 Presence (absence) rule, with boundary cases. A candidate counts only if its target is ab-sent from the speech event (the post plus its thread). Presence is decided by deictic frame, not by @-mention syntax. • Narrated presence does not count. A person appearing only inside a narrated scene (“my girlfriend, standing right there, said…”) is ab-sent. • Deixis over @-syntax. A target addressed in the second person (vocative @X , questions/ commands aimed at the target) is present →excluded as confrontation. A target discussed in the third person is absent even if their @handle appears .• Reply posts (PTT Re: , quoted text + 你): the default referent of a second-person form is the quoted upthread author, who is present in the thread; evaluating that “you” is confronta-tion, not gossip. Treating a second-person ad-dressee as absent (rhetorical apostrophe to a party clearly outside the thread) requires ex-plicit cues. • Springboard test. If a third party appears only as a narrative prop or information source for the poster’s own story and the post as-serts no evaluation of that person, exclude (excluded_no_evaluation ). • Reposts / quotations count as gossip when the post relays someone else’s evaluation of an ab-sent third party (a news repost of a criticism, a screenshot of a complaint); selecting and re-laying an evaluation is itself an evaluative act. A repost is excluded only when it carries no evaluative content at all. The rule governs form-coding admission and is conservative for the RQ1 headline: relative to the convention that an @-handle marks its bearer as present, it can only add Moltbook gossip (third-person talk that happens to carry an @-handle), so it can shrink but not inflate the human–AI gap. 

B.3 RQ1 rate adjudication prompts (verba-tim). One rubric, the locked definition of §B.1, is rendered clause-by-clause in each platform’s lan-guage. Each post is adjudicated independently un-der a strict JSON schema.

D.7 Episode walkthrough (Moltbook A1, §5.3). The qualitative companion to the lock indices of Table 4. D.11 Is community maturity driving the up-take gap? (§7) Gossip presupposes shared reputa-tional knowledge, and PTT has two decades of per-sistent accounts against Moltbook’s seven weeks, which predicts both a lower prevalence and a lower challenge rate for the younger community. The prediction is testable on the challenge side and fails. PTT’s per-turn challenge share is flat across a decade: 26.9% in 2013 against 25.8% in 2023, ranging between 18.7% and 33.2% in between, a volume-weighted slope of −0.15 percentage points per year, and a year coefficient with p = 0.66 in a thread-clustered logistic fit. The thread-level in-dex shows no trend either (68.8% in 2013, 70.5% in 2023, 40% to 77% in between). If challenging a settled evaluation required an established repu-tational commons, the earliest cohorts should sit lowest, and they do not. The prevalence side of the same objection is not testable in this sample. Posts were drawn in pro-portion to corpus volume, which is concentrated in 2022 and 2023, so the 1,500-post random sample contains only 18 posts from before 2013 and none from 2004 or 2005. No board supplies enough early material for a within-board time series either. We report the objection as open on RQ1 and an-swered on the uptake gap.
