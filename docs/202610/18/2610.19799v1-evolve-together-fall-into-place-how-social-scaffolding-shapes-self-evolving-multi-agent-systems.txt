Abstract 

Recent work lets LLM agents evolve their own harness, up to the team itself. Yet in human groups, the same abilities achieve different out-comes under different social conditions. Bor-rowing the notion of scaffolding from devel-opmental psychology, we investigate whether the social scaffolding of a self-evolving multi-agent system (MAS), namely its team composi-tion and structure of interdependence (e.g., col-laboration, conformity, or competition), shapes how the team evolves. We present AGORA 

(Agents Growing through Organization and 

Relational Arrangements), a framework that varies the two axes independently across three evolution mechanisms. Across web search, codebase understanding, and document QA, we find that (1) evolution alone rarely helps, while the right scaffolding surpasses a strong agent evolved alone by up to 25 points; (2) it does so by moving authority to the most capable member while the others step back; and (3) this member, having grown within the team, outper-forms the same model alone. Thus, designing a self-evolving MAS requires designing its so-cial scaffolding, which shapes not only how its agents learn but also who gets to lead. 

1 Introduction 

As Large Language Model (LLM) agents take on increasingly complex tasks, they rarely work as bare models. They run inside a harness that shapes their performance (Meng et al., 2026). Prior work has focused on its technical design, from what a single agent sees and is allowed to do (Lee et al., 2026; Lou et al., 2026) to how LLM calls are com-posed into workflows (Zhang et al., 2025). The same holds for multi-agent systems (MAS) (Liu et al., 2026b), and a growing line of work goes further and lets agents evolve their own harness, by rewriting their skills, memories, and even the team

However, as shown in Figure 1, evolution in hu-man groups never happens in a social vacuum. Or-ganizational and evolutionary theories suggest that people with the same abilities can achieve and be-have differently, depending on how they compare with those around them and how much they rely on one another (Deutsch, 1949; Johnson and Johnson, 2009). Much like a human group, a self-evolving MAS consists of agents that differ in ability, de-pend on one another, and change through experi-ence. This raises a natural question: does the social layer of its harness shape how the team evolves, as social conditions do for human groups? 

Prior work on self-evolving MAS either fixes this social layer as a design (Liu et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025) or folds it into the evolution process itself (Hao et al., 2026). We in-stead treat this social layer as the central variable of study. We borrow the developmental-psychology notion of scaffolding, the support a learner’s so-cial environment provides during development and withdraws once it is no longer needed (Wood et al., 1976), and call this layer social scaffolding . We op-erationalize it for MAS along the two factors above: 

team composition , which sets how capable each agent is relative to its teammates, and the structure 

1arXiv:2610.19799v1 [cs.CL] 14 Oct 2026 of interdependence , which sets how agents share information and decide on team changes. To isolate its effect on what a team evolves into, we build AGORA (Agents Growing through 

Organization and Relational Arrangements), a framework that extends the three-level evolution loop of Meta-Team (Hao et al., 2026). After each batch of training tasks, every agent improves itself (L1), agents take notes on their teammates when a structure of interdependence is in place (L2), and the team revises itself as a whole (L3), with each team revision checked on a held-out set. AGORA varies the two axes independently: team composition sets the mix of agent capabil-ities (onlyweak, onlystrong, or weakstrong), and the structure of interdependence (none, collabora-tion, conformity, or competition) sets which per-missions agents hold, how they share information at L2, and how team changes are decided at L3. We cross these axes with three evolution mechanisms that govern L1, Meta-Team (Hao et al., 2026), Skil-lOpt (Yang et al., 2026), and MemRL (Zhang et al., 2026c), holding everything else fixed so that differ-ences can be traced to social scaffolding. Using AGORA on three benchmarks spanning web search (GAIA), codebase understanding (Lo-CoBench), and document QA (OfficeQA Pro), we investigate three research questions: whether so-cial scaffolding changes what a self-evolving MAS achieves (RQ1); how the team reorganizes under the right scaffolding (RQ2); and why the reorga-nized team outperforms the same model working alone (RQ3). Experiments yield three findings. • Evolution alone is rarely enough (RQ1). Evolv-ing under no structure of interdependence rarely improves a team, whereas the right social scaf-folding does, surpassing a strong agent evolved alone by up to 25.0 points. Which structure helps depends on who is in the team. • Authority moves to the most capable member (RQ2). Effective scaffolding hands the chair to the most capable member, as human groups gain from recognizing expertise, and the others step back unless they remove a bottleneck. Unlike human groups, they drop out rather than loaf. • The team grows its leader (RQ3). The member left leading, having learned from its teammates, outperforms the same model evolved alone. These findings show that plugging an evolution mechanism into an off-the-shelf MAS is not enough; the social scaffolding under which the team evolves matters just as much, deciding not only how its agents learn, but who gets to lead. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Social Dynamics in Human Groups 

In human groups, individuals with identical abilities can reach different outcomes depend-ing on their social scaffolding. Collaboration 

raises achievement over competing or working alone (Deutsch, 1949; Johnson and Johnson, 2009), but members may loaf when the work is shared (La-tané et al., 1979; Karau and Williams, 1993). Com-petition raises motivation, but equally able mem-bers feel less capable among stronger peers (Marsh, 1987). Conformity reduces individual error but can suppress innovation (Henrich and Boyd, 1998), and since people obey even wrong authority (Mil-gram, 1963) and groups that suppress dissent de-cide poorly (Janis, 1972), the outcome depends on the decision-maker. 

2.2 Evolution and Social Dynamics in LLM Agents 

Evolving agents improve their external state with-out updating model weights, refining memory (Zhu et al., 2026b; Choi et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026c; Wang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026c) or skills (Zhang et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026), and recently agent composition and interaction (Yuan et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Dang et al., 2026). MAS gain more over single agents when their sub-agents are weaker (Ke et al., 2026), suggesting that well-organized weak agents can close much of the gap to stronger ones. In MAS, collaboration does not always help (Tran and Kiela, 2026; Pappu et al., 2026), competition can increase systemic failure (Mori and Johnson, 2026), and conformity varies with group structure and roles (Weng et al., 2025; Cho et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2025; Bito et al., 2026; Han et al., 2026; Bellina et al., 2026; Choi et al., 2026a). Social structures are also used to improve agents through self-play, mutual checks (Liu et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025), or orchestra-tion (Qi et al., 2026), and Meta-Team (Hao et al., 2026) jointly evolves the agents, their interactions, and the team. These methods, however, fix a single interdependence structure, often on closed-answer tasks. 3 AGORA Framework 

As shown in Figure 2, to test whether social scaffolding shapes how a self-evolving MAS evolves, we design AGORA as a controlled testbed. AGORA keeps the basic loop of Meta-Team (Hao et al., 2026), in which a chairman-led team evolves its external state at the individual (L1), relational (L2), and team (L3) levels, but turns its fixed so-cial layer, where the chairman alone decides team changes, into the variable under study. AGORA varies team composition and the structure of inter-dependence while swapping in different L1 evolu-tion mechanisms, separating their effects. 

3.1 Overall Framework Formulation 3.1.1 Team Definition 

A team is a set of role-specific agents, defined by a team configuration (e.g., chairman, team budgets), a constitution of team-wide rules that can be revised as the team evolves, and the agents themselves. Each agent has a configuration (e.g., model and tools), a prompt, and an evolution state such as a skill document or a memory bank. The chairman receives the task, calls on the other agents, and submits the final answer. 

3.1.2 Session Execution 

Each task is handled in a single team session, whose final answer from the chairman is scored. Within a session, agents operate concurrently rather than taking turns, interleaving tool calls (e.g., web search) with messages through a shared mailbox, under a budget on messages, and time that is re-ported after every call. Detailed limits are given in Appendices B and F. 

3.1.3 Three-Level Operation 

Under a given social scaffolding, AGORA repeats an evolution loop of a team session followed by three levels of evolution and a held-out gate (Fig-ure 2 and Table 1). L1 improves each agent through its own evolution mechanism, L2 records what agents learn about one another, L3 revises the team as a whole, and the L3 gate reverts a revision only if it clearly hurts the team on held-out data. 

L1 evolution (Individual) After completing a task as a team (Figure 2, ‘Team Session’ ), each agent evolves through its own evolution mecha-nism (Figure 2, ‘L1 · Individual’ ). Specifically, it updates a skill with its gate record in SkillOpt, a memory bank of Q-valued entries in MemRL, and a prompt patch with self-written skills in Meta-Team. Unlike L2 and L3, L1 operates in the same way un-der every social scaffolding; the scaffolding affects it only indirectly, through the trajectories and L2 memos from which the agents learn. 

L2 recording (Relational) L2 models what team-mates learn about one another through social inter-action (Figure 2, ‘L2 · Relational’ ). Once L1 has finished, each agent writes a memo on every team-mate it has worked with, recording strengths, weak-nesses, collaboration notes, and contradictions; a judge model flags contradictions but does not block the memo. How much agents share and whether memos are reviewed before saving depend on the 

structure of interdependence (Section 3.3.2), and L2 is skipped under none . 3.1.4 L3 Held-out Gate 

The L3 held-out gate reverts a team revision only if it clearly harms the team. The current and revised teams each solve the same 20 validation problems with the same seeds, so that the two are compared on equal footing (Figure 2, ‘L3 Gate’ ). The valida-tion set is disjoint from the train and test sets, and the gate is applied identically under every evolution mechanism and structure of interdependence. All L3 changes of an iteration are reverted if the revised team succeeds less often and a one-sided binomial test against the current team’s success rate finds the drop significant. For example, if the cur-rent team solves 10 of the 20 problems, the changes are reverted only if the revised team solves 8 or fewer. This is because L3 revisions are decisions the team made under its own structure of interde-pendence; it blocks only clear harm and leaves the rest to the team. We use a relaxed α = 0 .3 because re-running the same team changes the outcome of about a third of the validation problems, so a strict level would rarely catch even real degradation. De-tails about thresholds are in Appendix D.5. Each iteration also applies at most two role additions or deletions and two patch bundles, so that each gate judges only a few changes and the team cannot grow without bound. 

3.2 Evolution Mechanism 

As shown in Table 2, we include three L1 mech-anisms that differ in what they update and what drives the update: an agent’s own reflection (Meta-Team), reflection on graded trajectories (SkillOpt), and task reward on a memory bank (MemRL). We adapt the original implementations to the multi-agent setting with minimal changes. The mecha-nism determines only L1. Details and all changes from the original are in Appendix D. 

Meta-Team Following the original design, Meta-Team runs a single iteration with its batch executed sequentially. Right after each task, each agent re-flects on its own behavior and edits its own prompt patch and skills with its own model, and the edits take effect immediately without a gate. 

SkillOpt SkillOpt runs for four iterations. At the end of each iteration, each role applies reflect, merge, and apply steps to its trajectories from that iteration, both successful and failed, together with their grading results, to produce a candidate skill document. The candidate replaces the current doc-ument in the role’s system prompt only if it passes the L1 gate, that is, only if it clearly improves per-formance on the validation set (Appendix D.5). 

MemRL MemRL runs for four iterations, with a separate memory bank for each role. Before a session, up to five memories are retrieved by com-bining their similarity to the problem with their Q-values. At the end of each iteration, the Q-values of the retrieved memories are updated according to task success, and each trajectory, successful or not, is summarized as a procedural script and added as a new memory. 3.3 Social Scaffolding Implementation 

In human teams, how members grow depends both on the capabilities of their teammates, including the gaps between them, and on how they depend on one another while working together. Following this, we implement social scaffolding along two axes: team composition and the structure of interdependence (Figure 2, top). 

3.3.1 Team Composition 

To realize capability differences among team mem-bers, we use two model tiers, weak and strong, drawn from the same model family so that the capa-bility gap remains the main variable and confounds from mixing model families are avoided. As shown in ’ Team composition ’ in Figure 2, we compare three configurations: onlyweak , onlystrong , and 

weakstrong . Onlyweak and onlystrong assign the weak and strong model, respectively, to every role. Weakstrong upgrades only the bottleneck role of each benchmark to the strong model (web_agent for GAIA, developer for LoCoBench, and reader for OfficeQA), while all other roles, including the chairman, remain weak. On GAIA, varying which and how many roles are strong shows that which role matters far more than how many: before evolution, a strong bottle-neck role alone gives 48.3, a strong non-bottleneck role at most 28.3, and further strong roles barely help (51.7–52.5). Details on model selection, bot-tleneck roles, role tools, and these variants are in Appendices B and K.1, and representative role prompts and the full constitution in Appendix L. 

3.3.2 Structure of Interdependence 

To realize how members depend on one another, we define four conditions: none , collaboration ,

conformity , and competition . As shown in Table 3, each condition differs not only in prompt wording but in tools, permissions, and decision rules, and intervenes at three corresponding points of the loop, marked (i)–(iii) in Figure 2. The three points are (i) 

permission tiers , which set who is Tier 1 and can read the whole team’s memos, while Tier 2 agents read only their own; (ii) L2 memos , which set how much agents share and whether memos are saved directly or staged for objection before finalized; and (iii) L3 decisions , which set who can propose, whether proposals go through peer review, and who makes the end-of-iteration decision by what rule.

4 Experimental Setup 

We cross every evolution mechanism with every combination of team composition and interdepen-dence structure on three benchmarks, comparing each evolved team with its unevolved self and a single agent of each tier (RQ1, Section 5), and then trace its final-evaluation sessions to see how it reor-ganizes and why (RQ2 and RQ3, Section 6). 

Benchmarks We use web-based reasoning (GAIA; Mialon et al., 2024), long codebase un-derstanding (LoCoBench; Qiu et al., 2025), and document-based numerical question answering (Of-ficeQA Pro; Opsahl-Ong et al., 2026), each split into non-overlapping train, val, and test sets (split sizes and task formats in Appendix A).

Preevolve baselines evaluate each of the five configurations (two solo setups and three team compositions) on the test set without evolution. Each benchmark thus comprises 47 experiments: five preevolve baselines, six solo runs, and 36 MAS runs.

The right scaffolding makes evolution pay off. 

An appropriate structure outperforms evolution alone in 24 of the 27 settings, and the best combi-nation of team composition and structure surpasses the strong agent evolved alone on every benchmark and mechanism, by up to 25.0 points (74.2 vs. 49.2 with SkillOpt on GAIA). The same evolution mech-anism thus yields far larger gains once the team is organized appropriately. 

The best scaffolding differs from team to team. 

Which structure helps depends on who is in the team. On GAIA with SkillOpt, the onlystrong team is best under evolution alone (60.8), but the weak-strong team is best under competition (74.2). On LoCoBench with Meta-Team, competition lifts the onlystrong team (70.0) but collapses the onlyweak team (22.6). Gains are large both when members clearly differ in ability and when all are strong, and no structure helps when one member is near zero: on OfficeQA Pro, where the weak model scores 2.7 alone, even the best mixed team (21.5) stays below the strong agent alone (28.5). Section 6 ex-plains why: what the right scaffolding changes is who leads the team. 

6 How the Team Reorganizes, and Why It Works 

To answer RQ2 and RQ3, we trace the final-evaluation sessions of the teams with the largest effects (Table 5) and relate them to human groups. Sections 6.1 and 6.2 answer RQ2, and Section 6.3 answers RQ3. Additional experiments with a much weaker model, different assignments of strong roles, and other model families lead to largely the same conclusions (Appendix K). 

6.1 Authority Moves to the Most Capable Member 

The first way the team reorganizes (RQ2) is in who leads it. GAIA weakstrong starts with the weak plan_agent as chair, which relays sub-tasks to the strong web_agent and doubles the LLM calls of the strong agent alone (46.8 vs. 22.6). Competi-tion removes this relay by handing the chair to the strong web_agent under SkillOpt and Meta-Team, which then works alone at solo-level cost (74.2 and 63.3). Promoting a weak role instead backfires: on OfficeQA Pro weakstrong, competition hands the chair to a weak role and drops the team below evolution alone (15.1 vs. 21.5 with SkillOpt; Ta-ble 4). Every chair change under competition, with the score that decided it, is listed in Table 13. Like human groups, which gain from their most capable member only when they recognize it (Littlepage et al., 1997) and pay for poorly suited leaders (Mil-gram, 1963; Janis, 1972), competition helps only when the right role rises.

The reorganized team outperforms the same model working alone (RQ3) because the agent left leading it has learned from its teammates. 6.4 What This Means for Team Design 

Taken together, the answers to RQ1–RQ3 suggest three principles for designing a self-evolving MAS. First, decide who leads before deciding how agents learn: on GAIA, the same weakstrong team scores 74.2 with the strong web_agent in the chair but at most 62.5 while the weak plan_agent keeps it. Sec-ond, let performance choose the leader, but check that it picks a capable role: competition helps when it promotes the strong web_agent and hurts when it promotes a weak role, as on OfficeQA Pro (Sec-tion 6.1). Third, keep teammates only where they remove a bottleneck: readers that share a long code-base cut timeouts on LoCoBench, whereas in an onlystrong team the work moves to the planner alone and the score rises (Section 6.2). 

7 Conclusion 

We investigate whether the social layer of a self-evolving MAS shapes its evolution, as in human groups, and build AGORA to vary team composi-tion and the structure of interdependence with all else fixed. Evolution alone rarely helps; the right scaffolding works by reorganizing the team: author-ity moves to the most capable member, the others step back unless they remove a bottleneck, and the member left leading, having learned from its teammates, outperforms the same model alone. An evolution mechanism alone is not enough, since the social scaffolding under which a team evolves decides how its agents learn and who gets to lead. 8Limitations 

Results are partly confounded with benchmarks, so differences in task type may contribute to the pat-terns we attribute to ability. Our experiments that change the models (Appendix K) partially separate the two, but a full factorial design over ability and task type is left to future work. Each session has a 900-second wall-clock limit, and up to six ses-sions run concurrently on shared GPUs. Because more active agents can raise the server load and slow down each call, the observation that the team improves when the others step back may partly reflect this serving setup. Due to cost, we run evolu-tion only once per condition. The reported standard deviations reflect only the noise of evaluating the same evolved team three times, not the variance of the evolution process itself.

11 A Benchmark Details 

For each benchmark, we fix 20 val problems and divide the remaining problems into train and test at a 4:5 ratio (at least 40 test problems, seed 42). Gates always use val, and final evaluation always uses test. Training tasks are drawn epoch-wise: the train problems are shuffled once per epoch and consumed in consecutive, non-overlapping batches. The last batch of an epoch may be smaller, after which the problems are reshuffled. Split sizes and scoring criteria are summarized in Table 7. For GAIA, we use only the 77 problems with-out attached files, and answers are scored by exact match on the text after “FINAL ANSWER:”. Web search uses the Serper API, with identical queries cached for reproducibility, and each task starts with an empty working directory. Since train has only 17 problems, the same 17 problems are repeated in a reshuffled order with the same seeds over the 4 iterations, so per-iteration batch success rates on GAIA are compared on the same problems. For LoCoBench, we use 199 Python scenarios. Because the whole codebase has to be read, a 200k-token context is required. A task counts as a suc-cess if its LCBS score, which is based on static analysis and ranges up to 5, is 3.0 or higher. Over the 4 iterations, each of the 80 train problems is solved once, so per-iteration batch success rates also reflect differences in problem difficulty. For OfficeQA, the Pro set consists of 133 hard problems. One to three relevant documents are copied into each task’s working directory, and an LLM judge (openai/gpt-5-mini) compares the an-swer with the ground truth and returns correct or incorrect.

D.2 L1: Evolution Mechanisms D.2.1 Meta-Team 

Meta-Team has no separate mechanism: right after a session, in the L1 phase, agents directly edit their own prompt patches and skills. There is no L1 gate, and as in the original design, batches run sequen-tially for a single iteration. L3 changes go through 13 the same L3 gate as in the other mechanisms. The preevolve runs give Meta-Team zero iterations, so the template team is evaluated on test as is. 

D.2.2 SkillOpt 

We use the reflect → merge → apply functions of the original SkillOpt without modification and run them concurrently for each role. The input to the analysis model follows the original format: trajecto-ries that pair each action with its outcome, the grad-ing result appended at the end (prediction, ground truth, and correctness, or the LCBS sub-scores), the failure reason, and the system prompt of that session. Each analysis call batches up to eight tra-jectories from the same role. If a batch exceeds the context limit, it is split evenly into the fewest smaller batches that fit, and the longest tool outputs are truncated first only when a single session alone exceeds the limit. Each iteration uses an edit budget of 4, 16 paral-lel workers, and the strong model as the optimizer model. Every reflect call writes to a fresh output directory so that revisions from previous iterations are not reused, and slightly malformed JSON in analysis responses is repaired with json_repair, a dependency of the original implementation. The resulting candidate skill documents are adopted only if they pass the L1 gate. The L1 gate uses the same 20-problem validation set and one-sided binomial test as the L3 gate (Section 3.1.4), but whereas the L3 gate keeps a revision unless it is clearly worse, the L1 gate adopts a candidate only if it is clearly better than the current skill. For example, if the current skill solves 10 of the 20 problems, a candidate must solve at least 12 to be adopted. The significance level is α = 0 .3,the same as for the L3 gate. Because merely re-running the same team changes the outcome of about a third of the validation problems, a strict level would fail to distinguish genuine improve-ments from noise and would reject almost every candidate (full thresholds in Appendix D.5). 

D.2.3 MemRL 

We use the query-based retrieval of the original MemRL runner. Only memories above the similar-ity threshold become candidates; they are ranked by 

0.5 z(sim) + 0 .5 z(Q), and up to five are selected. With probability 10%, the selection is random in-stead, with the random state fixed by the session seed, and memories with Q < −0.8 are excluded. All selected memories are added to the session prompt, each marked with its success or failure and its similarity. Every selected memory then receives a reward of r = +1 (success) or −1 (failure), and 

Q is updated as Q ← (1 − α)Q + αr with α = 0 .3.To create a new memory, the trajectory, includ-ing actions but excluding the system prompt that contains the retrieved older memories, is summa-rized as a procedural script. Failed trajectories are also stored, as “scripts of failed attempts.” The out-put is capped at 12,800 tokens and each memory at 12,000 characters. If the similarity to an existing re-trieval key is 0.8 or higher, the memory is attached to that key; otherwise, it is indexed under a new key. MemRL has no gate. We made five changes to the original. First, we use a local bge-m3 embedding model instead of the original text-embedding-3-large, and therefore re-tuned the threshold per benchmark so that about 16% of val–train pairs pass, using only the unla-beled task inputs; no labels or scores were used: 0.38 for GAIA, 0.62 for LoCoBench, and 0.56 for OfficeQA (the original also uses a different value per benchmark, ranging from 0.25 to 0.62). Sec-ond, the retrieval key uses only the problem con-tent, without the shared prompt template; with the template included, even unrelated GAIA problems reached a similarity of 0.64. Third, each role has its own memory bank, and every experiment starts with an empty bank. Fourth, updates are applied together at the end of each iteration rather than after each task, because sessions in a batch run concurrently. Finally, the model that writes memo-ries is fixed to the strong model regardless of team composition. 

D.3 L2: Relational Notes 

L2 produces two artifacts: a profile of each team-mate and collaboration notes for each teammate. A profile holds at most 5 strengths, 5 weaknesses, 3 notes, and 5 recorded contradictions, newest first, together with a cumulative observation count. Tier 1 can read the notes of the whole team, while Tier 2 can read only its own. Under collaboration and competition, a staged note is finalized once every teammate the author interacted with in the task has responded; if such a teammate finishes its L2 phase without responding, the note is finalized automati-cally. To check for contradictions, a strong judge is asked whether a new observation contradicts ear-lier ones and answers either NO or YES: <one sen-tence> . A contradiction is only flagged and does 14 not block saving. Since sessions in a batch run con-currently, if the file at save time differs from the version a session saw at its start, the notes written by other sessions are kept below the new note. In SkillOpt and MemRL, L2 notes are also passed to the L1 update as reference text. 

D.4 L3: Proposals and Review 

A new role is proposed as a config and prompt in a new folder, and a removal is proposed by marking the config as deleted. Proposals are not applied immediately but accumulate in a iteration buffer. Tier 2 agents cannot make proposals directly, but they can send a suggestion, which is added to the chairman’s review list. Under collaboration and competition, proposals go through peer review. A proposal passes once a majority ( ⌊m/ 2⌋ + 1 ) of the teammates who took part in the task, excluding the proposer, have com-mented on it. Without a majority, it still passes 180 seconds after its last edit, and teammates who did not comment are recorded as non-responses. Re-views are stored per version of each proposal file, so editing one proposal does not erase the reviews of another. However a session ends, whether by a timeout or by the chairman skipping, proposals not yet applied are passed on to the gate at the end of the iteration. As safeguards, the model of a new role is reset to the strong model in onlystrong teams and to the weak model otherwise, including weakstrong. 

D.5 Gates 

Both gates score candidates on the same 20 val problems, but they point in opposite directions: L1 accepts a change only if it is clearly better, whereas L3 accepts a change unless it is clearly worse. L1 follows the original SkillOpt, which keeps a new skill only if it scores higher than the current one, and adds a binomial test against the noise of the small val set. L3 instead checks structural changes that the team itself decided under its structure of interdependence, so it blocks only changes that clearly hurt and otherwise leaves the decision to the team. Both use α = 0 .3. Given the noise in 20 val problems (re-running the same team changes the outcome of about a third of the tasks), we use this relaxed threshold instead of a strict significance level. D.5.2 L3 Gate (single_check) 

The L3 gate first merges the proposals from all sessions of the iteration. For the same file, the last proposal wins, and the config and prompt of a new role are bundled into a single role addition. It then drops invalid proposals: removing the chairman, a new role without a config, and invalid paths. Under competition, proposals that change the chairman are also dropped, since the ranking decides the chairman. Next, the decision rule of the team’s structure 15 of interdependence (Table 12) determines which proposals to keep and in what order. The decision is made by the actual model of the team’s chair-man, and if its response cannot be parsed, all pro-posals are kept. Following this order, at most two role changes and two patch bundles are kept, so that each gate judges only a few changes; this cap applies to every condition, including none. In pre-liminary runs without the cap, teams that started with six roles grew to as many as 17 (LoCoBench, Meta-Team weakstrong). E Structure of Interdependence Specification 

We confirmed over repeated calls that both the weak and strong models follow the response for-mat of the decision prompt ( {"keep": [...], "order": [...]} ). Under collaboration, the decision follows an explicit rule that classifies each review as SUP-PORT, OPPOSE, or NEUTRAL and counts them, so the decision model’s own opinion is not taken into account. 

E.1 Competition: Promotion and Chairman Change 

At the end of each iteration, a score is computed for each role, and the sole top scorer becomes Tier 1 and the chairman for the next iteration; in case of a tie, the current Tier 1 stays. The score depends on the mechanism. Under SkillOpt, it is the current gate score of the role’s skill (the iteration-0 baseline or the last accepted score); under MemRL, it is the mean Q of the role’s memory bank; and under Meta-Team, it is the success rate on tasks the role took part in during its single training iteration. When the chairman changes, the original chair-man is recorded in the team configuration, and a notice of the change is added to the session prompts. The new chairman is told that it has taken over the chairman’s authority (receiving the task, calling roles, and submitting the final answer) and that it can still call the original chairman as a teammate. The original chairman and the other roles are told who the chairman is and that their own duties are unchanged. In sessions without a promotion, the prompts are identical byte for byte. The promoted chairman decides on its own whom to call, and we treat this behavior itself as an object of observation. The score has no minimum sample size, so a rarely called role can reach first place through an extreme value from a small sample (e.g., 1/1). We keep this rule and report, for every promotion, the role, its score, the sample size, and performance before and after (Table 13). Across the three bench-marks, the chairman changed 20 times in 16 of the 27 competition runs. Of the 12 changes under SkillOpt and Meta-Team, whose scores count tasks, five rested on three or fewer tasks, and on GAIA, file_agent, which takes part in only 2–9% of ses-sions (Table 22), became the chairman three times. The new chairman ran on the weak model in 13 of the 20 changes, including every change in Of-ficeQA Pro weakstrong, where the strong roles are the readers. 

F Execution Engine Details 

This section describes how sessions and reflection are run. 

F.1 Session Execution 

Up to six sessions run concurrently in training batches, gate scoring, and final evaluation. Ordi-nary messages are delivered when the recipient is waiting, whereas a phase-transition notice, if un-read, is attached to the agent’s next tool result and delivered immediately. If an agent sends the same recipient a message identical to its previous one, or retries a send that just failed (to a nonexistent recipient, to itself, or without content), the message is not sent and the agent waits until a new mes-sage arrives. Without this safeguard, agents never waited; they repeated the same message hundreds to thousands of times without ever receiving a re-ply. The remaining steps, team messages, and time are given as the last message of each call and are not stored in the conversation history, and the sys-tem prompt stays fixed throughout a session to keep the prefix cache. Once the history exceeds 151 mes-sages, only the latest 120 are kept. F.2 Deferred Reflection 

Reflection is deferred: sessions in a batch wait after finishing their task, and reflection starts once every task in the batch is done, three sessions at a time. Reflection has its own time budget, separate from task time: 300 seconds for L1, 900 for L2, and 900 for L3 (at most 2,160 seconds per session in total, including a margin). Debates are capped at 20 rounds in L2 and 30 in L3. Meta-Team is excluded, since its batch runs sequentially and it reflects right after each task. We defer reflection because, when it ran right after each task, finished sessions entering L2 re-prefilled their whole history while other sessions were still solving tasks. The resulting prefill load raised timeouts even in gate sessions (48/120 vs. 21/120 at step 0 in one LoCoBench run). 

F.3 Seeds and Repeated Evaluation 

Training and gate sessions are seeded by task ID, and final evaluation by a hash of (task ID, run), so all experiments share the same set of seeds. Since outcomes still vary across repeats (Appendix G), we report avg@3 with its SD. The SD reflects eval-uation noise only, not the variance of the single evolution run per condition. Going from three to five passes would shrink the standard error only modestly (from 2.9 to 2.25 points on the 99 Lo-CoBench test problems) at 67% more cost.

I Process Metrics 

Social scaffolding can change final performance only through what happens during evolution. We therefore trace its effect in stages: the structure of interdependence changes how agents reflect, re-flection changes the team structure, and the team structure changes how agents collaborate and how efficiently they solve tasks, which in turn deter-mines performance. We define metrics for each stage so that a difference in performance can be attributed to a specific change in process. All met-rics are computed from three records kept for every experiment: per-session event logs, which record every message, tool call, LLM call with its token counts, timeout, and session duration; per-iteration snapshots of the team, including its roles, prompts, and iteration result; and the decisions of the L1 and L3 gates.

I.6 Linking Process to Performance 

We connect the process metrics to performance in three ways. First, across all evolved conditions of the three benchmarks, we correlate the change in avg@3 from the preevolve baseline with the change in team size, timeout rate, and messages per session. Third, for conditions with large effects, we com-pare the evolved skills and team configurations directly with the templates. Where performance is near the floor, as for the OfficeQA onlyweak team, differences in avg@3 carry little information, and we describe these conditions through the process metrics instead.
