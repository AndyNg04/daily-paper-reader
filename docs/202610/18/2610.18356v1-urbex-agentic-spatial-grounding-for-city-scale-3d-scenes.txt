Urbex: Agentic Spatial Grounding for
City-Scale 3D Scenes
Yu Wang1,2, Rui Dai2,†, Wei Chen1, Yi Wang2, Bo Dang1
Kaikui Liu2, Xiangxiang Chu2,‡, Yansheng Li1,‡
1Wuhan University, 2DreamX Team, Alibaba Group
†Project leader, ‡Corresponding authors
City-scale 3D grounding is commonly formulated as candidate ranking, which requires precomputed
instance proposals and becomes inefficient in large outdoor scenes. We propose Urbex, an agentic
vision-language framework that instead casts grounding as active spatial search. Urbex represents
a city-scale 3D scene as an interactive multi-view environment, allowing a VLM agent to query
landmarks, zoom into local regions, render oblique views, and commit a grounded bounding box.
We optimize this tool-use policy with reinforcement learning on CityRefer, using smooth localization
rewards and lightweight shaping rewards for efficient exploration. Experiments show that Urbex
achieves the best Hit@1 and IoU on CityRefer, supports candidate-free grounding without ground-
truth boxes, and is substantially faster than the strongest city-scale ranking baseline. Zero-shot
results on STPLS3D-Refer further indicate improved cross-scene robustness.

Introduction
Grounding objects in 3D scenes from natural language descriptions (Liu et al., 2025a) is a core capability for
embodied intelligence, with applications in autonomous driving (Cheng et al., 2023), robotic navigation (Roh
et al., 2022), and aerial inspection (Li et al., 2025b). While indoor 3D grounding has been extensively studied
(Chen et al., 2020; Achlioptas et al., 2020), city-scale 3D grounding is still underexplored (Miyanishi et al.,
2023). Outdoor urban scenes are much larger than indoor scans, often containing hundreds of thousands
to tens of millions of points per scene (Hu et al., 2022). The associated referring expressions are also more
abstract, as a query may describe a small object through a named building, a road direction, or a distant
landmark, e.g., “a white car parked next to the orange car to the left of the West Midlands Police Custody
Suite.” Solving such queries requires both fine-grained visual recognition and reasoning over long-range spatial
cues.
Existing 3D grounding methods designed for indoor scenes (Chen et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023) typically
downsample raw point clouds to a fixed size, such as 50,000 points. When applied to city-scale scenes, this
strategy discards substantial fine-grained geometry and often leads to suboptimal performance, as illustrated
in Figure 1b. To avoid processing the entire point cloud, recent city-scale methods instead formulate grounding
as candidate ranking (Miyanishi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025a), as shown in Figure 1c. They first obtain
object proposals from ground-truth annotations or an off-the-shelf instance segmentation pipeline (Jiang et al.,
2020), and then compute text-instance matching scores for each candidate. Although effective in reducing
input scale, this formulation remains tightly coupled to precomputed candidates: its accuracy depends on
proposal quality, while its inference cost grows with the number of candidates to be scored (Luo et al., 2022).
More importantly, it reduces grounding to a static retrieval problem, whereas localization in a vast urban
environment is naturally sequential and exploratory (Chaplot et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019).
In this work, we instead formulate city-scale grounding as an active spatial search problem. Given a referring
expression and an initial global observation, a model should parse the target attributes and spatial cues,

We propose Urbex, an agentic spatial grounding framework for city-scale 3D scenes. As illustrated in Figure 1a,
Urbex starts from a global bird’s-eye view (BEV), uses landmark queries to establish coarse spatial anchors,
zooms into promising regions for fine-grained inspection, renders oblique views for 3D confirmation, and finally
commits a box that is projected back to world coordinates. Urbex represents each scene as an interactive
multi-view environment built around a BEV image and a consistent view-to-world coordinate mapping.
The VLM reasons over the accumulated interaction history and either calls another tool or commits a final
bounding box in a known BEV-aligned view. The system then projects the box back to world coordinates and
evaluates it against the target instance. We train Urbex with reinforcement learning on CityRefer (Miyanishi
et al., 2023). The grounding episode is modeled as a multi-step decision process. A terminal reward encourages
high overlap with the target and provides a dense center-distance signal when overlap is initially sparse. In
addition, tool-level shaping rewards guide intermediate behavior, such as zooming toward the target region.
This objective optimizes both final localization accuracy and the agent’s intermediate search policy. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose Urbex, an agentic framework that reformulates city-scale 3D grounding from exhaustive
candidate ranking to active spatial search.
• We design a city-aware interactive tool interface that enables a VLM agent to search large urban scenes
through landmark querying, local zooming, and oblique-view verification, and optimize this multi-step
policy with reinforcement learning.
• Urbex achieves the best Hit@1 and IoU on CityRefer, substantially improves online inference efficiency
over the strongest ranking baseline, and demonstrates strong zero-shot robustness on STPLS3D-Refer.

2.1
3D Scene Grounding
3D scene grounding aims to localize a specific object within a 3D environment based on a natural-language
referring expression. A large body of prior work has predominantly focused on indoor scenes, where early
methods typically encode entire point clouds or multi-view images, fuse them with a language encoder, and
rank candidate instances via cross-modal matching (Chen et al., 2020; Achlioptas et al., 2020; Huang et al.,
2022). Subsequent research has significantly advanced this paradigm by incorporating relation modeling,
multi-view context, and transformer-based architectures (Yuan et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023), and exploring
proposal-free, bottom-up localization to avoid error accumulation from segmentation models (Luo et al., 2022;
Chen et al., 2022b). Recent zero-shot and open-world methods further exploit foundation models, scene
parsing, multi-view reasoning, or scene graphs to reduce dependence on task-specific supervised training
(Huang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2024). These methods are highly relevant
to the goal of reducing proposal dependence, but they are primarily designed for indoor or object-centric 3D
scenes and do not directly address city-scale landmark reasoning, very large search spaces, or online efficiency
over dense urban instances.
City-scale 3D grounding presents challenges that are substantially different from those in indoor scenes.
Outdoor urban environments cover much larger spatial extents, contain many repetitive instances such as
vehicles and buildings, and often require reasoning over long-range spatial cues and named landmarks. As a
result, existing city-scale methods typically adopt a static candidate-ranking pipeline, where instance boxes
are obtained in advance, multimodal features are extracted offline, and a scoring model ranks all candidates
during inference (Miyanishi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025a). Although this formulation avoids directly processing
the entire city-scale point cloud, it remains constrained by the quality of precomputed proposals, requires
costly feature preprocessing, and becomes inefficient when many candidates must be scored. More importantly,
it treats localization as a one-shot retrieval problem. In contrast, Urbex frames city-scale grounding as
sequential spatial interaction, actively searching the scene through a small number of targeted observations.
2.2
Agentic Vision-Language Models
Modern vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated exceptional capabilities in visual question answer-
ing, fine-grained reasoning, and instruction following (Zheng et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2024;
Bai et al., 2025; Achiam et al., 2023). RL further improves reasoning capabilities (Chu et al., 2026). Building
upon these foundation models, recent research has evolved from passive perception to active, agentic systems
capable of calling tools, manipulating graphical interfaces, and interpreting complex visual environments
(Surís et al., 2023; Gupta and Kembhavi, 2023; Xie et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2026). Instead of outputting a
single-pass prediction, such agentic frameworks interact with their surroundings through a continuous loop of
observation, reasoning, action, and feedback (Yao et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2026; Yuan et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2026c,b).
However, much of the agentic VLM literature focuses either on 2D tasks, such as web navigation and open-
ended image reasoning, or on indoor embodied navigation (Ma et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024). Recent
work has begun to formulate 3D grounding itself as an agentic or sequential process, including dynamic
2D-to-3D reconstruction with visual tools (Wang et al., 2026a), active cognition over open-world object lookup
structures (Huang et al., 2025), graph-guided VLM reasoning over 3D scene structure (Liu et al., 2025b),
and task-oriented sequential grounding in indoor scenes (Zhang et al., 2024). While conceptually close to our
active-search view, these works operate in indoor RGB-D or graph-structured settings and are not optimized
for city-scale 3D environments. Urbex specializes the agentic VLM paradigm for this challenging domain by
providing city-aware spatial tools and an RL objective tailored for efficient exploration.
3
Method
Urbex formulates city-scale 3D grounding as active spatial search rather than static candidate retrieval. The
central design choice is to expose a large 3D scene through a small number of coordinate-aligned visual
observations that a VLM can inspect and act upon. We first describe how the environment converts city-scale
geometry into a VLM-operable multi-view space, then define the tool-use reasoning loop over these views, and
finally present the reinforcement learning objective that turns prompted tool use into a learned search policy.

3.1
Interactive environment for city-scale 3D grounding
Given a city-scale 3D scene S and a natural-language query q, the goal is to localize the referred object o⋆
and output its spatial extent. A conventional candidate-ranking method assumes a candidate set C = {oi}N
i=1
and learns a score s(q, oi) for each candidate, making the quality and size of C central to both accuracy and
efficiency (Li et al., 2025a; Miyanishi et al., 2023). In contrast, Urbex receives no candidate set and exposes
the scene through views V that the agent can extend through tool calls. It predicts a free-form box ˆbv in
a known BEV-aligned view v, which the environment maps to a world-space extent. Ground-truth boxes
provide training rewards and evaluation targets, but are not inference inputs.
View representation.
The initial view is a global bird’s-eye-view image Iglobal. Each BEV-aligned view v ∈V
contains an image observation Iv and a mapping Tv from the view coordinate frame to world coordinates. The
agent expresses boxes in a normalized 2D coordinate frame. The environment maintains the corresponding
view-to-world transform, so a box predicted in any such view can be projected back to world coordinates.
This design gives the VLM a simple and consistent spatial interface while preserving metric grounding in the
original 3D scene.
City-aware tools.
The environment provides three complementary tools for global anchoring, local detail
recovery, and 3D verification, keeping the action space compact. Landmark Query resolves a named landmark
from the query, such as a building or road name, and returns its world position and its projection in a selected
view. This tool provides global spatial anchors when the expression contains proper nouns. This design is
motivated by the geography-aware nature of city-scale referring expressions, where named buildings, roads,
and other landmarks provide strong global anchors (Miyanishi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025a; OpenStreetMap
contributors, 2026). Zoom-in maps a selected box to the original high-resolution BEV and returns a local
crop with a new view identifier and transform. The original BEV has a long side typically exceeding 10,000
pixels, so cropping recovers genuine detail absent from the downsampled global observation, where small
objects occupy only a few pixels. Oblique Rendering renders a selected candidate region from a specified
cardinal direction, producing a 3D-aware view that helps verify object shape, height, and spatial placement.
The oblique image is used as supporting evidence rather than as the coordinate frame for the final submission.

3.2
Agentic reasoning with multi-view tool use
The policy πθ is a VLM that receives the current observation ot and the interaction history ht. The history
contains the original query, previous tool calls, returned view identifiers, coordinate conventions, and visual
observations. At step t, the policy produces a reasoning trace and an action:
(thoughtt, at) ∼πθ(ot, ht).
(1)
The action at is either a tool call with structured arguments or a final answer. A tool call may specify
landmark names, a source view, a 2D crop box, a candidate box, or an oblique rendering direction. The
environment executes the tool and appends a textual response and, when applicable, a new image observation
to the history.
The resulting inference procedure naturally decomposes into global search, local observation, and candidate
confirmation. The agent first parses the expression to identify target attributes and spatial anchors in the
BEV, then zooms into promising regions to resolve small objects or dense local context, and finally renders
oblique views when top-down evidence is insufficient to verify the 3D object. Because every tool response
is appended to the interaction history, the model can refer back to previous views and maintain coordinate
consistency while switching between global and local evidence. This protocol can be used directly with a
prompted VLM, yielding a zero-shot tool-use baseline, but the search space remains large. The model must
learn not only how to interpret each view, but also when to query, zoom, render, or stop, which motivates the
RL training described next.
3.3
Reinforcement learning for active spatial search
We model an episode as a finite-horizon Markov decision process (Sutton and Barto, 2018). The state st
consists of the dialogue history, the current set of views, and tool feedback. The action space includes valid
tool invocations and final answer submission. Given an action and a scene, the environment transition is
deterministic; trajectory-level stochasticity comes from policy sampling. The episode terminates when the
agent submits an answer or reaches the maximum number of assistant turns.

4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental setup
We evaluate Urbex along three axes, namely in-domain performance, cross-scene generalization, and component
sensitivity. CityRefer (Miyanishi et al., 2023) is used as the main benchmark for comparing grounding accuracy
and inference efficiency, while STPLS3D-Refer, our evaluation set constructed from STPLS3D (Chen et al.,
2022a), tests whether the learned policy transfers to scenes with different geometry and visual appearance. We
convert both datasets into the same BEV-based interactive environment and use the same action protocol and
evaluation code for all tool-using models. Detailed dataset statistics are provided in Appendix B, while training
and inference protocols, hyperparameters, and other implementation details are provided in Appendix A.
Baselines.
We compare three groups of methods that stress different aspects of the problem. General-purpose
multimodal models, including GPT-5.4 and Qwen3.6-plus, provide strong visual reasoning priors. We connect
them to the same Urbex tool environment to test whether a powerful off-the-shelf VLM can solve the task
through prompting alone, without task-specific policy optimization. Indoor 3D grounding methods reveal
two different failure modes when moved to city-scale scenes. EDA follows the common indoor practice of
processing a downsampled point cloud, which discards much of the fine-grained geometry needed for small
urban objects (Wu et al., 2023). InstanceRefer instead operates in a candidate-ranking setting with provided
instances, which is easier than direct full-scene localization and therefore performs more reasonably than dense
point-cloud grounding methods (Yuan et al., 2021). City-scale methods such as CityRefer and CityAnchor
are closer to our setting, but they still primarily rely on ranking precomputed candidates rather than actively
searching the scene (Miyanishi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025a). Details on each baseline implementation are
provided in Appendix A.3.
Evaluation settings and metrics.
We report results on the novel description (ND) and novel object (NO)
splits: ND uses unseen descriptions in scenes observed during training, while NO uses targets from held-out
scenes that do not appear in training. In With GT, candidate-ranking baselines may use the ground-truth
candidate instance set and are evaluated with Hit@1, Hit@3, and Hit@5. Urbex does not use candidate boxes
during inference even in this setting; it always outputs a free-form BEV box, which is matched to candidates
only after prediction: candidates are ranked by 2D IoU with the predicted box, and Hit@k records whether
the target is among the top k, without a minimum overlap requirement. This measures target identification;
thresholded Hit@1 and direct overlap metrics additionally assess box quality (Appendix D.5). In Without
GT, methods cannot access ground-truth candidate boxes and must directly output a spatial prediction; we

4.2
Main results on CityRefer
Table 1 reports the main CityRefer results. GPT-5.4 and Qwen3.6-plus obtain non-trivial accuracy after being
connected to the Urbex tool environment, showing that strong VLMs can exploit the interactive views and
tool feedback to perform partial active search. However, their performance remains far below Urbex, showing
that these prompted tool-use baselines do not recover the accuracy of the trained policy. The indoor baselines
illustrate why directly transferring indoor 3D grounding pipelines is also insufficient. EDA almost fails on
both splits, consistent with the severe information loss caused by downsampling city-scale point clouds before
grounding. InstanceRefer performs better because it is evaluated as a simpler candidate-ranking problem with
provided instances, but it still remains far below city-scale methods and cannot directly solve candidate-free
localization.
Among city-scale baselines, CityRefer is limited when evaluated as a full ranking problem because its
original setting assumes that the target appears in a small 10-instance candidate pool rather than requiring
discrimination over all scene instances (Miyanishi et al., 2023). CityAnchor is much stronger because it scores
candidates with multimodal large language models, but this design requires applying the scoring model to
many instances and therefore incurs high inference cost. In the With-GT setting, Urbex achieves the best
Hit@1 and Hit@3 on both test splits and the best Hit@5 on NO. CityAnchor retains the highest Hit@5 on ND:
exhaustive ranking can retain several plausible GT candidates, including a target that Urbex misses during
regional search or confuses with a local distractor. Thus, broader candidate recall and precise single-box
search have different failure modes (Appendix D.6).

Inference efficiency.
Urbex is also substantially more efficient than the strongest ranking baseline under the
online timing protocol above. CityAnchor, the second-best-performing method in the With-GT comparison,
requires about 32.1 seconds per example because it scores many candidate instances, whereas Urbex takes
about 3.2 seconds. This amounts to an order-of-magnitude inference-speed improvement, since Urbex inspects
a small number of informative regions rather than exhaustively scoring all instances. The tools account for
about 0.36 s/query. Building the optional oblique cache takes approximately 2.3 hours across 12 CityRefer
scenes; rendering on demand instead gives an estimated 4.4 s/query (7.3× faster). These comparisons exclude
offline scene preparation; Appendix D.7 separates the measured cache cost from other, unmeasured preparation
components.

Zero-shot generalization on STPLS3D-Refer
We next evaluate zero-shot transfer to STPLS3D-Refer. The agent is trained only on CityRefer and is directly
applied to STPLS3D-Refer after converting scenes into the same BEV-based interactive environment. This
setting tests whether the model has learned a reusable spatial search strategy rather than a dataset-specific
ranking function. As shown in Table 2, the new scenes are challenging for all compared methods: indoor

after being connected to the Urbex tool environment, again showing that strong VLM reasoning can exploit
interactive observations even without task-specific training. However, Urbex achieves the best score on every
reported accuracy metric. These aggregate results suggest, but do not by themselves prove, that the RL-trained
policy learns reusable search behaviors such as anchoring, zooming, verifying, and stopping. To examine

adding distance shaping improves them to 30.6 and 10.9, tool-level shaping is also beneficial, and the full
reward performs best. Distance shaping is especially useful when early predictions have no overlap with the
target, while tool-level rewards make tool use more stable. Their combination shows that localization quality,
distance progress, and efficient tool use are complementary.
4.5
Analysis
Tool-use policy.
We further examine the learned policy using a fixed Landmark Query →Zoom-in →Oblique
Rendering →Answer workflow (Appendix D.2). In the reported experiments, the base Qwen model reaches
only 2.7 Hit@1, whereas RL-trained Urbex reaches 10.0 under the same tool order. The fixed workflow still
requires the model to predict tool arguments and the final box. The improvement therefore suggests that
training helps the model use the observed scene to make spatial decisions, such as selecting a crop and locating
the target within it. The workflow is also useful for a stronger prompted VLM: GPT-5.4 maintains similar
Hit@1 while reducing token use from 16,321 to 7,734. This suggests that the learned tool sequence provides
an efficient way to organize search, while the trained model contributes to how the tools are used.
Spatial search.
The landmark experiments further show the importance of geographic cues for city-scale
search. On CityRefer-NO expressions with named landmarks, the 10 m coordinate-noise setting lowers Hit@1
by only 1.2 points, whereas missing or incorrect landmarks cause drops exceeding ten points (Appendix D.3).
This suggests that a moderately inaccurate anchor can still guide the search, but losing the reference required
by the expression is substantially more harmful. Figure 10 illustrates errors at different stages of this process.
In (a), the first zoom excludes the target, and the agent selects another car within the crop. In (b), the
zoom contains the target, but the agent selects vehicles in a different parking row. Both predictions remain
unchanged after oblique inspection. The first case calls for revisiting the search region, while the second
requires closer comparison of nearby instances using the query’s spatial relations. These examples motivate
improving both region selection and local verification (Appendix D.6).
Localization quality.
We also examine the quality of the free-form boxes produced by Urbex (Appendix D.5).
On CityRefer-NO, replacing the original IoU matching rule with positive-overlap filtering or CIoU changes
Hit@1 by at most 0.29 points, indicating that target identification is stable under these matching rules.
However, additionally requiring the first-ranked target to have BEV IoU ≥0.25 reduces Hit@1 from 23.53 to
18.71. A prediction can therefore identify the correct candidate while still including substantial surrounding
context. The area-ratio statistics show the same issue: the predicted-to-target area ratio has median 1.40
and P90 9.92, indicating that some boxes are considerably larger than the target. These results suggest that
refining the box after target selection could further improve localization. We report direct IoU and point F1
alongside Hit@k to assess both target identification and the accuracy of the predicted spatial extent.

5
Conclusion
We presented Urbex, an agentic framework for city-scale 3D grounding. Rather than ranking a large set of
precomputed candidates, Urbex formulates grounding as active spatial search in an interactive multi-view
environment. We further optimize this tool-use policy with reinforcement learning under a limited interaction
budget, encouraging accurate and efficient exploration. Experiments on CityRefer show that Urbex achieves
strong grounding accuracy, supports candidate-free localization, and substantially improves online inference
efficiency over ranking-based baselines. Zero-shot results on STPLS3D-Refer further suggest that active spatial
search provides a robust alternative to static candidate retrieval for large-scale 3D scene understanding.

Implementation Details
A.1
Hyperparameters and Hardware
Urbex is initialized from Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025). Training and evaluation use the same
city-tool interface, action schema, coordinate convention, and execution code for both CityRefer and STPLS3D-
Refer. Unless otherwise specified, each trajectory allows at most eight assistant turns: up to seven tool calls
and one final answer turn.
Training configuration.
Urbex is trained on the CityRefer training split with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024):
rollout batch size 256, group size 8, 300 optimization steps, and one PPO epoch per rollout batch. The actor
learning rate is 1 × 10−6, the KL coefficient is 0.001, and bf16 training freezes the vision encoder. The reward
combines final IoU, center-distance shaping, a step cost, and tool-level shaping rewards, with λiou = 1.0,
λdist = 0.3, τ = 80m, and λstep = 0.05 in Equation 2.
Hardware and timing protocol.
Training uses 16 NVIDIA H20 GPUs. Local-model inference and timing use
a single NVIDIA H20 GPU; closed-source VLMs are accessed through their APIs. We report average online
inference time per query, including model generation and online tool execution, while excluding offline scene
preprocessing such as cached renderings and candidate-pool preparation for all methods.

B.2
STPLS3D-Refer Construction
STPLS3D-Refer is a cross-domain zero-shot benchmark for testing whether a CityRefer-trained policy
transfers to new urban geometry, appearance, and geography. It is derived from public STPLS3D RealWorld
photogrammetry point clouds (Chen et al., 2022a) and used only for evaluation. Because STPLS3D lacks
CityRefer-style expressions and object instances, we combine geometric instance extraction, geographic
registration, OpenStreetMap landmarks (OpenStreetMap contributors, 2026), and VLM-assisted expression
generation to build a diagnostic, semi-automatic benchmark.
Source scenes and target categories.
From four STPLS3D drone-photogrammetry scenes, we retain the two
with reliable public geographic context: University of Southern California Park Campus (USC, 94.7M points,
1083×807m) and Wrigley Marine Science Center (WMSC, 9.98M points, 490×293m). We exclude one scene
dominated by a single mega-building and one without verifiable public geographic context. Targets are limited
to Building and Vehicle.
Instance extraction.
Since STPLS3D provides semantic labels but not instances, we cluster semantic masks
with DBSCAN: buildings use planar xy coordinates with eps= 2.0m, min_samples= 80, and footprint filter
[4, 5000]m2; vehicles use xyz with eps= 0.6m, min_samples= 30, and footprint filter [1.5, 30]m2. From more
than 1,000 pseudo-instances, we remove fragmented clusters, merged objects, ground/noise artifacts, and
projection-ambiguous candidates through geometric filters and manual inspection. The verified pool contains
59 buildings/62 vehicles in USC and 11 buildings/17 vehicles in WMSC, with axis-aligned 3D boxes and point
masks computed for each retained cluster.
Geo-registration and landmarks.
Each scene is registered to WGS-84 by fitting a first-order affine transform
from 5–7 ground-control points selected against recent satellite imagery, with RMS residuals of 0.72m for USC
and 2.19m for WMSC. We query OpenStreetMap features using tags including building, amenity, highway,
leisure, tourism, office, and natural, project them back to local coordinates, and match landmarks to building
instances when applicable. The two scenes contain 278 real OSM landmarks.
Scene conversion and baseline compatibility.
Each scene is converted to the CityRefer interface: global BEV
image, view-to-world mapping, Zoom-in crops, optional oblique renderings, and annotations for candidate-
based and candidate-free evaluation. We also export candidate metadata and per-instance features required
by CityAnchor-style pipelines (Li et al., 2025a); ranking methods use explicit candidates, while Urbex receives
only the visual tool environment and submits a free-form BEV box.
Referring-expression generation and filtering.
For each target, we render a centered BEV crop together
with oblique side views from the four cardinal directions, mark the target and nearby same-class distractors
in all rendered views, and give the target color, size, distractor relations, and nearest OSM landmarks to
Qwen3.6-plus to generate three concise CityRefer-style expressions. The oblique views supply 3D-aware cues
such as building height, facade appearance, and relative layout that are difficult to disambiguate from the
BEV alone. Automatic filters check length, meta-language, landmark-name validity, projection validity, and
token-level duplication. The pipeline first yields more than 1,000 preliminary target-expression samples;
manual inspection then removes unclear targets, incorrect landmarks, severe instance artifacts, and ambiguous
descriptions, leaving 445 high-confidence landmark-grounded samples.

E
Limitations
Urbex depends on a task-specific interactive environment: reliable BEV rendering and view-to-world calibration.
Errors in calibration or rendering can project a plausible 2D box to the wrong world location, while absent,
ambiguous, outdated, or missing landmarks force the agent to rely more on visual search and local context,
reducing efficiency.
The current policy submits a BEV-aligned bounding box, which is efficient and compatible with city-scale
search but still leaves room for improving fine-grained 3D segmentation accuracy. A natural extension is to
expose multi-view 3D instance segmentation models as additional tools, allowing the agent to refine the final
prediction from a localized 2D region into a more accurate 3D instance mask or box.
Broader impacts and mitigation.
Beyond beneficial applications such as navigation, urban planning, and
accessibility, accurate language-driven localization at city scale risks misuse for surveillance, persistent target
tracking, sensitive-site identification, or automated monitoring. We mitigate these risks by training only
on public benchmarks (CityRefer, STPLS3D) without personal identifiers, releasing code, checkpoints, and
STPLS3D-Refer under a research-only license that prohibits unconsented surveillance and individual tracking,
and recommending privacy-preserving preprocessing (e.g., blurring faces and license plates) for downstream
adaptations.
