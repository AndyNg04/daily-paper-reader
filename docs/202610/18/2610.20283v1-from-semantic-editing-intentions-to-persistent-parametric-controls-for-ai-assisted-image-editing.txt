## Abstract 

AI-powered image editing increasingly allows users to express high-level editing intentions, yet how these intentions are translated into concrete editing actions often remains difficult to understand. We present Atelier, a semantic image editing tool that uses image se-mantics and user intent to determine which editing controls to surface and how to initialize them. Guided by a formative study

## 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in multimodal foundation models and genera-tive image models have expanded image editing toward semantic, instruction-driven interaction, allowing users to express desired changes to objects, regions, styles, and other high-level visual at-tributes [14, 15, 55]. However, expressing what should change still leaves the system to determine how that intention should be trans-lated into concrete edits. Making an image “more dramatic,” for example, might involve changes to contrast, lighting, color, or spe-cific regions. Subramonyam et al. [48] describe a related challenge as the gulf of envisioning : the cognitive distance between a user’s intention and an instruction that anticipates how an AI system will realize it. This challenge is particularly relevant in creative work, where intentions often develop through exploration. When an AI’s interpretation is expressed only through an edited result, users can evaluate the outcome but have limited opportunity to engage with the decisions that produced it. We therefore investigate how these interpretations can instead become manipulable parts of the editing interface. Prior work has helped users formulate editing instructions [2, 55], specify changes through visual and multimodal interaction [14, 37], and manipulate generative outcomes through graphical controls [18, 38]. Generative and malleable interfaces further demonstrate how AI can construct interaction elements at runtime [10, 35, 39, 54]. We investigate a complementary question: how can AI use the content of an image and a user’s intention to decide what should become directly manipulable? A formative study with eight image-editing practitioners showed that participants often began with semantic goals before translating them into technical parameters, worked with localized image regions, and valued AI assistance while wanting to retain the ability to refine and override its suggestions. Guided by these findings, we present Atelier , a canvas-based image editor that uses visual context and user intent to compose context-specific editing controls. Drawing on the notion of inter-action substrates [33], Atelier treats images and editable regions as persistent places where controls can be situated and refined. A vision-language model interprets the current image and the user’s intention to identify relevant editing dimensions and spatial tar-gets, and translates these into familiar parametric controls with proposed initial values. Because these controls use the same opera-tions as Atelier’s conventional manual interface, users can adjust, remove, combine, and reuse AI proposals rather than treating them as finished edits. Thus, AI proposes what may be useful to manip-ulate, semantic regions determine where those manipulations are organized, and users retain control over how they are ultimately configured. We evaluate AI-composed controls in a within-subjects study with 12 intermediate-to-professional image-editing practitioners, comparing them with manually configured controls in the same editing environment. Participants strongly preferred working with AI-composed controls and described them as supporting creative exploration, while continuing to substantially refine AI propos-als. At the same time, participants reported significantly lower felt control with AI composition, while comparative responses about control were more mixed. Interviews suggest that control depended not only on whether AI proposals could be modified, but also on whether participants understood why particular controls and values had been proposed. These findings suggest that making AI inter-pretations manipulable does not necessarily make them legible. Through this work, we contribute: (1) Formative study findings on AI-assisted image-editing practices, showing how practitioners move between semantic goals and editing parameters, work with localized image regions, and seek opportunities for manual intervention when using AI. (2) Atelier , an image-editing system that translates semantic inter-pretations of an image and editing goal into persistent, context-specific parametric controls that users can inspect and revise. (3) Empirical findings showing how practitioners use AI-composed controls as starting points for exploration and refinement, while revealing a tension between the ability to manipulate AI pro-posals and the ability to understand the interpretations behind them. 

## 2 Related Work 

Our work builds on two related directions: research that helps users express high-level visual intentions and manipulate their realization in image editing, and research that externalizes AI interpretations as adaptive, persistent interaction artifacts. These areas motivate Atelier’s approach of using image context and user intent to de-termine which familiar editing operations should become directly manipulable, where they should apply, and how they should be initialized. 

## 2.1 Semantic Intent and Parametric Image Editing 

Generative image editing increasingly allows users to describe de-sired changes at a semantic level instead of specifying individual editing operations. Recent systems combine language with sketches, masks, spatial annotations, and reference images to give users more direct ways to express both what should change and where that change should occur. MagicQuill [32], for example, combines coarse spatial interaction with generative editing, while Block and De-tail [45] and SketchFlex [31] support editing at different spatial and semantic granularities. Canvas-to-Image [16] combines text, spatial layouts, and references to specify different aspects of an intended result, and PromptCharm [55] makes relationships between tex-tual concepts and image regions inspectable and adjustable. work addresses the ambiguity of high-level intentions: Specify-and-Edit [28] decomposes underspecified instructions into more concrete edits, whereas Creativity-VLA [46] presents multiple pos-sibilities that users can explore instead of committing to a single interpretation. These systems reduce the need for users to formulate a complete low-level editing procedure in advance. A related line of work turns semantic properties themselves into objects or dimensions that can be manipulated. GANSpace [27], StyleCLIP [40], and Concept Sliders [22] expose interpretable prop-erties of generative models as continuous directions or controls. Interaction research has examined how such dimensions can be presented to users: GANSlider [17] demonstrates both the opportu-nities and interaction costs of exposing multiple semantic dimen-sions, while Tweeq [25] focuses on compact and precise parameter manipulation for creative professionals. More recent systems move beyond a fixed set of semantic dimensions. DesignWeaver [53] struc-tures text-to-image exploration around design-relevant dimensions, while Brickify [47] turns visual properties into reusable design tokens that can be directly manipulated. Across these works, some systems help users specify high-level intentions, while others expose semantic properties as interactive controls. Less explored is how context-dependent intentions can be translated into the familiar parametric operations of conven-tional image editing. This gap highlights an opportunity to bridge semantic intent and direct manipulation without requiring users to reconstruct that mapping themselves. 

## 2.2 Reifying AI Interpretations as Interactive Artifacts 

The idea that interfaces can adapt to users, tasks, or context predates modern generative AI. Mixed-initiative interaction emphasizes that automated assistance should remain observable, correctable, and under appropriate user control [26]. Systems such as SUPPLE [21] demonstrated that interfaces could be generated from models of users, devices, and tasks, while research on adaptive interfaces identified a recurring tension between relevance and predictability: changing which functionality is exposed can make an interface more useful in context while also making its behavior harder to anticipate [19]. Recent work uses generative models not only to produce content but also to construct interaction mechanisms. DynaVis [54] trans-lates natural-language visualization edits into persistent graphical widgets that can subsequently be manipulated. Bespoke [36] uses model reasoning to compose task-specific interfaces around inter-preted user intent, while generative and malleable interfaces allow interaction structures themselves to evolve with users’ tasks [10, 12]. These approaches shift part of the model’s contribution from gen-erating a result toward determining how users can subsequently interact with it. Several systems further emphasize reification : turning otherwise transient prompts, interpretations, or configurations into persistent interactive objects. Cells, Generators, and Lenses [29] introduces reusable objects for specifying inputs, combining model configu-rations, and inspecting outputs, allowing users to work with LLM interactions without reconstructing them through repeated conver-sation. AI-Instruments [44] similarly embodies prompted graphical commands as reusable instruments that users can inspect, apply, and refine. PromptCanvas [1] organizes parameterized prompt wid-gets on a persistent canvas. Across these systems, externalizing intermediate AI representations creates opportunities for direct ma-nipulation, reuse, and reflection that are difficult to support when the interaction remains confined to a transient prompt–response exchange. This perspective also connects to work on interaction substrates. Mackay and Beaudouin-Lafon [33] describe substrates as persistent places that organize information and the operations that act upon it. Related work uses compositional structures as substrates for organizing human–AI co-creation [9]. DesignTrace [41] applies this idea to visual design by reifying design rationale into inter-connected semantic and visual artifacts that can be transformed, revisited, and reused across an evolving design process. Its seman-tic substrate supports extracting, creating, and composing design attributes, while its visual substrate supports global and localized visual editing. These works show the value of making AI-mediated interactions persistent, reusable, and directly manipulable. At the same time, adaptive and generated interfaces introduce a tension between con-textual relevance and predictability: the more a system determines what interaction elements should appear, the more important it becomes that users can understand, revise, and continue working with those choices. This gap highlights an opportunity for inter-faces that make AI interpretations persistent parts of the interaction space without separating them from the familiar structures and operations through which users already work. 

## 3 Formative Study 

To understand how professional editors currently approach AI-assisted image editing and identify opportunities for improved interaction, we conducted a formative study with eight participants (F1-F8). Participants had experience with AI-powered editing tools such as Adobe Photoshop, Lightroom, Figma, and generative AI features. Through a semi-structured interview, we explored their editing workflows, use of AI-assisted tools, strategies for selecting and manipulating image regions, challenges encountered during editing, preferences for interaction techniques, and expectations for future AI-assisted interfaces (Table 1). Interview responses were an-alyzed using deductive thematic analysis by two researchers [3–5]. First the two researchers familiarized themselves with the data, and independently coded the interviews aligned with the preliminary themes. The preliminary themes included: goal-orientated editing, mixed initiative interaction, context-aware editing, transparent AI as-sistance, and reflection. Then the two researchers discussed, refined, or eliminated interpretations until consensus was reached.

Across participants, editing was described as a process that begins with semantic and perceptual goals, selectively incorporates AI to reduce effort, and relies on context-appropriate controls while preserving user agency. 3.1.1 Users Think in Semantic Goals Rather Than Technical Param-eters. Participants often described their editing intentions in terms of the visual or perceptual outcome they wanted to achieve, rather than the technical parameters needed to produce it. F3 described thinking in terms of “warmer, darker, more dramatic” and added, “I think in feelings.” F6 described this distinction more explicitly: “I usually start with goals first [...] like ‘make this warmer’ or ‘bring focus to the subject,’ or ‘make the background less distracting.’ That helps me define what I want the viewer to feel rather than getting stuck in numbers.” Technical parameters were still important, but often entered later as a means of realizing these intentions. F6 de-scribed shifting from an initial goal into adjustments of brightness, contrast, saturation, curves, and color balance, characterizing the process as “a back-and-forth process.” Thus, participants did not necessarily think exclusively in either semantic or technical terms. Instead, their workflows often began with a desired visual outcome and subsequently translated that outcome into concrete editing operations. 

3.1.2 Users Value AI Assistance but Want to Remain in Control. 

Participants valued AI for reducing the effort required for time-consuming editing operations, but did not view it as a replacement for manual editing. Instead, they selectively moved between AI and manual tools depending on the task. F1, for example, described this division as “For fast editing I prefer AI, but for final presentation I prefer manual editing.” At the same time, participants were cautious about losing control over the final result. F8 characterized AI as “a hit or miss and you lack control for the final result.” This uncertainty meant that AI-generated results were often treated as intermediate rather than final outcomes, with participants returning to manual tools when greater precision was needed. For some participants, retaining control was not only a response to imperfect AI outputs, but also an important part of creative ownership. F3 (Hobbyist) explained, “I think that art and editing is something that is very personal, each person has its own taste, its own trained eye of what they like and what not [...] It’s something personal that an AI can’t take over.” 

3.1.3 Users Want Context-Aware Editing Controls. Participants em-phasized that useful editing controls depend on both the image content and the particular attribute being manipulated. They en-visioned interfaces that adapt their controls to the current editing context. F8, for example, explained, “I think every control is fine de-pending on the context. For skin color maybe a tuned down color picker would be nice. For night and day a slider, for something else a toggle might be fine.” . Other participants described context-awareness in terms of the relationships between elements in an image. F4, for example, wanted assistance that could account for lighting rela-tionships rather than treating individual elements independently, describing a tool that could identify “where the light source is com-ing from” and use this information to match lighting and shadows within the composition. This suggests that context-aware assistance may need to reason not only about individual objects, but also about their visual relationships. Participants also envisioned AI helping identify the semantic content of editable regions. F6 wanted to 

“immediately understand and refine what the tool thinks that region is (skin, hair, sky, shadow, etc.), instead of just seeing a flat mask.” 

They further suggested “Contextual controls based on selection would be very useful, especially if they are intelligent and minimal” but only as an addition, never a replacement for the general controls practitioners already know (F3). 

3.1.4 Transparency and Recoverability Support Trust. Participants emphasized that working effectively with AI required being able to inspect and evaluate its behavior, particularly because gener-ated results were not always predictable. F8 characterized AI as 

“a hit or miss,” while F5 described the difficulty of repeatedly ad-justing prompts without reaching the intended result: “Sometimes you improve your prompt, but still you are missing the expected re-sult.” . These experiences made mechanisms for understanding and evaluating AI-generated changes particularly important. Before-and-after comparisons were one recurring strategy for inspecting changes. F3 explained, “I usually use the toggle functionality to see a before and after—when it comes to AI I think that transparency is key.” F1 similarly emphasized comparison when evaluating ed-its, stating, “I want to see the before and after.” Such mechanisms allowed participants to assess AI-generated changes against the original image rather than accepting an output at face value. Par-ticipants also wanted greater visibility into how AI-assisted effects were produced. F6 explained, “If I can’t see how an effect is being applied, it becomes harder to trust the result or refine it properly.” 

Here, transparency was not necessarily about understanding the internal workings of the model, but about making the relationship between an editing action and its visual consequence legible. Fi-nally, the participants emphasized the importance of being able to recover from unsuccessful results. These responses indicate that participants did not expect AI to always be correct; instead, they wanted failures to remain inexpensive to inspect, reverse, and refine. 

## 3.2 Design Goals 

Based on these findings and prior work, we derived three design goals for AI-assisted image-editing interfaces. 

DG1 Bridge semantic intentions and parametric operations. 

Participants often began with high-level visual goals before considering the technical parameters needed to realize them. Interfaces should therefore translate semantic intentions into inspectable and editable parametric operations, without re-quiring users to identify the relevant controls themselves. 

DG2 Decompose editing into spatially scoped sub-tasks. Par-ticipants expected different image regions to benefit from context-appropriate controls while having access to familiar general-purpose tools. Interfaces should let users decompose an image into the regions they are already thinking about — an object, a subject, a sky — and scope controls to that region, whether those controls are configured manually or composed by an AI, while keeping the full general-purpose surface available at all times. 

DG3 Preserve user control and authorship. Participants em-phasized that trust in AI develops incrementally as users inspect and evaluate its behavior during collaboration. Inter-faces should therefore support comparison, previews, undo where appropriate, and localized refinement, as these mecha-nisms are essential to preserving users’ sense of control when AI composes the editing controls. 

## 4 Atelier 

Based on the design goals we build Atelier, which is a canvas-based image editor that augments a conventional image editing interface with AI-composed controls. Images, extracted regions, and editing widgets coexist on the canvas, while a standard control panel provides access to the full set of conventional editing operations. Users can invoke AI assistance as needed and refine the proposed controls through direct manipulation. We provide a supplementary video to this submission showing the key interactions supported by the system. 

## 4.1 Interface and Interaction 

4.1.1 Interpret Image and Intent. Once an image 1 is uploaded, Ate-lier analyzes its visual content and characteristics, including recog-nizable objects and regions, dominant colors, tonal properties, and other features that may provide context for editing. As illustrated in Figure 2, this process begins with the current image (Figure 2-1) and an analysis of its visual context (Figure 2-2). Users can then express an editing intention in terms of the visual result they want to achieve rather than the technical operations required to produce

it. For example, a user may request to “make the leaves pop” (Fig-ure 2-3). Atelier interprets this intention together with the current image rather than treating the phrase as a fixed command. What it means for the leaves to “pop” depends on properties such as their current brightness, contrast, color, and visual relationship to their surroundings. Atelier’s context-aware interpretation therefore establishes the semantic starting point for DG1 : users can begin with a desired vi-sual effect without first identifying the technical parameters needed to achieve it. 

4.1.2 Identify Relevant Editing Dimensions. Based on this interpre-tation, Atelier identifies a small set of editing dimensions that may contribute to the intended effect. These dimensions describe percep-tually meaningful aspects of the image that could be manipulated, such as lighting, contrast, color, or detail. Editing dimensions can also be identified without an explicitly stated intention. After analyzing the image, users can invoke Sug-gest something , as shown by the lower branch in Figure 2. Atelier then uses the image context itself to identify a potentially useful editing direction and proposes corresponding dimensions (Figure 2-4). These dimensions support DG1 by turning a high-level intention into concrete aspects of the image that can be edited. At this stage, Atelier determines what may be useful to manipulate. 

4.1.3 Compose Parametric Widgets. Atelier translates the iden-tified editing dimensions into concrete parametric operations. A single stated intention can produce a composition of one to six independently refinable controls. Figure 3 illustrates this transla-tion for the intention “make the leaves pop.” Atelier composes two operations ( Leaf contrast and Crisp detail ) that together realize the identified editing dimensions (Figure 3). A planner selects which operations from Atelier’s predefined registry should participate in the composition, while a resolver assigns their initial parameter values. Because the operations come from this registry, their pa-rameter types, valid ranges, and corresponding interface controls are already defined. Instead of applying the system’s interpretation as a finished transformation, Atelier externalizes it as familiar editing operations and proposed parameter values that users can inspect and manipu-late (DG1) . AI composition does not replace Atelier’s conventional editing interface. A dedicated sidebar provides persistent access to the complete set of standard editing operations, allowing users to manually choose and adjust controls independently of the semantic composition process. AI-composed widgets and sidebar controls draw from the same operation registry and modify the same un-derlying editing state. A change made through an AI-composed widget is reflected in the corresponding conventional control, and vice versa. All operations use the same widget vocabulary. As shown in Fig-ure 4, widgets share a common shell while their parametric body adapts to the represented operation. The examples show White Balance, Levels, and Color, whose bodies expose different controls while retaining common interaction elements. Canvas ports addi-tionally keep widgets visibly connected to the image or region they affect. 4.1.4 Identify and Scope Regions. Alongside determining what may be useful to manipulate, Atelier determines where an edit should apply. As illustrated in Figure 5, the spatial target may encompass the complete image when an adjustment is global (Figure 5-A), correspond to a recognizable object selected in Object Mode (Fig-ure 5-B), or describe an arbitrary region defined manually with the lasso tool (Figure 5-C). An adjustment initially proposed for the complete image can instead be redirected to a particular object, or an object-level target can be further narrowed using a lasso or mask. Once established, a target remains available on the canvas be-yond the operation that created it. Objects selected in Object Mode or via Lasso can be dragged out of the image and positioned inde-pendently on the canvas (Figure 5-D). Dragging the object back onto its source image rejoins it at its original position while preserving the edits made to the extracted object. The extracted content is rein-tegrated into the source as a separate layer. In line with DG2 , these interactions allow users to decompose an image into regions corre-sponding to the sub-tasks they are already considering and bind controls to the specific target for which an adjustment is relevant. In this way, Atelier provides spatial granularity .

4.1.5 Revisit, Compare, and Reuse. Once created, Atelier’s controls and visual elements remain on the canvas. This persistence allows users to return to earlier decisions, compare alternative directions, and recombine existing controls and targets as their editing process develops (Figure 6). 

Revisit. Previous controls remain directly manipulable, allowing users to return to an earlier edit and continue adjusting it. As shown in Figure 6-1, a previously created Curves control remains connected to its target and can be readjusted as the desired appearance changes. Users can similarly expand composed controls, modify individual parameters, reset them, or remove proposals that no longer fit their intention. 

Compare. Users can duplicate an image or extracted object and continue editing the copy independently, allowing alternative im-age states to coexist on the canvas. Figure 6-2 illustrates this by showing two versions of the same image with different adjustments. Rather than committing immediately to one result, users can keep these alternatives visible and compare how different controls or pa-rameter configurations affect the image. Earlier states can therefore become starting points for further exploration. 

Connect and reuse. Controls and visual targets are linked ex-plicitly on the canvas and can be reconnected without recreating the underlying edit. As shown in Figure 6-3, a single widget can be connected to multiple targets, allowing the same editing config-uration to be reused across all. Conversely, multiple widgets can be connected to the same target, allowing independently created adjustments to contribute to a shared result. Users can reuse an existing edit by redirecting or extending its connections instead of specifying the same operations again. These interactions keep editing persistent and revisable: AI-composed operations and their spatial targets remain visible, while unsuccessful directions can be adjusted, removed, or compared 

(DG3) . This persistence can also support sensemaking and intent-making [8, 49], as users revisit prior edits, compare alternatives, and refine both their understanding of the current state and what they want to achieve. 

## 4.2 Technical Implementation 

Atelier uses a decoupled frontend–backend architecture (see Fig-ure 7). The React frontend renders the canvas, node workspace, and editing controls, while a Python backend built with FastAPI manages the parameter state that determines the rendered image and stores outputs produced by the vision-language models. 

4.2.1 Image Analysis and Operation Planning. When an image is loaded, Claude Opus 4.7 produces a structured image context de-scribing properties such as salient regions, dominant colors, tonal characteristics, and potential editing issues. This context is cached and reused by subsequent model calls. Given this image context, the user’s intention, and any selected target region, a Claude Opus 4.7 2

planner selects relevant operations from Atelier’s predefined tool registry. The tool registry specifies the available operations, their parameters and valid ranges, and their associated control types. The planner determines which operations should be exposed and grouped together, while their concrete parameter values are re-solved in the subsequent composition stage. The same mechanism supports autonomous suggestions. When the user requests a sug-gestion without specifying an edit, the planner uses the cached image context to identify a potentially useful editing direction. For corrective suggestions tied to measurable image properties, Ate-lier additionally checks the relevant condition on a downsampled rendering before presenting the proposal. 

4.2.2 Widget Composition and Value Resolution. After the planner selects relevant operations, a second Claude Opus 4.7 call acts as the resolver , assigning their initial parameter values. Related pa-rameters are resolved jointly to form a coherent editing proposal. Each parameter is then rendered using the control type defined in the editing registry—such as a slider, curve, color control, or toggle—and bound to the same shared editing state as the con-ventional controls. ## 5 Study Design 

Our evaluation focuses on using image context and editing inten-tions to compose manipulable parametric controls. We therefore designed a within-subjects, mixed-methods study comparing Ate-lier’s AI-composed controls with a Baseline that retained the same canvas, conventional editing controls, and commonly available AI-assisted capabilities, including object segmentation, lasso and generative fill. By holding these capabilities constant and varying the semantic and parametric control-composition layer, we aimed to examine how AI involvement in selecting and initializing famil-iar editing operations changes practitioners’ editing experience, interaction behavior, and sense of control. We conducted the study with 12 image-editing practitioners. The study was reviewed and approved by the institution’s Research Ethics Committee. Within this comparison, we investigated the following research questions: 

RQ1 How can image-editing interfaces translate high-level edit-ing intentions into context-specific, directly manipulable parametric controls? 

RQ2 How does translating editing intentions into AI-composed parametric controls affect practitioners’ creative exploration and editing experience compared with manually configuring controls? 

RQ3 How does AI involvement in selecting and initializing para-metric controls affect practitioners’ felt control and sense of authorship when its proposals remain adjustable, reversible, and optional? 

## 5.1 Experimental Setup 

Participants. Our target users are intermediate to professional image-editing practitioners familiar with conventional tools and workflows. We focus on users with some experience of AI-assisted editing, without assuming expertise in prompting. We recruited 

> 7https://replicate.com/black-forest-labs/flux-fill-pro

twelve participants, evenly split between intermediate and pro-fessional experience based on self-assessment. Nine participants identified as male and three as female; mean age was 28.4 years (𝑆𝐷 = 2.2). Participants were recruited through convenience sam-pling (details provided in Appendix F). 

Conditions. We chose two conditions: AI-composed and the base-line condition, that had the same interface, including the canvas, the side panel with the photo editing controls, and the command palette. The AI-composed condition added autonomous sugges-tions (4.1.2), and composed parametric widgets (4.1.3). The baseline condition did not include the context-aware adaptive widget, but did include the AI-backed capabilities click-to-segment selection (4.2.3), generative fill (4.2.4), and the palette’s Ask mode (4.2.2). This allowed us to study how this changes the experience of the context aware, granular editing, while keeping the baseline comparable to common photo editing applications. 

Photo-Editing Briefs. Participants were asked to edit two images (in Appendix B) and received a three-part brief. The first part of the brief was a corrective task, asking to restore the degraded photo so the scene looks natural and true to life, each brief naming its image’s defect. The target image was given, to allow the editing steps to stay comparable across participants and conditions. The second part stated a region goal under the protection constraint of brightening or restoring the subject so its color reads clearly, without changing the background. By stating the region goal in the brief guaranteed that every participant performed the selection-aware, region-scoped work the pre-study found. The third part was open-ended and creative, asking participants to develop the image toward a final look of their own choosing. 

## 5.2 Study Procedure 

The study was conducted remotely over Zoom and lasted 60 to 90 minutes. We asked participants to think aloud, collected screen and audio recordings and logged system events (with consent). To prevent order effects we counterbalanced with a 2x2 latin square (Photo x Interface Condition). The study protocol followed four steps: (1) Introduction: First participants received a consent form and a short demographics questionnaire. Then a recorded introduction video of the tool was shown that gave every participant the same walkthrough of the editor. (2) Photo-editing tasks: (2x20 minutes) The participants were asked to edit two different images, with a three part brief to cover different use cases with the tool (Appendix B). For this, the participants edited one photo with the baseline and the other with the AI layer on, in a counterbalanced order. Each block presented one photograph and the same three-part brief, following the format of a plausible editing session from a global correction to a region-scoped fix to an open editorial finish. The participants were asked to work as they normally would. The brief order was fixed, because the parts built on one another. After each block, the participant completed the per-condition questionnaire. (3) Comparative Questionnaire: Participants answered 7 items that compared the two systems (Appendix D.2). (4) Semi-structured Interview: A semi-structured interview was conducted that followed a six-theme guide following the research questions. The themes included: interface and widget affordances, creativity and exploration, control, trust and authorship, workspace and history, and reflection. 

## 5.3 Data collection and analysis 

During the study, we collected screen and audio recordings from the think aloud tasks and the interviews. We also collected ques-tionnaire responses, using NASA-TLX [23, 24], System Usability Score [6], and Creativity Support Index (CSI) [13]. We also defined a set of trust and control items grounded in the trust-in-automation constructs identified by Lee and See [30] (Appendix D.1), and a di-rect between-condition contrast, which we self-defined (see Appen-dix D.2). We also recorded an event journal where we logged time and distinct operations for every part. The exit interview covered questions about the interface, creativity, authorship, workspace and reflection. We analyzed the data as follows: 

• Questionnaire Data : The paired measures were computed under paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with effect sizes, de-pending on the normality computed with Shapiro-Wilk on the paired differences. 

• Logged System Data: The interaction logs were analyzed be-tween the two conditions, comparing time on task, individual parameter-value writes per minute through manual and AI, and mask use. The interaction events included widgets created, ac-cepted, deleted, updated, prompts entered, and compare hold. In the AI-composed conditions descriptive statistics were computed for analysis, analyzing widgets creation, coexistent widgets, AI acceptance rate and refinement behavior. 

• Interview Data: The recordings were transcribed and translated, as the interviews were conducted in German. For this, we used Whisper from OpenAI locally on a secure computer [42]. Our research team is fluent in English and German, which allowed us to ensure an accurate translation. We listened to the recordings and checked the correctness of the translation. We analyzed the transcripts using a deductive thematic analysis, as in the forma-tive study (Section 3), applying the methodology from Braun and Clarke [3–5]. 

## 6 Results 

In this section, we present the results of our mixed-methods study. We start by reporting the descriptive statistics from the question-naire. Then we present the results of the analysis of the logged system data. Finally, we present the results of the thematic analysis, which allows us to get a detailed understanding of the participants experience with the tool. 

## 6.1 Questionnaire analysis 

6.1.1 Usability and workload were comparable, but participants perceived less mental effort with AI composition. The SUS scores (see Figure 10) and the NASA-TLX results did not reach significance between the conditions, and no individual NASA-TLX item differed significantly between conditions (Table 7). The final comparison survey however showed a clearer subjective preference regarding mental effort: six participants felt that the AI-composed version required less mental effort, three reported no difference, and three favored the Baseline (Figure 8). Thus, while the workload measures did not provide evidence of a significant reduction, participants more often perceived the AI-composed workflow as requiring less mental effort when directly comparing the two versions. 

6.1.2 Exploration and enjoyment were higher with AI composition. 

The overall CSI score was not statistically significant (Table 2). At the factor level, Exploration (𝑝 = .024) and Enjoyment (𝑝 = .049) were significantly higher with AI composition, while Expressive-ness, Immersion, and Results Worth Effort showed no significant differences between conditions. The final comparison was consis-tent with the higher Exploration scores. Eight of twelve participants felt that the AI-composed version helped them explore ideas or be more creative, while four reported no difference and none favored the Baseline (Figure 8). These results indicate that the clearest creativity-related benefit of AI composition concerned exploring possibilities and enjoying the editing process. 

6.1.3 Participants preferred AI composition despite some loss of con-trol and authorship. The final comparison (Figure 8) reflected this tension. Eleven of twelve participants preferred the AI-composed version overall, while one rated the two versions equally and none preferred the Baseline. Yet this preference did not mean that AI com-position increased participants’ sense of control or authorship. Six of twelve participants felt more in control with the Baseline, com-pared with three who favored AI composition and three who rated the conditions equally. This pattern was also reflected in perceived control (Table 8), which was significantly lower in the AI-composed condition ( 𝑝 = 0.046). Four participants felt that the final image was more their own work with the Baseline, compared with one who fa-vored AI composition; seven perceived no difference in authorship. At the same time, participants favored AI composition on other as-pects of the experience, including satisfaction with the final image. Future-use responses followed the same pattern. Nine of twelve participants wanted to use AI composition for corrective edits, and nine wanted to use it for open-ended creative edits. These findings show that participants’ overall preference for AI composition did not depend on feeling more in control or experiencing stronger authorship. Instead, they appeared willing to accept some reduction in these qualities when AI composition provided sufficient benefits elsewhere. 

6.1.4 Exploratory differences by expertise level. An exploratory analysis by expertise ( 𝑁 = 6 intermediate, 𝑁 = 6 professional) found few clear differences between the two groups. None of the combined measures, including SUS, NASA-TLX, and CSI, showed a significant difference in how the two groups responded to AI composition. At the factor level, mental demand was the only measure that differed nominally significantly by expertise ( 𝑝 = .027): it decreased with AI composition for intermediate participants, while changing little for professionals (Table 10, Appendix). No CSI factor showed a significant difference between expertise groups, although overall CSI was descriptively higher with AI assistance in both groups (intermediate: 𝑀 Baseline = 74 .17 , 𝑆𝐷 = 18 .33 vs. 𝑀 AI-composed =

## 6.2 System data analysis 

Interaction logs allowed us to analyze how participants used the AI-composed widgets, complementing their self-reports (Table 3). Time on task did not differ significantly between conditions (AI-composed: 𝑀 = 16 .57 min; Baseline: 𝑀 = 16 .82 min; 𝑝 = .791). How-ever, interaction density differed substantially. Total interaction events increased from 13 .34 to 39 .43 events per minute ( 𝑝 < .001), while participant-authored parameter edits increased from 10 .98 to 26 .15 edits per minute ( 𝑝 = .001). This suggests that AI composition increased hands-on editing activity rather than replacing it. Focusing on behavior within the AI-composed condition (Ta-ble 4), participants created an average of 10.58 widgets per session (𝑆𝐷 = 5.05). These were distributed between manually invoked widgets ( 𝑀 = 4.58), prompted AI proposals ( 𝑀 = 4.50), and au-tonomous AI proposals ( 𝑀 = 1.50), indicating that explicit user invocation was substantially more common than autonomous sug-gestion. Participants also kept several widgets active simultane-ously, with an average session maximum of 6.58 coexistent widgets. AI proposals were accepted without modification at a mean rate of .35 ( 𝑀𝑑𝑛 = .23, 𝑆𝐷 = .38). Participants accepted an average of 2.00 proposals and dismissed 2.58. Refinement was also frequent, with an average of 18.33 refinement episodes per session and 7.58 refinement actions per episode. These behavioral patterns suggest that participants primarily treated AI-composed controls as starting points for further manipulation rather than as finished outputs. 

## 6.3 Interview analysis 

The preliminary themes we got from our thematic analysis included: 

interface and widget affordances, creativity and exploration, control, trust and authorship, workspace and history, and reflection. Through the deductive analysis, we unified the themes creativity and explo-ration, Efficiency and Navigating Editing Complexity, Control, and Authorship and AI Involvement .

6.3.1 Creativity and Exploration. Our findings indicate that the AI-composed parametric controls inspired experimentation and unexpected directions. However this guidance also was perceived as steering and constraining exploration, being phrased as dilemma of expanding but also limiting creativity. 

AI suggestions encouraged experimentation and unexpected direc-tions. Participants described AI-composed controls as providing starting points they could experiment with rather than fixed out-comes they had to accept, tying in with subsection 6.2. P10 captured this exploratory attitude directly: “I’m allowed to mess around, right? [...] I can just go ahead and click on things and see what happens” 

(P10). Unexpected results could themselves become new creative directions. When an AI-generated edit replaced the requested grass background with a Swedish lake, P10 responded, “Oh, oh, I didn’t expect that. But now we’re just in Sweden. I think that’s great” (P10). P2 similarly became more playful as he grew familiar with the sys-tem, shifting from immediately solving the task toward exploring what the AI might suggest. This meant that accuracy was not always the only criterion by which participants judged AI suggestions. P1 estimated that sug-gestions were only “about 50 percent or maybe 60 percent” accurate, but considered this acceptable during exploration because “that’s what you want – to be able to try out different things. And maybe you also want to see what isn’t right” . Participants also valued that this experimentation remained open to further manipulation. P11 described the interface as providing “a lot of room to work with” 

and allowing users to “really let your creativity run wild” . Thus, suggestions could be useful not only when they correctly realized an existing intention, but also when they provided alternatives that participants could explore, reject, or develop further. 

AI guidance could steer and constrain exploration. The same sug-gestions that expanded the space of possibilities could also shape which possibilities participants considered. P1 described this ex-plicitly: “you’re more likely to accept those suggestions if they fit the project – even if I wouldn’t have come up with them myself” (P1). P4 also mentioned that the AI suggestions could implicitly shape the creative direction: “because you’re just working with what you have in front of you” . P5, one of the intermediate participants, saw this as an opportunity as the AI suggestions could help participants continue experimenting when they became stuck. P9 experienced a similar shift of the AI guidance. With AI composition, he became more absorbed in exploring “what options the AI was actually sug-gesting” and felt “a little more creative” , but also acknowledged, “I got a little hooked on what the AI was suggesting to me. And then I used my brain a little less” (P9). P1 characterized the effect as a dilemma: “[the creativity was] not really limited, but it’s just being guided in a certain direction [...] And of course, it’s also expanded it. So it’s just a bit of a dilemma [...] It goes both ways–a bit of a positive and a negative.” .This can be a problem if participants want to prompt unexpected or non-normative things. P3 for example wanted to change the color of the boat to green, but struggled with it. She talked about that “the AI understood what I wanted and which object. But there were some issues with the execution. It understood ‘brighter and more natural’ pretty well—brighter, warmer, and things like that. But when I wanted the boat to be green, the boat didn’t turn green” .This is important, as the AI suggestions guide the edits and also prime the user, P11 put it: “Even if you just catch a brief glimpse of an image, like the one the AI had in mind, you’re already being primed very quickly” . This is something they did not want, because they wanted to bring their own visual language and thinking into the image. Therefore, they continued to value the option to step away from AI guidance altogether. This also allowed them to work “old-school” , like working in “the ‘Photoshop 2006’ way, so to speak – build your own stuff and then you have your own result, which is completely in your hands” (P11). So, AI composition changed which possibilities participants encountered and where those possibilities came from, while making self-directed editing an important alternative.

6.3.3 Control, Authorship, and AI Involvement. One aim of this research was to understand the impact of the AI-composed, ma-nipulable controls on the perceived control and authorship of the participants. We found during the interviews, that participants located the authorship in how they directed and refined the AI-composed controls themselves. The possibility to adjust and reverse also preserved the perceived control, however this had its limits. 

Participants located authorship in how they directed and refined AI. Participants generally did not describe AI-assisted editing as either entirely their own work or entirely AI-generated. Instead, authorship depended on how the AI contributed and on participants’ ability to decide what remained in the final image. P10 described AI as “a tool I use” , emphasizing that “the AI does not choose the photo or the type of editing” (P10). Even when the AI introduced unexpected content, such as replacing the requested grass with a Swedish lake, they still located the final decision with herself: “at the end of the day, I’m the one who decides whether I want to keep it that way or not” . So, they characterized the resulting image as “a mix – it’s a co-op” (P10). However, participants also distinguished between different kinds of AI involvement. P7 did not consider AI adjustment of existing parameters such as exposure or contrast to constitute meaningful co-creation, drawing a distinction between parameter-level assis-tance and changes to the actual image content. An important part of this distinction was that users could manually refine AI suggestions. Rather than repeatedly prompting the AI to correct an edit, they valued that “here I can just adjust the sliders or the numbers myself, to fine-tune it exactly to my preferences” (P7). 

Adjustability and reversibility preserved control, but had limits. 

Participants felt more comfortable with AI involvement when its suggestions remained adjustable, reversible, and optional. P9, for example, did not feel pushed toward the AI’s suggestions because “I could just remove them again” . Similarly, P10 described AI assistance as “a task I assign” while emphasizing that “I still retain control over that task” (P10). In this sense, delegating part of the editing process to AI did not necessarily mean giving up control, as long as partici-pants could subsequently inspect, adjust, or reject its contribution. However, this control depended on the available range of adjust-ment. P2 found the suggested defaults reasonable, but in some cases the controls did not allow her to push an effect far enough: “when I turned the sliders all the way up, the effect didn’t go far enough” 

(P2). She also recognized that the relevance of generated controls depended partly on how clearly she communicated her intentions, noting that “it can only make suggestions as good as how precisely I ask” (P2). Thus, manual refinement provided a way to recover from imperfect suggestions. Beyond adjusting individual suggestions, some participants wanted control over whether AI intervened at all. P11 emphasized that it was “important that you can turn it off completely” (P11), reflecting a concern that continuously presented AI suggestions could subtly steer the editing process. Participants valued control at multiple levels: specifying their intentions, refining or rejecting individual outputs, and deciding when AI assistance entered the workflow in the first place. 

## 7 Discussion and Future Work 

In this work, we investigated how AI-assisted image-editing in-terfaces can translate high-level editing intentions into directly manipulable parametric controls. Specifically, we examined (RQ1) how such intentions can be translated into context-specific para-metric controls that remain open to refinement, (RQ2) how this translation affects practitioners’ editing experience and creative From Semantic Editing Intentions to Persistent Parametric Controls for AI-Assisted Image Editing , , 

exploration, and (RQ3) how AI involvement in selecting and ini-tializing controls affects practitioners’ felt control and sense of authorship. Across these questions, our findings suggest that se-mantic interface generation changes the role of AI in image editing. Rather than only producing an edited result, AI can participate in constructing the space of actions through which users continue to work. This can make relevant operations easier to access and provide starting points for exploration, but it also gives the system influence over which possibilities become visible and salient. We discuss these implications below. 

## 7.1 Translating Intentions into Refinable Parametric Controls 

Addressing RQ1 , our findings suggest that high-level editing inten-tions are most useful when translated into parametric controls as starting points for refinement rather than completed edits. Atelier interprets an intention in the context of the image and proposes relevant operations, spatial targets, and initial values that remain inspectable and adjustable. Participant behavior supports this ap-proach. In the AI-composed condition, interaction density nearly tripled and participant-authored parameter adjustments more than doubled, while task completion time did not differ significantly. Participants continued to refine, supplement, and reject AI pro-posals. So, the system helped identify relevant operations without replacing direct manipulation. This extends prior work on graphical interaction with generative systems [18]. Rather than responding to an unsatisfactory result by reformulating a prompt [51], users can directly adjust the system’s interpretation. Importantly, the proposed value need not be correct for the translation to be use-ful: a temperature control may appropriately represent “make this warmer” even if its initial setting is too strong. Similar to DesignTrace [41], Atelier keeps intermediate represen-tations available for continued interaction. However, rather than maintaining a semantic substrate of persistent design attributes, Atelier introduces a parametric substrate : semantic intent is trans-lated into familiar editing operations and values, such as exposure, white balance, curves, or color adjustments. Because AI-composed and manual controls share the same operation registry and edit-ing state, users can move between AI assistance and conventional editing without switching modes. The translation can also specify where an operation applies through persistent spatial targets. While this may help users track which controls affect which regions, our NASA-TLX results do not demonstrate reduced cognitive demand, and the Baseline offered the same segmentation capabilities. We therefore leave this as a direction for further study [52]. So, Atelier suggests one approach to semantic-to-parametric translation. Identify a small set of plausible domain-native opera-tions, initialize them as useful starting points, and keep both their values and their composition open to further manipulation. Instead of hiding technical editing behind a semantic command, semantic reasoning can help users reach the technical operations through which their intention can be realized. 

## 7.2 AI Suggestions Both Expand and Shape Exploration 

Addressing RQ2 , AI composition affected exploration. Exploration and Enjoyment were significantly higher with AI-composed con-trols. No participant preferred the Baseline for supporting explo-ration. AI suggestions supported exploration by turning possible interpretations into controls participants could immediately try. Even unexpected suggestions become useful starting points, expos-ing directions participants might not otherwise have considered. Interaction could help users develop their intentions instead of requiring those intentions to be fully formed beforehand. However, AI suggestions also shaped exploration. Participants sometimes became absorbed in proposed options or relied less on their own reasoning. Choosing which controls to surface therefore directs attention toward some possibilities while making others less visi-ble. Higher exploration should consequently not be interpreted as necessarily more self-directed or comprehensive exploration. Sim-ply exposing more options is unlikely to solve this tension, since additional controls can increase interaction difficulty [17]. Instead, semantic interfaces could help users move between AI-guided and self-directed exploration, for example by offering contrasting in-terpretations or preserving easy access to the full conventional toolset. Rejection can also inform this process. Rather than treating a rejected suggestion only as a failed prediction, systems could use it to infer users’ authorial boundaries [20] and offer meaningfully different alternatives. Because high-level intentions often admit multiple plausible realizations, semantic interfaces should support alternative interpretations rather than presenting a single transla-tion. 

## 7.3 Actionability Does Not Guarantee Felt Control 

Addressing RQ3 , our findings reveal a tension between prefer-ence and perceived control. Eleven of twelve participants preferred the AI-composed condition, yet participants reported significantly lower felt control with AI composition. At the same time, most did not report a weaker sense of authorship, and participants valued being able to adjust, reject, or remove AI proposals. This aligns with prior work showing that selection and manipulation can preserve agency in human–AI workflows [20, 34]. One explanation is that users retained control over how AI proposals were adjusted, but had less control over how those proposals were initially formed . Atelier gives users substantial execution control over AI proposals, but the AI still interprets the intention, selects relevant operations, and initializes their values. Users can refine what the system proposes while having less influence over how the editing space is initially structured. Direct manipulation can thus make AI interpretations action-able without making them fully legible. Future semantic interfaces could expose more of the translation process by allowing users to replace operations, redirect targets, request alternative interpreta-tions, or inspect why particular controls were proposed. Following mixed-initiative principles [26], evaluations of AI-assisted creative tools should therefore distinguish between control over execution, control over framing, and authorship. , , Amin et al. 

## 7.4 Limitations and Future Work 

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the study involved twelve practitioners completing two time-limited tasks in a single session. The small sample, limited task set, and use of think-aloud constrain generalizability. Longitudinal stud-ies with participants’ own images are needed to examine whether the observed exploration and refinement patterns persist over time. Second, the Baseline retained segmentation, generative fill, the canvas workspace, and conventional editing operations. This iso-lated the semantic control-composition layer, but means that effects cannot be attributed to spatial persistence or segmentation alone. Future studies could compare persistent regions with conventional panels and transient selections. Third, the current prototype provided limited visibility into why controls and initial values were proposed, and some interactions were constrained by parameter ranges, latency, and implementation limitations. Future work should investigate explanations, prove-nance, alternative semantic-to-parametric mappings, and mecha-nisms for modifying the structure of an AI proposal rather than only its values. Finally, our study focused on interaction rather than the aesthetic quality of final images. Future work could combine interaction mea-sures with expert evaluation or measures of alignment with users’ intentions, and examine how semantic-to-parametric mappings develop through longer-term use. Taken together, Atelier suggests that AI can support domain-specific tools by surfacing contextually relevant operations rather than replacing them. Such translations should remain provisional, revisable, and legible, giving users both the ability to manipulate AI proposals and sufficient authority over how those proposals are formed. 

## 8 Conclusion 

We introduced Atelier, a semantic interface generation approach that translates high-level image-editing intentions into context-specific, manipulable controls. Rather than producing only a fin-ished edit, Atelier externalizes the AI’s interpretation as widgets that users can inspect, refine, combine, or reject. In a within-subjects study with 12 image-editing practitioners, participants strongly pre-ferred AI-composed controls and reported higher Exploration and Enjoyment. AI composition also increased manual editing. Partic-ipants performed substantially more interactions and parameter adjustments while contributing most edits themselves. Yet they felt significantly less in control, showing that editable AI proposals do not necessarily preserve felt control when the AI determines which actions become available and salient. Together, our findings position semantic interface generation as a mixed-initiative process in which AI helps construct the space of possible actions. Future interfaces should therefore allow users to refine not only the re-sulting edits, but also the AI’s interpretation of what should be manipulated.

## A Implementation A.1 Executing Parametric Edits With WebGL 

The AI models do not directly perform ordinary adjustments such as exposure, contrast, white balance, HSL, or curves. Instead, once Claude has selected operations and proposed parameter values, these values are written into Atelier’s shared editing state. The frontend then executes the corresponding operations through its WebGL rendering pipeline. The registry links each editing operation to its valid parameters, interface representation, and rendering implementation, allowing an AI-set value and a manually set value to produce the same deterministic image operation. Thus, Claude acts primarily as a semantic decision-making layer, while WebGL remains the actual engine that computes and renders the parametric image transformations. 

## A.2 Runtime Stack 

The frontend is a single-page React app with no server-side ren-dering; it runs from one build either in the browser against a local backend or as a Vercel static bundle against a remote backend. The backend is a FastAPI app whose Pydantic schemas are the single source of truth for the wire format and the generated TypeScript types. ## B Task Stimuli and Scoring 

The three participant-facing briefs, verbatim (the sign versions state the mirror-image defect and its own background): (1) Corrective: This photo came out of the camera too dark and too cold. Adjust it so the whole scene looks natural and true to life, the way it would have looked if you were standing there. (2) Region under protection: The boat is still too dark. Brighten the boat so its color reads clearly, without changing the sea. (3) Open: To finish, make the photo your own. Explore what the editor offers and take the image toward a final look you would be happy to publish. There is no right answer. 

(a) Boat: too dark and too cold (under-cold).

The two photographs (Figure 9) are degraded from their retained originals by a fixed, invertible recipe baked into the DNG raw file: an exposure curve and a white-balance shift applied in opposite directions across the pair, so the boat comes out too dark and cool and the sign too bright and warm. Working from the raw file lets the prototype’s own raw-development path reproduce the defect exactly, and because the recipe is invertible the degradation is recoverable.

## C.1 Agent-Loop System Prompt 

The static base, extended at runtime with extra paragraphs when the request carries pre-selected targets, pinned objects, or reference images. 

You are an editing agent for a photo editor . The user gives an editing request ; you fulfil it by CALLING TOOLS , not by replying in prose . To apply any tonal / colour adjustment ( contrast , warmth , exposure , mood , etc .) you MUST call propose_adjustment_widgets with target_image_node_id set to an existing node id ({ targets }) and a short intent describing the change . To put an object on its own layer first , call copy_object_to_image_node , then propose_adjustment_widgets on the image_node_id it returns . Do not stop until you have called at least one tool that satisfies the request . 

The slot {targets} is the comma-joined list of node ids, or “the active image node” when none is supplied. Optional appended blocks cover forced targets, pinned mask ids, and reference nodes the model must not edit. 

## C.2 Planner Prompt 

Fully static; user intent and image context arrive as the user mes-sage. 

You are a photo - editing composition planner . Given a user intent and image context , return a stack of 1 -6 conceptually - grouped photo - editing widgets . Each widget can carry 1 -5 raw ops that belong together conceptually . Each widget becomes ONE card on the user 's canvas they can independently refine . Rules : - A widget is ONE perceptual intention the user would name out loud (" lift the shadows ", " warm it up ") . Group ops into the same widget only when a user would refine them together as a single unit . The ` category `

field is a strong default : ops with the same category usually belong together unless you have a specific reason to split . BAD grouping : levels ( tonal fade ) + grain ( texture ) in one widget just because both feel " vintage " - a user refining grain does not expect the blacks to move . - Prefer the MINIMUM set of ops that achieves the intent . Never add asecond op that pushes the same perceptual axis as one already planned (e.g. light exposure AND levels brightening ) -overlapping ops multiply and overshoot . - Give each widget a short , descriptive ` widget_name `

(2 -4 words ) describing the EFFECT , not the op (e.g. " Lifted blacks ", not "Levels op ") . - Give each widget a ` driver_label `: a 1 -2 word noun naming the INTENT AXIS its strength slider will control (" Blackness ", " Warmth ", " Drama ") - the quality the user asked for , not an op name . The frontend renders one slider with this label that scales the whole widget from " as shot " (0) to your resolved values (100) . - Prefer raw ops over presets unless the intent matches apreset closely . - You may unfold a preset 's ops as starting points and modify them . - Order widgets by intent priority ( most defining effect first ). - Emit the plan via the ` emit_plan ` tool . Example for " vintage film " ( abridged ): {" plan ": [{" widget_name ": " Lifted blacks ", " driver_label ": " Faded blacks ", " ops ": [...]} , {" widget_name ": " Warm fade ", " driver_label ": "Warmth ", " ops ": [...]} , {" widget_name ": " Film grain ", " driver_label ": " Grain ", " ops ": [...]} ]} , , Amin et al.

## C.4 Resolver Prompt (Whole Stack) 

The one-call variant that resolves the entire stack together, so over-lapping axes share the correction budget.                                                                               

> You are resolving numeric parameter values for an ENTIRE stack of photo - editing operations at once , given the user 's intent and image context . The stack 's COMBINED effect must achieve the intent . Budget overlapping axes : if two ops both raise brightness (e.g. exposure and ashadows lift ) , split the correction between them -never let each op independently achieve the full intent on its own . Prefer restrained values ; the user can push further . Use each op 's starting_params as astrong prior if provided . Emit one emit_stack_params call covering every ( entry_index , op_id )in the plan .

## D Questionnaires D.1 Trust and Control 

Two scales on a seven-point agreement scale (Strongly disagree to Strongly agree), grounded in the F4 dimension the pre-study surfaced as decisive (subsection 3.1) and the trust-in-automation constructs [30]. The control-and-trust scale (six items) is answered in both conditions. 

Control and trust (both conditions): 

(1) I felt in control of the editing process. (2) The final image reflected my own intentions. (3) I could predict what each control would do before I used it. (4) When I did not like a change, it was easy to undo or adjust it. (5) I always understood the current state of my edit. 

AI suggestions (AI-composed block only): 

(1) I understood why the system suggested what it did. (2) I could see what an AI suggestion would change before I accepted it. (3) It was easy to reject or adjust the AI’s suggestions. (4) I trusted the AI’s suggestions. (5) Working with the AI felt like a collaboration rather than something done to me. (6) The AI’s behavior was predictable. (7) The AI suggestions appeared without me asking and got in the way. (reverse)
