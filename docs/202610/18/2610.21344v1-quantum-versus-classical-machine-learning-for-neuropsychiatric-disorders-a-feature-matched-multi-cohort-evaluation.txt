# Quantum Versus Classical Machine Learning for Neuropsychiatric Disorders: A Feature-Matched Multi-Cohort Evaluation 

Utsav Poudel , Anuj Nepal , Vyas Viswanath, and Subramaniyaswamy Vairavasundaram 

Abstract —Resting-state functional connectivity is widely used in machine learning-based classification of neuropsychiatric dis-orders. Quantum machine learning models have been proposed for functional connectivity data, but few studies have evaluated them under leave-site-out validation or against matched classical models. We compared six quantum model families with classical ones using identical correlation-derived features across four pub-lic cohorts (4,308 subjects). Leave-site-out validation was used for the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE I), ADHD-200 (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder), and REST-meta-MDD (major depressive disorder) cohorts, while the UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics (UCLA-CNP) was evaluated using stratified 10-fold cross-validation. The quantum kernel was statistically indistinguishable from a matched classical kernel across all four cohorts, with the average accuracy difference within 1.4 percentage points at 95% confidence. On ABIDE I, a hybrid quantum convolutional network achieved similar accuracy to its classical counterpart (0.645 vs. 0.641), while a trainable quantum kernel exceeded its classical counterpart by 0.064 in area under the curve (AUC). Quantum convolutional networks also achieved higher AUC than their matched classical networks on this cohort and showed smaller train–test gaps on all four cohorts, without an accuracy gain. Overall, most quantum models matched their classical counterparts, but none consistently outperformed them. This study, among the first quantum evaluations of ABIDE I under leave-site-out validation, provides a feature-matched benchmark of quantum and classical models comprising 284 matched comparisons. We further analyse fidelity-based graph construction and account for the observed quantum–classical parity through effective-dimension and eigen-spectrum analyses. Code and per-fold results are available at https://github.com/r11up/qml-fmri-baseline. 

Index Terms —Functional magnetic resonance imaging, graph neural networks, machine learning, mental disorders, quantum computing. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being applied to psy-chiatric diagnosis, prognosis, and decision support systems [1], and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) is widely used as one of the few tools that enable non-invasive analysis of intrinsic functional brain activity. rs-fMRI pipelines begin by building a functional connectivity graph whose edges encode statistical dependency between regions of interest (ROIs). The Pearson correlation coefficient remains one of the most widely used measures because of its computational efficiency and interpretability [2]. However, it captures only linear, pairwise relationships and may overlook non-linear or higher-order dependencies between brain re-gions. For conditions with subtle, heterogeneous presentation, such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [3], non-linear de-pendence may carry complementary information, as reported for schizophrenia [4], but whether it improves generalisation to unseen acquisition sites remains an open question. Graph neural networks (GNNs) address this limitation through a different mechanism. Rather than replacing the linear edge measure, they keep the correlation graph and recover non-linearity downstream, composing non-linear func-tions of a node’s neighbourhood across successive message-passing layers. This paper investigates whether introducing non-linearity at the connectivity level, in the graph edges themselves, is advantageous compared with introducing it only within downstream models. The downstream route has a cost, since stacking layers runs into over-smoothing, where repeated aggregation over a dense, noisy graph pulls node representations toward a common mean [5]. The problem is worse when the topology is static and dense, which is the typical shape of a Pearson correlation matrix in fMRI. A classical vector encoded into a quantum state maps into a Hilbert space where relations that were non-linear in the original coordinates can become close to linear [6]. We encode correlation-derived regional features as quantum states, use the fidelity between them to initialise the graph’s topology, and train a classical edge kernel and the classifier on that support. The design is therefore a quantum-fidelity-initialised adaptive graph rather than an end-to-end quantum one. The graph topology is determined by the encoding component of the circuit alone. Following standard connectivity pipelines, every model in this study receives features derived from the correlation matrix, so quantum and classical models see identical inputs and any difference between them reflects the similarity function rather than the input representation. This design sits between two bodies of work that have, until recently, developed largely along separate tracks: classical adaptive graph learning, which treats the brain connectivity graph as a learnable object, and quantum machine learning applied to neuroimaging and other healthcare data, which has rarely been evaluated against matched classical baselines under arXiv:2610.21344v1 [cs.AI] 15 Oct 2026 PREPRINT 2

Machine learning has advanced quickly across psychiatry over the past decade [7], and two lines of that work converge on this problem: classical adaptive graph learning, which treats the connectivity graph as a learnable object rather than a fixed correlation matrix, and quantum machine learning applied to neuroimaging and other biomedical data. Neither has yet produced a system that builds a functional-connectivity graph from quantum measurements and trains it against a clinical classification objective. 

A. Classical Adaptive Graph Learning for Functional Con-nectivity 

The limits of static correlation graphs have pushed several groups toward making the graph itself a learnable parameter, optimised jointly with the network weights. AGMGC [8] replaces a predefined connectivity assumption with a learner module that infers structure from raw time series, augments it with a partial-correlation graph, and reaches 84 .65% accuracy on the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE), ahead of population-graph baselines. Its adjacency is still a softmax over classically learned projections: a more flexible classical similarity function, but a classical one. A second trend folds in auxiliary modalities to refine the graph. DAGMNet [9] fuses structural MRI (sMRI), fMRI, and phenotypic data (age, sex, and similar covariates) through a phenotype-guided population-graph module that prunes con-nections unsupported by clinical priors, reaching 91.59% accuracy and a 96.80% area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) on ABIDE I. Section X-F examines what that figure measures. Auxiliary context has also been paired with EEG, where a dual-tower model combining EEG with a graph-encoded environmental prior improved affective-state classification over EEG alone [10]. 

B. Quantum Machine Learning in Neuroimaging and Health-care 

A 2025 systematic review of quantum deep learning in neuroinformatics describes itself as a pioneering effort to compare quantum and classical approaches on brain data [11], and a 2025 survey of quantum machine learning pairs the promise of hybrid models with the noise and qubit-scaling limits of current hardware [12]. What does exist clusters around a few entry points into brain data. One entry point is the direct application of a quantum or hy-brid circuit to a time series or an image. Choi et al. use a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1-D CNN) front end with a quantum convolutional neural network (QCNN) on rs-fMRI [13]; Park et al. use a quantum time-series transformer on the same modality [14]. For ASD, Saranya and Menaka [15], Bagoun et al. [16] and Turjya and Fawakherji [17] apply quantum classifiers to electroencephalography (EEG). None builds an inter-regional adjacency matrix. The one quantum model we found evaluated on ABIDE I functional connectivity is QG-WRN [18], which places a four-qubit parameterised circuit inside a graph convolutional residual network on the 116-region Automated Anatomical Labelling (AAL-116) atlas and reports 68 .49% accuracy on a random 80/20 split of 775 subjects, a protocol its authors note does not decouple inter-site scanner variance. 

C. Quantum Architectures Beyond Static Fidelity 

The constraint this paper measures, that pairwise state fidelity concentrates as the register widens, is specific to kernels built from static pairwise overlaps, and a recent line of work avoids it by changing the quantum primitive rather than the register. The quantum time-series transformer of Park et al. [14] realises attention through a Linear Combination of Unitaries and the Quantum Singular Value Transforma-tion. Applied to resting-state fMRI, it processes temporal dependencies directly instead of collapsing them into a sim-ilarity matrix, operates at polylogarithmic complexity, and reports performance competitive with classical transformers on the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) and UK Biobank cohorts. It targets the bottleneck that Section VIII-A measures without widening the register, and reports its gains as most pronounced in small samples, the regime in which our quan-tum arm overfits less (Section X-A) without gaining accuracy (Section X-D). We do not evaluate it here and identify it as the most natural next comparison for this benchmark. 

D. Positioning of This Work 

Against the classical adaptive graph learning of Sec-tion II-A, the difference is the similarity function: quantum fidelity in place of softmax attention or phenotype gating. Against the quantum-neuroimaging classifiers of Section II-B, the difference is architectural: an explicit adjacency, fidelity-initialised and classically weighted, feeding a graph-attention network rather than a per-region classifier. Against QG-WRN [18], the closest prior quantum model on the same cohort, the difference is protocol: whole acquisition sites are held out, the PREPRINT 3

200-region Craddock (CC200) parcellation is used, and every quantum model is set against a classical model matched on features, capacity, and training budget.

C. Quantum Fidelity as a Connectivity Metric 

The design evaluated in this paper defines graph connec-tivity by the fidelity between quantum-encoded regional rep-resentations, which are themselves derived from correlation-based features. The fidelity between two pure quantum states 

|ψi⟩ and |ψj ⟩, representing two brain regions, is the squared overlap:

Fidelity is equivalent to a quantum kernel K(xi, x j ), and because the feature map involves non-linear trigonometric terms, it defines a non-linear similarity function, whose re-lationship to linear and RBF kernels we assess empirically through matched performance, effective dimension, and eigen-spectrum. Fidelity also measures the geometric similarity of the encoded states, a non-linear function of all of a region’s features at once. Finally, in high-dimensional Hilbert spaces, most pairs of states are close to orthogonal, a consequence of measure concentration, so fidelity can give a sparser adjacency than a dense correlation matrix. Sparsity does not establish de-noising, however: concentration pushes every overlap toward zero whether or not the pair is related, so it can suppress informative relationships along with noise.

IV. M ETHODOLOGY : T HE ARCHITECTURE UNDER TEST 

A. Dataset and Preprocessing 

The architecture is developed on the Autism Brain Imag-ing Data Exchange, ABIDE I [23], [24], which contributes 

1,035 subjects ( 505 ASD, 530 controls) across 20 acquisi-tion sites after quality control. It is then evaluated on three further cohorts in Section VII: ADHD-200 [25] for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), REST-meta-MDD [20] for major depressive disorder (MDD), and UCLA-CNP [26], for 4,308 subjects in total (Table I). The cohorts are reported separately and never pooled: their releases disagree on parcel-lation width ( 200 CC200 regions for ABIDE and REST-meta-MDD against 190 for the others) and they were preprocessed with different pipelines (the Configurable Pipeline for the Analysis of Connectomes (C-PAC), Athena, the Data Pro-cessing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI) toolbox, and our own), so pooling would confound preprocessing with site. The heterogeneity across scanner types, acquisition protocols, and demographics within each cohort is what the leave-site-out protocol of Section IV-D is designed to stress-test. Fold counts reflect sites carrying both classes, and Table I reports the subjects contained in these folds. This affects ADHD-200 alone, whose release holds 767 subjects, of which 619 fall in sites carrying both classes. The other 148 come from two control-only sites, which cannot serve as test folds because sensitivity, specificity, and AUC are undefined on a single class; they are used for training in every fold and never tested, so no test label is exposed and every quantum and classical model trains on the same subjects. The remaining three cohorts are used in full. Supervised feature selection: Unsupervised PCA maximises variance rather than class separation, so it can discard precisely the directions that distinguish groups, and published pipelines on these cohorts generally use supervised selection instead. Every matched quantum–classical comparison therefore uses the top-k connections ranked by absolute t-statistic ( k = 8 for the matched models, k = 2 ,000 for the unrestricted ones), fitted on the training sites only. Each matched model thus receives Pearson correlation coefficients rather than BOLD time series, so quantum and classical models see identical inputs and differ only in the similarity they apply. Selection is performed inside each fold because ranking features on the full cohort would leak held-out labels into the feature stage, a form of leakage shown to inflate connectome-based prediction performance [28]. 

B. System Architecture Overview 

The evaluated system is a hybrid pipeline: a simulated quan-tum circuit handles state preparation and fidelity computation, and a classical processor handles edge learning and graph classification, as shown in Fig. 2. A quantum feature map generates node representations and graph support, which are subsequently processed by a classical GAT. 

The Quantum Feature Encoder maps regional connectiv-ity features into quantum states in a Hilbert space. Circuits are simulated with a batched PyTorch statevector simulator, with PennyLane as the reference implementation (Section IV-C). The register carries n qubits per region, one per retained PCA component; the value of n is determined through fidelity-density and computational-cost analyses. Fidelity at n = 16 

leaves too few usable pairs at three of the four floors tested (Section VIII-A), while wider variational circuits are afford-able only up to a point (Section VIII-B). • Sparsification: to control noise and keep the GAT’s computation manageable, we apply k-nearest-neighbour masking, keeping only the top k = 20 connections per node, 10% of the 200 regions, which keeps the graph sparse while leaving each node a choice of neighbours for attention to weight. The result is left directed and is not symmetrised: region i may retain an edge to j without the converse, since j may have twenty stronger neighbours of its own. Attention is computed over incoming edges, so the asymmetry is well defined. 

The Graph Attention Network (GAT) performs the binary classification. Each node’s quantum latent becomes its initial GAT feature, h(0) 

i = zi, so gradients from the classification loss reach the classical attention layers and the adaptive-edge kernel. The resulting adjacency, Aquantum in its support and W

The first layer maps each region’s n = 8 -dimensional latent to 32 features in each of four attention heads, concatenated to 128 ; the second maps these to 32 features with a single head. Both use exponential linear unit (ELU) activations and dropout p = 0 .6, chosen to limit overfitting given the sample size. Global mean pooling gives a graph-level embedding, which a linear layer maps to the two classes with a softmax read-out. 

C. Training Setup 

Only the classical components are trained. Before training, the PCA basis is fitted on the training sites, the circuit is eval-uated once for every region, and the latents and the fidelity-selected support are cached; each training step then passes the cached latents through edge learning and the GAT to the cross-entropy loss L = − P2

c=1 yc log ˆ yc, where yc is the one-hot label and ˆyc the predicted class probability. Gradients from PyTorch autograd update the GAT, the edge-learning MLP, and α, β with Adam [29] (learning rate 0.01 , weight decay 

5 × 10 −4, 30 epochs). In the principal graph runs (all rows of Table II but the trained-encoder variant), the quantum encoder, with every encoding gain equal to one, and the fidelity-derived topology therefore remain fixed throughout training, while only the classical edge-learning and GAT parameters are updated; the circuits of the model suite (Section VI) are trained separately. For the kernel models, bandwidth is selected by kernel-target alignment (KTA) [30] on the training sites of each fold, with no inner cross-validation loop, and reported metrics are averaged across folds. D. Experimental Design and Evaluation Metrics 

The design principle throughout is that a quantum model is compared against the classical model it corresponds to (same features, same folds, same optimiser, same training budget) rather than against the best classical number available anywhere; Section X-E describes the artefacts this matching prevents. The evaluation protocol follows three rules. 

Leave-site-out (LSO) validation: the dataset is split by acquisition site. In each fold, one site is held out entirely for testing while the rest train the model, which reduces the ability to exploit site-specific scanner characteristics, a well-known confound in ABIDE-based work. UCLA-CNP, with two scanners, is the exception (Section IV-A). 

Baselines: linear classical (support vector machine (SVM) with a linear kernel; graph convolutional network (GCN) [32] with a Pearson-correlation graph) and non-linear classical (SVM with an RBF kernel; GCN with an RBF-kernel simi-larity graph), with both SVMs applied to flattened correlation matrices (Tables II–IV).

B. Register Width, Accuracy and Computational Cost in Vari-ational Models 

Section VIII-A concerns kernels , which compare states pairwise. A QCNN reads a single Pauli-Z expectation instead, so concentration need not bite the same way, and whether width helps a variational model was an open question rather than a measured one. Training a QCNN at four, eight and sixteen qubits on a 

300 -subject pool with a held-out site gives test accuracies of 0.491 , 0.503 and 0.520 , with the overfit gap narrowing from +0 .149 to +0 .110 . The present experiment suggests that fidelity concentration does not directly translate into degraded QCNN performance. However, the observed performance gain was achieved at substantial computational cost: sixteen qubits cost 163 × the runtime of eight ( 685 .5 s against 4.2 s) for 

+0 .017 accuracy, and statevector memory doubles per qubit. Training accuracy never exceeds 0.65 at any width, so the model underfits while simultaneously overfitting by 0.11 to 

0.15 . This is a single-site diagnostic rather than a cross-validated result, and no accuracy here should be read as a cohort figure; Section IX-C tests the remaining option, adding capacity at fixed width. 

C. The Variational Layer Is Invisible to the Graph 

A third limitation is algebraic rather than empirical: it constrains what the fidelity graph can learn at any register width. Our encoder prepares |ψ(x)⟩ = U V (x)|0⟩, where V (x)

applies the trainable encoding gains and U is the entangling ring followed by the variational rotations. U is unitary and applied identically to every region, so it cancels exactly in the fidelity, because U †U = I:

Fij = ⟨0|V (xi)†U †U V (xj )|0⟩ 2 = ⟨0|V (xi)†V (xj )|0⟩ 2.

(12) Everything after the data-dependent encoding leaves the fidelity kernel invariant and receives no gradient from the graph. We confirmed this numerically: perturbing the varia-tional parameters by O(1) changes the fidelity Gram matrix by 1.2 × 10 −6, and their gradients through the graph are 

1.0×10 −7 and 3.8×10 −8 against 51 .5 for the encoding gains. Through the Pauli-Z read-out path the same parameters receive gradients of 0.128 and 0.298 , so the circuit is not inert. It is only its contribution to the graph that vanishes. The encoding gains are therefore the only circuit parameters that enter a fidelity graph: eight parameters for a 200 -node topology, rather than the full variational stack the architecture appears to provide. In the principal graph runs, the encoder is evaluated once, without gradients, at its initial parameters, and the top-k support selected from that encoding stays fixed while the edge kernel and the classifier are optimised; training the encoder as well, with the support re-selected every epoch, leaves the graph at chance (Section VIII-D). The invariance therefore does not affect any reported result; it constrains how a fidelity graph can be trained. The argument extends to any architecture in which identical post-encoding unitary operations are applied before fidelity is computed. In such constructions, only trainable parameters that precede or are interleaved with the data-dependent en-coding can influence the fidelity; a unitary block placed after the final encoding layer and applied identically to every input cancels regardless of its depth. Even when every parameter enters the fidelity, the support is fixed by a top-k selection that carries no gradient, so a fidelity graph is trainable in its edge weights but not in its topology. The identity reaches beyond graphs to any hybrid model that compares data by state overlap, including fidelity kernels in support vector machines and kernel-alignment training whose optimised block follows the encoding. In each case, the kernel’s gradient with respect to those layers is exactly zero at every parameter setting, unlike the exponentially small gradi-ents of barren plateaus [37], [38], so barren-plateau mitigations such as tailored initialisation [39] or layer-wise training [40] cannot recover it, while the cancelled layers still cost depth, simulation time and, on hardware, gate noise. Trainable simi-larity therefore has to sit where it survives: inside the feature map, as in trainable embedding kernels [35]; between repeated encodings, as in data re-uploading [41], where only the last trainable block cancels; or in classical layers applied after the overlap, such as graph attention. Perturbing the post-encoding parameters and checking whether the Gram matrix moves is enough to tell which case a published hybrid falls into. 

D. Performance Limitations of the Fidelity-Graph Pipeline 

The graph-attention pipeline stays at chance in every config-uration we tried, with accuracies of 0.504 for the fidelity graph with attention, 0.512 for its GCN ablation and 0.513 with a trained encoder, and in almost every fold it assigns the whole held-out site to one class (MCC 0.000 for both fidelity-graph models; Table II). Modifying the kernel, changing the read-out, supplying node features that a linear classifier separates at AUC 0.739 , and training the encoder jointly while re-selecting the support every epoch did not materially improve performance, and a quantum graph transformer behaved the same way. Because informative node features did not help, the remaining candidate explanations include sparsification effects, the quality of the fidelity-selected topology, limitations of attention learning and graph over-smoothing; the present study does not isolate their individual contributions, so we report the graph arm as a negative result, and every quantum– classical comparison in this paper rests on the feature-based models. E. Sources of Apparent Quantum Advantage 

Four mechanisms can produce an apparent quantum advan-tage, and the matched controls of this study are designed to remove them. Each is common in the published literature, and evaluation defects of this kind have been documented in previous studies: a recent audit of a hybrid quantum– classical skin-lesion classifier found 22 .8% of lesions on both sides of the train–test split, inflating every previously reported metric [43]. 

An unmatched feature budget. Comparing an eight-qubit model against a classical model given 2,000 features measures dimensionality, not the kernel; an unmatched con-figuration shows a quantum deficit for exactly this reason, and it disappears under matching (Section V-B). The budget itself is costly: on REST-meta-MDD, compressing 19 ,900 

pairwise correlations to eight components costs more than any quantum–classical difference measured in this study, while widening the register to admit more features increases the concentration of pairwise fidelity (Section VIII-A). 

Reliance on a single cohort. Positive results on one cohort did not replicate on the others anywhere in this study: TQEK gains +0 .028 on ABIDE I but loses on REST-meta-MDD and UCLA-CNP (Section VI), and intervention results on six-fold subsets did not carry over to the full cohorts. 

A comparator that fails to train. On ADHD-200, all three quantum models beat the classical CNN at p ≤ 0.031 , which may initially be interpreted as a quantum advantage until one observes that C-Linear beats it by a larger margin: the 1-D CNN sits at chance on the small cohorts (Section IX-C), and a single classical comparator would have made this a positive finding. 

Unreported hyperparameter selection. The fidelity kernel spans effective dimensions from near-constant to near-identity as bandwidth varies (Section V-D); a bandwidth chosen post hoc can place it almost anywhere, and its default leaves it memorising the training set. These are the reasons every comparison in this paper carries more than one classical arm, reports all cohorts rather than the favourable ones, and states both uncorrected and corrected significance.

XI. L IMITATIONS 

Network-level interpretation. The models operate on whole-brain connectivity without regional priors, and several perform close to chance on some cohorts; Table I and Fig. 1 therefore summarise previously reported findings rather than results of this study. Attention weights are available but are not readily interpretable when classification performance is close to chance. 

Correlation-derived inputs. Every classifier in this study, quantum or classical, receives features derived from Pearson correlation: selected entries of the correlation vector, its prin-cipal components, or regional connectivity profiles. Whether quantum similarity captures non-linear dependence between raw regional BOLD signals, which motivates this work, was therefore not tested; doing so would require encoding the time series directly. 

Simulation and register width. The main results come from exact statevector simulation; the kernel result holds under simulated shot and depolarising noise (Section V-C), while gate-level noise and decoherence on hardware remain untested. In the evaluated datasets, the usable fidelity range narrowed as register width increased (Section VIII-A), while feature capac-ity demands the opposite, and although fidelity concentration need not limit variational models (Section VIII-B), simulation cost does. Whether the trend holds on hardware that does not pay that cost remains open. 

Statistical scope. The reported intervals are computed across folds rather than across initialisations. Repeating the ABIDE I suite at four seeds indicates the size of the omitted variation: the largest standard deviation of mean accuracy across seeds is 0.017 (QSVM-Pegasos), and the two kernel models without a stochastic component are identical at ev-ery seed. Differences smaller than 0.017 between stochastic models should therefore be interpreted cautiously; the matched differences reported here nevertheless keep their sign across seeds, with the single exception of VQC against its matched MLP. The permutation null covers the kernel arm on ABIDE I only; the neural and graph arms were not permuted. For the graph pipeline, the test is less informative, because every fold of both fidelity-graph models predicts a single class. 

Graph construction and baselines. The adjacency is directed and unsymmetrised, and k is fixed at 20 , so the effect of either choice on node distinctiveness and accuracy was not measured. The evaluated baselines were intention-ally selected to support matched comparisons rather than to represent the latest adaptive, dynamic or hypergraph learning architectures; re-implementing such a model under leave-site-out folds would position these results relative to the state of the art. XII. C ONCLUSIONS 

This study benchmarked near-term quantum models against matched classical baselines for functional-connectivity clas-PREPRINT 18 

sification on four cohorts, three of them under leave-site-out validation. Most quantum models matched their classical counterparts, but none consistently outperformed them, so the results do not demonstrate a reproducible quantum advantage across the evaluated cohorts. For fidelity-based graph construction, each added qubit leaves fewer region pairs above every fidelity floor tested, reflecting kernel concentration [36] rather than an implemen-tation fault, and any trainable layer applied after the encoding cancels in the fidelity, so trainable similarity must be placed inside or between encodings, or in classical layers after the overlap (Sections VIII-A and VIII-C). At eight qubits, the quantum kernel and a classical RBF given the same eight features are statistically indistinguishable on all four cohorts across 62 folds (Section V-B), and under a random-effects model the 95% confidence interval excludes an average ad-vantage above 1.4 percentage points (Fig. 10). Four attempts to lift the resulting feature cap, widening the register, learning a projection, ensembling over disjoint blocks, and adding data re-uploading, each helped the matched classical twin at least as much as the quantum arm in the evaluated experiments. Only the parity-read-out architecture consistently underperformed its matched classical counterpart. One of the most broadly applicable findings concerns model capacity. Models with higher training accuracy did not achieve higher held-out accuracy in the evaluated comparisons: train-ing fit tracks the overfitting gap closely on both well-powered cohorts ( r = +0 .95 on ABIDE I, r = +0 .98 on REST-meta-MDD) while relating negatively to held-out accuracy, and capacity added by any mechanism, quantum or classical, raised training fit without raising held-out accuracy. Across 

284 matched comparisons, the quantum arm leads significantly in 30 ; 18 of these involve a predictor that is not classifying or fails to train, and half of the remaining 12 are on AUC, a ranking metric, with none surviving Bonferroni correction across all 284 comparisons. These findings are specific to classically simulated circuits, fidelity-based topology and the model families tested here. The matched evaluation protocol and released per-fold results provide a reproducible benchmark for future quantum and classical studies. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
