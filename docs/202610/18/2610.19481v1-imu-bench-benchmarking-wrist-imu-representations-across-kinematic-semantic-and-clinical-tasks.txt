# IMU-Bench: Benchmarking Wrist IMU Representations Across Kinematic, Semantic and Clinical Tasks 

Yiquan Li ∗, Aidan Bradshaw ∗, Chenfeng Gao, Karan Ahuja 

> Northwestern University

Wrist-worn inertial measurement units (IMUs) provide a common sensing modality for tasks rang-ing from activity and gesture recognition to pose estimation, inertial odometry and clinical motor assessment. Existing wearable benchmarks evaluate performance across multiple tasks, but leave open how supervision for one task affects transfer to others and whether these tasks benefit from a shared representation. We present IMU-Bench, a benchmark of ten tasks from eight datasets for studying IMU multi-task performance and cross-task transfer. We compare six self-supervised methods pretrained on unlabeled recorded and simulated wrist motion, evaluating classification, pose and velocity estimation, and IMU–language retrieval. We find that our masked autoencoder, InrtlWrist, achieves the best or joint-best frozen-probe performance on nine of ten tasks, and fine-tuning improves on training from scratch on nine of ten tasks, including full-body pose, odometry and retrieval. Across transfer and joint-training experiments, supervision that benefits one target can reduce performance on others, with supervised sources transferring within kinematic estimation while, under frozen probing, none approaches the pretrained encoder on language retrieval, and joint-training gains that come at a cost to the paired task. These findings distinguish broad downstream usefulness from complementary task supervision. Code, task cards and a leaderboard are publicly available.

1. Introduction 

Wrist-worn inertial measurement units (IMUs) provide a common sensing modality for studying human movement. Embedded in everyday smartwatches, accelerometers and gyroscopes capture motion relevant to activity recognition [78], clinical motor assessment [65] and human–computer interaction [33, 71], with additional cues for articulated pose estimation and inertial odometry. Like the shadows in Plato’s allegory of the cave [50, Book VII, 514a–517a], different downstream tasks reveal distinct aspects of the same underlying human motion, each offering only a partial view [28]. These perspectives raise the question of whether these views share a representation. Self-supervised learning has demonstrated substantial reuse of unlabeled wearable recordings. Yuan et al. [78] show transfer from large-scale free-living accelerometry to activity recognition across datasets and populations. Inertia-1 examines objectives, architectures, scale and sensing configurations across activity recognition, freezing-of-gait detection and disease prediction [75]. For wrist IMUs, we ask both how broadly pretrained features transfer and whether supervision for one motion target improves or compromises their usefulness for others. We present IMU-Bench, a benchmark of ten tasks from eight datasets addressing these questions (Figure 1). We compare six self-supervised methods pretrained on unlabeled activity and daily-living recordings together with simulated wrist motion. Evaluation spans classification, pose and velocity estimation, and IMU–language retrieval, using six-channel inputs from a single wrist. Beyond frozen probing and fine-tuning, we examine supervised source-task transfer and selected joint-training pairs. The results reveal a distinction between broadly useful pretraining and compatible task supervision. Full-body pose and odometry improve over training from scratch after fine-tuning, and each kinematic task’s best frozen source is another kinematic task, whereas under frozen probing no supervised source approaches the pretrained encoder on language retrieval. Five of the six kinematic transfers survive, at about half their size, when the tasks are evaluated on disjoint participants, and the observed trade-offs show why individual-task performance alone is insufficient to characterize a shared wrist representation. We provide InrtlWrist, a masked autoencoder pretrained on unlabeled recorded and simulated wrist motion. Among the six controlled methods, it achieves the best or joint-best frozen-probe performance on nine of ten tasks. 2. Related Work 

Wearable sensor benchmarks. Wearable-IMU representations are mostly evaluated on activity recognition: pretraining objectives [12, 22], cross-dataset generalization [26] and foundation models on activity and health outcomes [1, 46, 62, 75, 78]; SlotFM adds gesture, gait and sports [49], NormWear physiological signals [40]. Sparse-IMU pose [27, 29, 43, 74, 76, 77] and inertial odom-etry [14, 24, 38, 52] are studied apart, though label-free physical decoders now link them to self-supervised learning [34]. Task-specific systems for activity [48], gestures [7, 33, 71], Parkinson’s disease [9, 65] and IMU–language alignment [44, 80, 83] are our reference points (Appendix B). None measures how supervision on one task affects the others, which IMU-Bench adds. 

Self-supervised learning for wearable sensors. Pretext tasks for wearable IMUs include transfor-mation prediction [55, 78], self-training [61], masked reconstruction [21, 23, 72], masked modeling with contrast [18], factorized multimodal contrast [37], relative contrast [73] and video and text supervision [16]; language models can also synthesize virtual IMU [35]. Each came with its own backbone, data and protocol, so we compare five with masked autoencoding under one corpus and matched capacity (Section 4.2). Transformers for multivariate series encode channels inde-pendently [19, 47], as whole-channel tokens [39] or in separate time and variate stages [82]; the first loses the accelerometer–gyroscope coupling and the second the patch-level resolution pose and odometry need, hence the alternating frame-wise and global attention of InrtlWrist, adapted from VGGT [69] (Section 4.1). 

Task transfer, probing and pretraining data. Task relationships have been measured by transferring supervision [2, 66, 79], by choosing tasks to train together [13, 59] and by comparing representations directly [28, 31]; we take the transfer route for wrist IMUs (Section 6). Because probe accuracy reflects the probe as well as the representation [3, 25], we fix light decoders and add untrained-encoder and raw-input controls (Section 4.3). Scaling laws and data pruning [30, 58] motivated wearable studies of data volume and participant count [46, 75]; we vary motion coverage at matched volume (Section 7).

Each recording is therefore split chronologically into 80% training and 20% evaluation, dropping one boundary window.

4. Methods 

4.1. Architecture 

Given a wrist-worn window X ∈ ℝ𝑇 ×6, we divide each accelerometer and gyroscope channel into 0.32 s patches, forming a 6 × 𝑃 token grid, where 𝑃 is the number of temporal patches. A 12-layer transformer 

𝑓 𝜃 with width 𝐷 = 384 and approximately 21 million parameters produces patch representations 

H ∈ ℝ6𝑃 ×𝐷 .Following VGGT [69], layers alternate between frame-wise attention across the six channels at each patch position and global attention across all channels and positions, including a learnable summary token. 

4.2. Training 

We pretrain InrtlWrist by masked reconstruction. The encoder processes visible patches, and a reconstruction decoder predicts the masked measurements. The reconstruction decoder is discarded after pretraining. To support different input durations, each batch is cropped to 1.28–10.24 s in multiples of 0.32 s. Positional embeddings are restricted to the crop, avoiding resizing or interpolation of the position code, following resolution-agnostic vision transformers [17]. We compare against LIMU-BERT (masked reconstruction), SimMTM (reconstruction and con-trastive losses), FOCAL (contrastive, orthogonality and temporal losses), RelCon (relative contrastive loss) and SSL-Wearables (transformation-prediction cross-entropy) using a shared pretraining corpus and matched encoder capacity. These objectives use plain attention over the flattened token grid. A plain-attention InrtlWrist variant controls for this architectural difference. Preprocessing and pretraining controls are detailed in Appendix A.

5.3. Semantic Alignment 

InrtlWrist achieves the highest mean IMU–language Recall@1 among the six matched methods under both protocols (Table 4). Frozen probing reaches 27.9, compared with 24.9 for LIMU-BERT, 23.2 for training from scratch and 9.5 for the random frozen encoder. Fine-tuning raises the mean to 38.8, with similar improvements in both retrieval directions. The advantage over scratch persists in the full gallery, where fine-tuned InrtlWrist reaches 5.1 IMU-to-text and 4.4 text-to-IMU Recall@1, compared with 2.1 in both directions for scratch. SSL-Wearables achieves the highest text-to-IMU score at 4.5. Although absolute recall falls with the larger candidate set, InrtlWrist remains ahead of scratch in both directions and under both protocols. The wrist-only UniMTS reimplementation, trained from scratch, reaches 24.9 IMU-to-text Re-call@1 but 1.5 in the reverse direction on galleries of 100. This asymmetry is associated with retrieval hubness, detailed in Appendix B. All encoders receive paired motion–text supervision during down-stream training, so these comparisons evaluate the pretrained representation’s suitability for learning alignment rather than zero-shot language alignment.

7. Architecture and Training Ablations 

Decoder capacity. We compare frozen pretrained and random encoders as decoder capacity in-creases (Figure 4). Pretraining reduces body MPJPE by 9.7 mm with a linear readout and 7.1 mm with the benchmark decoder. Increasing decoder width changes this gap little, whereas adding temporal layers reduces it below 1 mm. A 12-layer odometry decoder similarly allows the random encoder to outperform every pretrained encoder (Appendix D). Stronger decoders therefore narrow or reverse the measured advantage of pretraining, motivating the light readouts used in the benchmark. 

Pretraining data and motion coverage. Holding the architecture, probes and training budget fixed, we vary the amount and coverage of pretraining data. We sample uniformly from a five-source pool of 1,959,288 windows or restrict sampling to subsets of 1,000 motion clusters obtained by 

𝑘 -means on a separately trained MAE’s features. At 50,000 updates, the 0.01% subsets trail the 0.1% subsets at every coverage level, while uniform draws from 0.1% to 100% of the pool perform alike. Increasing motion coverage generally improves performance, particularly when few clusters are available (Figure 5). These findings are conditional on the training budget and sampling scheme, since restricting clusters also changes source composition. Appendix H reports the full grid, normalization and probe protocols.

8. Conclusion 

We present IMU-Bench, ten wrist-IMU tasks evaluated under shared protocols, and the pretrained reference encoder InrtlWrist. Among the six matched methods, InrtlWrist achieves the best or joint-best frozen primary score on nine tasks and outperforms training from scratch on nine after fine-tuning. Source-task supervision transfers between kinematic tasks; on IMU–text retrieval no frozen supervised source approaches the pretrained encoder. Joint training improves the partner tasks at a cost to full-body pose and language retrieval. Decoder capacity can obscure differences between frozen representations. At the tested pretraining budget, additional broadly sampled windows provide no consistent downstream gain. These findings distinguish broad downstream usefulness from mutually beneficial task supervision. 

Limitations. Our scaling study does not isolate motion coverage from data volume and source composition. It also does not establish how downstream performance scales with participant count, the balance of recorded and simulated data, or sensor placement. The benefit of larger corpora at longer training budgets remains unresolved. 

Reproducibility statement 

Every number in this paper comes from a registry-driven harness that we release with the benchmark: one task card per task fixing its inputs, decoder, split rule, metric and budget, and one encoder card per model. Appendix B gives each task’s split rule, metric and subject counts, Appendix A the backbone, decoders and optimization settings. Appendix C records the corpus-against-evaluation-set audit, including the duplicated participants and mirrored recordings we had to merge before splitting. Code, task cards and the leaderboard are available on the project page, https://spicexlab.github. io/imubench/ ; the per-cell result files and pretrained weights will be released there.

A. Implementation Details 

Harness and provenance. Every number in this paper is produced by one harness from a task card (data, split, input window, decoder, recipe, selection rule, scorer) and an encoder card (checkpoint, architecture, input normalization, pretraining corpus). Each result file records the run, the selected checkpoint step and the scorer, and every table is generated from these files by a script released with the benchmark. 

> 18 IMU-Bench: Benchmarking Wrist IMU Representations Across Kinematic, Semantic and Clinical Tasks

Input. All tasks use six channels (accelerometer in m/s 2, gyroscope in rad/s) from one wrist in a common forward–left–up (FLU) wrist frame. Each window is z-scored per channel before it enters any encoder, in pretraining and downstream alike. During pretraining only, every window is rotated by one of the 24 proper 90 ◦-multiple rotations of both sensors [63], drawn afresh at each visit; these rotations permute and negate axes, so they commute with per-channel z-scoring. Downstream training uses no augmentation except IMU–text alignment, which applies random 3-D rotations for every encoder alike. 

Pretraining. The self-supervised encoders share one 12-layer, 384-wide ViT over 0.32 s patches (21 M parameters), one corpus and one optimizer recipe; they differ in the objective and, for the five external objectives, in using plain rather than alternating frame/global attention. 

Controls. The random frozen encoder has the InrtlWrist architecture with random initialization (seed 42) and is never trained. The raw-input control is the same model with zero transformer layers, so the decoder reads a frozen random 384-dimensional linear projection of each 16-sample patch plus position embeddings, which preserves the input patch. Both are probed with exactly the decoders, recipes and selection rules of the other rows. 

Compute. Every pretraining and downstream run uses a single NVIDIA A100 or H100 GPU. 

B. Tasks and Metrics 

Splits and subjects. Evaluation is subject-disjoint for microgesture (43 OpenWatch subjects, canon-ical identities after the duplicated participants of Table 12 are merged), Parkinson’s disease (469 PADS participants) and HHAR, MHEALTH and PRISM (9, 10 and 31 subjects); session-disjoint for the three Nymeria tasks, whose 235 participants recur across sessions; the dataset’s own split for IMU–text retrieval; and split in time for Transf HAR (10 subjects), because most of its 60 activities were performed by a single participant (Appendix B.1). 

Pose estimation (Nymeria). Input is the real right-wrist IMU of Nymeria, output one set of 6-D joint rotations per 0.32 s patch of a 10.24 s window. The arm task predicts five right-arm joints (collar, shoulder, elbow, wrist, hand), scored by the forward-kinematics position error of these joints with ground-truth ancestors, in cm; the full-body task predicts the 23 SMPL body joints, scored by root-relative MPJPE in mm. Nymeria is split by recording session (867 training and 101 evaluation sessions). All 82 participants of the evaluation sessions also appear in training, in 271 of the 867 training sessions, so the pose and odometry tasks measure generalization to new recordings of seen people; we keep the session split (a participant-disjoint one would remove 31 % of the training sessions) and state its scope. 

Inertial odometry (Nymeria). The model predicts pelvis velocity in the wrist frame, four values per 0.32 s patch of a 2.56 s window. For scoring, each predicted velocity is rotated to the world frame with the ground-truth wrist orientation at the window start propagated by integrating the raw gyroscope, the rotated velocities are summed into a trajectory, and the absolute trajectory error (m) is averaged over all evaluation windows. This uses the true initial orientation and is easier than full inertial navigation; numbers are not comparable to world-frame odometry systems.

C. Data Audit 

Two kinds of leakage. Inductive leakage places evaluation participants in downstream training and leaks labels; transductive exposure shows unlabeled evaluation inputs to self-supervised pretraining and leaks only the test participants’ input distribution. Both inflate evaluation numbers. 

Pretraining pools. The reference corpus contains no IMU-Bench evaluation set. Commonly used activity pools, ours included, bundle WISDM [70], PAMAP2 [54], RecoFit [45], SAMoSA [42], Shoaib [57], UT-Watch [6] and UTD-MHAD [15], so we exclude these datasets from evaluation; UTD-MHAD is listed in our pool but its clips are shorter than one window and contribute nothing to the corpus, which is why Table 1 omits it. An earlier eleven-source corpus that we used during development shows how easily exposure happens: it contained the Nymeria and HumanML3D training splits and OpenWatch recordings under the release’s random 80/10/10 partition, whose training part contains all five IMU-Bench gesture test participants. That corpus touches this paper only in Appendix H: in the session-level sweep, reported for reconstruction loss alone; in the encoder whose embeddings define the motion modes; and in the grid’s validation set, used only for reconstruction loss and checkpoint selection. The reference encoder is validated on the five pretraining sources alone.

Sensor units, and overlap inside HumanML3D. EatingSpeed stores gyroscope data in deg/s while all other sources use rad/s; per-window z-scoring makes our encoders invariant to this scale, but a pipeline keeping physical units would mix scales by a factor of 57, so the harness converts units on load and rejects a source whose still windows have a median gyroscope magnitude above 0.3 rad/s or whose 99th percentile exceeds 40 rad/s. Every HumanML3D split list pairs each clip with a mirrored copy; Table 1 counts unique motion only. The official split also cuts some AMASS recordings into several clips, so 437 of the 2,192 test clips share frames of a source recording with a training or validation clip; this affects every encoder equally but makes the retrieval task partly transductive. 

D. Controls and Decoder Capacity 

We probed the same frozen encoders on full-body pose with decoders of increasing capacity: a linear readout, the benchmark decoder (one cross-channel attention layer of width 192), one cross-channel layer of width 384, the same plus four temporal layers, and a four-plus-eight-layer decoder of width 384. A never-trained encoder with the InrtlWrist architecture falls 9.7 mm behind InrtlWrist with the linear readout, 7.1 mm with the benchmark decoder and 7.6 mm with the wider single layer, but only 0.3 mm once four temporal layers are added and 0.8 mm with the deep decoder; on odometry a 12-layer decoder lets the untrained encoder beat every pretrained one. Deep decoders learn the task from whatever the frozen encoder passes through, so the kinematic probes use the light decoders of the original task configurations (arm pose 2 cross-channel plus 2 temporal layers, full-body pose 1 cross-channel layer, odometry 1 temporal layer, all of width 192). The raw-input control is sensitive to token pooling on recognition and retrieval; its near-chance scores therefore characterize this readout configuration rather than learning from raw IMU in general.

G. Embedding Space of the Reference Encoder 

Figure 6 projects with t-SNE [64] the reference encoder’s frozen pooled embeddings of 12,961 windows from eighteen sources (twelve labeled activity datasets, the three mocap-derived collections, Nymeria, OCDetect and EatingSpeed). Every number below is the mean fraction of a window’s ten nearest neighbors (cosine) that share a property with it, so it measures local geometry, not the projection’s layout; the labels are the datasets’ own, 261 activities in all. 

Activity structure is local and carried by whole-body motion. 38.8 % of a window’s ten nearest neighbors share its activity label (chance 1.7 %) and 65.6 % its semantic family (chance 14.5 %), yet neither labeling clusters globally (negative cosine silhouettes), so the projection is not a taxonomy. Restricting the neighborhood to windows from other datasets separates what travels between corpora: locomotion 0.512 (chance 0.059), cycling 0.466 (0.026) and stairs 0.415 (0.042), 8.7 to 18 times chance, against household, hand and desk work, posture, gestures and gym exercise at 1.7 to 4.5 times chance. By activity, cycling, running, jogging, walking and stairs travel, while sitting, cooking, typing, washing dishes and folding stay inside the dataset that recorded them, partly because per-window standardization removes the gravity offset that distinguishes static postures.

Synthesis separates the space; pretraining exposure barely does. The three sources synthesized from motion capture are isolated regardless of exposure (BONES-SEED 0.981 and Motion-X++ 0.899, in the pool; HumanML3D 0.942, held out): mocap-derived windows have 0.96 of their ten nearest neighbors inside their own dataset against 0.59 for recorded windows, a contrast of +0.364. The exposure contrast over the recorded windows is +0.027 and changes sign under leave-one-source-out (from −0.060 without MHEALTH to +0.090 without Nymeria); the two least isolated sources are EatingSpeed at 0.335, in the corpus, and Nymeria at 0.338, never seen. Recorded corpora therefore occupy the same region whether or not they were pretrained on, while this objective keeps synthesized inertial data apart even when it trains on a large amount of it, a caution for enlarging a corpus with motion-capture synthesis. UTD-MHAD [15] (0.980) and OpenWatch (0.865) are isolated by an artifact: their short clips are wrap-padded 2.91 and 3.41 times to reach 10.24 s, a periodicity no other source has, so they are excluded from every exposure contrast. 

What this supports. One self-supervised encoder places heterogeneous recorded corpora, seen or unseen, in a shared region with activity structure inside it at about 23 times chance, strongest for gross locomotion and weakest for fine manipulation; it does not support a claim of a dataset-invariant representation, since dataset identity remains the strongest neighborhood signal (0.632 against chance 0.058), and only the task measurements of Sections 5 and 6 establish what the representation is worth. 

H. Pretraining Volume and Motion Coverage 

Coverage saturates within the first percent. A uniform draw of 0.1% of the pool (1,959 windows) already contains 82% of the motion modes, a 0.5% draw 99.7%, and every draw of 5% or more all of them; the reference corpus, at 10% of the pool, covers every mode. 

Downstream performance is flat across a thousand-fold range of data. With all modes available, pretraining on 1,959 windows gives a reconstruction loss within 0.3% of the full pool and an arm-pose probe indistinguishable from the other volumes; full-body error varies by 0.4 mm, odometry velocity error by 1.5% and MHEALTH, PRISM and gesture F1 by at most 4 points, without a trend. No classification probe improves from 1% to 100% of the pool (per task in Figure 7). 

Restricting motion modes matters most at the extreme. At 1,959 windows, training on a single mode raises InrtlWrist loss from 0.797 to 0.936 and takes the eight-probe average to −96% of the full pool’s gain, almost back to a never-trained encoder, collapsing every classification probe (gesture F1 30.3 against 59.5 with all modes). From ten modes onward the kinematic probes are within 5% of the 1,000-mode model in raw error, which for full-body pose and odometry is still 10 to 41% of the full pool’s gain over a never-trained encoder; from five modes onward the activity and gesture probes fluctuate without a trend (Figure 5). Because modes are added largest first, the mode-count axis also shifts source composition (the single-mode subset is 98 % EatingSpeed, the 100-mode subsets about 90 % OCDetect), so this comparison bounds the effect of the number of modes rather than isolating it. 

Scope and caveats. These results hold for one architecture and one data pool at 50,000 steps, and they do not separate diversity from volume: the uniform draws change size and coverage together, and the mode-restricted draws change source composition with the mode count. Enlarging the corpus brings no consistent downstream gain at this budget, coverage of the motion modes is complete long before the corpus is, and whether more data pays at larger budgets is open.
