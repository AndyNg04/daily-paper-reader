Abstract 

User feedback enables LLMs to refine reusable personalized skills, but often mixes persis-tent preferences with request-specific correc-tions. Indiscriminately retaining both risks negative transfer, motivating selective reten-tion at the level of individual requirements. We frame this challenge as selective transfer over atomic rules and propose S KILL GRAIN , a framework for learning reusable personalized skills from user feedback. S KILL GRAIN de-composes feedback into independently action-able rules, predicts their transferability, and retains only those suitable for future requests. To learn these decisions, we combine rubric-guided skill rewards with fine-grained credit assignment that separately evaluates rule con-tent and retention decisions. We further jointly optimize skill induction and skill-conditioned generation through reinforcement learning, en-suring that learned skills support downstream personalization. We construct a benchmark spanning five personalized writing tasks to evaluate cross-request generalization. Exper-iments across three base models in both in-domain and out-of-domain settings show that SKILL GRAIN achieves the best overall aver-age performance among competing methods. Our code is available at https://anonymous. 4open.science/r/SkillGrain-C210 .

For instance, feedback on writ-ing style or organization can capture a user’s per-sistent preference, whereas instructions involving specific entities or facts are often tied to the current request. Incorporating such heterogeneous infor-mation indiscriminately into persistent skills may introduce inappropriate constraints and lead to neg-ative transfer. Therefore, effective skill learning requires models to identify the transferability of individual feedback components and selectively re-tain information that can generalize across future requests. Prior work has explored experience reuse and personalization from different perspectives. Skill-evolution methods distill execution experience into reusable procedures and validate their effective-1arXiv:2610.19380v1 [cs.CL] 14 Oct 2026 ness through task performance (Lin et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026b). However, evaluating a skill as a whole does not directly reveal which indi-vidual feedback requirements should be retained. History-based personalization models user pref-erences through memories or summaries (Zhang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026), but does not explic-itly supervise whether each new correction should become a persistent preference. Scope-aware meth-ods distinguish stable from situational preferences or restrict adaptation to relevant contexts (Gao et al., 2026; Stephan et al., 2024), focusing on when preferences apply rather than which requirements within mixed feedback should be retained. More directly, correction-driven systems filter transient requirements or maintain rules with applicability conditions (Yang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026), yet do not separately optimize the accuracy of rule extraction and retention decisions. Despite these advances, learning from feedback that mixes trans-ferable preferences with request-specific correc-tions remains challenging. This raises a key ques-tion: can models learn to identify and selectively retain transferable preferences from such mixed feedback to better personalize future responses? In this work, we formulate personalized skill learning as a selective transfer problem and intro-duce S KILL GRAIN . Our framework decomposes mixed feedback into atomic rules and predicts their transferability, retaining reusable preferences while excluding request-specific corrections from persis-tent skills. To train these decisions, we combine rubric-guided skill rewards with fine-grained credit assignment, providing separate learning signals for rule content and transferability. We further jointly optimize skill induction and skill-conditioned gen-eration through reinforcement learning, using dis-tinct rewards for skill updates and downstream re-sponses. To evaluate our approach, we construct a benchmark spanning five personalized writing tasks. Experiments across three base models in both in-domain and out-of-domain settings show that S KILL GRAIN achieves the highest average score on each model. Our main contributions are as follows: • Feedback-Driven Personalization as Selec-tive Transfer. We formulate personalized skill learning as selective transfer and con-struct a five-task writing benchmark that tests whether feedback-derived skills preserve reusable preferences without carrying over request-specific information. • Transfer-Aware Skill Induction and Credit Assignment. We introduce S KILL GRAIN ,which pairs atomic rules with learned transfer-ability decisions and assigns separate credit to rule content and retention, jointly optimizing skill induction and downstream application. • Generalization Across Models and Do-mains. We evaluate S KILL GRAIN across three base models in both in-domain and out-of-domain settings, achieving the highest over-all average score on each model and demon-strating the effectiveness of our approach. 

2 Related work 

2.1 Skill Evolution in LLM Agents 

Recent work on LLM agents has increasingly ex-plored how interaction experience can be distilled into reusable knowledge. Early approaches summa-rize task outcomes as verbal reflections or extract reusable lessons from multiple trajectories (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024), while subsequent methods induce and retrieve workflows or skills to guide future tasks (Wang et al., 2024). More re-cent work further studies continual skill evolution, allowing skills to be created, evaluated, revised, and maintained as new experience is collected (Lin et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026b; He and Yang, 2026; Tang et al., 2026a). These methods focus on ac-cumulating and refining reusable task knowledge from interaction experience. Our work extends this direction to personalized skill learning, where explicit user feedback must be selectively incorpo-rated into skills that are reused across subsequent requests. 

2.2 Large Language Model Personalization 

LLM personalization commonly exploits interac-tion history through user representations or prefer-ence learning. One line of work aggregates histo-ries or infers compact personas so that generation can be explicitly conditioned on user attributes (Liu et al., 2025; Balepur et al., 2025), whereas an-other learns shared and user-specific preference signals across users, for example through collab-orative learning or preference calibration, to bet-ter adapt to sparse or unseen users (Choi et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026). Research on long-horizon personalization further organizes user experiences with episodic and semantic memories (Zhang et al., 22025), combines short-term deviations with long-term trends (Sun et al., 2026), and distinguishes sta-ble preferences from context-dependent ones (Gao et al., 2026); recent personalized agents also cou-ple memory updating with action selection (Zhang et al., 2026). Taken together, these approaches primarily model personalization by extracting and maintaining user-specific information from accu-mulated interactions. We complement this line of research by distilling user feedback into reusable personalized skills.

4.2 Rubric-Guided Rewards 

Since correctly retaining transferable preferences and producing high-quality downstream responses are distinct objectives, we use separate rewards for personalized skill induction and skill-conditioned generation. For skill induction, the reward is de-signed to encourage two behaviors: preserving transferable preference information and preventing non-transferable information from being incorpo-rated into the reusable skill. Let D denote the set of rubric dimensions that characterize transferable user preferences. For each dimension d ∈ D , we define cd(s) ∈ { 0, 1} to indicate whether the can-didate skill s makes the correct update for that di-mension. A correct decision may either incorporate a reusable preference revision identified from the current feedback or retain the existing rule when no revision is required. We separately evaluate whether the candidate skill incorrectly retains infor-mation whose applicability is limited to the current interaction. Let nlocal (s) denote the number of dis-tinct non-transferable elements incorporated into 

s, such as request-specific entities or one-off re-quirements. We define the corresponding reward as 

rnontrans (s) = 2 −nlocal (s). (9) This reward reaches its maximum when no interaction-specific information is retained and de-creases as more non-transferable information is in-corporated into the reusable skill. We further define

5.3 Ablation Study 

To assess the contribution of each component in SKILL GRAIN , we conduct a series of ablation stud-ies. w/ outcome-based reward replaces the direct rubric-based skill reward with the reward of the subsequent response generated using the candidate skill; w/o atomization replaces atomic skill induc-tion with holistic KEEP/UPDATE updates while retaining matched-span local credit; w/o advantage allocation removes the rubric-guided local advan-tage assignment; and w/o skill-update RL disables RL optimization of skill-update trajectories and trains only skill-conditioned response generation. As shown in Table 2, removing atomization or skill-update RL causes the largest performance drops, while other ablations also reduce performance, sup-porting the contribution of each component.

6 Conclusion 

We study feedback-driven personalized skill learn-ing for writing tasks and propose S KILL GRAIN 

to handle feedback containing both transferable and task-specific information. S KILL GRAIN de-composes feedback into atomic rules, learns their transferability, and uses rubric-based rewards with fine-grained advantage assignment to jointly op-timize skill updating and utilization. We further construct a benchmark spanning five writing tasks, and experiments across multiple backbones demon-strate the effectiveness of our approach. 8Limitations 

Our study has several limitations. First, some user profiles in our benchmark are synthesized by LLMs, which may not fully capture the diversity and complexity of real user preferences. Besides, due to computational constraints, our experiments mainly focus on models in the 1.7B–4B range and writing-oriented tasks. Evaluating S KILL GRAIN 

on larger models, real-world users, and more com-plex task domains remains an important direction for future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

Personalized skills may retain or reproduce user-specific details from interaction histories, and source corpora may contain identifiable or objec-tionable text. Incorrectly transferring a preference or a factual detail to a new request may also pro-duce misleading personalized responses. Our non-leakage rubric measures selected forms of factual carryover; it does not establish privacy protection or content safety. These risks require separate re-view before releasing derived data or deploying the method with real users.

A.3 Rubric-Grounded Feedback Construction 

Directly prompting an LLM to generate user feed-back can introduce substantial variability and make the supervision difficult to control. To obtain reli-able yet realistic feedback, we first use determin-istic rubric rules to identify revision targets from the model response, including reusable preference corrections such as length or stylistic adjustments, as well as interaction-specific requirements such as missing current-request information or irrele-vant content carried over from previous interac-tions. This design allows the feedback to reflect the mixture of transferable preferences and local requirements that may naturally occur in user in-teractions. We then use a fixed Qwen3-4B model with thinking disabled and deterministic decoding to verbalize these structured revision targets into concise natural-language feedback. The verbal-izer is instructed to preserve the original revision requirements without introducing additional prefer-ences or facts. 

B Experimental Details 

B.1 Fine-Grained Credit Assignment 

For local credit assignment, we deterministically align each generated atomic rule with the structured revision targets retained during feedback construc-tion. For preference-related rules, we map each target to a canonical direction (e.g., more/less con-cise or more/less first-person) and match the gener-ated rule using predefined lexical and paraphrase patterns, while also checking for the opposite direc-tion. For interaction-specific rules, we first match grounded entities or key phrases and then verify whether the associated directive expresses the ex-pected action, such as including or removing the information. Ambiguous, conflicting, or unsup-ported matches are treated as abstentions and retain the original sequence-level advantage. 

Omission penalty. Field-level credit only super-vises atoms that are actually generated, so an en-tirely omitted transferable target would otherwise receive no local negative signal. Let M denote the set of confidently missing transferable targets and T + the set of transferable targets in the feed-back. We apply an omission penalty only when the unmatched transferable targets can be confidently identified: 

comit = − |M| |T +| .

The score is assigned to the sequence-closing token. When it is unclear whether an unmatched target has already been expressed by a generated atom, no omission penalty is applied.
