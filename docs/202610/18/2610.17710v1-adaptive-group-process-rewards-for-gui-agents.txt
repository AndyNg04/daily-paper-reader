ABSTRACT 

Training graphical user interface (GUI) agents with reinforcement learning suffers from sparse outcome rewards, leading to inefficient learning. Process rewards of-fer a promising direction for credit assignment to intermediate actions; however, accurately evaluating an action requires identifying evidence across long-horizon GUI trajectories, as its value may depend on information beyond its immediate state and observation. In this paper, we propose an adaptive-group process re-ward framework that dynamically partitions a GUI trajectory into semantically coherent subgoal groups, enabling process reward estimation at both the action and group levels. At the action level, a critic evaluates each action based on the visual changes and subsequent actions within the group for action correct-ness and contribution to the subgoal. At the group level, the framework aggre-gates action-level evaluations to assess subgoal completion and contribution to the overall task, and further refines these assessments by leveraging the context of all groups in the trajectory. The process reward of each action is obtained by combining its action-level evaluation with the refined group-level evaluation. We further augment GRPO’s outcome advantage with the proposed process rewards to provide finer-grained guidance for GUI agent training. Experiments on process reward benchmarks and downstream RL training demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in large language models and vision-language models have enabled graphical user interface (GUI) agents Cheng et al. (2024); Hong et al. (2024); Qin et al. (2025); Lin et al. (2024); Xu et al. (2025); Gao et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2025). These agents translate user instructions and visual observations into executable interactions across digital devices, with applications such as soft-ware testing, office assistance, and industrial automation. GUI tasks typically involve long-horizon, multi-turn interactions Zhou et al. (2023); Rawles et al. (2024); Xie et al. (2025), requiring agents to maintain multimodal interaction context across changing visual states and coordinate sequences of actions. Reinforcement learning (RL) Li et al. (2025); Xiao et al. (2025); Lu et al. (2025); Tang et al. (2026) is well suited to such interactive tasks, allowing agents to improve their policies based on feedback from the GUI environments.

(2025); Li et al. (2025): a trajectory is judged successful if the final application state satisfies the instruction requirement, and unsuccessful otherwise. Consequently, the outcome-level signal is assigned to all intermediate actions in a trajectory, failing to distinguish useful actions from redun-dant or incorrect ones and making credit assignment unreliable. Process rewards Lightman et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2023); Chen et al. (2025) are therefore needed to evaluate intermediate actions. However, constructing reliable process rewards for GUI trajectories is challenging because GUI en-vironments rarely provide direct supervision for intermediate actions. Whether an action is correctly executed can often be verified from the current and subsequent observations, whereas whether its semantic purpose is correct may only become clear after subsequent actions. Judging the action requires evidence from a broader trajectory context than the immediate state change after that action alone. Existing GUI process reward methods typically organize trajectory evidence and assign credit through two types of preset context scopes: fixed-window step rewards Chen et al. (2025); Xiong et al. (2025); Xiao et al. (2025); Lu et al. (2025); Wan et al. (2026) and milestone rewards Zheng et al. (2026); Hao et al. (2026); Li et al. (2026a); Dai et al. (2026a); Zhai et al. (2026). Fixed-window step rewards evaluate an action within a fixed local context (figure 1(a)). Short windows may miss long-range evidence needed to assess the action’s effect, whereas longer windows may introduce irrelevant context. Milestone rewards instead predefine task progress around fixed check-points or subgoals (figure 1(b)). Because GUI tasks may admit multiple valid execution paths, such milestones can under-credit progress that does not follow the predefined path and overlook the inter-mediate actions connecting checkpoints. Thus, an effective process reward should adapt its evidence scope and credit assignment to the executed trajectory, rather than relying on fixed local scopes or predefined checkpoints. In this paper, we introduce an adaptive-group process reward framework for GUI trajectories (fig-ure 1(c)). Using the task instruction and the agent’s responses, the executed trajectory is dynamically partitioned into consecutive groups of actions that share the same subgoal or semantic operation, rather than by a fixed partition rule. This grouping defines the context for judging the actions in each group and supports two complementary levels of evaluation. At the action level, the critic assesses whether each action is correctly executed based on the local visual change and whether it advances the group’s subgoal based on later actions in the group. At the group level, the critic aggregates the action-level assessments within each group to assess its subgoal completion and con-2Preprint tribution to the whole task. Then, the produced group-level assessments are revisited in the context of all groups, allowing the critic to judge each group’s role beyond its local evidence. The process reward of each action is obtained by combining its action-level assessment with the refined group-level assessment of its group. We further use the generated process rewards to shape the GRPO Shao et al. (2024) advantage at each step, providing finer-grained credit assignment during policy opti-mization. We evaluate the framework on process-reward benchmarks and in downstream RL on OSWorld. The results show consistent improvements in process-reward accuracy and task success rate, demonstrating the effectiveness of adaptive-group process rewards. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce an adaptive-group process reward framework for GUI agents, which dynam-ically partitions trajectories into semantically coherent subgoal groups to organize the evi-dence needed for process reward evaluation. • We design a two-level evaluation that assesses execution and contribution at both the action level and the group level, and obtain the process reward of each action by combining these assessments, making correct execution and group-level contribution jointly necessary. • Experiments on process reward benchmarks and downstream RL training demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 GUI A GENTS 

Recent progress in large language models and vision-language models has enabled GUI agents that interact with applications through visual observations and executable actions. Early systems mainly relied on prompting or supervised fine-tuning, while recent open-source models and frame-works, such as ShowUI Lin et al. (2024), CogAgent Hong et al. (2024), AGUVIS Xu et al. (2025), UI-TARS Qin et al. (2025), OpenCUA Wang et al. (2025), Agent S Agashe et al. (2024), and TongUI Zhang et al. (2025), improve grounding, planning, and cross-platform interaction through larger GUI data and stronger vision-language-action modeling. More recently, reinforcement learn-ing has become a central direction for GUI agents because it can optimize long-horizon interaction from environment feedback rather than only imitating fixed demonstrations. DART Li et al. (2025) decouples multi-turn rollout from training for GUI RL, UI-Genie Xiao et al. (2025) builds a reward-guided self-improvement loop for mobile GUI agents, Orcust Lu et al. (2025) combines principle-constrained reward modeling with online VM-grounded trajectory construction, and ClawGUI Tang et al. (2026) provides a full-stack infrastructure for GUI agent RL and deployment. However, most RL pipelines still rely heavily on sparse outcome rewards, which makes it difficult to assign credit to intermediate actions. Our work addresses this limitation by constructing dense process rewards over adaptive groups and the actions in those groups. 2.2 PROCESS REWARD MODELING 

Process reward modeling supplements sparse outcome rewards with feedback on intermediate steps, enabling more fine-grained credit assignment during training. In reasoning Lightman et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2023) and coding Le et al. (2022); Liu et al. (2023); Dai et al. (2026b) tasks, intermediate feedback can often be verified from execution results, compiler errors, unit tests, or other determin-istic checks. GUI tasks provide no comparable automatic signal for most intermediate operations: the value of a click, text entry, menu selection, or file operation depends on visual state changes, task requirements, and subsequent operations. GUI process reward modeling must therefore infer both whether an action is correctly executed and its semantic role from trajectory context. Existing GUI process reward methods can be broadly organized into fixed-window step rewards Chen et al. (2025); Xiong et al. (2025); Xiao et al. (2025); Lu et al. (2025); Wan et al. (2026) and milestone rewards Zheng et al. (2026); Hao et al. (2026); Li et al. (2026a); Dai et al. (2026a); Zhai et al. (2026). Fixed-window step rewards judge actions within a fixed local context. GUI-Shepherd Chen et al. (2025) learns step-level scores and rationales from human annotations, while GUI-PRA Xiong et al. (2025) selects historical context and actively perceives UI state changes to improve step-level judgment. SeekJudge Wan et al. (2026) further targets fine-grained step-level judgments for use 3Preprint within reinforcement learning. Milestone rewards organize feedback around predefined subgoals, checkpoints, or broader task-level structures. ADMIRE Zheng et al. (2026) dynamically distills verifiable milestones from successful explorations and applies asymmetric credit assignment to suc-cessful and failed trajectories. StainFlow Hao et al. (2026) tracks entity evidence to derive task stages and links relevant steps for key-node verification, while GUIDE Zhai et al. (2026) decom-poses completed trajectories into subtask units for structured diagnosis. These approaches organize action judgment within preset local contexts or task-level structures, whereas our adaptive-group framework dynamically partitions the executed trajectory into semantically coherent subgoal groups and combines execution and contribution at both the action level and the group level to assign each action a process reward.

Conditioned on this observation and the interaction history, it generates a response ct with an executable action at. Exe-cuting at changes the environment and produces the next observation ot+1 . As shown in Tables 1 and 2, our frame-work achieves the highest overall accuracy ( 81 .3), 3.6 points above GPT-5 with OpenCUA-Reflector (77 .7) and 6.4 points above GPT-5 with SE-WSM ( 74 .9). 5 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we present an adaptive-group process reward framework that dynamically partitions a GUI trajectory into semantically coherent subgoal groups, enabling process reward estimation at both the action and group levels. The process reward of each action is obtained by combining its action-level evaluation with the refined group-level evaluation. These process rewards are used to shape GRPO’s step-level advantage during policy optimization. Experiments on process reward benchmarks and downstream RL training demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework.

C.1 ERROR ANALYSIS 

We analyze 63 critic error steps on CUARewardBench, which fall into four categories (Table 9). Insufficient application knowledge ( 41 .3% ) is the largest share: the critic does not know the correct menu, setting, syntax, or operator, so its reference treats a wrong path as the intended procedure (e.g., Password Manager instead of cookie settings, Insert-Comment instead of View-Notes, or a Calc sheet reference written with “!” rather than “.”). Visual or interaction misreads ( 22 .2% ) are screenshot-level mistakes: a later date read as earlier, a filter chip taken as on, a slider taken as max, or a click that did not register. Missed instruction constraints ( 19 .0% ) are cases where g already specifies the requirement—working directory, numeric target, time range, “on sale”—but the critic scores the step without enforcing it. Overestimated group contribution ( 17 .5% ): the group-level score still treats a subgoal as helping the task when it does not (e.g., a group that resizes one layer when the task is to resize the whole image).
