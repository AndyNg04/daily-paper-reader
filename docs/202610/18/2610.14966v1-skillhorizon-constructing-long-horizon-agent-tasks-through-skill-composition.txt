## ABSTRACT 

Long-horizon agents must complete workflows in which later activities depend on earlier outputs and changes to shared state. A key challenge for evaluation is to expand task requirements and their dependencies while keeping tasks executable and outcomes verifiable. We introduce SkillHorizon, a benchmark that constructs long-horizon tasks through skill composition in seven business environments. Its generators use domain-specific rules to turn reusable skill procedures into concrete business activities. These activities are connected through input–output depen-dencies, shared resources, and state updates, allowing workload and coordination demands to grow together. Successful reference executions validate task feasibility and measure interaction workload, while programmatic evaluators assess local completion and whole-task success. We evaluate five language models on 1,050 tasks with reference interaction workloads ranging from 64K to 1M tokens. Across these scales, mean local-goal completion falls from 90.4% to 46.8%, as completed work fails to keep pace with growing requirements. At larger workloads, agents more often submit unsuccessful final answers with substantial work unfinished and tool-call allowance remaining. Trace analysis also identifies cases where scripts ex-ecute successfully but their outputs fail downstream business checks. SkillHorizon provides a configurable testbed for studying sustained progress, dependency coor-dination, and complete task delivery in growing workflows. Our code is available at https://github.com/KnowComp/SkillHorizon .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Language agents increasingly carry out business workflows that involve multiple activities and sustained interaction with tools and data (Xu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). The outputs of one activity become inputs to later stages, while resource commitments shape subsequent choices. Changes in the environment may also require completed work to be reviewed or revised. Agents must therefore maintain valid intermediate results and coordinate activities as they work toward the final goal. Studying this ability requires executable tasks that can grow in required work and dependencies, with persistent state and verifiable outcomes. Existing research provides foundations for long-horizon evaluation and skill reuse. WorkArena++ combines atomic tasks into enterprise workflows with reference executions and validation func-tions (Boisvert et al., 2024). TheAgentCompany evaluates work across business applications, code execution, and communication in a simulated workplace (Xu et al., 2024), while AgentLongBench studies information use over extended interaction histories (Fang et al., 2026). In parallel, Voyager de-velops an executable skill library, and SkillsBench evaluates packaged skills across tasks (Wang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026c). AgentSkillOS further studies skill retrieval and graph-based coordination (Li et al., 2026a). These studies support complex workflows and procedural reuse. Despite this progress, existing evaluations offer limited insight into how agents perform as more business activities are added and linked to earlier work. At the same time, resource allocations and completion checks must match the expanded task. The key challenge is to build these larger workflows from reusable skills while keeping them executable and their outcomes verifiable. We introduce SkillHorizon, a benchmark that constructs long-horizon tasks through skill composition in seven business environments spanning finance, field maintenance, social campaigns, logistics, re-search, documentation, and manufacturing. Domain-specific generators turn reusable skill procedures into business modules with concrete objects and goals. They connect modules through input–output dependencies and configure shared resources and state updates. Adding modules increases required work, while their connections determine which earlier results must be reused, combined, or revised. The generators create task goals and initial states together. Each environment provides public tools, skill resources, and programmatic evaluators. Successful reference executions validate task feasibility and measure interaction workload; evaluators check local-goal completion and whole-task acceptance. Figure 1 summarizes the pipeline. We evaluate five language models on 1,050 tasks with reference interaction workloads ranging from 64K to 1M tokens, yielding 5,250 model–task executions. Mean local-goal completion falls from 90.4% to 46.8% across these scales, as completed work fails to keep pace with growing requirements. We also analyze failed final answers with recorded completion and tool use. At 1M, 54.0% of these runs complete less than 25% of local goals while retaining over 50% of the tool-call allowance. The corresponding share is 2.4% at 64K. Trace analysis further identifies cases where scripts execute successfully but their outputs fail downstream business checks. These findings reveal difficulties in sustaining progress and using local results to complete larger workflows. Our contributions are threefold: 

1. Skill-based task construction. We introduce a method that builds business activities from reusable skills and connects them through input–output dependencies, shared resources, and state updates. The method supports configurable expansion of required work and coordination demands. 

2. An executable and scalable benchmark. We build seven persistent business environments with task generators, public tools, skill resources, reference executions, and programmatic evaluators. These resources support reproducible evaluation across task instances and workload scales. 

3. An empirical study of long-horizon execution. We evaluate five models across increasing workloads and identify gaps between required and completed work, including unfinished execution with unused tool allowance and downstream failures of script outputs. 2Preprint 

## 2 THE SKILL HORIZON BENCHMARK 

2.1 BUSINESS ENVIRONMENTS AND SKILLS 

SkillHorizon comprises seven executable business environments: FinanceOps, FieldOps, SocialOps, LogisticsOps, ResearchOps, DocOps, and FactoryOps. Each environment maintains a persistent business world of objects, resources, and artifacts, governed by domain-specific rules. Operations produce artifacts, allocate resources, and update business state. Their effects persist across interactions: later activities consume earlier outputs and must satisfy the resource and validity constraints of the current state. Appendix A.1 details the resources and business rules of each environment. Business operations are exposed as Model Context Protocol (MCP) tools (Model Context Protocol Contributors, 2025). Through domain-specific gateways, agents query business objects, invoke operations, and inspect artifacts. The environment checks requested operations against the current state and applicable business constraints, returning execution results or diagnostic feedback. Each environment E provides a library SE of reusable skills that organize public tool operations into business procedures. We characterize a skill s ∈ S E as 

s = ( αs, I s, P s, O s), (1) where αs specifies applicability conditions, Is required inputs, Ps the procedure, and Os expected outputs and state effects. Skills follow the Agent Skills package format (Agent Skills, n.d.), with procedural documentation and optional scripts and reference materials. During task construction, domain-specific rules instantiate these requirements and effects over concrete business objects to define local work. Agents select and apply these procedures through public tools. 2.2 DEPENDENCY -S TRUCTURED TASK CONSTRUCTION 

A business module is the unit of task composition. It specifies a piece of required business work by applying one or more skill procedures to concrete objects and inputs. Domain-specific rules determine its input requirements Im, required outputs and state effects Om, and completion conditions gm:

m = ( Im, O m, g m). (2) A module may combine several skills to fulfill its business requirements, while the same skill can be reused across modules with different objects and goals. We connect modules when completing one requires outputs produced by another. The resulting dependencies form a directed acyclic graph G = ( M, D ). Each edge e = ( mu, m v , d e) ∈ D

specifies the outputs that mv requires from mu, together with conditions on their content, provenance, quantity, or validity. Environment operations and completion checks enforce these requirements. Shared resources and mutable business state further constrain when modules can proceed and whether previously produced outputs remain usable. For a construction configuration c and sampling seed ξ, the environment-specific generator produces 

(G, x 0, g ) = Gen E (c, ξ ). (3) The configuration controls work scope, dependency patterns, resource conditions, and updates. The seed selects concrete objects, attributes, and connections within these rules. Construction couples the dependency graph G with an initial state x0 that supplies the corresponding business objects and resources, and a goal g that requires the generated work and its joint delivery conditions to be satisfied. Agents pursue this goal through public environment operations. We extend tasks by adding required business modules and connecting them to existing work through domain-specific composition rules. Sequential extension makes an earlier output a prerequisite for further work; joins require multiple upstream outputs to jointly satisfy a downstream requirement; long-range reuse carries earlier results into later stages. These connections determine which results must be combined and how far their content and validity must be maintained. Task expansion therefore controls both the amount of required work and the dependency structure through which that work contributes to the overall goal. Task expansion also determines the business state needed to support the generated work. Domain-specific rules derive resource quantities and allocations from task requirements while imposing 3Preprint capacity and scheduling constraints. For tasks with updates, these rules specify when changes occur and which artifacts or subsequent operations they affect. Existing results retain or lose validity according to the domain rules, creating requirements for revision and downstream coordination. Figure 1 summarizes the construction pipeline, and Appendix A.2 details the domain-specific rules. Public reference execution then verifies that each instance’s business requirements can be satisfied. 2.3 REFERENCE VALIDATION AND WORKLOAD CALIBRATION 

We validate generated instances with a rule-based reference policy that executes business activities through public environment tools, following the executable-oracle approach used in compositional benchmarks (Boisvert et al., 2024). The policy queries the current state and carries out the required work under the instance’s dependency, resource, and update conditions. An instance passes validation when its resulting artifacts and final business state satisfy the local completion requirements and global delivery constraints. We calibrate workload from these successful reference traces. We measure reference workload as the cumulative tool-interaction volume of a successful reference execution. For a trace containing R tool calls, we define 

Lref =

Fixed serialization and tokenization count each call–observation pair, including repeated queries. Thus, Lref measures tool interaction as the reference policy fulfills the task’s business requirements. To obtain tasks at different workload scales, we adjust domain-specific work-scale parameters under the selected construction rules and measure the resulting successful reference executions. Instances are assigned to workload bands according to their measured Lref . Alongside this measure, we record business work counts and dependency properties, including depth, joins, and reuse spans where applicable, to characterize the amount and organization of required work. Appendix A details the calibration parameters, workload-band boundaries, and instance-selection rules. 

## 3 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate agents on generated tasks that vary in the amount of required business work and the depen-dencies connecting it. Across seven executable environments, we measure terminal business-goal com-pletion and whole-task success to examine agents’ ability to sustain progress toward an overall goal. 3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models and harness. We evaluate Grok 4.6, Qwen3.8-Flash, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and GPT-5.6 Sol using a unified Harbor + Inspect framework across all seven environments (Harbor, n.d.; UK AI Security Institute, 2024). Agents interact with persistent business state through public tools, guided by skill documents and supplied scripts. Summary-based context compaction is triggered when estimated input length reaches 200,000 tokens, within an input capacity of 480,000 tokens (Anthropic, 2025). Business state and generated artifacts remain accessible through public tools. Appendix B specifies model configurations and execution settings. 

Task set. Tasks span seven business environments and five bands: 64K, 128K, 256K, 512K, and 1M tokens, assigned by measured reference workload Lref . Each band contains 210 tasks generated under approximately 100 parameter configurations spanning business scale, dependency structure, and update conditions. Appendix A details task sampling and construction profiles. 

Tool budget. We scale tool-attempt allowances using each task’s public reference execution: 

Bref = R + ⌈λA ⌉, λ = 0 .5,

4Preprint where R counts all reference tool calls and A counts business-execution calls. Queries, business operations, and corrective attempts share the allowance. Appendix B specifies accounting and actual allowances. 

Evaluation metrics. We evaluate task outcomes by checking the final environment state (Trivedi et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024). Local-goal completion (LGC) measures the fraction of local business goals satisfied at termination, averaged equally across tasks. Task success (TS) measures the proportion of tasks satisfying all final requirements, including cross-artifact dependencies and delivery constraints. Appendix C details the scoring and aggregation rules. 3.2 MAIN RESULTS

Performance declines with workload, and model rankings vary across scales. As shown in Table 1, all five models achieve lower LGC and TS at 1M than at 64K. Grok leads overall with 86.0% LGC and 71.8% TS, but its LGC falls from 95.5% to 73.4%, and its TS from 90.0% to 52.4%. Qwen and DeepSeek have similar overall LGC, at 72.2% and 72.1%, respectively, yet Qwen leads by 5.4 percentage points at 64K while DeepSeek leads by 7.2 points at 1M. Their TS ranking also reverses. Overall averages therefore do not fully characterize performance on longer tasks. 

High local-goal completion can coexist with low task success. From 64K to 1M, Gemini’s FieldOps LGC falls from 98.6% to 4.6%, while TS declines from 96.7% to zero. GPT’s FinanceOps LGC remains high, declining from 93.7% to 85.8%, while TS falls from 66.7% to zero. 

Task success follows different workload-scaling patterns across environments. As shown in Figure 2, Grok, DeepSeek, and Qwen each retain task success above 50% at 1M in SocialOps and DocOps. LogisticsOps and FieldOps show wider differences between models: Grok reaches 83.3% and 70.0%, respectively, while the other models achieve zero success in LogisticsOps and at most 33.3% in FieldOps. In FinanceOps, no model exceeds 20.0% task success at 1M. In ResearchOps and FactoryOps, all five models reach zero task success at this scale.

Substantial local progress does not guarantee complete task delivery. Across seven environments, 656 executions complete at least three-quarters of their local goals but leave the remaining work unfinished. These executions account for 9.5–14.6% of tasks within each workload band. Among executions ending with LGC of at least 80%, task success falls from 92.1% at 64K to 65.7% at 1M. Even at a 90% completion threshold, success falls from 95.3% to 71.6%. Thus, substantial local progress is less consistently accompanied by complete delivery at larger workloads. 

Successful local computation does not ensure valid downstream use. In SocialOps, downstream submissions can reproduce the outputs of successful skill-script executions and still be rejected. The task-averaged rejection rate of these submissions rises from 3.4% at 64K to 16.5% at 1M. Most rejections concern historical-source requirements or current audience loads. These failures occur despite agreement between the submitted values and script outputs, showing that skill composition also depends on whether those outputs satisfy the receiving operation’s business constraints. 4.3 RECOVERY OVER LONG HORIZONS

## 5 RELATED WORK 

Long-horizon benchmarks. AgentLongBench studies information synthesis over extended histo-ries (Fang et al., 2026), while LOCA-bench scales environment states to increase context without changing task semantics (Zeng et al., 2026). WorkArena++ and Toolathlon evaluate compositional workflows and cross-application execution (Boisvert et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). AgencyBench assesses extended real-world tasks through deliverables and rubrics (Li et al., 2026b), while Long-Horizon-Terminal-Bench provides subtask-level grading (Li et al., 2026d). SkillHorizon focuses on expanding skill-grounded business activities, artifact dependencies, and shared-state constraints to evaluate local progress and whole-task delivery as requirements grow. 

Agent skills. Reusable skills encode procedures that agents can apply across tasks. Voyager accumulates and composes executable skills (Wang et al., 2023), while the Agent Skills specification packages instructions with optional scripts and reference resources (Agent Skills, n.d.). SkillsBench compares task performance without skills, with curated skills, and with self-generated skills (Li et al., 2026c). AgentSkillOS combines hierarchical skill retrieval with DAG-based orchestration (Li et al., 2026a). These works study skills for task solving. SkillHorizon instead uses skills to define business activities and task dependencies, leaving execution plans to the agent. 

MCP-based environments and benchmarks. MCP standardizes access to external tools and data sources (Model Context Protocol Contributors, 2025). MCP-Universe, MCP-Bench, and MCPMark evaluate agents through real-world tool integrations, multi-step coordination, and programmatic outcome checks (Luo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Related API-based benchmarks, including AppWorld and τ -bench, assess task completion through application-state and database-goal checks (Trivedi et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024). However, expanding these task suites still involves task-specific generation, review, or verifier design, leaving systematic variation of workload and dependencies less directly supported. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced SkillHorizon, a benchmark that constructs long-horizon business tasks by composing skill-grounded activities and their dependencies across seven executable environments. Configurable generators expand required work and its connections, while public reference executions establish feasibility and programmatic checks evaluate business outcomes. Across five models and 64K–1M reference workloads, effective progress falls behind growing task requirements. Trace analyses reveal gaps between successful local execution and accepted business delivery, together with heterogeneous recovery and cross-compaction dependency-use patterns. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating sustained progress and downstream validity alongside local execution success. SkillHorizon provides a configurable testbed for studying how context management, skill use, and execution strategies support complete business workflows. Its generators support evaluation on new instances and larger workloads to assess whether improvements transfer across task scales. 9Preprint 

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

Tasks use synthetic objects in isolated environments, with no changes submitted to real business services. Private evaluation data are separated from public resources. Raw execution records preserve operational events independently of the paper’s outcome aggregation. External code and resources retain their provenance and applicable licensing requirements.

The following rules connect required business outcomes through upstream artifacts and shared constraints; agents choose legal operation sequences. 

Financial sources and stage closures. FinanceOps adds source work packages within logical stages. Its families require format and identity selection, evidence-backed component adjustments, or remittance reconciliation against original invoices and credit evidence. Stage closures combine all required source views with the preceding closure; review and publication cover the whole release. Checks assess numerical results and identity or payment bindings. Source corrections invalidate affected outputs and downstream closures while preserving immutable historical views and unrelated valid work. Business facts vary independently of opaque identities; public decision metadata supports input selection. 

Maintenance and service commissioning. FieldOps expands device-maintenance and service-commissioning networks. Upstream commissioning records supply pressures, flows, identities, and revisions that constrain downstream inlet conditions and maintenance windows. Repairs and commissioning require qualified crews, compatible spares, and shared flow capacity. Configurations vary devices per service, parent joins, and physical requirements. The selected set has 75 normal and 75 recovery tasks. Recovery scenarios introduce two progress-triggered supply changes or a local device fault, requiring renewed certification or revised repairs and downstream certifications. Valid repairs and unrelated work remain reusable. Corrective re-isolation checks scheduling and dependencies before reopening restored boundaries. 

Campaign waves. SocialOps links campaign waves through measured reports. Staggered, dis-tributed, alternating fork–join, and protected-branch patterns vary parent origins and joins. New variants combine parent winners’ audiences and calls to action with arm-specific permissions and newly consented audiences. Each wave adds publication, measurement, reporting, and deployment obligations under scheduling and exposure conditions. Rights updates require affected unpublished drafts to obtain valid assets and approvals while preserving published history. 12 Preprint 

Itinerary and custody chains. LogisticsOps adds route-leg bookings and baggage deliveries. Cohorts share ground transfers; staggered or distributed patterns vary delivery spans and joins. Each freight movement consumes a release or predecessor manifest establishing custody at its departure hub and produces the next handoff. Route-specific services, schedules, handling permissions, and weight-based shared capacities constrain feasible connections. Completion requires both passenger arrangements and baggage delivery. Carrier withdrawals require affected custody plans to be revised while valid arrangements are retained. 

Calibration and evidence rounds. ResearchOps adds calibration anchors and studies by acquisition round. Derived anchors compose earlier calibration maps, and at least one compatible study uses each new anchor. Contributions bind current annotations for the named calibration and its ancestors. Metric, population, and unit rules determine usable evidence. Corrections revise derived interpretations and withdrawals change eligible contributions; prior committed reviews retain their historical identity. 

Document stages. DocOps links each page to its previous-stage counterpart. Chain–join adds periodic cross-lane parents, braided adds eligible cross-lane parents at each level, and distributed also adds older parents. Local grants specify purpose and expiry; parent contracts enumerate consumers, weights, and allocation limits. Publication combines parent contracts with local fields under schema, purpose, expiry, and capacity rules. Public bulletins announce amendments, preserving an unaffected lane and valid history. Page acceptance requires a current publication and matching tracker binding. In reference executions, 200 updates across 100 tasks each affect 2–29 published pages while preserving 3–56 others. 

Production levels. FactoryOps assigns each order a preceding-level supplier and another compatible supplier from the preceding, an earlier, or the root level. Every nonterminal order supplies later work. Terminal demand propagates backward through the bill of materials to determine upstream quantities and raw stock. Produced units carry lineage and quality measurements downstream; completion requires terminal dispatch. Equipment occupancy, maintenance, calibration, and selective rework constrain execution. Tooling drift affects future work while accepted units retain their status. A.3 GENERATION AND WORKLOAD CALIBRATION

C.2 EXECUTION AND RECOVERY EVENTS 

Dependencies are traced through the identities, versions, and contents of artifacts produced and consumed by business operations. F.1 INSTANCE GENERATION AND HORIZON EXTENSION 

A renewable source of tasks. SkillHorizon specifies task families through business rules, con-struction parameters, and seeds. Seeds sample business objects, attributes, and connections within a configuration. Construction parameters vary the amount of required work, dependency structure, and resource or update conditions. Together, they support replenishing evaluation sets and testing transfer across configurations. Task diversity is characterized by variation in these business properties and the decisions they require. 

Extending required business work. Task composition provides a route toward indefinitely extensi-ble workflows: add required activities, connect them to earlier outputs, and extend the associated resources and completion requirements. Sequential dependencies lengthen chains of obligations, joins combine multiple results, and long-range reuse connects later work to earlier evidence. Shared resources and updates introduce dependencies through changing business state. Public reference execution checks that the enlarged workflow remains feasible. The released builders use bounded construction profiles; further scale extension couples broader profiles with resource, update, and reference-execution validation. This approach makes business obligations and their connections the basis of horizon growth. 

Business horizon and context length. Longer workflows generate cumulative interaction histories that can be processed through repeated compaction under a fixed input capacity. Business state and artifacts remain accessible through public tools, allowing agents to recover earlier evidence and continue execution. Reference workload measures the interactions of the public reference execution, while active-context length measures the information presented at a particular step. Recording both alongside business work counts links execution demands to information retention and recovery. F.2 A T ESTBED FOR IMPROVING LONG -H ORIZON AGENTS 

Context and dependency management. One future use is to compare summarization, retrieval, and dependency-aware memory on identical generated tasks, with the same environment, business checks, tools, and execution allowance. First-attempt correctness, recovery from stale or missing inputs, final completion, and evidence-retrieval cost capture both the reliability of continuation and the effort required to maintain it. 

Skills and execution frameworks. Another direction is to evaluate changes to skill documents, supplied scripts, and agent controllers (Liu et al., 2026b;a;c). Fixing each candidate before testing on held-out seeds and configurations allows evaluation of transfer to new tasks and larger workloads. State-based business checks provide common outcome measures across systems that choose different action sequences. Process measurements connect changes in scheduling, handoffs, and recovery to sustained progress and complete delivery. 

Iterative and recursive self-improvement. Continual generation also offers a testbed for repeated improvement cycles. A prospective protocol alternates revision of skills, memory policies, or controllers on development trajectories with evaluation of the resulting agent on fresh tasks. Held-out configurations test transfer to new dependency and update patterns, while improvement and execution costs quantify the resources used across cycles. Recursive self-improvement research can extend this protocol to evaluate changes to the improvement procedure itself, alongside changes to the task-solving agent. 29
