Human-in-the-loop optimization refers to systems that present choices to a human decision-maker and query
their judgment to optimize system parameters iteratively. The user interface typically provides either an
absolute rating scale or asks for a preferred option. However, these approaches can potentially suffer from
calibration problems, inconsistencies, and completeness violations of user preferences in such a sequential
evaluation. This paper revisits the design space of opinion measurement interfaces for human-in-the-loop
optimization. We conducted a study (N=360) using text summarization and image color enhancement tasks
to evaluate human sequential feedback in six representative opinion measurement interfaces. Based on our
analysis, we recommend using a listwise approach to support sequential opinion measurement, which coun-
terweighs individual rating and preferential choice limitations and conveys more information for subsequent
use. We further discuss the trade-offs in different user interface designs and provide guidelines to inform
future research.

Introduction
Human-in-the-loop [46] (HITL) optimization has received increasing attention in HCI [11, 76]. Such
systems query decision makers for their judgment among machine proposals, e.g., using Bayesian
optimization (BO) [34, 64] as the background AI and then optimize underlying system parameters
toward user preferences according to the feedback. One of the benefits is that this adaptation
can support users to explore a design space [11, 32, 75] defined by finite criteria or retain more
control over pre-configured system decision strategies [63]. However, the opinion measurement
interface [51] used as input component in HITL optimization has many design parameters itself.
Depending on the types of human feedback required, the whole process has different limitations due
to the design of the input interface. For example, a commonly used pointwise user interface (UI) asks
the user to rate a given option as utility-based direct feedback, see Figure 2a. Although, literature has
shown that humans are not good at absolute judgments [70] and tend to give local judgments [8].
Therefore, a rating interface could suffer from miscalibration and noisy human feedback, thus
reducing human-AI cooperation performance. Another popular pairwise design [8, 13] is to ask
humans which of the two given system outcomes is preferred (preference-based indirect feedback).
However, indirect preferential feedback is less efficient in measuring human opinions and requires
querying more preferential feedback to model a latent preference function [24, 49, 66]. Additionally,
it might suffer from interpretation ambiguity, intransitive and circular preferences (e.g., prefer A
over B, and prefer B over C, but prefer C over A, known as rock-paper-scissors relation), which also
presents a challenge for input precision and overall system performance.
To overcome the limited number of evaluations at a time and collect more feedback, one can
extend pointwise (𝑛=1) and pairwise (𝑛=2) feedback to listwise ranking [10, 66] (𝑛> 2), where
𝑛is the number of alternative options. However, many input interface design variations depend
on the queried feedback type. Specifically, for direct rating feedback, the listwise approach needs
to use 𝑛different rating scales; for indirect preferential feedback, the listwise approach requires
a UI to allow users to rank the given outcome alternatives. Despite different queried feedback
types, a listwise interface always implicitly facilitates comparisons, as it presents more options
simultaneously. Recently, Ou et al. [54] presented a new approach to overcome current limitations
using a 𝑛-alternative ranking approach which provides a “I don’t know” option for signal items.
However, the effectiveness is not yet understood. Thus, the main objective of this paper is to
understand the following two questions:
RQ1 What is the impact of using different opinion measurement interfaces for HITL optimization?
RQ2 What is the best-suited interface to use in a HITL optimization to leverage human opinions?
Due to the many design choices influencing opinion measurement interfaces, we first derived
the design space for opinion measurement interfaces on HITL optimization. We identified six
design variations, including pointwise, pairwise, and listwise approaches depending on the type
of feedback queried. They can also be varied to support expressing indifference and incomplete
preferences. Based on our design space, we conducted an online between-groups study (N=360) to
understand the impact of six opinion measurement interfaces on HITL optimization. We selected
a text summarization task and an image color enhancement task to evaluate the different opinion
measurement interfaces in two domains. In both tasks, humans express their feedback using
different user interfaces regarding the quality of text summarization or color enhancement.
As a key result, we recommend using a listwise approach and allowing users to express ranking
distance to support sequential opinion measurement. This design counterweighs individual ratings
and preferential limitations and conveys more direct and effective information for subsequent
usage in HITL optimization. The main contribution of this paper is an empirical investigation of

Related Work
In this section, we will discuss the assumptions made when measuring human opinions and how
they relate to actual human decisions and preferences. Then, we will discuss how these assumptions
could impact modeling human feedback with BO. Lastly, we will revisit the opinion measurement
interfaces commonly used today in non-sequential measuring contexts and how existing HITL
optimization systems design their opinion measurement interfaces.
2.1
Assumptions in Human Preferences and Decisions
Behaviorism argues that objective decisions and judgments of a human can be observed through
their behavior. However, this observation may be limited by the ambiguity of interpretation [68]
due to a lack of observation and impermeable states of mind [55]. Opinion measurement is an
important complement to behaviorism [51]. An opinion measurement UI measures “tendencies to
evaluate an entity with some degree of favor or disfavor, ordinarily expressed in cognitive, affective,
and behavioral responses” [22], which reflect human preferences or decisions under uncertainty.
Traditional behavioral economics has widely studied rational choice [19], and the corresponding
algorithmic modeling to infer preferences relied on the transitivity and completeness assumptions.
The formulation of transitivity includes two aspects: strict and weak. Strict transitivity states that
one choice option is strictly preferred over another, while weak transitivity allows indifference
between given choice options. Transitivity as a modeling assumption assumes that people can give
consistent ranks to given choice options. However, this assumption may be violated mainly in a
sequential judgment context [5, 17] since a previous preference may be inverted later in the process
and result in a rock-paper-scissors relation due to context changes and cognitive biases [70].
Moreover, completeness assumes that people always have a preference, but this does not hold
for decision-making under uncertain information, where in some cases, the preference may be
undecided [52]. Identifying the violation of the completeness assumption is more fundamental and
complex by definition, as it is harder to define from a behavior perspective. Consequently, violating
these assumptions can be framed as preference or decision uncertainty, and it is often strongly
assumed that an inverted choice constitutes a noisy observation (e.g., [4, 24, 39, 58]) without a
detailed inspection from the human perspective.
2.2
Modeling Human Feedback with Bayesian Optimization
Bayesian optimization [47] is a technique used for the global optimization of expensive-to-evaluate
functions. It builds a probabilistic model of the objective function and iteratively selects the next
point to evaluate based on an acquisition function that balances exploration and exploitation.
The model is updated as new evaluations are made, improving its accuracy over time. Bayesian
optimization is beneficial when the objective function is black-box, expensive to evaluate, and
multiple local optima exist. It has been successfully applied to various optimization problems in
machine learning, engineering, and science [64].
In addition to engineering aspects, BO can also model human feedback and enables HITL
optimization [64] utilizing, for example, biological measurements [18, 29], interaction behavior [12],
and measured preferential choices [8, 24, 33]. It learns a posterior from the measured human
feedback and aims to search for a global maximum of an unknown function by exploration and
exploitation. In conjunction with measured human feedback, two popular genres of BO are actively
being researched, as shown in Table 1. Utility-based BO [47] requires measuring human feedback as

When dealing with preference-based human feedback, PBO as a specialized category of BO has
received increasing development in recent studies [24, 44]. While BO learns based on absolute
utility (e.g., absolute ratings of a given option), PBO learns relative rating or preferential selection
as ranking information. To avoid the violations of the transitivity described above, the recent
extensions [4, 32, 66] of PBO transited from using a binary pairwise comparison to using a reasonable
amount of options, resulting in a listwise comparison. These extensions could largely prevent
violations of transitivity and infer more information at a time because they either consider choosing
a set of options as winners among all given options [4, 32]; or provide a ranking of all given options,
where options may share the same level of rank [66]. Note that more ranking elements may also
increase the uncertainty for users and cause them to make imperfect decisions [46] due to increased
workload. Thus, one should carefully consider the presented number of elements. Although PBO
has used pairwise comparisons to mitigate various issues regarding unstable human judgment,
these current approaches could also violate preference assumptions due to cognitive limitations.
Tversky and Kahneman [70] have widely presented how heuristic biases might influence the
choice behavior. External causes can also produce a considerable amount of noisy observations
in choice [27]. To overcome the violation of preference assumptions, PBO has also considered
handling noisy inputs [36] and guarantees theoretical convergence when dealing with unstable
choices. Despite all these developments in PBO, we still observe two challenges in practice. The first
is that a human might change their objective during the optimization process, even when using
pairwise comparisons. However, PBO assumes a fixed implicit underlying choice function that it
can learn. Although PBO can handle noisy input, the other challenge is that it requires much more
iterations to let the optimization converge. This is usually very costly when involving a human.
We, therefore, need to design the UI carefully to mitigate these challenges and reduce errors.

2.3
Opinion Measurement Interfaces
Conventional opinion measurement interfaces have different absolute measures, such as categorical
choice and ordinal Likert-scale measures. The underlying analysis is based on Thurstone’s law of
comparison [69]. The optimal design of a pointwise measuring UI has been explored in psychology
to reduce measuring error, for example, by asking repeated questions and checking the overall
consistency[65], regarding the influence of ticks [43], or by allowing users to express uncertainty
about their own input [25]. Previous work also discussed improving rating accuracy in the HITL
context [21, 50].
A first systematic evaluation regarding the design space was provided by Nobarany et al. [51].
They also observed that the rating interface might not be ideal for measuring opinions and discussed
how to support ranking in the interface. In a way, this is similar to how PBO was designed [7, 8, 24];
opinion measurement interfaces that support comparison were also found beneficial here. Kalloori
et al. [28] discussed a few reasons for better-measured opinions using preference-based feedback:
1) Explicit rating utility suffers from calibration of drift, for concepts with different types of
interpretations might lead to differently rated results. 2) Users might change their opinion from
time to time due to anchoring [57]. 3) Relative questions might potentially be faster and easier
to answer. Due to the increased complexity of feedback types, ranking UIs are rarely explored,
although there is initial work in this direction [41, 48].
Interfaces used in the Real World. Opinions are frequently measured in real life for a variety of
purposes. We can generally observe three genres of these UIs: pointwise, pairwise, and listwise UIs,
as shown in Figure 2.
Interfaces used in HITL Optimization. As shown in Figure 3, many designs for opinion measure-
ment interfaces are used in HITL optimization. Specifically, the presented examples can be treated
as a mix of pairwise and listwise comparisons. The underlying feedback type that models the
UI’s input form is either utility- or preference-based. Because of this diversity, we will provide a
taxonomy to categorize them and determine our study design in the next section.

3
Design Space
We revisit the design space of opinion measurement interfaces and extend it to include multiple
design variations of the listwise approach. Table 2 provides an overview of the design space of
opinion measurement interfaces. The design space formally contains these two dimensions: the
number of alternative options and the underlying feedback type.
The number of alternative options encodes the complexity when measuring opinion. With more
alternative options, we can gather more feedback from a user simultaneously. An interface is
pointwise if it only measures the opinion regarding one item. Similarly, interfaces that measure
information about two paired items are pairwise. With more than two options, the interface is
listwise. For example, in Figure 2a all interfaces are pointwise as they measure opinions regarding
a single instance. Conceptually, a listwise interface also covers interfaces that potentially allow
exploring infinite options (n=∞, e.g., Koyama et al. [33]). However, regardless of the number of
options, since a user has to select or mark a subset of preferential relations among all options,
e.g., Wilber et al. [73], a multiple-item selection can be considered a strict preference between
selected and unselected ones. In contrast, the preference order among (un)selected ones remains
unspecified. Note that although an interface presents paired options to measure human opinion, it

could still ask humans to estimate the distance between two options, which only conveys absolute
distance utility as a rating regarding one item to the reference. Furthermore, although Figure 3f
presents four alternatives and asks how close they are to what the user is looking for, this is
an absolute rating only concerning the user’s idea of an optimum (which remains unknown to
everybody else). These considerations imply that the underlying feedback type, which the framed
UI presents as a usage instruction, is also crucial for opinion measurement.
The underlying type of feedback can be either utility-based or preference-based. Utility-based
interfaces query a human to provide an absolute ordinal or cardinal value regarding an option (the
interpretation of the value can differ depending on the context). Preference-based interfaces only
measure relative information regarding paired options and, therefore, need further consideration
to infer a latent utility. On the one hand, since utility-based interfaces only provide numeric
information directly associated with the option, the relative information between options may not
be explicit. However, we can sort the utility value. On the other hand, preference-based interfaces
only provide local information. A hybrid feedback type provides a subtle design consideration to
mix utility- and preference-based information. Similarly, we have two design alternatives, either
strict or weak, depending on the type of preference.
3.1
Applicability
We can place the previously presented examples into this design space. Figure 2a contains 1-RS
and n-RS: Twitter and Tinder only utilize binary responses regarding one option (like or not). The
Amazon “by feature” rating example is n-RS but evaluates multiple dimensions using scales. In
Figure 2b, there are two examples of 2-AFC or 2-ANFC. Figure 2c shows three listwise interfaces:
n-AFR (songs are ranked without allowing ties and without information about how they are being
ranked), n-AFRD (options ranked with a distance, items with the same utility value are forced to
be placed on different ranks). Figure 3a and Figure 3b are 𝑛-ANFR because they require humans
to rank options (permitting ties) without specifying a distance to other options, Figure 3c and
Figure 3d are 2-AFC because they are judging two options and cannot tell indifference through the
UI, Figure 3e is 2-ANFC because it offers “can’t tell” to its user, Figure 3f is 𝑛-RS, and Figure 3g is
1-RS because it is judging how far one item is away from the other, and only judges one option.
Figure 3h is 𝑛-AFR because it selects one out of all options as top-1 ranking. In fact, Figure 3a and
Figure 3h also use a meaningful 2D layout to present the alternative choices instead of a plain list.
While this may improve the efficiency and user experience in specific tasks, it does not change the
general structure and, therefore, the classification of the interface. Lastly, Figure 3i is 𝑛-ANFRD
that asks users to rank options with a distance but also allows ties.
4
Hypotheses
Using this design space, we identified six promising opinion measurement interfaces, i.e., 1-RS,
2-ANFC, 𝑛-RS, 𝑛-AFR, 𝑛-ANFR, 𝑛-ANFRD (UI1 to UI6), see Figure 1. Our selection is informed by
results from related work, which hints that some might be better than others. Thus, we formulate
four major hypotheses concerning the performance of HITL optimization when using such an
interface:
Hypothesis 1: Baseline. Early work on HITL optimizations has shown great performance when
using 2-ANFC [7–9]. Their results showed that 2-ANFC systems generally perform better than
1-RS and greatly support HITL systems. We use their initial findings as our baseline comparison
and to validate our apparatus. Thus, we hypothesize (H1) that the preference-based interface will
outperform the utility-based interface regarding performance measures (UI1 vs. UI2).

Hypothesis 2: Impact of Listwise Approach. If H1 is true, this indicates that giving the user more
options for comparison will improve the overall system performance. In fact, this is what findings
by Cao et al. [10] suggest. Thus, we hypothesize that a listwise comparison (𝑛> 2), will outperform
comparisons with fewer options. As Listwise is the most flexible in terms of feedback, we next
investigate the different strategies that emerged from related work [32, 33, 44]. In this work, we
investigate design variations in which the human can either give preferential ratings (e.g., Koyama
et al. [30]), provide a strict ranking selection (e.g., Koyama et al. [32]), a weak ranking selection
(e.g., Marks et al. [42]), or a combination of those (hybrid). Consequently, we hypothesize (H2a)
that a listwise interface will outperform a pointwise interface when using utility-based feedback (UI1
vs. UI3). And we hypothesize (H2b) that a listwise interface will outperform a pairwise interface
when using preference-based feedback (UI2 vs. UI5).
Hypothesis 3: Impact of Listwise Design Variations. Based on the initial findings by Mikkola et al.
[44], we additionally hypothesize H3a and H3b. In detail, we hypothesize H3a that a preference-
based listwise ranking interface will outperform a utility-based listwise rating interface (cf. [7–
9]) while allowing for expressing indifference (UI3 vs. UI5). Further, regarding indifference, we
hypothesize H3b that being able to express indifference in the interface (weak preference) will be
better than a forced choice (strict preference) (UI4 vs. UI5).
Hypothesis 4: Impact of Hybrid Approach. Based on hypotheses H1-H3 and assuming that their
outcome in this work is in line with prior work, Ou et al. [54] used a new hybrid feedback interface.
For this, n-ANFRD (UI6), we pick what we consider to be the best to support the HITL optimization,
thus, getting the most information from the user. Thus, we hypothesize that the hybrid listwise
interface will outperform: H4a the pairwise interface (UI2), H4b the listwise rating interface (UI3),
H4c the listwise strict ranking interface (UI4), and H4d the listwise weak ranking interface (UI5).

User Study
We conducted a user study to investigate how different opinion measurement interfaces impact
the overall HITL optimization performance to answer our research questions (RQ1 and RQ2 in
Section 1) and verify our four hypotheses. Here, we compare the six user interfaces (6 conditions:
UI1-UI6, Table 2 and Figure 1) using a between-subject design (Interface). We used tasks from
two domains as a between-subject variable (Domain) to shorten the participation time and prevent
a learning effect. One group did text summarization tasks from the natural language processing
domain. The other group did image color enhancement tasks from the design domain. As dependent

5.1
User Interfaces
As shown in Figure 1, we selected six generic interfaces from this design space (1-RS, 2-ANFC,
𝑛-RS, 𝑛-AFR, 𝑛-ANFR, 𝑛-ANFRD) to facilitate our study design better. All interfaces are shown in
Figure 4 for both tasks. The following describes the behavior of the six user interfaces:
UI1 1-RS uses a bipolar slider-based Likert scale, similar to 𝑛-RS.
UI2 2-ANFC uses two radio buttons for preference indication.
UI3 n-RS uses a bipolar slider-based Likert scale to indicate the preference for each option.
UI4/5 𝑛-AFR requires a full ranking of all options, and the interface does not allow expressing
indifference in this case, whereas 𝑛-ANFR (UI5) can, which characterizes strict and weak
preferences.
To guarantee a pure ranking behavior in the interface, 𝑛-AFR (UI4), and 𝑛-ANFR (UI5)
automatically adjust the distance between options. For example, if a user dropped two boxes
on the 1st and 4th choice container box. Then the 4th choice will automatically move up to
the 2nd choice and inform users that their ranking is automatically aligned and normalized.
UI6 𝑛-ANFRD is the most flexible listwise ranking interface that permits users to place the
option box in any given container box.
Additionally, all interfaces include either checkbox options or container boxes to allow users to
express incomplete preferences.
5.2
Task
As many applications use HITL optimization, any domain with a set of system parameters to
be determined could be used as a HITL optimization task. Examples of such domains are text
summarization [54, 67], image color enhancement [31–33, 54], melody composition [76], 3D mesh-
ing [53, 54], 3D material design [8], 3D animation [7], and 3D illumination [42]. In line with previous
work [54], we selected text summarization and image color enhancement for the following reasons:
First, text and image responses between client and server are easy to handle, lightweight, and
fast. Second, the quality of text summarization and image color enhancement is a partly aesthetic
judgment, for example, regarding the exact wording and color style. Therefore, it is still challenging
to measure this quality objectively [6, 54], making it plausible to bring a human into the loop.
5.3
Apparatus
We developed the frontend UI1 to UI6 (see Figure 4 and Supplement Video that illustrates the
use of all interfaces) using Material UI1 and React DnD2. We wrote out backend in Go for easier
concurrency management. It serves our frontend interfaces, data collection, and communications
with other dedicated computing microservices, including two domain services and an optimizer
service. We deployed the backend on our institute infrastructure (Ubuntu 20.04, 8-Core Intel Core
i9-9900K, 64GB RAM, and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti with 11GB of GPU memory).

We use Nucleus sampling [26] as a stochastic text decoding strategy4 for our inference use
case because it allows for a bounded parameter space (between 0 and 1) and can generate diverse
human-like sentences in the inference phase. Since we designed our user task to consider the length
of summarization as a decision criterion, we used a summarization ratio parameter as a hard limit
that controls the text generation length and a length penalty parameter as a selected soft limit
that encourages the model to generate shorter text. As a result, our hosted text summarization
service allows 4 adjustable system parameters at every model inference stage: 1) summarization
ratio, 2) length penalty, 3) top-p, and 4) temperature.
5.3.2
Domain Services: Color Enhancement. For image color enhancement, we used a parameterized
photo enhancer [31–33] as an image processing service. It allows 5 adjustable system parameters
that can range from 0 to 1: 1) brightness, 2) contrast, 3) saturation, 4) temperature, and 5) tint.
5.3.3
Optimizer Service and Bayesian Optimizers. We used Bayesian optimization to give users
new options based on their input history. As there is no one Bayesian optimizer to cater to the
needs of all six interfaces, we selected the best Bayesian optimizer for each interface. In detail,
UI1 and UI3 used naive BO [45], UI2 used EUBO [39], and UI4-UI6 used the same advanced EUBO.
While this could lead to performance differences, we perform benchmark comparisons to ensure
they have equal performance; see Appendix A for full details.
We used BoTorch [2] to implement all selected optimizers, which reads the user responses to
estimate the next optimal system parameters for exploration. The Bayesian optimizer for rating-
based interfaces (UI1 and UI3) used the Gaussian process as a surrogate model with Martern 5/2
kernel. The acquisition function is an expected improvement acquisition function that models noisy
inputs [36]. We used EUBO [39], a BO variate cf. Section 2.2, as a state-of-the-art PBO optimizer
that considers noisy inputs to estimate system parameters for the pairwise interface (UI2). The
acquisition function is the Analytic Expected Utility Of Best Option acquisition function [39]. The
design of the EUBO algorithm guarantees that the current optimum is included in pairwise or
listwise comparisons, and we can use the maximum posterior expected utility [14] to understand
the impact on outcome utility.
To guarantee fair comparisons and control experiment variables, instead of using different
optimizers for all other listwise interfaces (i.e., UI4, UI5, and UI6), we extended EUBO to fit all
listwise interfaces5: We first fit a Gaussian process using the user’s rank data, then use the optimizer
learned latent utility 𝑓(·) to fit another Gaussian process and infer the next batch of exploration
positions. As EUBO maximizes the outcome utility difference between iterations, one can use the
utility to analyze the optimization process. Moreover, in UI2 and UI4-UI6, although EUBO does not
assume noiseless observations, we discarded indifference and incomplete preference input instead
of generating a random preference order as 1) EUBO does not model indifference, 2) providing noisy
input can decrease optimization performance and make a strong assumption about the preference
model. These three Bayesian optimizers can optimize for our objectives and outperform pure
random exploration in various simulated tasks, see Appendix A for comparisons.

5.4
Procedure
The overall procedure for the participants is visualized in Figure 5. Participants started the study
with an informed consent form and answered initial demographic questions, including their age,
gender, and levels of language proficiency.
The opinion measurement interface then presents a set of evaluation options to the participants.
The main task is to provide feedback, using the UI, to the AI to summarize the given news articles as
much as possible while preserving the meaning of the article or enhance the image color as much as
possible according to the preferences of the participants. Because the optimizers need initialization
samples, in the first 4 iterations, a participant evaluated outcomes produced by quasi-random Sobol
sampled [60, 74] system parameters. After acquiring these preference priors from participants,
starting from the 5th iteration, the optimizer is used, and participants can freely terminate when
satisfied with the current system outcomes. The task ends automatically after 20 iterations to limit
participation time. After termination, participants answered six questions about their satisfaction
with the system outcomes and their experience using the interface to provide feedback.
Each participant completed three Latin square-shuffled 1) news articles selected from the CNN
daily mail6 dataset, which covered topics of sports, politics, and entertainment7, or 2) photos8
selected from the Sequential Gallery by Koyama et al. [32].
5.5
Participants
We recruited participants worldwide via Prolific. Because participants had different median com-
pletion times in different experimental conditions, we paid between £3 to £9 upon completion,
corresponding to an hourly wage of £9/h. Participation was under informed consent, and the study
adhered to the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). In total, we collected data
from 635 participants from 25 countries. To guarantee high-quality responses, we only consider
participants if they: 1) had an approval rate of 95%, 2) completed the study only once, 3) answered
with consistent demographics, e.g., not more than five years of age difference in the study compared
to the platform registration information, and 4) provided their response in at least a reasonable
amount of time, i.e., spent longer than 3 seconds in each iteration to read the summarized text,
longer than 1 second in each iteration to check the image and interact with the interface according
to our pilot study observations. Therefore, we will report our results based on 360 participants (171
female, 185 male, and 4 diverse; age 𝜇= 28.14, 𝜎= 8.39, range 18-66), with varied self-indicated

5.6
Performance Indicators and Measurements
A high-performance HITL optimization loop is considered successful from the human side when the
human spends less time on providing feedback, can achieve a satisfactory result in fewer iterations,
and the interface allows the human to give more precise and clear feedback. From the machine side,
the underlying optimizer can learn human feedback more effectively and propose outcomes of high
objective quality faster. Therefore, we measured participants’ interaction details in each interface
as behavior measures. Next, we recorded participants’ provided feedback and corresponding system
outcomes as feedback measures for the optimization loop. Lastly, we measured users’ subjective
experience with the system after each completion as questionnaire measures.
5.6.1
Behavior Measures. We measured the participants’ interaction in each interface using six
measurements. 1) Decision Time per Iteration, measured from when the evaluation options were
presented to the last time when participants interacted with the interface, 2) Decision Time per
Item, the Decision Time per Iteration divided by the number of items shown, 3) number of iterations
per task, 4) Number of Ranking Interactions, the number of operations to rank the given system
outcomes in listwise interfaces, 5) Number of Indifferent Preference, number of pairs that are rated
the same, and 6) Number of Incomplete Preference counted as the number of times the “I don’t know”
option was ticked per task.
5.6.2
Feedback Measures. To evaluate the optimization loop on the human side, we collected
participants’ rating and preferential ranking data in each iteration. Here, we used the normalized
number of iterations as Progress over the course of the HITL optimization. Moreover, we quantify
human feedback using the user feedback from direct rating (UI1, UI3, and UI610) or the optimizer
learned latent utility from preferential rankings (UI2, UI4, UI5, and UI6). The learned latent utility
is a Laplace approximation of the posterior mean of the estimated utility values [14]. As EUBO
maximizes the posterior expected utility [39], it represents the estimated user feedback when the
query is chosen in the input space based on the available data and the current Gaussian process
model. By comparing the posterior mean values at different iterations, we can gain insights into
how the optimizer performs and whether it converges toward the user’s expected solution.
On the machine side, we computed the Length of the text, the ROUGE (ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2,
and ROUGE-L) [38], and BLEU [56] scores for the optimized text summarization to measure the
objective quality of summarized texts as they correlate positively with human evaluation. Ou
et al. [54] used the exact same measures in their HITL text summarization task. ROUGE measures
how many human-summarized words appear in the machine-generated summaries, and BLEU
measures precision regarding how many words in the machine-generated words appear in the
human reference summaries. For the image color enhancement, we computed Saturation, Contrast,
and Brightness using a mean of pixel-wise subtraction between source and outcome in the H, S,
and V channels correspondingly. Furthermore, we computed the mean pixel-wise difference of
U channels in YUV color space for Tint changes and similarly in the V channel for Temperature
changes. These five measures are also in line with prior work by Ou et al. [54].
Note: While the computed quality metrics are not true measurements of quality per se, together
with the strategy of the optimizer to always show the current best choice, a consistent trend here
can be seen as an indication of continued improvements and considered a successful optimization if

5.6.3
Questionnaire Measures. In line with work by Ou et al. [54], we asked six questions to obtain
100-point bipolar Likert scale responses for robust input [23] without ticks to prevent biases [43].
Q1) I strongly agree the final result matches my expectation of the results; Q2) I firmly believe I can
do better a result alone than this AI; Q3) I am strongly satisfied with the final result; Q4) I strongly
believe the AI indeed improved the result based on my feedback.; Q5) I think the “I don’t know” option
helped me to express my feedback to the AI more precisely, and Q6) I believe I had given clear feedback
to the AI. Here, Ou et al. [54] showed that Q1-Q4 have a high correlation and therefore used the
aggregated value as an indicator for Satisfaction. Q5 indicates whether expressing “I don’t know”
was considered helpful, and Q6 indicates whether users think that the UI can give feedback clearly.
Lastly, we obtained open-ended responses from participants with the instruction “Describe your
experience while working with this AI.”
6
Results
In this section, we first present participants’ behavior regarding interface interactions, including
their total iterations, decision time per iteration, and how they express indifference and incomplete
preferences. Then, we show the effectiveness of the feedback optimization loop regarding the
objective quality and feedback utility. Finally, we present subjective responses and comments from
the questionnaire. Since our hypotheses H1 and H2 in Section 4 compare two UIs, we analyze
normally distributed data using a t-test or a Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test in case the Shapiro-Wilk
test [59] indicates that the normality is violated. We use linear mixed models (LMMs) [3, 35] for
H3 and H4 to report our results and to quantify the optimization loop statistically. The models
include participants as a random effect and compare pairwise and listwise UIs between groups. For
non-significant independent two-sample tests, we conduct either a Bayesian independent t-test or
a Bayesian Mann-Whitney test and analyze the Bayes Factor [62, 71] to quantify the likelihood
of hypotheses. The full results are available from reproducible scripts in supplemental materials:
https://github.com/changkun/rankui.

6.2
The Optimization Loop
The overall optimization loop produces three types of data: 1) the optimized system outcomes, 2)
user feedback (either absolute ratings or preferential rankings), and 3) an optimizer-inferred (latent)
utility function. To analyze the optimization loop, we selected a similar approach that analyzes the
stationarity of rating responses [53] and the optimizer predictions [54]: in a successful optimization
loop, the system outcomes should align and come closer to user expectations over iterations based
on user feedback. Otherwise, the system outcomes should go back and forth or further away
from user expectations. As a result, to compare how the optimization loops work differently in
different UIs, we should expect to observe: 1) The objective quality (see Section 5.6.2 for the selected
metrics) of optimized system outcomes progress significantly in at least one objective. 2) User
feedback should give higher utility responses progressively. 3) Optimizer learned latent utility
(see Section 5.3.3) increases progressively.
6.2.1
Objective Quality of System Outcomes. The goal of all Interfaces is to reduce the text length
while preserving the meaning of the text or to enhance the image color towards participants’
preferences depending on the Domain.
In summary, our results of the objective quality validate that all interfaces can optimize objective
metrics with a consistent trend. In more detail, for the text summarization domain, we measured
objective quality using Length, ROUGE (ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L) and BLEU, which
compare the system outcome throughout the optimization loop. We show the progress of the HITL
optimization over time for the measurements in Figure 7a, and all statistical results are consolidated
in Table 4. Regarding summarized text Length, UI6 outperforms UI2 and UI4, and the effect of
Progress is statistically significant. Moreover, UI6 outperforms UI3 with respect to ROUGE-1,
and the effect of Progress is statistically significant for ROUGE-1. For the image enhancement
domain, we computed how five different metrics changed with Progress and looked for consistent
trends. Namely we investigate Brightness, Contrast, Saturation, Temperature, and Tint. We show the
progress of the HITL optimization over time for the measurements in Figure 7b, and all statistical
results are consolidated in Table 4. Here, UI6 outperforms UI3 in terms of Brightness. Moreover, the
effect of Progress is statistically significant for all color metrics except Saturation.

(for preference-based optimizers) that give local preference relations between system outcomes.

7.1
Validating Hypotheses
H1: Baseline Apparatus Verification. Based on our results, the 2-ANFC pairwise interface (UI2)
outperforms the 1-RS pointwise interface (UI1) because it has a significantly lower decision time
per item and fewer iterations. The 2-ANFC also helped participants to express indifference and
their incomplete preference for better system parameter exploration and exploitation. Participants
using 2-ANFC also reported significantly higher satisfaction compared to 1-RS. This result verifies
that our apparatus is valid and aligns with discussions in prior work [7–9].
H2: The Listwise Approach. In terms of UI interactions, our results showed that listwise interfaces
(𝑛-RS/𝑛-ANFR, UI3/UI5) provide the following advantages (compared to 1-RS/2-ANFC, UI1/UI2):
1) they allow users to evaluate more options without increasing decision time and iterations;
2) users express more indifferent preferences and less incomplete preferences; 3) they allow implicit
comparisons among all given options. However, the direct use of the listwise approach (i.e., using
more sliders to rate more items than 1-RS or rank multiple items without distance in 𝑛-ANFR) did
not significantly improve the performance of the optimization loop.

7.2
Interpretation and Implications
While we initially assumed using a listwise approach that increased the number of parallel com-
parisons could improve the overall performance, the results did not support this assumption,
particularly for acquired decision time. Based on the validation of H3, neither 𝑛-RS (UI3, utility-
based) nor 𝑛-ANFR (UI5, preference-based) show a significant advantage and only increased the
overall decision time in the loop compared to 2-ANFC (UI2, preference-based). This might be
because neither 𝑛-RS nor 𝑛-ANFR provided enough support for participants to reduce the overall
noisy observations, and there is room for improvement when using a BO optimizer specifically
designed for listwise UIs. In 𝑛-RS, despite multiple given alternatives, participants might not notice
the relative position between different sliders; in 𝑛-ANFR, the UI did not demonstrate enough
absolute cues to participants on how pure ranking can influence their input, and a pure local
ranking may not give participants information on a global overview.

Instead, the 𝑛-ANFRD (UI6) hybrids collect utility-based and preference-based feedback by
providing rating scales in the regular listwise ranking UI. Similar to other listwise UIs, based on the
validation of H4, although 𝑛-ANFRD increased total decision time, our results show the general
advantage of preference elicitation (expressing more indifference, fewer ranking interactions,
fewer incomplete preferences, and better performance in the optimization loop). This could be
interpreted as the UI starting to offer participants implicit recall support for their past ratings if
their ranking positions are improving. In addition, 𝑛-ANFRD resulted in fewer ranking interactions
and made it clearer for participants to express a ranking decision compared to other purely listwise
ranking interfaces. Furthermore, our results regarding designing UIs to support indifference and
incomplete preferences offer implications for future modeling algorithms. In Bayesian optimization,
indifference preference behavior could be an indicator to inform the acquisition function to avoid
exploiting these areas; incomplete preferences might be another indicator to balance exploration
and exploitation. Depending on the context, one might design the algorithm to a) avoid exploring
a space where people had an incomplete preference to reduce input uncertainty, or alternatively
b) explore it even more for creativity support and then integrate the measured preferences with
more interaction behavior, such as interface features and task completion time [20], to better
support the optimization process.
7.3
Design Trade-Offs
As we validated in our hypotheses, there are many subtle differences between pairwise and listwise
interfaces when permitting users to express indifference and incomplete preferences. This gives us
evidence of how they could impact HITL optimization performance. Below, we discuss potential
trade-offs when using these interfaces.
7.3.1
Guideline 1: Pointwise Approach. The pointwise slider-based interface has proven useful in
many disciplines, such as psychology, subjective scale measures, etc. It is useful to identify social
agreement among populations and measure the direct utility given by a human. The technique for
processing these types of data is also mature. But it is precarious and problematic when directly
applying it to individual sequential measurements. Future HITL optimization systems or any
sequential measurement should generally avoid using this approach.
7.3.2
Guideline 2: Pairwise Approach. A pairwise interface only requires a human to judge based on
the comparison of two given items. Since there is no measured utility and it only provides relative
information about two items, the modeling of the latent utility function could suffer from a change
of criteria and intransitive preference when the anchoring point between two sessions is not chosen
properly. When an iteration receives an incomplete preference, the underlying optimizer could not
receive much useful modeling information. However, when a design objective is to optimize for
fast decisions, the pairwise approach (2-ANFC) is a winner among alternative listwise designs.
7.3.3
Guideline 3: Listwise Approach. Listwise interfaces establish a comparison by increasing the
number of alternatives. Similar to the pairwise interface, a listwise interface without measuring
ranking distance (𝑛-AFR and 𝑛-ANFR in our study) requires a properly sampled anchoring option
between iterations to establish a ranking function. Otherwise, there is no direct association between
iterations. Instead, a listwise interface that allows expressing ranking distance (𝑛-RS and 𝑛-ANFRD
in our study) conveys an implicit association between different iterations regardless of whether the
human provides local or global judgments it is more suited to use in a HITL optimization context.
Furthermore, 𝑛-ANFRD is better than 𝑛-RS because a concentrated participant may not notice the
relative slider position between parallel judging options in 𝑛-RS, but 𝑛-ANFRD explicitly visualizes
the ranking in the UI for users, which helps users more clearly express their opinion in a sequential

task. As design suggestions, when there is a limited measuring budget, e.g., one can only query
humans for a very limited number of opinions, applying listwise interfaces is considered better
than pairwise because it provides more measurement of their opinion and does not decrease the
optimization performance. Furthermore, allowing users to express the ranking distance (𝑛-ANFRD)
is the winner of all listwise variations (𝑛-RS, 𝑛-AFR, and 𝑛-ANFR).
7.3.4
Guideline 4: Measuring Indifference and Incomplete Preferences. When measuring more
accurate user opinions, allowing them to express indifference and incomplete preferences is crucial.
For the pointwise interface, these two different types of preference were implicitly ignored and
treated as being indicated by centering the slider. This interpretation of a central slider position
may be true with proper task wording. Still, it may be easily misused and produce more noisy input
when a user does not know how to express their opinion with the interface. Therefore, in the HITL
optimization scenario, it is better to explicitly design these two functionalities to support users
in expressing their indifference and incomplete preferences. This information could also inform
optimizers to avoid exploring these areas. This is particularly important when the evaluation budget
is very limited in the HITL optimization context.
7.4
Limitations and Future Work
Our study used a text summarization task and an image color enhancement task to explore the
impact of interfaces in HITL optimization. While these domains are common and make it easy to
run a study online at scale, we did not verify our results on more domains. Although we did not
find a large difference between the text and image domains, the same study paradigm could be
evaluated in other domains, such as the 3D domain, where visual decisions are harder to make due
to the complexity of geometry, which may result in different observed behaviors. This might be
interesting for future investigation.
Since our optimization context is highly dependent on humans and modeled human feedback
as objective functions, the system performance highly depends on the quality of human input.
The responses collected online may be influenced by participants’ environments, such as slow
decision time due to external distractions. Moreover, for the preferential settings, depending on
the underlying optimizer (e.g., Brochu et al. [8]), the decision time might be different if the system
repeatedly returns the current optimum.
Our study evaluated opinion measurement interfaces, not optimizers. However, differently
designed listwise interfaces could benefit from dedicated Bayesian optimizers. Without a specialized
BO optimizer, our suggested 𝑛-ANFRD interface supports the user to express a ranking distance
and still outperforms other interfaces in various aspects, such as fewer ranking interactions, more
clearly stated indifference, and rarely expressed incomplete preferences. We also did not look into
a detailed inspection of the cause of indifference and incomplete preference. This leaves a large
potential for 1) future HCI research to study factors, such as involved expertise, and 2) future
BO algorithmic research to model the influence of different optimizers on the same interface, the
interaction effects between interfaces and optimizers, and more realistic modeling of indifference
and incomplete preference.
Lastly, we focused on the design space for aggregated preferences and used Bayesian optimizers
that assumed one latent preference function. However, this could be limited by the human preference
aggregation process, where multiple criteria may not be expressed simultaneously. Future work
can extend the design space to multi-criteria opinion measurement interfaces and further exploit
multi-objective Bayesian optimization methods [4, 16]. Similarly, our discussed taxonomy and
design space only considered structured measures. Hence, we did not explore UIs that utilize
unstructured input, such as text prompts [15] and human-sketched edits [12] of generative models.

Conclusion
In this paper, we revisited the design space of opinion measurement interfaces in the HITL opti-
mization context. Concretely, we aimed to determine the best-suited interface to query human
judgments in sequential evaluation. We found subtle differences between pairwise opinion mea-
surement interfaces and listwise ones: In particular, listwise interfaces helped users express more
indifference preferences over incomplete preferences than the pairwise interface. Moreover, the
hybrid interface that mixes the utility-based and preferential ranking-based feedback to counter-
balance the limitations of each approach could better emphasize the relative relation of options for
users and achieve better HITL optimization performance. As winners, both 𝑛-ANFRD and 2-ANFC
have unique advantages. The design decision between them is a trade-off of decision time, the
precision of measured human feedback, and overall optimization loop performance.
Looking ahead, the exploration of interface designs for HITL optimization does not end here:
Our considered design space only covers structured human inputs bound to suitable optimizers,
i.e., utility and preferential data. With the increasing developments in large language models, it
would also be interesting and challenging to explore, quantify, and evaluate human-in-the-loop
interfaces that allow unstructured inputs such as using a text prompt [15] to alter media data using
diffusion models [61]. Our results here provide a starting point in this direction.

compares to a random exploration (baseline). Higher y-axis utility values mean better exploitation performance.
The upper row shows exploitation performance using a random sampler for the initial samples. The bottom

As described in Section 5.3.3, for utility-based UIs (1-RS/𝑛-RS, UI1/UI3), we implemented a utility-
based BO; for preference-based UIs, we use EUBO [39] that is designed for pairwise comparisons
(2-ANFC, UI2); but also for all listwise interfaces (𝑛-AFC/𝑛-ANFC/𝑛-ANFCD, UI4/UI5/UI6), to not
