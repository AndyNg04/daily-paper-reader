## ABSTRACT 

Real-time interaction requires agents to perceive evolving environments and act under temporal constraints, yet existing evaluations of real-time streaming models focus on conversational quality and streaming perception, leaving effective action under strict temporal constraints untested. We introduce Realtime-Bench, a suite of carefully curated dynamic tasks where delayed actions directly affect success. Under Wall Clock, where environments advance according to actual inference la-tency, the best success rate reaches only 44% and the best Passˆ3 is only 36%, demonstrating that real-time interaction remains challenging. To separate serv-ing latency from models’ real-time streaming capability, we compare Wall Clock with Paused Clock, which removes inference delays as an idealized reference, and introduce Token Clock, which advances simulated time according to generated to-ken counts, testing temporal awareness independently of physical serving speed. We find that temporal costs limit success in evolving environments and that higher reasoning effort has mixed effects under time pressure, reflecting a trade-off be-tween decision quality and timely execution. These findings suggest directions for improving real-time multimodal agents, including adaptive reasoning with tempo-ral awareness and continuous perception and action. 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Many real-world interactive scenarios require decisions to be made under strict temporal constraints. For example, when purchasing a highly demanded ticket, detecting availability is not enough: an agent must observe the changing interface and act before the opportunity disappears. Such sce-narios require real-time streaming capabilities , where a model continuously perceives an evolving environment and produces timely actions in response to newly arriving information. Recently, a growing family of real-time streaming models, such as GPT-Realtime (OpenAI, 2025) and Gemini Live (Google, 2026a), has begun to make this setting practically possible. In contrast to asyn-chronous models that process inputs only after they are fully received, these models can consume streaming inputs and react to new information with low latency. Unlike previous benchmarks that evaluate models in static environments (Zhang et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026) where the environment does not continuously evolve, evaluating streaming models re-quires dynamic settings that test whether models can perceive and act effectively under strict tempo-ral constraints. However, existing evaluations of real-time systems largely focus on capabilities such as streaming perception, conversational quality, and interruption handling (Lin et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026b), with limited coverage of interactive tasks in which actions affect subsequent environment states and delayed execution can change the outcome. We first characterize real-time streaming capability along three complementary dimensions: tem-poral state awareness, adaptive deliberation, and time-aware action selection. These dimensions capture an agent’s ability to track relevant changes and information freshness, adjust deliberation to the available time, and select actions that remain useful when executed. Building on this framework, we introduce Realtime-Bench , comprising 50 interactive game and web tasks in which evolving states and limited opportunities make success depend on both action choice and timing. We evaluate 12 frontier models spanning standard asynchronous models, streaming models and decision models. As shown in Figure 1, real-time interaction remains challenging, with the best success rate reaching only 44.0% and best passˆ3 is below 37% under Wall Clock , which advances the environment according to actual inference and serving time, following prior interactive-agent evaluations (Kang et al., 2025; Froger et al., 2026; ValsAI, 2026; Parekh et al., 2026).

Under each clock, we test low and high reasoning effort to examine the trade-off between decision quality and timely execution. Our analysis reveals that substantial performance gaps relative to Paused Clock persist under Token Clock, highlighting the difficulty of translating task competence into timely action even when temporal costs are standardized independently of serving speed. Higher reasoning effort improves success for all six models under Paused Clock but has mixed effects under temporal pressure. Further experiments show that relaxing the temporal cost of generation generally improves performance but does not ensure task success. These findings motivate future work on adaptive reasoning with temporal awareness and continuous perception and action, enabling agents to coordinate deliberation and execution with the time available. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 STREAMING MULTIMODAL MODELS AND BENCHMARKS 

Video multimodal models have progressively evolved from offline video understanding (Maaz et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b;a; 2023) toward real-time streaming interaction, where models continuously perceive incoming observations and decide when and how to respond as the stream unfolds (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Qian et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2026). Existing training and evaluation formulate real-time interaction as responding to a continuously arriving but sparsely sampled audiovisual stream, commonly at around 1–2 FPS (Chen et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026b). Under this setting, existing benchmarks focus on evaluating the abilities of streaming perception (Lin et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026), response timing (Wang et al., 2025a; He et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026b), interruption handling (Lin et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026b), and conversational quality (He et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026b) in scenarios such as surveillance anomaly detection (Wang et al., 2025a), question answering during gym exercises and shopping, and step-by-step task guidance (Lu et al., 2026b), leaving untested whether models can act effectively in closed-loop environments where the timing of their decisions affects subsequent states and task outcomes. In contrast, Realtime-Bench evaluates closed-loop interaction in web environments and games, where task success depends on both what actions models take and when they take them. 2.2 REAL -T IME EVALUATION IN INTERACTIVE ENVIRONMENTS 

Existing interactive evaluations assess asynchronous vision-language agents in games that pause during model inference, allowing their perception, reasoning, and planning abilities to be evaluated independently of response speed (Tan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Ouyang et al., 2026). To evaluate agents under real-time pressure, VideoGameBench (Zhang et al., 2026), GameWorld-RT (Ouyang et al., 2026), GPTNT (Parekh et al., 2026), and Gaia2 (Froger et al., 2026) allow game or web environments to continue evolving during inference, making task success depend on both decision quality and wall-clock latency. Although such wall-clock capture the consequences of slow responses, they also tie evaluation to deployment-specific inference speed. Similar to Real-Time Reasoning Gym (Wen et al., 2026), we adopt Token Clock, which maps gener-ated tokens to environment time at a shared rate, preserving the temporal cost of deliberation while decoupling evaluation from physical serving speed. 

## 3 SETTING FORMULATION 

3.1 REAL -T IME STREAMING CAPABILITIES 

We define real-time streaming capability as the ability to perceive, reason, and act effectively while the environment continues to evolve. We characterize this capability along three complementary di-mensions. (1) Temporal state awareness (RT-Perceive) requires models to use available observations to track relevant changes, information freshness, and opportunity timing; (2) Adaptive deliberation (RT-Deliberate) requires models to adjust their reasoning length and depth according to the avail-able time and the expected benefit of further computation; (3) Time-aware action selection (RT-Act) 

3Preprint requires models to determine which action will remain useful when executed, including whether to wait or act immediately and what action to take. These dimensions jointly shape how an agent responds to an evolving environment. For example, an agent purchasing a ticket must track the sale status and assess whether observed availability is still current (RT-Perceive) , limit further deliberation when the purchase window is closing (RT-Deliberate) , and choose between waiting for sales to open and purchasing while tickets remain available (RT-Act) .3.2 CLOCK PROTOCOLS 

As shown in Figure 2, we use three clock protocols to distinguish task performance under actual inference latency, performance without inference delays, and performance under a standardized temporal cost of token generation. • Wall Clock. To evaluate performance under real-world inference latency, Wall Clock ad-vances the environment according to the actual time spent on inference. The environment continues to evolve while the model generates a response, so action timing depends on both model computation and serving infrastructure. • Paused Clock. To assess task performance without the temporal cost of inference, Paused Clock pauses the environment while the model generates a response. This provides an idealized reference for Wall Clock, allowing us to compare performance when inference does and does not delay actions in environment time. • Token Clock. To retain the temporal cost of deliberation while removing dependence on serving speed, Token Clock advances the environment at a fixed rate per generation token. An action is applied at the simulated time reached when it is committed, so consuming more tokens delays execution regardless of actual inference latency. To formalize Token Clock, let B denote the number of generation tokens corresponding to one observation interval and f the observation rate in frames per simulated second. The token-to-time rate is κ = Bf , measured in generation tokens per simulated second. If the model generates n

tokens after receiving an observation and before committing to an action, the action is applied after a simulated-time delay of 

∆t = nκ = nBf .

In both Token Clock and Wall Clock, when generation spans multiple observation intervals, inter-mediate frames are skipped, and the model receives the most recent scheduled frame available at the resulting simulated time. This provides a simplified analogue of humans returning attention to the current scene after deliberation, and tests whether the model can respond before relevant events or action opportunities pass. Both protocols thus require the model to balance the benefits of additional deliberation against the cost of delayed action.

## 5 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

5.1 OBSERVATION AND ACTION INTERFACE 

The environment provides observations at a fixed rate of f frames per simulated second. At each scheduled observation frame, the model receives the current screenshot together with the retained interaction history. To prevent GUI grounding from becoming a bottleneck for evaluating real-time decision making, we augment the screenshot with a read-only Set-of-Marks (SoM) representation following common GUI-agent practice (Yang et al., 2023), which identifies currently interactable elements and exposes their associated semantics and supported actions. At each decision step, the model generates executable GUI actions from a common GUI action space (e.g., click , type ,

scroll , and wait ) (Xie et al., 2024). The detailed implementation are provided in Appendix A.1. 5Preprint 5.2 CONTEXT MANAGEMENT 

To minimize harness-induced overhead when evaluating models’ real-time capabilities, we follow Qwen-CUA (Lu et al., 2026a) in removing older history in blocks rather than at every step. This keeps the history prefix unchanged between folds, allowing models to reuse its KV cache across consecutive steps and reducing repeated prefill computation caused by frequent history truncation. All models follow the same history retention policy. Implementation details are provided in Ap-pendix A.3. 5.3 EPISODE EXECUTION

## 6 EXPERIMENTS 

6.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models. We evaluate 12 frontier models spanning (1) standard asynchronous models, includ-ing GPT-6-Astra (OpenAI, 2026b), GPT-5.6 Sol (OpenAI, 2026), Claude Fable 5.1 (Anthropic, 2026), Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026a), Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google, 2026b), Deepseek V4.1 Flash (Deepseek, 2026) and Kimi-K3 (Team et al., 2026); (2) real-time streaming models, which are optimized for low-latency interaction, including GPT-Realtime-2 (OpenAI, 2025), Gemini 3.1 Flash live (Gemini, 2026) and Gemini 3.8 Live (Google, 2026); and (3) decision models, which directly re-turn structured decisions rather than generate free-form text, including OpenAI Decisions (OpenAI, 2026a) and Jev 1.13 (TypeSafe, 2026). 

Evaluation settings. To evaluate model performance in real-world deployment settings, we first evaluate all 12 models under Wall Clock. We report the pass rate for each run, defined as the fraction of tasks successfully completed, along with passˆk and pass@k for k ∈ { 1, 2, 3}. Following τ -bench Yao et al. (2025), passˆk measures the probability of succeeding in all k attempts at a task, whereas pass@k measures the probability of succeeding in at least one of k attempts. To further distinguish task performance under actual inference latency, without inference delays, and under a standardized temporal cost of token generation, we further compare Wall Clock, Paused Clock, and Token Clock on six representative models: GPT-Realtime-2, Gemini 3.1 Flash live, GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 5, Gemini 3.8 Flash, and Kimi-K3. We exclude decision models from this analysis because they do not report output token counts, which are required for Token Clock evaluation. For each clock protocol, we test both low and high reasoning effort to examine the tradeoff between decision quality and timeliness. Crossing the three clock protocols with the two reasoning levels yields six settings: WL and WH for Wall Clock, PL and PH for Paused Clock, and TL and TH for Token Clock, where L and H denote low and high reasoning effort, respectively. 

Temporal configuration. We set the observation rate to f = 1 FPS, following the frame-sampling recipes primarily used in current training and evaluation of real-time models (Xia et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Team, 2026). Relaxing temporal pressure helps but does not ensure success. To examine the role of this temporal cost, we hold reasoning effort at low and vary B ∈ { 32 , 64 , 128 , unlimited } at a fixed observation rate (Figure 6). ## 7 DISCUSSION 

7.1 ADAPTIVE REASONING WITH TEMPORAL AWARENESS 

Our results highlight a trade-off between the potential benefits of further reasoning and the delay it introduces. In an evolving environment, additional deliberation may improve a decision while leaving insufficient time to execute it effectively. Adaptive reasoning should therefore account for the time available to act, alongside task difficulty (Anthropic, 2026b; Wu et al., 2026). In Tetris, for example, a model may spend more time comparing placements while a falling piece is far above the stack. As the piece approaches the stack, however, it must shorten deliberation to leave enough time to execute the required moves. The appropriate reasoning effort thus changes with the environment, even within the same task. Future work should train models to recognize these changing temporal constraints and allocate deliberation by weighing the expected benefit of further computation against the risk of acting too late. 8Preprint 7.2 CONTINUOUS PERCEPTION AND ACTION 

Adapting deliberation also depends on receiving timely observations and updating actions as the environment changes. At the 1 FPS observation rate used in our experiments, brief events or rapid changes may occur between observations, even when inference itself incurs no temporal cost. For example, an enemy may change direction between two frames, preventing the agent from adjusting its action until the next observation. Higher observation rates could provide more timely feedback, but realizing this benefit requires models and harnesses that can process incoming information and revise actions with sufficiently low latency. 

(1) Model design. Higher temporal resolution presents two coupled challenges: processing more frames increases inference cost, while compressing visual inputs risks discarding brief changes that determine the next action. Addressing these challenges requires optimizing both (1) processing ef-ficiency, for example by replacing cascaded architectures with an end-to-end single Transformer using block-causal attention (Huang et al., 2026), and (2) the preservation of action-relevant infor-mation through compact temporal representations (Ding et al., 2025) or codec-aware visual encod-ing (Sarkar et al., 2026). Future work should explore these two directions to support higher frame rates with lower latency while preserving important information. 

(2) Interaction harnesses. Real-time harnesses must manage context and memory while coordinat-ing observations and actions in a continuously changing environment. ## 8 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Realtime-Bench, a suite of diverse dynamic game and web tasks for evaluating real-time interactive capability in multimodal agents. Our evaluation of 12 frontier models shows that real-time interaction remains challenging: under Wall Clock, the best success rate reaches only 44.0%, and the best Passˆ3 is only 36%. To disentangle the effects of serving latency and models’ real-time streaming capability, we compared three clock protocols, introducing Token Clock to pre-serve the temporal cost of deliberation independently of physical serving speed. Substantial gaps relative to Paused Clock persist under Token Clock, highlighting the difficulty of translating task competence into timely action. Across the six models analyzed under different reasoning efforts, higher effort improves success under Paused Clock but has mixed effects under temporal pressure. Relaxing the temporal cost of generation generally improves performance but does not ensure suc-cess. These findings motivate adaptive reasoning with temporal awareness and continuous percep-tion and action, allowing agents to coordinate deliberation and execution with the time available. Realtime-Bench provides a controlled setting for studying these capabilities and measuring progress toward effective real-time interaction. 10 Preprint

## A EVALUATION IMPLEMENTATION 

A.1 OBSERVATIONS AND ACTIONS 

Observations. The agent receives a screenshot and a read-only description of currently inter-actable elements. Screenshots preserve the desktop aspect ratio and are resized to a maximum dimension of 768 pixels, yielding 768 × 432 images for the 1920 × 1080 desktop. We adopt this reduced resolution to reduce visual processing overhead and inference latency. Recent studies also suggest that SoM representations preserve visual grounding performance under downsampling with minimal loss (Xie et al., 2024; Kaesberg et al., 2026).The Set-of-Marks representation uses P# for discrete target points and R# for continuous regions. A region location is specified as R#@u,v ,where u, v ∈ [0 , 1000] are local coordinates. Marks refer only to the observation in which they appear. Raw screenshot coordinates remain available when no suitable mark exists and are mapped to desktop coordinates before execution. 

Actions. Each decision produces a GUI action. Table 1 gives the shared action schema.                           

> Action Arguments Semantics
> click target, button Click the specified point.
> right click target Right-click the specified point.
> double click ,triple click target Apply the corresponding click sequence.
> mouse down ,mouse up target, duration Press or release the mouse button.
> move target, duration Move the pointer.
> type text Enter the supplied text.
> key key, repeat Tap a key or key combination.
> hold key key, duration Hold a key or key combination for the specified time.
> scroll target, dx, dy Scroll horizontally or vertically.
> drag start, end, duration Drag between two points.
> wait duration Advance without issuing another GUI input.

Table 1: Shared GUI action schema. Arguments include optional fields; action durations lie in [0 , 10] 

seconds and key.repeat lies in [1 , 100] .A.2 CLOCK EXECUTION AND TOKEN ACCOUNTING 

Action timing. For a decision based on an observation at time ti, the token clock assigns an action timestamp tact  

> i

= ti + ni/(Bf ), where ni is the charged output-token count. The harness advances the environment to this timestamp before applying the action. Finishing early does not provide an extra observation: the next observation follows the scheduled frame grid. If generation crosses observation boundaries, the corresponding intermediate observations are skipped. Physical inference and GUI execution latency do not advance simulated time. Browser animation updates use a persistent 60 Hz simulation grid, separate from the agent’s observation rate, so splitting an interval around an action does not change the number of animation updates. 

Charged tokens. For API-based models, we charge both reasoning tokens and output tokens us-ing the usage reported by the API for the complete response. As Gemini Live natively produces audio rather than text, we configure it to emit GUI actions through native function calls instead of spoken responses. We charge its reported thought tokens and non-audio response tokens, excluding generated audio. A.3 CONTEXT MANAGEMENT 

Visual and textual history are retained separately. When the active visual history reaches 21 obser-vations, the oldest 10 screenshots and their associated interactables are replaced with fixed place-holders, while the corresponding text and action records remain. When detailed interaction history 16 Preprint exceeds 50 turns, the oldest 10 complete turns are replaced by a compact executed-action ledger. This block policy retains recent evidence while limiting changes to reusable context prefixes. Streaming models maintain the retained history within their sessions, while asynchronous models resubmit the retained context. To maximize cache reuse and reduce inference latency, we preserve the order and content of retained messages and remove older history only in blocks when the reten-tion limits are exceeded. This keeps the shared context prefix stable across consecutive decisions, allowing models to reuse cached computation where supported. Both mechanisms implement the same logical history policy. A.4 MODEL CONFIGURATION 

Standard Asynchronous Models and Real-time Streaming Models. Our standard asynchronous models include GPT-6-Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5, Claude Fable 5.1, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and Kimi-K3. We access GPT-6-Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Fable 5.1, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash through OpenRouter, Claude Opus 5 through the An-thropic API, and Kimi-K3 through the Kimi API. Our real-time streaming models include GPT-Realtime-2, accessed through OpenAI, and Gemini 3.1 Flash Live and Gemini 3.8 Live, accessed through the Google Gemini Live API. Where configurable, we control reasoning effort through provider-exposed API fields, selecting the corresponding low and high settings for our experimental conditions. 

Decision models. We access Jev 1.13 and OpenAI Decisions through OpenRouter API. For these models, the harness constructs candidate GUI actions from the current observation and submits them as a choice question. Text entry can be composed through successive choices before executing a single typing action. Jev 1.13 does not natively support screenshot inputs. We therefore represent its Web observations as structured text using the browser accessibility tree (AX tree), interactable elements, and appearance descriptors. This representation does not adequately capture the visual state of our game tasks, so we evaluate Jev 1.13 only on Web tasks. OpenAI Decisions additionally receives screenshots. ## C QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS 

C.1 SCOPE AND PROCEDURE .By inspecting failed trajectories, the first author identified three failure modes and developed an annotation codebook specifying operational definitions, inclusion criteria, and exclusion rules. For lack of temporal awareness (RT-Deliberate), we annotated failures under TL and TH to examine whether models accounted for the temporal cost of deliberation under standardized token-based timing; Paused Clock removes this cost, whereas Wall Clock additionally reflects deployment-dependent latency. For failure to track the evolving state (RT-Perceive) and misjudging whether action is needed (RT-Act), we annotated failures under PH, which pauses the environment during generation and uses high reasoning effort, allowing us to examine state tracking and action necessity judgments without environment changes during inference. We then prompted Codex to apply the codebook to the recorded action trajectories, timing information, available model reasoning, and observations. All failed trajectories in the selected conditions were screened, with targeted inspec-tion of video frames to verify candidate events rather than exhaustive frame-by-frame review. The second author checked the resulting annotations against the source evidence. C.2 ANNOTATION CODEBOOK .

Prompt 1: Failure-mode annotation codebook 

ANNOTATION SCOPE 

Annotate valid failed episodes from the selected runs. Assess RT-Deliberate only in TL and TH, where charged output tokens advance simulated task time. Assess RT-Perceive and RT-Act only in PH. An episode–category pair outside these settings is outside the selected annotation scope, not a negative judgment. 

FAILURE MODES 

1. Lack of temporal awareness (RT-Deliberate). Generation latency is mismatched to the avail-able response window, consuming time needed to observe, act, or complete a required se-quence. Qualifying evidence includes generation that exhausts a model-visible or task-stated deadline, leaves insufficient time for necessary subsequent steps, or delays a response to a vis-ibly evolving hazard. Cumulative generation costs may qualify when they contribute before the opportunity becomes irrecoverable. A timeout, long generation, or final rejected action alone is insufficient. Do not infer a usable response window solely from hidden backend timing when the visible deadline has already expired. Short generation does not qualify merely because more time remained. Distinguish charged output tokens from reasoning tokens. Generation-related latency does not apply in PH; explicit waiting there is assessed under RT-Act. 2. Failure to track the evolving state (RT-Perceive). The model fails to incorporate task-relevant current information and subsequently behaves according to a stale or incorrect state. Examples include overlooking an updated request, reverting to superseded preferences, misidentifying the currently active game object, and incorrectly assuming that a workflow has completed. Both external changes and action-induced interface changes qualify. 

20 Preprint 

Prompt 1: Failure-mode annotation codebook (continued) 

Require evidence of a discrepancy between the available current state and the model’s repre-sentation or behavior. Generic coordinate errors, repeated actions on an unchanged screen, and incorrect predictions of future states do not qualify by themselves. 3. Misjudging whether action is needed (RT-Act). The model executes an action when it should wait, or explicitly waits when intervention is required. Examples include committing before a prerequisite is satisfied, waiting through an actionable opportunity, and unnecessarily repeating a completed action in a way that reverses progress. Require evidence about the current readiness state, the executed action or wait, and its con-sequence. Rejected actions, parser-induced fallback waits, and reasonable waits for pending processing do not establish intentional action-timing errors. 

EVIDENCE REQUIREMENTS 

For each candidate event, identify the relevant observation and timestamp, proposed and executed be-havior, contribution to failure, and plausible alternative explanations. Preserve source references to the trajectory, execution feedback, and supporting frames. Task instructions establish objectives and known constraints; observations establish information avail-able to the model; execution records establish which actions were applied. Final outcomes may cor-roborate consequences, but hidden evaluation state alone cannot establish that the model ignored an observable change. Assign each eligible episode–category judgment an evidence status: • Confirmed : direct evidence satisfies the operational criterion and supports a relevant contribu-tion to failure. • Probable : affirmative evidence supports the category, but an alternative explanation remains or the response window is inferred. • Insufficient : available evidence does not establish the category; this does not establish its ab-sence. Record out-of-scope episode–category pairs, successful episodes, and invalid runs separately. These labels characterize observable behavior, not verified internal cognitive mechanisms. 

AGGREGATION AND INTERPRETATION 

The main figure counts confirmed and probable judgments. Retain confirmed-only results as a sensitivity analysis. Count each episode at most once per category. For each model, divide RT-Deliberate assignments by its valid failed episodes pooled across TL and TH. Divide RT-Perceive and RT-Act assignments by its valid failed PH episodes. Pool episode counts rather than averaging setting-level percentages. 

Other denotes valid failed episodes without any selected-category assignment at the reported evidence threshold. Divide this count by the model’s total valid failed episodes across TL, TH, and PH. It in-cludes residual errors and unresolved cases; invalid actions and misunderstanding game mechanics are illustrative examples, not separately quantified subcategories. Categories may overlap in PH, and the plotted denominators differ. Percentages therefore need not sum to 100%; Other is not computed by subtracting the three category percentages from 100%. Assign labels according to evidence without imposing target frequencies or coverage rates. Interpret the distributions as evidence-supported attributions under the selected settings, rather than an exhaustive causal decomposition of failures.

## D PROMPTS 

D.1 PROMPT TEMPLATES .Prompt 2 specifies the GUI actions, how to use the SoM marks or screenshot coordinates to select an action’s location, and the required output format. For each episode, we combine these shared instructions with three components. The domain instruction identifies the environment as a game or a web task and specifies how the agent should interact with it, defined in Appendix D.1.1. The clock instruction explains the clock protocols, specified in Appendix D.1.2. The task instruction states the specific objective and requirements for the current task, corresponding to the success criteria. D.1.1 DOMAIN INSTRUCTIONS .The domain instruction specifies the interaction setting and behavioral constraints. Game tasks use Prompt 3, while web tasks use Prompt 4. D.1.2 CLOCK INSTRUCTIONS .The clock instruction specifies how observations, model outputs, and action execution relate to en-vironment time: • Token clock (Prompt 5): charged output tokens determine the simulated delay between observation and action. • Paused environment (Prompt 6): output length does not advance simulated time; each action is applied at the observed tick before the environment advances one frame interval. • Wall clock (Prompt 7): environment time follows physical elapsed time.

Prompt 5: Token-clock instruction 

TOKEN CLOCK: observations are scheduled at f=1 frames per simulated second. Physical inference and GUI execution latency do not advance simulated time. Produce at most one action per observation. B=64 charged tokens/frame; kappa=B*f=64 charged tokens per simulated second. After n provider/native output tokens, an action lands n/kappa simulated seconds after the observation. B is a conversion rate, not a maximum response length. A decision may use more than B tokens, cross scheduled frame boundaries, and miss the corresponding observations. Finishing early does not provide an observation before the next scheduled frame boundary. The task ends at its fixed simulated-time limit T, with at most kappa*T charged tokens over the episode. Executed actions and their outcomes are retained in your context. 

Prompt 6: Paused-environment instruction 

TOKEN CLOCK: observations are scheduled at f=1 frames per simulated second. Physical inference and GUI execution latency do not advance simulated time. Produce at most one action per observation. B is unlimited: output length does not affect simulated time, so each action lands at the tick it observed. The environment then advances exactly one frame interval (1/f seconds), and the next observation includes the action’s effect. 

Prompt 7: Wall-clock instruction 

WALL CLOCK: observations are scheduled at 1 fps. Environment time follows physical elapsed time. Produce at most one action per observation and use execution feedback to track which actions were applied.
