# What Makes a Social Agent’s Interactions Effective? Learning from Verifiable Outcomes in Paired Worlds 

Caixin Kang 1,2 , Ke Wang 1, Yuchuan Wu 1, Xiaoqiang Wang 1,Ling Li 1, Bingxue Qiu 1, Yongbin Li 1∗, Yoichi Sato 2

## Abstract 

Effective social interaction requires more than fluent responses: agents must reach plans that fit the participants’ needs and circumstances. Existing evalu-ations of social agents mainly rely on LLM judges that rate the conversation, without checking the resulting plan. We introduce HICO-Bench, a benchmark of 180 everyday coordination scenarios, such as resource allocation, schedul-ing, and authorization, in which two agents with private information converse and then independently record the plan they agreed on. A program checks 

Feasibility (the two records match and satisfy both participants’ private con-ditions), and an LLM judge checks Integrity (the conversation supports the recorded plan); passing both is a Verified Success . Each scenario has two ver-sions that share the same public context but differ in one private condition, so the correct plan differs between them. Across thirteen LLMs, 36% of conver-sations rated highly for goal completion fail Verified Success. To learn from outcomes that arrive only at the end of a conversation, we propose Paired-Credit Policy Optimization (PaCPO). After each turn, PaCPO asks the agent for a tentative plan, checks it in both versions of the scenario, and rewards the turns after which the tentative plans fit their respective versions. PaCPO raises Qwen3-8B’s Verified Success from 20.4% to 28.3%. 

# 1 Introduction 

LLM agents increasingly help people coordinate everyday matters. Consider Maya asking her colleague Leo, “Could you give me a hand with tomorrow’s client pitch?” (Figure 1). Whether Leo should review her slides or present in her place depends on facts that only Maya knows: whether she can attend, and what help she wants. A reply such as “Don’t worry, I’ll take over” sounds supportive, yet commits to a plan that fails if Maya can present herself. Splitting a bill, scheduling a meeting, and approving a request share this structure: an acceptable outcome depends on private conditions , such as budget, availability, or authority, revealed through dialogue. Existing evaluations of LLM social agents focus primarily on the conversation. SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024) uses an LLM judge to rate agents on goal completion, believability, and related dimensions, and subsequent work trains agents to improve these ratings (Wang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Such ratings do not verify whether the agreed plan satisfies the participants’ conditions, and model-based judges can overestimate agents’ social abilities (Wang et al., 2024). Negotiation and tool-use benchmarks do verify outcomes automatically (Lewis et al., 2017; Yao et al., 2024), but not in everyday coordination, where the decisive facts must be elicited through conversation.

We introduce HICO-Bench (Hidden Information and Checkable Outcomes), a benchmark that evaluates social interaction by its outcome. After each conversation, both agents independently record the agreed plan in a structured form. A program checks Feasibility : whether the two records match and satisfy all private conditions. An LLM judge checks Integrity : whether the conversation supports the recorded plan, for example without invented authority or a record that contradicts what was said. An interaction is a Verified Success only if it passes both checks. Each scenario has two versions, or worlds , that differ in a single private condition (e.g., whether Maya can attend) and admit disjoint sets of correct plans; an agent that responds identically in both worlds therefore fails at least one. Across thirteen LLMs, 36% of conversations with Goal ≥ 8/10 fail Verified Success. Verified outcomes can also serve as a training signal, but they arrive only after the final turn, leaving the contribution of individual turns unclear. We propose Paired-Credit Policy Optimization (PaCPO), which uses the paired worlds for credit assignment. After each turn, we probe the agent, outside the conver-sation, for a tentative plan it would record at that point, and check this plan in both worlds. Before Leo learns whether Maya can attend, his tentative plan is the same in both worlds and can fit at most one; once she answers, the plan formed in each world should fit that world but not its counterpart. PaCPO rewards the turns at which this occurs, redistributing the terminal reward without changing each conver-sation’s total return. On Qwen3-8B, PaCPO improves Verified Success from 20.4% to 28.3%, compared with 25.0% for PPO and 26.3% for GRPO trained on the same number of dialogues. Our contributions are as follows: • HICO-Bench , a benchmark of 180 paired scenarios across six coordination families, evaluated by rule-based Feasibility and judge-based Integrity alongside SOTOPIA ratings. • PaCPO , a credit-assignment method that compares tentative plans across paired worlds once the private condition that differs between them has been disclosed. • An evaluation of thirteen LLMs and five RL baselines , showing that highly rated conversations can yield failed plans and that outcome training improves Verified Success. 

# 2 Related Work 

Agent reasoning and interaction. Within broader research on agent reasoning and interaction (Yao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025a; Wang & Liu, 2025; Wang et al., 2025), Social IQa and FANToM test social 2commonsense and who knows what in a conversation (Sap et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2023). SOTOPIA evaluates agents in role-played social scenarios, with an LLM judge rating goal completion, believability, and other dimensions (Zhou et al., 2024); SOTOPIA-ToM extends this setting to information seeking and privacy (YS et al., 2026). Multimodal work also studies truthfulness and deception (Kang et al., 2025b;a), personality judgments (Kang et al., 2026), and speaker alignment (Ouyang et al., 2026a), alongside in-teractive digital humans (Cai et al., 2025) and controllable multi-person video generation (Ouyang et al., 2026b). HICO-Bench focuses on the plan reached through dialogue: whether it fits both participants’ private conditions and is supported by what they said. We retain SOTOPIA’s ratings as complementary measures of conversation quality. 

Task-oriented dialogue and negotiation. Several interactive benchmarks verify outcomes with rules. In Deal or No Deal and CaSiNo, agents divide items and the final split is scored against each agent’s private values (Lewis et al., 2017; Chawla et al., 2021); τ-bench and τ2-bench check whether a customer-service agent leaves a database in the correct state (Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025); Social Gym uses games whose winner is decided by rules (He et al., 2026). These settings target fixed, domain-specific goals. HICO-Bench extends outcome verification to everyday coordination and additionally checks that the conversation supports the plan. 

Clarification and information seeking. Prior work trains or evaluates models on asking clarifying ques-tions, for example by rewarding questions that reveal user preferences (Andukuri et al., 2024) or by scor-ing questions at checkpoints (Zhao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024). Related work on proactive assistance studies when to offer help from ongoing visual observations (Gong et al., 2026b;a). We do not reward questions directly: in HICO-Bench, the decisive fact may be volunteered, already known, or elicited, and only the correctness of the resulting plan is rewarded. 

Credit assignment in reinforcement learning. When feedback arrives only at the end of an episode, a learner must estimate which earlier steps deserve credit. Process supervision labels each reasoning step (Lightman et al., 2024); VinePPO estimates a step’s value by sampling continuations from it (Kazemne-jad et al., 2025); GiGPO and GraphGPO compare steps that share the same state across rollouts (Feng et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026). For social agents, SOTOPIA-RL and SAVOIR distribute LLM-judge ratings over the utterances of a conversation (Yu et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026). In free-form dialogue, however, identical states rarely recur across rollouts, and LLM ratings are noisy. PaCPO instead compares tenta-tive plans across the two worlds of a scenario and checks them with rules, so that each comparison is verifiable. 

# 3 HICO-Bench 

3.1 Interaction Protocol 

Each scenario involves two roles, such as Maya and Leo in Figure 1. Both roles see the same public context 

p: the client pitch is tomorrow at 10 a.m., Maya is due to present, and Leo may present only if Maya cannot attend. Each role i also receives a private role card vi that the other role does not see; Maya’s card states whether she can attend and what help she wants. The roles then alternate free-form turns, producing a history h. Each role observes only p, its own card, and h; it never sees the partner’s card or the set of acceptable plans. When the conversation ends, each role independently fills in a structured record ci describing the plan it believes was agreed, following a public template C (e.g., who presents and what Leo will do). Inde-pendent records test whether the roles reached a shared understanding (Clark & Brennan, 1991). The 

verifier state z stores the private conditions of both roles. A scenario is thus x = ( p, v1, v2, z, C).3Table 1: Interaction families in HICO-Bench. Each family has 16 training, 4 development, and 10 test scenarios, each with two worlds.

3.2 Paired Scenarios 

Each scenario has two worlds, w0 and w1, that share the public context and differ in exactly one private condition, chosen so that no plan is correct in both. In our example, Maya can attend in w0, so the correct plan is that she presents and Leo reviews her slide; in w1 she cannot attend, so the correct plan is that Leo presents. Following contrast sets (Ribeiro et al., 2020; Gardner et al., 2020), an agent that makes the same offer in both worlds must fail one of them. Table 1 lists the six coordination families. We adapt social situations from SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024), write role cards for both worlds, and encode the acceptance rules as a program, which is tested on correct and incorrect plans. The 180 scenarios are split into 96 training, 24 development, and 60 test scenarios, with both worlds in the same split. Evaluating each role in each world separately yields 60 × 2 × 2 = 240 

test tasks (96 development tasks). 

3.3 Outcome Evaluation 

Feasibility: does the plan work? Each record specifies an outcome kind and its terms . The kind is 

agreement (a plan is settled), decline (no plan satisfies both roles), or defer (the request requires approval from someone outside the conversation). In w0 of our example, a correct record is {kind: agreement, terms: {presenter: Maya, leo_task: review_budget_slide}} . A program checks that (1) the two records are identical up to the ordering of fields and items; (2) the kind is correct for the world; and (3) the terms satisfy the family’s rules (Table 1), e.g., in resource allocation the shares sum to the $240 bill and respect each person’s limit. Any plan satisfying the rules is accepted; in scheduling, for instance, any mutually free slot passes. Fz(c1, c2) = 1 if all three tests pass and 0 otherwise. 

Integrity: does the conversation support the plan? A feasible record may still be unsupported by the conversation: both records may state that Maya presents while Leo promised to take over, or Leo may approve extra work by claiming authority he lacks. An LLM judge reads the full conversation and both records and flags four violations, each with quoted evidence: (i) an uncorrected factual error, such as invented authority or resources; (ii) repeated exchanges without progress; (iii) a record that contradicts the spoken agreement; and (iv) output-format text leaking into the dialogue. Iz(h, c1, c2) = 1 if no violation is found. Corrected proposals and truthful referrals are allowed.

# 4 Paired-Credit Policy Optimization 

PaCPO trains the agent with a terminal reward but redistributes it across turns. After each turn, we ask which plan the agent would record if the conversation ended there, and compare these tentative plans across the two worlds of a scenario. Before Maya reveals whether she can attend, Leo’s tentative plan is the same in both worlds and can fit at most one; once she does, the plans formed in the two worlds should diverge so that each fits its own world. PaCPO rewards the turns at which this divergence appears. Figure 2 illustrates the pipeline; Algorithm 1 (Appendix E) details the update.

All RL methods in our experiments optimize the same terminal reward 

R(h, c1, c2, z) = 0.8 Fz(c1, c2) + 0.2 Iz(h, c1, c2), (2) which gives partial credit when only one check passes. PPO and GRPO receive R at the final turn; PaCPO redistributes it across turns.

5.3 Learning from Verified Outcomes 

Table 3 compares RL methods trained with the same terminal reward and number of dialogues. PaCPO achieves the highest Verified Success, improving Base by 7.92 points to 28.33%. PaCPO and GRPO reach the same Feasibility, but PaCPO has higher Integrity, so its gain comes from conversations that better support feasible plans. Although SOTOPIA ratings are not optimized, PaCPO also improves Goal and Believability over Base (Appendix C). In a blinded pilot with two expert annotators, human judgments agree with Feasibility on 100% of cases and with Qwen3.8-Max Integrity on 82.22%; the annotators also prefer PaCPO over Base for helpfulness more often than the reverse (8 vs. 5 of 24 pairs; Appendix K.2). 

Where outcome learning helps. Gains vary by family (Figure 3). The largest improvements over Base are in bounded quantities (+22.50 points) and authorization (+10.00 points). In bounded quantities, PPO and PaCPO reach the same Feasibility, so PaCPO’s additional gain again comes from Integrity. Re-source allocation stays at 17.5% Verified Success for Base, PPO, and PaCPO, and both scheduling families remain difficult (Appendix J). 

What changes in the conversation? Figure 5a breaks down the aggregate gains into changes across individual scenarios. Improvements and regressions occur within the same family, including the difficult scheduling settings. Figure 5b shows one such improvement. Finley and Harper disclose nonoverlapping windows for using a shared table. Base incorrectly calls them overlapping, and its partner accepts that error. PaCPO instead identifies the four-minute gap and obtains agreement. Both runs ultimately submit the same feasible records, but only PaCPO’s conversation supports them. 5.4 Self-Play and Interactive Transfer 

We also evaluate self-play, transfer to SOTOPIA, and training on other interaction tasks. 

Self-play. Self-play replaces the frozen partner with another copy of the evaluated checkpoint, testing robustness when both participants change. Across three generation repeats, PaCPO reaches 28.33% mean Verified Success, exceeding Base by 6.39 points and PPO by 1.11 points (Figure 4, left). Gains persist in this setting, with a smaller margin over PPO than against the fixed partner. Full results appear in Appendix H. 

SOTOPIA transfer. SOTOPIA (Zhou et al., 2024) tests direct transfer. Using iStar’s partner settings and GPT-4o scoring (Liu et al., 2026), we evaluate HICO checkpoints on 70 environment–character pairs without further training. PaCPO leads in both partner settings (Figure 4, right), connecting outcome training to social performance beyond HICO’s checks (Appendix H). 

Training on other interaction tasks. MultiWOZ (Budzianowski et al., 2018) constraint communication and CaSiNo (Chawla et al., 2021) negotiation test task-specific training (Table 4; Appendix I). PaCPO gives a small communication gain on MultiWOZ and leads agreement and normalized Nash surplus on CaSiNo. Gains are larger in bargaining than in constraint communication. 95.5 Credit Ablations 

Table 5 isolates three design choices. Replacing the other world’s plan with a second plan from the same world tests whether the paired worlds matter, and shuffling credit across turns tests whether its timing matters. Both reduce Verified Success to 26.25%, 2.08 points below full PaCPO (Appendix E). Opening the gate before the differing condition is disclosed reduces Verified Success to 25.83%, the largest drop, consistent with assigning credit only after disclosure. During training, paired credit is nonzero at 55.6% of gate-open turns (Appendix J); otherwise, the two worlds’ plans are equally feasible, and only the within-world and terminal terms apply. 

# 6 Conclusion 

Effective social interaction should be judged not only by the conversation but also by the plan it produces. HICO-Bench evaluates this with paired scenarios and two checks, Feasibility and Integrity, whose con-junction defines Verified Success. Across thirteen LLMs, high conversation ratings often accompany plans that fail these checks. PaCPO uses the paired worlds to turn end-of-conversation outcomes into turn-level credit, improving Verified Success over the base model and RL baselines. These results sug-gest that verified outcomes complement conversation ratings, both for evaluation and as a training signal. The benchmark currently covers six coordination families with structured outcomes, and Integrity relies on an LLM judge; extending verification to less structured interactions and expanding human validation are important next steps (Appendices L and K).

HICO-Bench groups scenarios by the coordination requirement that determines which arrangements are feasible. These requirements apply across settings such as work, family, and commerce. Table 1 gives examples and verification rules; Figure 6 shows the balanced distribution. Each family has 30 scenarios: 16 training, 4 development, and 10 test, with two worlds per scenario. Training procedure. Both worlds of a scenario are sampled under the same behavior-policy version. The two trajectories are not forced to have identical language or identical length. After each turn of the tested role, PaCPO generates one tentative plan from the visible prefix. Tentative plans at matching turn indices are checked in both worlds. When one conversation lacks a corresponding turn or a valid tentative plan, the paired credit is zero, while within-world feasibility remains available. Algorithm 1 gives the complete rollout update. The GiGPO and GraphGPO rows in Table 3 use their released advantage-computation routines within the HICO dialogue trainer (Feng et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026). We load the numerical function bodies unchanged from a pinned verl-agent checkout; the environment interface, observation representation, rollout schedule, and policy-update loop use our HICO implementation. 1

Rollout groups and task feedback. Each group contains G = 4 independent dialogues for the same scenario, world, and tested role, against the frozen Qwen3-8B partner. Each turn of the tested role, in-cluding the final record, is one step. The terminal reward R is assigned to the final step; earlier rewards are zero. The collection budget is 384 dialogues: 48 scenarios × two worlds × four samples, with bal-anced interaction families and preassigned roles. Both methods are critic-free and generate no tentative plans. 

Anchor-state representation. An anchor key hashes the exact actor-input token sequence together with a flag distinguishing dialogue turns from final records. It therefore contains the actor’s role card, public instructions, and full visible history. Matching is restricted to the same scenario, world, and role. We disable approximate string matching and semantic state merging; partner-private fields and verifier state are not added to the key. This keeps decision-relevant history intact while making shared anchors depend on identical visible prefixes. 

GiGPO advantages. For a dialogue ending at step T, GiGPO computes Ut = ∑Tu=t 0.95 u−tru, then compares these discounted returns among occurrences of the same anchor. The resulting step advantage is added to an episode-level advantage computed from R, with both coefficients equal to one. We use the released mean_std_norm mode: subtract the group mean and divide by the sample standard deviation plus 10 −6. Episode normalization retains the upstream cross-step default, repeating each dialogue’s R

at its steps when computing the statistics. Step normalization instead uses the corresponding anchor group; a singleton anchor has zero centered step advantage. 

GraphGPO advantages. GraphGPO builds a directed graph separately for each rollout group. Nodes are the same exact-history anchors, and unit-weight edges connect successive actor steps. A dialogue with R = 1 connects to a shared success node; other terminal nodes remain trajectory-specific. The released routine computes shortest-path distances to success. With distance normalization disabled, the step score is 10 (0.10 )D(st+1), where D is the successor’s distance to success. Unreachable nodes receive the upstream fallback distance, one greater than the largest finite distance. These scores are normalized within anchor groups and added to the same episode-level advantage, again with unit coefficients. This path score determines credit; it does not replace the terminal reward. 

Policy update and scope. Each computed step advantage is broadcast to the tokens of that turn. Both adapters use the clipped actor loss and reference penalty above, with clipping 0.2, reference coefficient 0.02, actor learning rate 2 × 10 −6, and two optimization epochs. Tokens are averaged within dialogues before dialogues are averaged within four-dialogue optimizer minibatches. No further rollout-wide ad-vantage normalization is applied, and partner tokens remain context only. Exact-history grouping supports local comparisons only at repeated anchors. Once dialogue histories diverge, unmatched anchors retain the episode-level component without a local step comparison. The reported results characterize this exact-history HICO adaptation; the grouping and state representation are part of the comparison, alongside the advantage estimator.

# I External-Task Training 

We evaluate task-specific training in two settings: database-grounded recommendation and bilateral negotiation. Table 4 reports their results. 

MultiWOZ: eliciting constraints for a recommendation. We select hotel and restaurant goals from MultiWOZ 2.2 dialogue-state annotations (Zang et al., 2020) and use the corresponding MultiWOZ 2.1 entity databases (Eric et al., 2020). Each source contributes one domain-specific recommendation task with explicit user constraints, such as area, price range, cuisine, or hotel amenities. We select goals with a database match and omit booking requests and requests for a preselected named entity. Train, de-velopment, and test retain their original source splits; repeated constraint profiles are removed across splits. Each pair retains the public request and database while changing one private user constraint. Both worlds have valid recommendations, and their valid entity sets are disjoint. The visitor holds the re-quirements privately; the assistant asks questions, searches the database, and recommends one entity by name. A successful episode requires that the selected entity satisfy every private constraint and be unambiguously identified in the final message. This constraint communication endpoint tests whether elicited requirements reach the actual recommendation. The test set contains 77 paired scenarios and 154 dialogues. 

CaSiNo: negotiating under private preferences. We retain CaSiNo’s campsite setting (Chawla et al., 2021), with three units each of food, water, and firewood. Each participant has private high-, medium-, and low-priority items, valued at 5, 4, and 3 points per unit, together with reasons for those preferences. To construct paired worlds, we keep the tested participant’s card fixed and replace the partner’s complete preference-and-reason card with one from the same original split. This changes which allocations benefit the partner while retaining the public negotiation setting. The participants negotiate a complete allocation that both accept. We report agreement rate and nor-malized Nash surplus, based on the Nash bargaining product (Nash, 1950). We take the product of 26 both participants’ nonnegative utility gains above the five-point walk-away value and divide it by the maximum attainable product in that world. Episodes without agreement receive zero surplus. These measures distinguish reaching an agreement from finding a mutually beneficial allocation. The test set contains 43 paired scenarios and 86 dialogues.

K.1 Cross-Judge Agreement 

We re-evaluate fixed model outputs with Kimi-K3 (Moonshot AI, 2026), retaining the Qwen3.8-Max In-tegrity messages, rubric, and evidence checks. Agreement between Qwen3.8-Max and Kimi-K3 is com-puted on the final binary Integrity decision for each model–task evaluation. 

Subset selection. A task position identifies a scenario, world, and tested role. The eligible pool is the intersection of positions with an available frozen Integrity request for Base, PPO, PaCPO, and GRPO (G = 4): 239 positions. We stratify by the six interaction families, sort family names and then task keys lexicographically, and shuffle each family’s keys using one Python random-number generator initialized with seed 20260924. Selecting the first ten keys per family without replacement yields 60 shared positions spanning 43 scenarios. Selection uses task identifiers and family labels, not Feasibility or Integrity scores, and is fixed before Kimi-K3 is queried. We reuse each method’s output at these same positions without regenerating dialogues, giving 60 evaluations per method and 240 in total. Identical requests are scored once and reused, producing 203 unique Kimi-K3 requests; reported statistics give equal weight to all 240 model–task evaluations.

K.2 Human Review 

Protocol. Two expert annotators review 48 dialogues from Base and PaCPO, covering 12 scenarios and both of their worlds, which form 24 matched Base–PaCPO pairs. For each dialogue, annotators see the public context, both role cards, the full conversation, and both records, with model identities hidden. 28 They judge whether the final plan satisfies the stated conditions (Feasibility) and whether the partic-ipants’ utterances support it (Integrity). For each matched pair, presented in random order (Zheng et al., 2023), they also judge which model provides more adequate help to the partner, citing turn-level evidence. 

Results. Human Feasibility judgments agree with the executable checks on 100% of dialogues, and human Integrity judgments agree with Qwen3.8-Max on 82.22%. In the helpfulness comparison, anno-tators prefer PaCPO in 8 pairs and Base in 5, rate the two as similar in 3, and judge both inadequate in 8. PaCPO thus receives more favorable judgments, while one third of the pairs reveal cases in which neither model helps adequately. 

# L Limitations and Future Work 

Scope of outcome verification. HICO-Bench covers six coordination families whose arrangements can be represented as structured records and checked against explicit conditions. This supports reproducible feasibility tests, but does not cover the full range of social interaction, such as emotional support or rela-tionships without a well-defined final outcome. Extending the benchmark requires identifying verifiable outcomes without reducing these interactions to inappropriate formal objectives. Feasibility success is feasibility under the specified conditions, not a general judgment of social appropriateness. 29
