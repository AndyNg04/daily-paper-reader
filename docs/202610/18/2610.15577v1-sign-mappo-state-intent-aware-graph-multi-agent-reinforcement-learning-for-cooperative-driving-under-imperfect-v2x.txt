# SIGN-MAPPO: State–Intent-Aware Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Cooperative Driving under Imperfect V2X 

Zide Fan, Member, IEEE , Jiahui Yuan, Kaixin Zhang, Junhao Fan, Xiaohe Li, Yiru Wang 

Abstract —Cooperative driving at mixed-traffic unsignalized intersections is challenged by imperfect vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication, human-driven vehicle (HV) intentions that cannot be directly communicated, and the potential incompati-bility of independently generated crossing decisions. This paper proposes State–Intent-Aware Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Cooperative Driving under Imperfect V2X (SIGN-MAPPO) to address these challenges through three coordinated mechanisms. Each decision-eligible connected and automated vehicle (CAV) first reconstructs a receiver-specific traffic graph from its ego state and the peer-state records available to it, from which a shared graph-attention policy generates a raw 

Stop/Go intention augmented with predicted HV-intent features. CAV intentions are then exchanged through timestamped vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) messages, and a local conflict-aware arbitration layer converts each CAV’s intention into a safe executable action despite potentially inconsistent peer information. In the princi-pal Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO) evaluation, SIGN-MAPPO achieves a 97.53% completion rate, a 0.10% collision rate, and a mean intersection traversal time of 35.28 s. Ablation studies further confirm the complementary contributions of HV-intent prediction and CAV-intent exchange to this performance. The trained policy also remains robust under the evaluated communication-delay and packet-loss ranges, and cross-scenario tests show complete traffic clearance across the tested settings, though safety margins narrow under compound unpredictable HV behavior. 

Index Terms —Connected and automated vehicles, graph atten-tion network, human-driven vehicle intent, multi-agent reinforce-ment learning, unsignalized intersection, vehicle-to-everything communication.

conventional HVs cannot directly exchange traffic informa-tion and exhibit heterogeneous driving behavior [2]. These differences complicate the anticipation and coordination of crossings at unsignalized intersections. Cooperative crossing depends in part on vehicle-state in-formation exchanged through vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication. Many intersection methods use a shared or synchronized traffic state [3], [4], but transmission delay and packet loss can deliver different updates to different CAVs or prevent some updates from arriving [5]. CAVs may therefore hold different information about the same surrounding vehicle, making their crossing decisions harder to coordinate. Vehicle states alone do not describe how traffic partici-pants intend to move. Existing mixed-traffic studies generally do not distinguish CAV intentions from HV intentions in their decision representations [6], [7]. CAV intentions can be communicated directly, whereas HV intentions must be inferred from motion histories and remain uncertain. These in-formation sources call for separate representations. Moreover, even accurate state and intention information does not ensure compatible actions when CAVs make decentralized decisions about conflicting movements. The combined problem of het-erogeneous intentions and decentralized action compatibility remains insufficiently addressed. Existing approaches address these challenges only partially. Rule-based and reservation-based methods [8], [9] provide interpretable coordination but generally assume synchronized or complete vehicle-state access, offering limited tolerance to inconsistent receiver-side information. Learning-based single-agent and multi-agent approaches [10]–[19] improve adapt-ability to interactive traffic but typically encode HV behavior implicitly within a shared state representation, without sepa-rating inferred HV intent from directly communicable CAV intent. Recent MARL-based cooperative-driving methods [6], [7] further improve coordination among CAVs but do not explicitly reconcile independently generated crossing decisions when V2X communication is imperfect. Consequently, no existing framework jointly addresses receiver-dependent state inconsistency, the distinct communicability of CAV and HV intentions, and the compatibility of decentralized decisions— the three factors that jointly determine whether cooperative crossing decisions remain safe and efficient once assumptions of synchronized communicable information are relaxed. To address these gaps, this paper proposes State–Intent-Aware Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Coop-erative Driving under Imperfect V2X (SIGN-MAPPO). CAV builds a receiver-specific traffic graph from the vehicle states available to it, with HV nodes augmented by intent features inferred from receiver-visible motion histories. A shared graph-attention policy generates raw CAV crossing intentions, which are exchanged through vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication. Each CAV then applies conflict-aware local arbitration to determine its executable action. Multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO) [20] trains the policy under centralized training and decentralized execution (CTDE) [21], while decisions remain local during execution. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows: 1) A receiver-specific graph decision model is developed for cooperative driving under imperfect V2X communi-cation. Each CAV constructs its own traffic graph from the vehicle-state information available to that receiver, yielding different graph representations when the re-ceived information differs across CAVs. 2) A decoupled HV- and CAV-intent modeling scheme is introduced. HV intentions are predicted from their ob-served motion histories and added to the corresponding HV node features, while CAV intentions are explicitly exchanged through a separate V2V communication path. 3) A conflict-aware local arbitration mechanism is designed for decentralized execution. Each CAV locally coordi-nates its own intention with the received intentions of other CAVs and converts the resulting decision into an executable action, improving the compatibility of actions selected by different CAVs. 4) Four groups of experiments are conducted, comprising baseline comparisons, module ablations, communication-robustness tests, and cross-scenario evaluations. The results show that SIGN-MAPPO achieves a 97.53% completion rate and a 0.10% collision rate while maintaining robust performance under communication impairments and across diverse traffic scenarios. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews unsignalized-intersection coordination, learning-based mixed-traffic decision making, graph and intent representations, and multi-agent learning under communica-tion uncertainty. Section III defines the mixed-traffic crossing problem and its optimization objective. Section IV presents the proposed SIGN-MAPPO decision model and its main components. Section V reports the comparative, ablation, communication-robustness, and cross-scenario experiments. Section VI concludes the paper. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Unsignalized-Intersection Coordination 

Early autonomous intersection management relies on ex-plicit right-of-way rules, reservations, or conflict-point con-straints. Dresner and Stone [8] formulated reservation-based intersection management, and Levin and Rey [9] optimized reservation timing through conflict-point separation. These methods provide interpretable safety constraints but generally require accurate vehicle states and coordinated access to the intersection. Formation-based and centralized cooperative controllers extend this line of work to connected vehicles [3], [4]. Mixed-traffic studies have examined cyber interactions among vehicles [22]; basic safety messages have been used to study driving decisions [23], while individual car-following styles inform cruise control design [24]. Recent intersection policies address random tasks and lane-graph structure [25], [26], but their performance still depends on the communication and controllability assumptions used to construct a common scene. 

B. Learning-Based Mixed-Traffic Decision Making 

DRL has been used to learn gap acceptance, crossing, and cooperative release policies from interaction data, within a broader progression toward environment-aware driving deci-sions. Isele et al. [10] studied deep Q-learning at occluded intersections, while Al-Sharman et al. [11] combined high-level reinforcement learning with feasible motion control. Demonstration-aided learning improves sample efficiency and constrains unsafe exploration [12], whereas social-value and uncertainty-aware formulations target interaction quality and branching efficiency [13], [14]. Transformer and multimodal representations have also been introduced for lane changing and urban driving decisions [15], [16]. For multi-vehicle cooperation, Zhuang et al. [6] introduced attention-based MAPPO with weighted local rewards. Wang 

et al. [7] developed a decentralized mixed-traffic controller and conflict-resolution mechanism for complex intersections. Related work has examined online interactive prediction, local-game reasoning, and soft actor–critic control [17]–[19]. These studies establish the value of learned coordination, but their traffic encodings do not jointly distinguish receiver-dependent state freshness and the different intent sources of CAVs and HVs. 

C. Graph and Intent Representations for Driving Policies 

Graph neural networks encode traffic participants as nodes and their interactions as edges, reducing the dependence on a fixed vector ordering. Graph attention networks (GATs) further learn neighbor importance through masked self-attention [27]. In autonomous driving, Ma et al. [28] combined latent driver-state inference with spatial–temporal graph relations, Mo et al. [29] modeled heterogeneous edges for trajectory predic-tion, and Ye et al. [30] learned spatial–temporal interaction representations using graph self-attention. Peng et al. [31] coupled GAT-based planning with a safety architecture, and Zheng et al. [32] used graph attention to capture multi-hop interaction dependencies at unsignalized intersections. Gated recurrent units (GRUs) provide a recurrent mechanism for encoding sequential observations [33]. SIGN-MAPPO builds on these components but assigns them specific information roles: graph nodes are constructed for each receiver, inferred HV intent enters the policy representation, and communicated CAV intent supports execution-side coordination. 3

D. Multi-Agent Training Under Communication Uncertainty 

Centralized-training/decentralized-execution methods re-duce multi-agent nonstationarity during optimization while preserving distributed policies at deployment [21]. Counter-factual credit assignment and value decomposition provide alternative cooperative-learning mechanisms [34], [35]. Proxi-mal policy optimization (PPO) supplies a stable clipped policy objective [36], and MAPPO has shown strong performance across cooperative multi-agent benchmarks [20]. Vehicular deployment adds a communication layer in which a received record may be valid but stale. Age-of-information research [37] highlights the importance of status-update freshness, while delay and packet loss have been studied in signal-free intersection control [5]. These findings motivate explicit timestamp tracking rather than treating every delivered packet as current. SIGN-MAPPO incorporates this principle through directed latest-valid caches and evaluates state-only as well as joint state–intent delay and loss. III. P ROBLEM STATEMENT 

We consider a four-leg unsignalized intersection with one left-turn lane and two through lanes on each approach. The area enclosed by the stop lines is the conflict area, and the upstream segment of each approach is the control region. Traffic comprises CAVs and HVs; the latter exhibit hesitant, nominal, or aggressive driving styles with stochastic within-style variation. CAVs exchange information via V2V commu-nication, while an RSU observes HV states and broadcasts them to CAVs. The V2V range exceeds twice the control-region length, allowing approaching CAVs to communicate before entering the conflict area. Both communication paths are subject to delay and packet loss. Fig. 1 illustrates the scenario and information flows. On each lane, the vehicle nearest the stop line is the lane-head vehicle. A lane-head CAV inside the control region is decision-eligible and receives a high-level crossing decision from the proposed method. Following CAVs remain under car-following control until becoming lane-head. 

A. Dec-POMDP Formulation 

Traffic interaction at an unsignalized intersection is modeled as a sequential decision-making problem. A decision made for a lane-head CAV affects its subsequent motion and therefore the traffic state at the next decision step. Multiple lane-head CAVs may require decisions at the same time, and each CAV makes its decision using receiver-specific and poten-tially incomplete traffic information. The intersection-crossing problem is therefore formulated as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP). A. Model Framework 

Decision making at mixed-traffic unsignalized intersec-tions is challenged by three coupled uncertainties. Imperfect 5

V2X communication leads to delayed, missing, and receiver-dependent vehicle-state information. Meanwhile, HV inten-tions are not directly observable and must be inferred from their motion histories. In addition, independently generated CAV decisions may become mutually incompatible when route-conflicting vehicles simultaneously request access, mak-ing reliable decentralized coordination difficult. To address these coupled challenges, this study proposes State–Intent-Aware Graph Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Coop-erative Driving under Imperfect V2X (SIGN-MAPPO), a co-operative crossing-decision framework for CAVs. As shown in Fig. 2, the framework comprises three parts: receiver-specific state reconstruction and graph construction; HV-intent predic-tion and a graph-attention decision policy; and timestamped CAV-intent exchange followed by a separate local conflict-aware arbitration layer. The following subsections introduce these parts in turn.

C. Implementation of the Algorithm 

At each decision step, each eligible CAV reconstructs its receiver-specific traffic state, builds the corresponding graph, and augments HV nodes with predicted intent features. The shared GAT actor generates a raw CAV intention, which is exchanged through the timestamped intent channel and converted into an executable action by local arbitration. During training, identity-indexed transitions are collected to compute aligned advantages and returns for updating the shared actor and critic, while HV histories supervise the intent predictor. During online execution, only state reconstruction, HV-intent prediction, the GAT actor, CAV-intent exchange, and local arbitration are retained. Algorithm 1 summarizes the complete procedure. V. This paper presented SIGN-MAPPO for cooperative CAV decision making at mixed-traffic unsignalized intersections under imperfect V2X communication. Each decision-eligible CAV constructs a receiver-specific graph from its available vehicle states and augments HV nodes with intent features predicted from observed motion histories. A shared graph-attention actor generates a raw Stop /Go intention, which is exchanged through timestamped V2V messages. Local arbi-tration then combines the available CAV intentions with state-derived priorities and conflict checks to determine the exe-cutable action.
