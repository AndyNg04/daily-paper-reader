1 Introduction 

Natural language is increasingly used to elicit user preferences in automated decision-making systems [2, 31, 34]. Existing methods capture natural language preferences either implicitly within a latent space [34] or explicitly by mapping input to a fixed set of static attributes [31]. However, natural language descriptions are inherently soft :they exhibit a high degree of semantic imprecision and admit multiple defensible interpretations without further specification [6]. In a latent-space paradigm, a single soft descriptor may conflate distinct semantic dimensions and make downstream rankings uninterpretable. For example, evaluating essays based on “originality” can obscure whether a score reflects conceptual novelty, stylistic deviation, or structural divergence. Mapping language directly to explicit attributes increases operational transparency, yet no direct mapping exists between soft language and formal criteria. This lack of a direct mapping stems from the fact that a soft description leaves open multiple valid ways to construct a computable function. Resolving such openness is not simply a matter of recovering a fixed meaning presumed to exist in the user’s mind, nor is it addressed by typical forms of linguistic ambiguity, such as lexical polysemy, semantic mapping, or vagueness thresholding [55]. These approaches resolve existing structures, for instance, polysemy selects among word senses, semantic mapping links expressions to fixed ontologies, and vagueness sets boundaries on scalar predicates. Soft descriptions, however, pose a different challenge: their interpretation is inherently subjective and user-dependent, and therefore cannot be fully determined from linguistic context alone. Moreover, users may be unable to articulate what they mean upfront, discover relevant distinctions only after seeing how their description is applied, or revise their preferences during the process. Constructing an attribute from a soft description is therefore an interactive specification problem: the user and the system must reach an operational meaning that can be inspected and applied consistently. One way to reduce the softness of a user’s description is by actively clarifying their intent through targeted questions [1] – an approach explored in prior conversational preference elicitation frameworks [2]. Clarification, however, incurs interaction overhead, making it unfeasible to query every possible interpretation. While some descriptions provide a sufficiently clear basis for computation, others leave open ambiguities that materially alter downstream outcomes. Effective systems must therefore identify when a description is genuinely soft so that clarification is requested only when its value outweighs the cost—an objective addressed by recent efforts in cost-aware clarification under ambiguity [57]. In this paper, we introduce AttriFy, a mixed-initiative visual analytics system for constructing computable attribute functions from natural-language descriptions. We develop a computational estimate of softness and treat it as a control signal for interactive attribute construction. Specifically, AttriFy uses estimated softness to identify when a description may benefit from clarification and guides the user through an iterative dialogue to construct an explicit operationalization. A user-facing agent proposes dataset-grounded refinements that help the user distinguish between competing interpretations, while a modeling agent translates the resulting specification into executable functions over the data. A visual interface exposes the provenance of this process, allowing users to trace the path from their initial description to the resulting functions and inspect or revise intermediate decisions. The resulting functions represent explicit operationalizations of the description rather than claims to its uniquely correct or ground-truth meaning. The paper is structured in the following way. After reviewing prior work, we establish the design goals of our interface. Next, to demonstrate that softness can serve as an effective signal, we formalize and validate it through a formative study. Our results show that people share an ordering of descriptions by softness, that this ordering tracks the diversity of their

These findings provide an empirical reference for evaluating our computational estimate. We then present our agent-based workflow and the interface design, concluding with an evaluation of the end-to-end approach comparing AttriFy to a baseline. In this evaluation, we assess whether the resulting attributes reflect users’ judgments and discriminate meaningfully among items. To summarize, this work makes three contributions: (1) Softness: We define and empirically ground softness as interpretive diversity and conduct a formative study showing that perceived softness corresponds to the number of human interpretations and changes systematically through clarification. (2) AttriFy: We introduce a mixed-initiative system that uses our proposed computational estimator of softness to decide when to clarify and when to generate executable attribute functions. 2 Related Work 

Below, we review related work along four core axes central to our approach: capturing user preferences, managing ambiguity in natural language, iteratively clarifying user intent, and designing visual interactive chat-based interfaces. 

2.1 Capturing User Preferences 

Several lines of work have attempted to operationalize subjective descriptors for use in computational pipelines. Li et al. [34] investigate how language models can actively elicit and infer human preferences through interactive questioning. Concept activation vectors are used to model soft attributes as directions in a learned embedding space [6, 22]. The approach has been extended to active critiquing - given a set of items, the technique determines the soft attributes about which to elicit user feedback [8]. More interactive approaches employ multi-turn Bayesian elicitation to iteratively refine user preferences [4], address noisy or inconsistent user feedback [16], as well as use function approximation to obviate the need for specific modeling assumptions [39]. Also item pairwise comparisons are used to learn preference models, e.g., in the latent space of an autoencoder [15]. Instead of yielding transparent, directly computable attribute definitions, these methods produce opaque representations that users can neither easily inspect nor adjust. Other systems focus on developing preference elicitation methods that can accommodate soft attributes by combining item-based and attribute-based feedback [8]. Across these approaches, a common limitation persists as they provide limited insight into how a subjective description is translated into measurable criteria, which constrains trust, user control, and suitability for practical decision support. Interactive tools for constructing LLM evaluation criteria [49] come closest, showing that users revise their criteria as they inspect outputs, but they assume a criterion can be stated and then aligned, whereas we address the prior question of whether a description is specified enough to be operationalized. 

2.2 Ambiguity in Natural Language 

Ambiguity is a hallmark of natural language. Speakers routinely omit details that are inferable from context, which enables concise communication but complicates computational interpretation. In machine translation, for example, a single source sentence can admit multiple valid renderings in the target language [17]. Even among human annotators,

4 Holter et al. seemingly well-defined examples can provoke disagreement [42], a phenomenon frequently attributed to underlying linguistic ambiguity [36]. These observations highlight the importance of establishing common ground (i.e., a shared understanding between interlocutors) before a task can be solved efficiently [48]. The same applies when a human interacts with a chatbot. Research on natural-language ambiguity has traditionally focused on word-sense and syntactic disambiguation, while recent work increasingly addresses ambiguity in open-ended questions and dialogue, especially in chat-based interfaces. It has been shown that LLMs often struggle to explicitly represent ambiguity [36], although the spread of their sampled outputs has been used as a proxy for it [11, 32]. A recent survey [51] shows that work has shifted toward interactive disambiguation approaches, where systems detect ambiguity and aim to resolve it. These include approaches like a pipeline that aligns LLMs to manage ambiguous queries by leveraging their own assessment of ambiguity (i.e., perceived ambiguity) [30], or a ‘round-trip’ approach, where the model generates possible question–answer interpretations, then uses them to generate additional interpretations that may have been missed, and verifies the resulting question–answer pairs to produce the final disambiguated output. At the same time, more benchmarks [40, 46] are created to improve and evaluate the disambiguation methods developed. According to Poesio, “ambiguity is one of the most important aspects of natural language, but one of the most difficult to define and distinguish from related (and unrelated) phenomena” [44]. Linguistic work distinguishes ambiguity from vagueness [29], semantic underspecification [20], and subjectivity [18]. Although each of these concepts has a distinct definition, as noted by Poesio, the boundaries between them are not always clear. Our work builds on these distinctions to isolate an operational property of natural language input. Specifically, we formalize softness as the diversity of valid interpretations a description permits, directly addressing the boundary between vagueness and underspecification, and provide empirical support for this characterization. We then use softness to trigger targeted clarifications and signal when to generate computational approximations. 

2.3 Iterative Clarification 

Clarification questions have long been considered a remedy for natural language ambiguity. In open-domain information-seeking conversations, it has been shown that asking a single well-chosen clarifying question improves retrieval performance [1]. Recent work has investigated how to train or prompt large language models to ask these questions proactively [2, 58]. Yet clarifying behavior remains scarce in practice. The primary application domain has been information-seeking dialogue, where the system must detect ambiguity in a user query and ask targeted follow-up questions [23, 54], with dedicated benchmarks developed to evaluate this capability [12]. Related research has introduced proactive chain-of-thought prompting approaches that equip dialogue systems with the ability to plan and take initiative toward a conversational goal [13]. Several machine learning approaches have been used to teach models to ask clarifying questions. For instance, Zhang et al. [58] simulate multi-turn interactions with synthetic users, scores candidate clarifying questions based on the accuracy of the final downstream answer, and use Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to train a model to ask targeted questions only when necessary. Chen et al. [10] introduce Action-Based Contrastive Self-Training (ACT), a data-efficient method for teaching LLMs conversational policies, particularly the ability to recognize ambiguity and ask appropriate clarification questions. They show that ACT can outperform DPO across several conversational tasks. Finally, Montazeralghaem et al. [41] introduce a training method inspired by diffusion models to teach LLMs how to ask clear, step-by-step questions to uncover user preferences. In our setting, clarification serves a different purpose: it is not about resolving a factual query which is often the target in prior work but about surfacing latent preferences that shape the meaning of a soft attribute [34]. This makes

First, there is no ground truth against which a clarification can be validated, since the attribute is inherently subjective. Second, the need for clarification is itself a personalized question (i.e., what is ambiguous to one user may be perfectly clear to another) requiring a mechanism that judges when further interaction is necessary and when it would be redundant. 

2.4 Visual Interactive Chat-based Interfaces 

Users frequently struggle to express multifaceted intentions through flat, linear text feeds [43]. Standard chat-based interfaces often fail to capture branching information topologies, leading to context loss and inefficient interaction. To address these limitations, recent work leverages structured, non-linear representations to externalize context and streamline human decision-making. Setlur and Tory [47] demonstrate how combining natural language interaction with visual semantics allows users to convey intent and navigate analytical conversations. They show that a system’s conversational interface heavily shapes user expectations regarding its underlying capabilities. When responses do not align with these expectations, successful interaction is not simply based on presenting an answer, but rather on providing actionable mechanisms for clarification, refinement, and repair. Aligning with these insights, systems like AmbigChat [38] map vague queries into hierarchical disambiguation trees to refine user intent, allowing users to progressively resolve ambiguities and select among alternative interpretations before receiving an answer. Other visual tools such as Needle [37] provide multi-level thread visualizations to alleviate cognitive load during complex content triage. Similarly, visual trees and graph structures enable users to construct coherent linearized dialogues [9] and manage historical context across multi-turn exchanges [35]. We present an interface that helps users model soft attributes through clarification while maintaining a complete interaction history. 

3 Design Goals 

In this section, we characterize the exact scope of the work, the challenges, and the main requirements that inform our proposed interface and workflow. 

3.1 Problem Scope 

Due to the nuanced meaning of several terms, it is important to clarify the scope of this work. In many practical ap-plications of multi-criteria decision support, such as ranking or recommendation, the central challenge is structuring items according to user preferences. In this context, we distinguish between attributes and preferences . An attribute 

is a property that describes each item in a dataset and for which every item has a corresponding value, for example, the flight duration of a travel itinerary. A preference , in contrast, is a user-imposed condition over one or more attributes, such as preferring shorter flight times. Eliciting or learning these preferences lies outside the scope of this work. This work focuses primarily on textual corpora, where the attributes of interest are not explicitly represented and must be constructed instead. In particular, we focus on soft attributes : subjective, user-dependent properties not explicitly encoded in the data. Examples include notions such as the novelty of a scientific contribution or the clarity of a piece of writing, which lack a single predefined representation. Our goal is to operationalize these soft attributes in a measurable form by computing corresponding attribute values for each text item. 

3.2 User Needs & Challenges 

Articulating soft attributes and refining how they are operationalized presents several challenges for users. The construction of attributes is inherently subjective and depends on users’ intentions and prior experiences [21, 22].

For instance, two users may both express interest in tennis articles for different reasons, with one following the professional circuit and another seeking to improve their own technique [34]. The attributes they need to capture these interests will therefore differ. This highlights why relevant attributes cannot be assumed a priori, but must be grounded in users’ intended meanings. Consequently, predefined or fully automated approaches fail to capture these meanings [6], motivating iterative and interactive refinement of user-described attributes .At the same time, users often struggle to explicitly articulate which properties matter to them [34, 56]. Their initial understanding of relevant attributes may be incomplete, creating a need for support for ideation and exploration of potential attributes. However, excessive system guidance can introduce bias by steering users toward particular dimensions [24]. This creates a tension between guidance and autonomy, making it necessary to balance system guidance with user-driven exploration .Attribute construction further relies on limited user interactions, providing only partial coverage of the total space of possible attributes. The selective nature of these interactions can introduce selection bias, as early or salient dimensions are favored while others remain unexplored [27, 52]. This concentration on a narrow set of dimensions can produce redundant or highly correlated attributes, limiting the resulting representation’s ability to capture diverse user-relevant distinctions [33, 59]. This highlights the need for diverse and discriminative attribute representations .In addition, a user’s description of a soft attribute does not necessarily specify how to determine its value for in-dividual items [6, 22]. Users’ descriptions may leave aspects of their intended meaning implicit, making it difficult to determine how to assess these attributes. The softer the description, the more assumptions a system may need to intro-duce to approximate the user’s intended meaning with a computational function. Reducing softness through refinement helps constrain these assumptions. Therefore, systems must support the translation of soft attributes into measurable quantities while preserving users’ intended meanings. This operationalization, in turn, introduces transparency challenges. Many existing approaches, such as activation-based methods, lack interpretability [22], making it unclear how user inputs influence the resulting attributes [5, 45]. This opacity limits users’ ability to understand, evaluate, and refine the constructed representations. As such, systems must provide a transparent mapping from user input to computed attributes .Finally, transparency must be accompanied by opportunities to act on what users observe. As users examine attribute values for concrete items, they may identify mismatches with their intended meanings or reconsider their initial definitions [45]. This requires systems to allow users to iteratively adjust, correct, and refine attribute definitions rather than relying on fixed or hard-coded assumptions. This underscores the importance of maintaining user control over the attribute construction process .

3.3 Description of Requirements 

Based on the challenges outlined above, we formulate the following requirements for a system that supports soft attribute modeling. R1: Iterative Refinement - Support the incremental refinement of soft attributes through repeated cycles of inspection and revision. R2: Balanced Guidance - Provide guidance that encourages user exploration while limiting system-induced bias. R3: Diverse and Discriminative Attributes - Support the construction of complementary attributes that capture distinct, user-relevant aspects of the data and meaningfully distinguish between items. R4: Measurability - Enable the transformation of soft attributes into computable values that reflect users’ intended meanings. R5: Transparency - Provide interpretable mappings from user input to computed attribute values. R6: User Control - Allow users to adapt, correct, and steer the attribute construction process. Together, these requirements motivate an iterative process in which users can inspect and refine how their intended meanings are translated into attribute values. Supporting this process requires an understanding of what makes an attribute soft and how that softness affects its operationalization. The next section develops this conceptual foundation. 

4 Studying Softness 

Our definition characterizes softness as the extent to which a description admits multiple valid interpretations without further specification. To ground this definition empirically and inform its computational modeling, we conducted a formative study to examine how people interpret descriptions, judge their softness, and revise those judg-ments when provided clarification. We investigated three research questions: (1) Is perceived softness associated with the diversity of interpretations a description elicits? (2) To what extent do people agree in their judgments of softness? (3) How do these judgments change when a description is clarified or supplemented with additional information? Our study focuses on descriptions of middle-school essay attributes, maintaining the application domain introduced earlier. This setting is familiar and the attributes can be described at varying levels of specificity without requiring specialist knowledge. The study comprised three tasks over a common pool of descriptions. Participants were first asked to provide interpretations without being introduced to softness, then judged softness through ratings and comparisons, and finally rated descriptions across successive turns of clarifying dialogues. 4.1 Study Design 

Stimuli — We constructed a set of descriptions of essay attributes spanning different levels of specificity across dimen-sions commonly used to assess argumentative writing [28]. For example, “an essay should include an introduction, a body, and a conclusion” specifies its organization, whereas “the ideas should be well argued” describes the persua-siveness of the content. Within each dimension, descriptions ranged from explicit criteria to qualities that different readers could interpret in different ways. This deliberate variation in specificity guided stimulus construction, while participants’ responses supplied the empirical measurements used to model softness. For the dialogue task, we constructed five types of exchanges with distinct predicted patterns of change in perceived softness. (1) Converging dialogues progressively clarified a description. (2) Floor dialogues began with an already specific description and were expected to remain low in softness, even as more information was provided. (3) Plateau 

dialogues stopped adding relevant context, either after an initial clarification or from the outset. (4) Re-opening dialogues introduced a new facet mid-way, while (5) Reversal dialogues directly contradicted an earlier more concrete statement. By design the latter two were expected to increase softness. Including these cases allowed us to examine whether judgments responded to the content of an exchange rather than simply decreasing with additional turns. We generated candidate descriptions and dialogues using Claude Opus 4.8 [3], following the criteria above, and manually removed duplicates and items that did not resemble plausible user descriptions. The final pool contained 50 descriptions and 20 five-turn dialogues. Generation prompts and full stimuli are provided in Appendix A.3. 

Participants — We recruited 70 participants through the crowdsourcing platform Prolific 1. Participants were required to be fluent in English and have a university-equivalent education, which we used as a proxy for some familiarity with argumentative writing. After applying our exclusion criteria (Appendix A.2), we retained data from 62 participants. 

Procedure — All participants completed a single web-based session comprising three tasks in a fixed order: (1) interpretation, (2) judgment, and (3) clarification. Sessions lasted an average of 27 minutes (SD = 8 minutes). In the 

interpretation task , each participant saw ten descriptions and wrote one sentence per description explaining how they would interpret or measure the described attribute. At the start of the judgment task , we introduced our definition of softness and a five-point rating scale (Appendix A.1). Participants then rated ten descriptions individually using this scale and ranked eight sets of three descriptions from least to most soft via a drag-and-drop interface. In the third 

clarification task , participants viewed five dialogues, each revealed one turn at a time. After each turn, they used the same scale to rate the softness of the description based on the information and context provided so far. No participant both interpreted and rated the same attribute description from the pool. Item order was randomized within each task. 

4.2 Empirical Analysis of Softness 

For the interpretation task , two coders independently grouped participants’ responses to each description. Responses were assigned to the same group when their interpretations would identify the same set of essays as satisfying the description (Appendix A.4). For example, one participant interpreted “each claim has some evidence behind it” as “statements or arguments are followed by facts , statistics, quotes, or specific real-world examples” while another responded more generally “look for examples or facts that support each claim.“ Although phrased differently, these responses were clustered together because they were judged to imply equivalent criteria for evaluating essays. The coders identified 28 of the 620 responses as unrelated to their corresponding descriptions and excluded them from the analysis. The two coders’ independent groupings were retained without consolidation. To account for unequal sample sizes, we used

Clarification Changed Perceived Softness in Both Directions — In the clarification task , softness judgments changed according to the information added during an exchange (Figure 4c). Converging dialogues decreased in softness by an average of 1.94 points on our five-point scale from the first to the final turn. Dialogues that introduced a new facet increased by 0.73 points, while those containing a reversal increased by 0.52 points. Reversal dialogues showed how later information could undo the effect of earlier clarification. Their mean rating initially fell from 3.02 to 2.00 as the description became more specific, then rose to 3.53 after a contradictory statement. Thus, an exchange could become softer again even after an initial reduction. Plateau dialogues decreased by 0.81 points overall, with the decline occurring at the first clarification and ratings remaining approximately level from the second turn onward. Floor dialogues remained broadly stable, with a net change of -0.10 points, although ratings dipped slightly before rising again. Together, these patterns motivate a measure that responds to the content of an exchange and accommodates decreases, stability, and increases in softness. 

4.3 Softness Algorithm 

The formative study findings informs our modeling approach in three ways. First, the association between possible readings and perceived softness supports interpretive diversity as a basis for estimation. Second, the alignment between aggregated ratings and comparisons provides a human reference for relative softness. Third, the dialogue results motivate a model in which softness can decrease, remain stable, or increase depending on the information added. We propose an algorithm that probes the latent interpretation space by generating operational readings and measuring their semantic diversity. The underlying hypothesis is that the separability of a relatively small sample of interpretations reflects the diversity of this broader space, providing an estimate of a description’s softness, in line with work that uses the diversity of sampled model outputs as a signal of uncertainty or ambiguity [11, 32]. 

Approach — Given an attribute description, a language model generates ten short operational readings, each naming a decision procedure a reader could apply to an essay. For example, well structured may refer to a topic sentence in each paragraph or logical paragraph ordering . We use high-temperature generation to encourage varied candidates, then embed them with a sentence encoder. We quantify their diversity using the Vendi score [19]. The score represents an effective number of distinct readings, ranging from one for identical embeddings to ten for mutually orthogonal embeddings. Unlike counting the ten generated candidates, it accounts for their similarity, assigning lower diversity to paraphrases of the same reading. A clipped linear transformation maps this value to a softness score between 0 and 100. For clarification dialogues, we first restate the transcript as a single current attribute specification, retaining key points while giving precedence to the latest revisions. We generate readings afresh from this specification after each turn, allowing estimated softness to increase when an exchange introduces new interpretive possibilities. This restatement step focuses interpretation on the user’s expressed intent and reduces the influence of incidental wording in the dialogue.

Agreement with human observations — We applied our softness algorithm to each description three times and aver-aged the resulting estimates. The intraclass correlation coefficient was 0 .887 for a single run and 0 .959 for the three-run mean. Across the 50 descriptions, mean estimated softness correlated with mean human ratings at 𝜌 = 0.742 (Figure 5a). The measure also aligned with changes in human softness ratings across turns in clarification dialogues (Figure 5b). For 100 turns of 20 dialogues, estimated and human-rated softness correlated at 𝜌 = 0.642. After subtracting each dialogue’s mean from its respective estimated and human scores, the correlation remained 𝜌 = 0.547, indicating an association within dialogues as well as between them. These results support the measure as an approximation of perceived softness in our study setting. 5 AttriFy 

In this section, we introduce AttriFy, our tool for interactive soft attribute modeling. We first describe how its workflow addresses the requirements outlined earlier, then present the agent framework that supports this workflow, and finally introduce the visual, chat-based interface through which users construct and refine attributes. 

5.1 Workflow 

We developed an iterative workflow to address the requirements outlined above. As illustrated in Figure 6, our system organizes the interaction around an iterative expand → constrain → steer loop that progressively refines attribute definitions to better align them with the user’s mental model. At the beginning of the interaction, users often hold only vague intuitions or tacit knowledge about the properties they care about. Consequently, their initial descriptions are frequently underspecified and admit multiple plausible interpretations – they are soft . Following our definition, these interpretations form a latent space of attribute candidates. Users encounter this space through candidate attributes proposed by the system, while other possible interpretations remain implicit. Users evaluate these proposals and can accept or reject each attribute, determining which interpretations are operationalized. Accepted attributes are then computed for individual items and added to an evolving attribute table ,

In the expand phase, the system surfaces exploratory prompts in the form of open-ended questions that help users broaden the space of possible interpretations. In the constrain phase, the system asks clarification questions that reduce the number of plausible interpretations of the attribute description. Abstract concepts are decomposed into more concrete aspects or additional constraints are introduced, progressively narrowing the space of attribute candidates and making them more amenable to operational-ization. This step can be repeated as users progressively articulate and refine their intent (R1). For example, the system may ask: “Do you mean novelty in terms of methodology, application domain, or empirical findings?” 

Finally, the steer phase allows users to provide their own input through user suggestions , introducing new directions or contextual information (R6). Users may also draw on the computed attribute values to identify mismatches between the system’s interpretation and their intended meaning. This mechanism preserves user agency and enables users to correct or redirect the system in response to such mismatches or as their interests evolve. Depending on the input, steering may broaden the space of interpretations or narrow it further. For example, a user might specify: “I am particularly interested in papers that propose simple but elegant ideas.”

On failure, the agent iterates through planning, generating, validating, executing, and observing until the function works or retries are exhausted. Functions that pass these checks are stored in a central registry and presented to users through descriptions of their computation. Users can accept proposals for dataset-wide computation or refine their intent to guide further generation. 

5.3 Visual Interface 

AttriFy uses a visual interface to guide the user through our workflow. The interface comprises four coordinated panels, shown in Figure 8: (A) the chat panel, (B) interaction history, (C) attribute table, and (D) coverage graph. 

Chat Panel — The chat panel supports natural-language conversation through a sequential question-and-answer exchange, presenting one question at a time. When the softness score falls below the target threshold, the chat reveals the possible function definition as a card containing its name and a detailed description of how it will compute the attribute values. This description allows users to assess what the function actually measures, since a broad name such as 

calculate_creativity may conceal a simple counting algorithm (R5). Users must accept, reject, or request refinement of each proposed function before the system asks the next question (R6). This sequence keeps the interaction focused

14 Holter et al. on the current question and its associated proposals. 

Interaction History — The interaction history presents a visual timeline of the session, with each interaction positioned vertically according to its softness score. Nodes are labeled with keywords from the user’s response and indicate the question type. Expanding a node reveals the full corresponding question and answer. Functions proposed at an interaction appear as linked cards similar to those in the chat and indicate whether they were accepted, rejected, or refined. These cards are placed at the zero-softness baseline to represent a single computational interpretation. The vertical distance from an interaction node to this baseline visually represents the interpretive gap (or approximation gap) bridged by the computational approximation, with greater distances indicating that more possible interpretations remained when the function was generated. Users can revisit earlier cards and revise their decisions, for example when a newly generated function makes a previously accepted attribute redundant (R6). The history thus traces how softness changes and which functions emerge over the course of the interactive development of the attribute space (R5). 

Attribute Table — When a user accepts a function card, the system applies the function to the dataset and displays the resulting attribute values in the table, connecting the natural-language dialogue to its computational results. Users can inspect these values alongside the underlying items through the same panel, allowing them to assess how well the functions capture their intended meanings. Histograms above the attribute columns summarize the distribution of values across the corpus. 

Coverage Graph — The coverage graph visualizes the separability of items under the current attributes (R3). Items are projected from the attribute table into two dimensions and displayed as points, with clusters highlighted to reveal groups that are similarly represented by the constructed attributes. As the attribute table changes, the projection updates to show how the representation of the corpus evolves. This view helps users identify groups of items that may warrant additional distinguishing attributes. The same clusters guide the Question Agent’s open-ended questions, which target the largest cluster to elicit attributes that could further distinguish its items. 

5.4 Illustrative Use Case 

Consider a teacher who wants to define grading criteria (i.e., attributes) for argumentative essays. The teacher tells AttriFy that an essay should “take a clear stance.” In this example, the Softness Agent estimates a softness score of 58 and the Function Agent proposes a computational approximation for review while also inviting further clarification. A proposed function might detect a thesis-like sentence in the first paragraph. Its card describes this procedure, allowing the teacher to consider whether it captures an aspect of the intended criterion. Accepting the proposal adds its computed values to the attribute table, where the teacher can inspect the distribution and compare values with individual essays. After proposal review, the Question Agent asks whether the stance must also be maintained throughout the essay. The teacher responds that the position should not change. This answer adds a constraint to the current specification, which the system reassesses before selecting its next action. If the estimated softness is sufficiently low, the system proposes functions for review and subsequently invites the teacher to suggest another aspect or begin a new topic. The interaction history links each proposal to the discussion that produced it. Function cards appear at the zero-softness baseline to indicate a selected computational interpretation. Together, the history and attribute table allow the teacher to trace proposed interpretations and inspect their consequences. This example illustrates the workflow rather than reporting a recorded study session.

5.5 Implementation Details 

AttriFy is implemented as a browser-based workspace supported by a Python service. The client is a React and TypeScript single-page application that presents the four components described above. The server is a FastAPI ap-plication that stores session state in MongoDB. Dialogue generation and softness scoring use Google Gemini, with 

gemini-3.5-flash for questions and restatement and gemini-3.5-flash-lite for sampling interpretations. These model choices reflect the different latency requirements of the two tasks. The softness algorithm combines sentence-transformer embeddings with a Vendi-derived score. Natural-language attribute descriptions are compiled into exe-cutable Python scoring functions by an external CodeAct service ( langgraph-codeact ). Accepted functions are applied to the essay collection, after which scikit-learn PCA and DBSCAN produce the clustered projection. 

6 User Study 

We conduct a controlled, web-based within-subjects study comparing AttriFy with an LLM-driven baseline. The evaluation examines support for attribute construction, allowing participants to develop and revise their intended specifications during the interaction. We address three research questions: 

RQ1: Clarifying intent - How does AttriFy affect participants’ perceived ability to express and clarify their intended attribute specifications? 

RQ2: Interaction experience - How does AttriFy affect participants’ perceived effort, perceived transparency, control and ownership over attribute construction?

6.1 Participants and Study Design 

We recruited 41 participants through Prolific, restricting eligibility to native English speakers aged 18 or older. Of these, 32 passed the attention check and completed the study. We refer to these participants as P1–P32 throughout the paper. We report participant demographics, prior experience with LLM tools, and exclusion criteria in the Appendix C.1. Each participant completed two blocks in a single session, one with AttriFy and one with the Baseline. Tool order and dataset assignment were counterbalanced across four allocation cells, crossing the starting tool (AttriFy or baseline) with the starting essay dataset (argumentative or narrative). Each participant saw different essay materials in each condition, so that tool comparisons were not confounded by familiarity with specific texts. The complete session lasted approximately 60-90 minutes, and participants received compensation at a rate of 12$ per hour. The study was approved by our institution’s institutional review board (details omitted for anonymous review). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. 

6.2 Baseline, Tasks, and Procedure 

Baseline — The Baseline provides an LLM chat interface with access to the same function-generation service as AttriFy. To ensure a fair isolated comparison, we also show the participants the same attribute table (Figure 8c) and projected coverage graph (Figure 8d). However, its conversation is not governed by our tool’s structured workflow or softness scores. Where AttriFy uses estimated softness to determine when to clarify and when to generate functions, the Baseline generates functions at every step. Participants still explicitly accept, reject or refine proposed functions through the chat. This focuses the comparison and evaluates the complete AttriFy workflow against an open-ended LLM driven conversation, with both workflows having access to the same generation tools and supporting panels. 

Tasks — Participants constructed attributes for collections of approximately 500-word essays, using a narrative essay dataset in one block and an argumentative essay dataset in the other. All the data used in the study is sourced from the ASAP 2.0 corpus comprising of 24,278 student essays 3.

Procedure — Each session lasted approximately 60-90 minutes. After consent and a brief introduction, participants completed a general tutorial using materials separate from the main tasks. Before each study block, they received a description of the assigned dataset and a tutorial specific to the tool used in that block. Participants then accessed the assigned tool and had 15 minutes to construct a set of attributes reflecting the properties they considered important in the assigned essay collection. A visible timer indicated the remaining time. The system initiated the conversation, after which participants could freely interact with the tool, discuss their ideas, and accept, reject, or refine proposed functions. At the end of each block, participants reviewed their resulting specifications and completed a questionnaire and written reflection. After both blocks, they indicated their comparative preference and explained their choice. Further procedural details are provided in Appendix C.2.

To comprehensively address our research questions, we employed a combination of self-reported and behavioral measures, complemented by qualitative analyses of participants’ free-text responses.

7 Discussion 

In this chapter we discuss what our two studies reveal about understanding softness and how AttriFy supports users compared to the LLM-baseline.

7.1 Softness 

Our findings provide empirical support for defining softness as the diversity of valid interpretations a description permits. Descriptions that elicited more distinct interpretations by study participants were perceived as softer, and aggregated ratings converged with comparative judgments. Together, these results ground softness in observable patterns of interpretation and judgment within the studied setting. In addition, our results show that participants were more aligned with our constructed softness degrees when making comparative judgements between descriptions than when assigning absolute ratings on our pre-defined scale. This suggests that softness may convey a useful signal without requiring a precise numerical value. One possible explanation is that people recognize softness or interpretive openness intuitively, but they lack a convention for expressing it explicitly. The limited separation between the two highest constructed softness degrees suggests that distinctions at the softer end were less clearly resolved. This reflects the asymmetry in the scale: low softness can be anchored in a single operational interpretation, whereas high softness reflects an open-ended latent space of possible readings. People may envision different portions of that space, assigning different scores to the same description. This variation itself reflects a property that a softness measure should capture to work effectively. Although interpretive diversity aligned with perceived softness overall, identifying the boundaries between inter-pretations remained difficult. Coders agreed more closely on the number of groups than on which responses exactly belonged together. Annotating responses that combined multiple readings or provided nuanced additional details in-volved considerable subjectivity as it was difficult to determine how these differences would be reflected in the selected essays. The annotations therefore support the presence and approximate extent of interpretive diversity, while leaving its precise structure uncertain. This motivates the need for clarification to gain insight into what exactly users mean and which distinctions matter to them. Our proposed softness measure shows strong alignment with the recorded human judgments, and its design reflects observations from the formative study. Rather than identifying exact interpretations or fixed clusters, the algorithm estimates how distinct a sampled set of plausible interpretations is. Our results suggest that this sampled diversity provides a useful proxy for perceived softness within this setting. 

7.2 Attribute Construction 

Our exploratory user-study yielded more mixed results. Within AttriFy, recorded softness increased almost as frequently as it decreased within discussion topics, suggesting that continued conversation does not necessarily produce a steady progression towards an executable attribute. One participant (P3), for example, specified increasingly detailed and nuanced requirements for evidence and its integration while softness was shown to increase. A possible reason is that clarification exposes additional complexity: relationships, exceptions, and contextual distinctions can make a construct more explicit in theory without making it easier to compute. These additional requirements may admit several plausible implementations, leaving ambiguity about how to combine them into a coherent measurement. This reflects a more general limitation of free-form verbal question-answering. Although the system can identify unresolved aspects and guide further clarification, it cannot ensure that subsequent responses reduce softness. Participants may instead introduce additional distinctions that expand the construct and make the operationalization more difficult than before. We expected the baseline’s larger approximation gap to lead participants to accept fewer attributes, as translating less explored descriptions into executable functions would require more assumptions about their intent. The observed patterns did not support this expectation, raising questions about what acceptance actually tells us about attribute 

> Manuscript submitted to ACM

Interactive Modeling of Soft Attributes 21 construction. The execution analysis reinforces this concern as many functions returned zero for the majority of essays. Although sparsity does not make an attribute invalid, acceptance alone probably cannot establish whether it reflects the intended behavior. This may stem from reduced user attention during the evaluation of AI-generated content. As Beck et al. [7] demonstrate, people frequently over-rely on AI suggestions and accept flawed outputs rather than inspecting them independently. This is particularly the case when verification demands additional effort or when users hold a strong bias toward AI capabilities. Together, these findings highlight the need to distinguish recognizing an attribute as relevant from assessing whether its implementation measures what was intended. 

8 Limitations & Future Work 

Our findings highlight opportunities for interactive soft attribute construction, while leaving open questions about functional alignment, the evaluation of attribute sets, implicit signals and integration with preference elicitation. We discuss these limitations and directions for future work below. 

Assessing Functional Alignment — Our study measured participants’ perceptions and decisions, but did not in-dependently assess whether generated functions captured their intended preferences. A preliminary exploratory in-spection revealed discrepancies between participants’ stated preferences and accepted functions, but did not establish how common these discrepancies were or whether participants knowingly accepted them. Acceptance and revision counts therefore cannot be interpreted as direct measures of functional alignment. To address this limitation, we plan a follow-up annotation study with our collected data. Independent annotators would assess alignment between partici-pants’ stated preferences, the generated descriptions, and the underlying implementations, initially without access to condition labels or participants’ decisions. Comparing these assessments with acceptance and rejection behavior would help assess whether participants accepted functions that aligned with their stated intentions. However, it should be noted that this analysis alone would not establish how critically participants assessed the outputs. Understanding their reasoning would require complementary evidence, such as retrospective interviews. 

Evaluating Attribute Sets — Assessing a final attribute set requires considering not only the attributes constructed, but also potentially relevant dimensions that remain unexplored. Users may not recognize these omissions until prompted by examples or questions, so self-reported satisfaction cannot establish adequate coverage of their interests. Behavioral findings are similarly difficult to interpret. Continued exploration may indicate discovery or difficulty, while moving on may indicate satisfaction or a desire to minimize effort. Our measures cannot confidently establish which of these explanations accounts for participants’ behavior. Therefore, future evaluations could look to use more concrete examples and downstream comparison tasks to reveal missing distinctions and examine how users revise their attribute sets. For long-form essays, such evaluations would require additional time or focused comparisons of selected items. Tracking these revisions alongside users’ explanations could help distinguish newly articulated interests from changes in what they consider important. 

Implicit Attribute Construction — The potential user over-reliance on AI outputs motivates a shift toward implicit preference inference, where systems infer constraints directly from natural dialogue rather than relying on upfront validation steps. In a potential adaptation of our workflow, our interface could passively track the ongoing dialogue to compile and test candidate Python functions in the background. By using each conversational turn to refine which functions hold, the system infers underlying preferences implicitly, avoiding real-time cognitive friction or the risk of uncritical acceptance of suggestions. While prior work has explored capturing feedback through targeted questioning [14, 34], structuring free-form dialogue so that routine responses yield reliable functional specifications remains an open challenge. Integrating Preferences — We scoped this work to attribute construction as a foundation for downstream preference elicitation, establishing the dimensions along which users can express likes and dislikes. However, users’ descriptions may already convey both an attribute of interest and a preferred direction or value. Treating these descriptions solely as input to attribute construction leaves this preference information unused. Future work could jointly support attribute construction and preference elicitation, allowing users to inspect their combined effects through item rankings or recommendations. These outputs could make the consequences of their specifications easier to assess than attribute definitions alone. For example, a user may initially favor adjective-rich essays but reconsider after inspecting highly ranked examples. Such feedback could motivate revisions to the attribute definition, the preference expressed over it, or both. This could also help address the attribute set evaluation limitation discussed above. 

9 Conclusion 

Before modeling what users like or dislike, it is necessary to establish the attribute dimensions along which their prefer-ences can be meaningfully expressed. We introduced AttriFy, a system for interactive construction of soft attributes from textual corpora. Our approach uses softness to characterize the space of plausible attribute interpretations and guide an iterative workflow of expanding, constraining, and steering. By combining conversational guidance, executable attribute functions, and visual inspection, AttriFy enables users to develop their intended meanings and examine how they are translated into computational representations. We view this work as a step toward tools that support users in shaping the dimensions through which they understand and compare data, while retaining control over how those dimensions are defined and computed.

A Softness Study A.1 Softness Scale 

Participants received the definition of softness as the extent to which a description admits multiple valid interpretations without further specification. The following anchors accompanied the five-point scale. (1) Not soft . One interpretation. Anyone applying the description would arrive at the same reading. (2) Slightly soft . One clear interpretation, with minor variation in details that would rarely change the outcome. (3) Moderately soft . A few defensible interpretations, roughly two or three, with one of them somewhat more natural than the rest. (4) Very soft . Several defensible interpretations, none of them clearly dominant. (5) Extremely soft . Many defensible interpretations, spread widely, with no natural starting point. 

A.2 Study Details 

Participants were recruited through Prolific with eligibility restricted to English fluency and university-equivalent education. The three tasks occurre dn a fixed order. Participants first interpreted ten descriptions, then rated ten descriptions and ranked eight triplets, and finally rated five dialogues over five successive turns. Interpretations were elicited before introducing the softness definition. No participant interpreted and individually rated the same description. This separation did not extend to the individual-rating and ranking tasks. Patticipants were compensated at a hourly rate of 12$ (9£) with the experiment advertised at an estimated duration of 30min. 

A.2.1 Attention Checks and Exclusions. The analysis retained 62 of 70 recorded sessions after removing seven incomplete sessions and one duplicate. All 62 retained sessions passed both attention checks. Our attention check in the interpretation task asked participants to answer “yes” in a particular field, while the ranking check explicitly marked three descriptions as most, medium and least soft and required participants to place them in the corresponding order. Completeness checks required the expected number of distinct items and valid responses in all three tasks, including all five ratings for each assigned dialogue. The retained data comprise 620 interpretations, 620 individual ratings, 496 triplet rankings and 1,550 turn ratings from 310 dialogue viewings. Across the 50 descriptions, individual ratings numbered 8–22 per description (median 11). Each triplet contributes three pairwise comparisons, giving 1,488 comparisons in total. 

A.3 Stimuli 

The pool contained 50 essay-attribute descriptions and 20 five-turn clarification dialogues. Candidate stimuli were generated using Claude Opus 4.8 (June 2026), and manually screened by the authors to remove duplicates and implausible user descriptions. Constructed softness degrees and dialogue classes express design intentions, rather than human measurements or exact numerical predictions of subsequent ratings.

A.4 Coding Details 

Two coders independently grouped the responses to each description by their operational meaning. Wording differences alone did not warrant a separate group. Responses belonged together when they implied equivalent criteria for deciding which essays satisfied the description. The coding guidance instructed coders to start a group with the first response and consider subsequent responses against the groups already formed. A response warranted a new group if it introduced a different idea or committed to a particular procedure that an existing group left open. Unique responses, and responses too vague to assign to an existing group, received separate groups. Responses completely unrelated to the description were marked as outliers. Coders could record notes about difficult decisions. The coders flagged 19 and 16 responses as unrelated, with seven overlapping flags. C User Study C.1 Participant Details 

We recruited 41 participants through Prolific, of whom 32 completed both conditions and passed the attention check, and are included in the analysis. The 32 participants (17 male, 15 female) were predominantly aged 25–34 (53%, 𝑛 = 17), with seven aged 18–24, three aged 35–44, two aged 45–54, one aged 55–64, and two aged 65 or older. C.2 Procedure 

After consent and background questions, participants completed a general tutorial about translating preferences into attributes, using movies as a separate example domain. A comprehension check asked the main purpose of the tool, with the intended answer “To help you identify and articulate what you value in a good essay.” The local protocol routes other answers to an attention-failure exit. Tool-specific checks explain that accepting a card indicates relevance to one’s preferences, rather than verification that the measurement is correct. Before each block, participants received the assigned dataset introduction and tool tutorial. T he opening question 

Manuscript submitted to ACM 34 Holter et al. was fixed by essay genre and shared across conditions. 

Argumentative essays. What makes an argumentative essay good in your view? Describe something that matters to you in your own words. 

Narrative essays. What makes a narrative essay good in your view? Describe something that matters to you in your own words. Participants were instructed to work for the full 15-minute block while a timer displayed the remaining time. They could articulate preferences and review proposed attributes. Before the second block, the protocol asked them to develop different attributes for the new collection instead of reusing their first list. At the end of each block, participants reviewed their attributes and answered the condition questionnaire, a task-completion question and a written reflection. The protocol also includes six workload ratings and, when a rejected proposal was available, a replay asking why it had been rejected. They then stated their preferred tool, explained their choice and compared what they could express with each tool.
