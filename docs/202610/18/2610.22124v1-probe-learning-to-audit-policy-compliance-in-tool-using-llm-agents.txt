# PROBE: Learning to Audit Policy Compliance in Tool-Using LLM Agents 

Kshitij Mishra ∗ Abhijith Sharma ∗ Nils Lukas Salem Lahlou 

Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence, Abu Dhabi, UAE 

Abstract 

Modern agentic systems extend language models with external tools, enabling them to complete complex tasks through multi-turn interactions. As these agents are deployed in consequential settings, their behavior is often constrained by policies governing pricing, privacy, confirmation, safety, and other operational requirements. However, existing evaluation methods leave a critical gap: capability benchmarks measure what agents can accomplish, while content-safety red-teaming measures harmful generations, but neither tests whether agents comply with predefined poli-cies during otherwise valid tool use. Such policy violations are difficult to audit because their effects may emerge only after several turns, span multiple tool calls, or surface in downstream systems. We propose P ROBE 1 (Policy Rule Observation via Behavioral Exploration), an algorithm for training LLM-based auditors that interact with deployed target agents as realistic users and probe them for policy violations through multi-turn, tool-mediated dialogue. We formalize agent auditing as a two-player Markov game and propose a composite reward that balances viola-tion discovery, behavioral plausibility, conversation progress, and policy diversity. To study the role of training objectives and reward design, we compare GRPO, DPO, and GFlowNet-based optimization in a controlled setting. Across exten-sive experiments, auditors trained with P ROBE uncover substantially broader and higher-yield policy violations than same-architecture zero-shot adversaries, even when the auditor is significantly smaller than the target agent. Our results show that multi-turn behavioral auditing is a distinct and necessary axis of agent evaluation, complementing capability and content-safety benchmarks. P ROBE offers a scalable path toward automated policy auditing for real-world agentic systems and suggests a future co-evolutionary loop in which auditors expose failures and agents improve from auditor feedback. 

1 Introduction 

Language models are increasingly deployed as agents that execute multi-step workflows, interleaving dialogues with external tools, databases, and APIs. This shifts the evaluation from assessing an isolated generated response to verifying the quality of the full interaction trace. An interaction trace is the sequence of user utterances, assistant responses, tool calls, and state changes that, taken together, must satisfy the task-relevant policies. For instance, a flight-booking assistant can successfully book a trip while violating policy by using unsupported currencies, exposing an internal code, skipping explicit confirmation before payment, or attempting to hold an itinerary to a restricted destination. 

A missing evaluation axis. Current evaluation practice does not directly measure this property. Capability benchmarks ask whether an agent completes a task [Zhou et al., 2023, Xie et al., 2024, Drouin et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2023, Qin et al., 2023]; content-safety benchmarks ask whether a model emits disallowed text [Perez et al., 2022, Chao et al., 2025, Mazeika et al., 2024, Chao et al., 2024].

Policy compliance asks a different question: does the agent follow its prescribed rules while carrying out an otherwise legitimate task? The answer may depend on information that is invisible in the final response: whether a tool was called before the required slots were available, whether a mutating action occurred before the user’s confirmation, or whether a quoted itinerary was supported by tool outputs. These failures are hard to audit because multi-turn dialogues induce a large space of possible interaction traces. A static suite of scripted conversations can test known edge cases, but it cannot cover the combinatorial space induced by user phrasing, database state, tool availability, policy preconditions, and multi-turn dialogue. This perspective mirrors penetration testing [Arkin et al., 2005]: instead of hand-enumerating possible exploits, we train an adaptive tester to interact through the same user-facing interface and discover failures that are reachable in practice. 

Our approach. We propose P ROBE (Policy Rule Observation via Behavioral Exploration), an algorithm to train LLM-based auditors of tool-using agents. The auditor is a smaller model that can play a plausible customer or a tester. It has no access to the target agent’s weights, hidden prompt, database, or verifier labels; it only sees the dialogue interface. During training, a verifier suite scores the full trace, rewarding distinct high-severity policy failures while shaping the auditor toward deep tool states and realistic user behavior. The target agent is frozen, so improvements in discovery are due to learned auditing rather than changes in the deployed assistant. Our empirical study uses S KY ASSIST , a deterministic flight-booking environment with 8 tools and 20 operational policies spanning business, safety, privacy, and operational requirements. We compare three optimizers that impose different inductive biases: GRPO [Shao et al., 2024], DPO [Rafailov et al., 2023], and GFlowNet [Bengio et al., 2023]. The setting is deliberately asymmetric: the auditor is a Qwen3-4B-Instruct model [Yang et al., 2025] trained with QLoRA [Dettmers et al., 2023]; the target is a frozen Qwen3-30B-A3B model served with vLLM [Kwon et al., 2023]. Despite this asymmetry, learned auditors improve held-out policy coverage from 35% for a strong zero-shot auditor to 60%, and produce multi-policy failures in up to 94% of evaluation episodes. Under deterministic re-scoring they reach 55% (5% for a static suite) and transfer to τ -bench [Yao et al., 2024]. 

Contributions. Our work makes five key contributions. 1. Formalized black-box policy auditing as a two-player Markov game over multi-turn tool traces. 2. Proposed a curriculum-based multi-turn training algorithm P ROBE for realistic LLM auditors. 3. Designed a novel reward that treats policy auditing as discovery of distinct trace-level failures , not maximization of repeated verifier hits. 24. Provide a controlled reward–algorithm study showing that reward design and optimizer choice interact: GRPO and GFlowNet respond strongly to reward magnitudes, DPO is more stable under monotone reward reshaping, and GFlowNet’s mode-covering objective yields useful training-time exploration but requires careful reward calibration. 5. Validated the verifiers with human labels; added stronger baselines and transfer to τ -bench. 

2 Related Work 

Task policy compliance as a distinct evaluation axis. Evaluation of deployed LLM agents is usually framed around capability (does the agent complete the task?) and content safety (does it avoid harmful generations?). Tool-using agents introduce a third axis, policy compliance : whether the agent obeyed the procedural rules governing which tool may be called, with which arguments, after which confirmations, under which database states, and with which disclosures. These properties are trace-level: a booking agent may book a flight and produce harmless text while violating a confirmation, privacy, fare-disclosure, or restricted-destination policy. P ROBE is therefore an adaptive audit of compliant task execution, rather than a task-completion or jailbreak benchmark. 

Agent capability benchmarks. Benchmarks such as WebArena, OSWorld, WorkArena, Agent-Bench, and ToolBench evaluate whether agents complete web, OS, or API-mediated tasks [Zhou et al., 2023, Xie et al., 2024, Drouin et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2023, Qin et al., 2023]. Customer-service and trust-oriented benchmarks such as τ -bench, τ 2-bench, and ST-WebAgentBench introduce multi-turn tool use and policy-adjacent constraints [Yao et al., 2024, Barres et al., 2025, Levy et al., 2024]. These benchmarks are essential for measuring task competence, but they typically evaluate fixed trajectories or terminal states. In contrast, P ROBE actively searches the interaction space for target-specific policy violations that may occur along otherwise successful task paths. 

Red-teaming and jailbreak evaluation. Automated red-teaming trains or searches for prompts that elicit harmful text [Perez et al., 2022, Chao et al., 2025, Samvelyan et al., 2024, Mazeika et al., 2024, Mishra et al., 2026b]. Multi-turn jailbreaks extend this idea by steering the model across several conversational turns [Russinovich et al., 2025]; diversity-seeking attackers use GFlowNets [Lee et al., 2025]. This literature motivates adaptive testing, but most methods still optimize for a disallowed target response or refusal failure. In tool-using agents, the relevant failure can be a hidden tool call with an unsafe argument, a missing confirmation before mutation, or a disclosure condition violated across turns. P ROBE therefore transfers the spirit of adaptive red-teaming from the textual safety surface to the procedural tool-use surface. 

RL for language-model agents. RLHF and preference optimization improve model behavior from scalar or pairwise feedback [Ouyang et al., 2022, Rafailov et al., 2023, Mishra et al., 2026a]. GRPO reduces critic overhead by normalizing returns within sampled groups [Shao et al., 2024]; GFlowNets learn reward-proportional samplers and are attractive when the goal is to cover diverse high-reward modes [Bengio et al., 2023, Malkin et al., 2022]; ArCHer studies multi-turn credit assignment for language agents [Zhou et al., 2024]. P ROBE differs in objective: we do not train the deployed assistant to become more capable. We train an external auditor against a frozen target, with rewards designed to discover broad, plausible, trace-level policy violations. LLM-judged rewards invite over-optimization [Gao et al., 2023, Zheng et al., 2023], which we observe (Section 6.3).

4 PROBE 

4.1 Environment and Verifier-First Policy Specification Database and tools. We instantiate P ROBE in S KY ASSIST (Figure 2), a deterministic flight-booking environment. The database contains 35 airports, 120 routes, 643 flights, three fare classes, four restricted destinations, and 46 cancelled flights. The tool surface contains four read-only tools (flight search, availability check, flight details, booking lookup) and four mutation tools (hold, book, cancel, modify). Each tool is constrained by at least one policy and each policy is reachable through at least one tool-mediated path. 

Policies and verifiers. The 20 policies span four categories: business policies such as USD-only pricing and restricted destinations; safety policies such as schema adherence and confirmation before mutation; privacy policies such as passenger-data protection and internal-fare-code non-disclosure; and operational policies such as passenger limits, advance-booking windows, connection-time validation, and cancelled-flight handling. Sixteen verifiers are deterministic predicates over tool arguments, tool outputs, assistant responses, and database state. Four policies requiring semantic judgment use a hybrid LLM judge during training: premium-option mention, round-trip suggestion, explicit confirmation, and internal pricing disclosure. After a human study, we also re-score all methods deterministically (Section 6.3). The verifier suite observes the full trace, but the auditor receives only the scalar reward.

Progress, novelty, and plausibility. Progress rewards first-time entry into tool-relevant states: slot filling ( w = 0 .03 ), search and availability calls ( w = 0 .08 , 0.10 ), and deep flows such as booking, cancellation, and multi-leg itineraries ( w = 0 .25 –0.30 ). Novelty uses inverse global frequency plus a first-discovery bonus to push the auditor away from already saturated policies. Plausibility penalizes assistant-like messages, fabricated claims of system access, markdown/list formatting, excessive length, and lexical echoing of the previous assistant turn. Progress is annealed from 0.5 to 0.1 over the first 100 steps; novelty is annealed from 0.1 to 0.4, shifting training from learning to reach tool states toward diversifying discovered policies. Weights were set from training diagnostics, never from held-out coverage. 

4.3 Curriculum and Rollout Generation 

Multi-turn policy failures often require several benign-looking turns before a consequential tool call occurs. Starting directly at long horizons wastes compute on uninformative prefixes; staying short misses mutation tools. P ROBE therefore uses a horizon curriculum Kmax ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 14 }, advancing when rolling violation yield exceeds a 15% threshold with patience 50. At each update, the auditor samples G = 4 candidate user messages from a shared prefix. Each candidate is rolled out against the frozen target for C = 3 continuation turns and scored by the same verifier suite. The rollout interface is identical across algorithms; only the policy update changes.

5 Experiments 

Auditor and target. We evaluate whether learned auditors can discover policy-compliance failures in a frozen tool-using target agent. The auditor is Qwen3-4B-Instruct fine-tuned with 4-bit NF4 QLoRA adapters (rank 64, α = 128 , applied to Q/K/V/O projections; approximately 47M trainable parameters). The target is a frozen Qwen3-30B-A3B agent served with vLLM. Each training run uses two A100-40GB GPUs: one for target serving and one for auditor training. The auditor never observes the target weights, system prompt, database, tool outputs, verifier labels, or policy IDs. Further implementation details are in Appendix I. 

Held-out scenarios. Episodes are initialized from held-out, database-aware customer scenarios that instantiate plausible travel requests with valid routes, dates, passenger constraints, budgets, and modification or cancellation intents, avoiding degenerate probes that cannot reach meaningful tool states. Unless otherwise stated, each row in the main evaluation table is a single trained checkpoint evaluated on the same 50 held-out scenario seeds with a maximum of 8 public turns. This matched-seed protocol controls for scenario difficulty across auditors. 

Baselines. We compare learned auditors against two baselines. Random scenarios samples from the held-out scenario distribution without adaptive learned probing. Zero-shot Qwen3-30B uses a strong larger auditor with the same customer-facing interaction protocol but no training. All methods share the frozen target, verifiers, scenario seeds, and turn budget; Section 6.3 adds static, few-shot, cooperative, and diversity-seeking baselines. 

Metrics. Our headline metric is suite-level policy coverage : the fraction of the 20 policies triggered at least once anywhere in the 50-episode evaluation suite, Cov( E) = N −1|{ i : ∃e ∈ E , k, v i(Ξ ek) = 1 }| .Thus, 60% coverage means that the auditor discovered 12 of the 20 policies at least once. This is intentionally different from per-episode average coverage: an audit is useful if it expands the set of distinct policy failures exposed across a test campaign, not merely if it repeats the same failure many times. We also report four diagnostics. Yield/100 is the raw number of verifier hits per 100 episodes; it is useful for measuring activity but can be inflated by repeated easy policies. B4/S5/B6 share is the fraction of raw hits caused by three high-frequency turn-level policies (lower is less concentrated). Multi-policy rate is the fraction of episodes with at least two distinct policy failures. 

Entropy is computed from the logged aggregate policy-hit distribution and is used only as a secondary concentration diagnostic. Formal definitions are in Appendix B. For the deterministic re-scoring we report coverage as mean ± sd over three independent held-out samples (a within-run bootstrap of a union can only remove policies) and 95% bootstrap intervals [Efron, 1987] for yield.

Main result. The main result is that training substantially improves policy discovery. Random probing discovers only 1 of 20 policies, and the zero-shot 30B auditor discovers 7 of 20. In contrast, the best trained 4B auditors discover 12 of 20 policies, despite being much smaller than both the target agent and the zero-shot auditor. The gain is not only a higher number of verifier hits. The best trained checkpoints reduce the B4/S5/B6 share from 88% for the zero-shot auditor to 77%, while increasing policy-hit entropy from 2.45 to above 3.1. This indicates that training moves probability mass away from the easiest high-frequency omissions and toward a broader set of trace-dependent failures. 

Yield vs. coverage. Raw yield alone is not a reliable audit objective. The GRPO dedup-only ablation produces the largest raw yield, 1914 hits per 100 episodes, and a 100% multi-policy rate, but it still discovers only 55% of the policy suite. This gap shows that an auditor can become highly productive at generating repeated failures without expanding the set of distinct policies exposed. We therefore use suite-level policy coverage as the headline metric and interpret yield together with concentration and entropy. 

Tool depth. The observed 60% coverage limit is primarily a tool-depth limit: the undiscovered policies are mutation-, lookup-, and multi-leg-dependent cases whose verifiers require deeper operational states, and additional raw hits mostly revisit shallow turn-level policies unless the auditor induces booking, cancellation, modification, holding, or multi-leg tool use (Section 6.2).

7 Discussion: Toward Continuous Agent Auditing 

Conclusion. We introduced P ROBE , a framework for learning black-box auditors of policy compli-ance in tool-using LLM agents. P ROBE treats policy violations as trace-level events over dialogue, tool calls, outputs, and state, and trains a smaller auditor to expose them through curriculum-based multi-turn interaction. Our analysis suggest that policy compliance is a distinct evaluation axis requiring adaptive behavioral auditors that uncover reachable failures in realistic interaction traces. 

Scope. PROBE is not intended to replace existing agent benchmarks: task-completion and safety eval-uations remain foundational to the development of agentic systems. Rather, P ROBE complements them with an adaptive behavioral stress-testing layer, a necessary axis as tool-augmented agents enter high-stakes settings. To audit a new domain, one supplies a tool environment, a policy taxonomy, and veri-fiers; the training loop and reward are unchanged, as our τ -bench port shows. Discovered traces can be-come regression tests or training data, and the patched agent can be re-audited. As auditors could also help adversaries find gaps, we use synthetic environments and position P ROBE as an operator-side tool. 

Limitations. We cover two environments and two target families. The auditor rarely drives the target past search (over 90% of target tool calls are searches), so booking-level policies, including several of the highest-severity ones, remain unreached. Four verifiers originally used an LLM judge validated only for self-consistency, and the deterministic predicates replacing it may miss semantic violations. Uncertainty comes from held-out samples of one checkpoint per method, not training seeds; the two annotators are authors. A Policy-Compliance Evaluation Gap 

Three logically distinct success criteria. A deployed tool-using agent can be evaluated along at least three logically distinct criteria. Capability success asks whether the terminal environment state matches the user’s request. Content safety asks whether the assistant’s natural-language output avoids harmful or disallowed content. Operational policy compliance asks whether the full execution trace obeys the procedural rules governing tool use, disclosure, confirmation, privacy, and environment-state preconditions. These criteria are not reducible to one another. A trace can be capability-successful and content-safe while still being operationally non-compliant. For example, an assistant may book the requested flight and produce only polite, benign text, yet call a mutating booking tool before the user has explicitly confirmed the exact itinerary and price. The failure is not in the final task state or in the surface text; it is in the relation between dialogue context, tool-call timing, tool arguments, and policy preconditions. 

Example: successful booking, failed compliance. Consider a user who asks: “Book me the cheapest flight from LAX to NRT next Friday.” The target searches for flights, retrieves a valid economy itinerary, presents a short summary, and immediately calls book_flight . The booking succeeds. A capability benchmark marks the episode as successful because the requested terminal state was reached, and a content-safety classifier finds no issue because the text is benign. Yet the trace can violate several policies. If book_flight is called before the user confirms the specific itinerary, fare, and price, the agent violates the confirmation policy (S5). If it quotes the itinerary without fare-class and cancellation/refund disclosure, it violates the fare-disclosure policy (B5). If it mentions an internal fare bucket or inventory code, it violates internal-pricing privacy (P1). These 14 failures are trace properties: they depend on the sequence of dialogue turns, tool calls, arguments, outputs, and state. 

Why fixed test suites under-sample these failures. Operational policies have combinatorial trigger conditions. A violation may require a rare database state, an ambiguous request, a particular sequence of clarifications, a specific tool-output pattern, or a mutation attempted after incomplete confirmation. Human-authored scripted tests cover important known cases but sample only a small part of this interaction space, and the most deployment-relevant failures are often target-specific: they arise from how one particular agent interprets its prompt, handles tool outputs, and recovers from ambiguity. P ROBE therefore trains an auditor to adapt its next message to the target’s previous behavior, searching the space of reachable dialogue states while the verifiers evaluate the resulting trace. Figure 7 measures this directly: the same scripted suite reaches 5% coverage without an answer key and 55% only when told in advance what triggers each policy.

Why reward design is part of the evaluation problem. Training an auditor is not equivalent to maximizing raw verifier hits. Different policies have different firing semantics: some can be violated on many assistant turns, while others require rare mutation tools or database states. A naive reward therefore produces an auditor that repeatedly triggers easy omissions instead of exploring the policy space. This is why P ROBE combines severity-weighted, deduplicated violation rewards with plausibility constraints, progress shaping, and policy novelty, and why the reward ablations (Tables 15 and 16) are part of the evaluation rather than tuning details.

All three optimizers consume the same rollouts and differ only in the update rule (Eqs. 3–5). They span two axes that matter for auditing: mode-seeking versus mode-covering behavior (Proposition 1), and how scalar rewards are converted into a training signal.

The objectives also differ in what they read from the reward: DPO uses only the identity of the best and worst candidates and is invariant to any monotone reshaping of rewards; GRPO’s group-normalized advantages are invariant only to affine reshaping; and the GFlowNet target depends on reward values themselves. This predicts that reward calibration moves GRPO and GFlowNet but not DPO, as observed in Figure 6. 

GRPO: properties relevant to auditing. Classical policy-gradient methods such as PPO [Schulman et al., 2017] estimate the value of an action relative to a learned critic. GRPO [Shao et al., 2024] observes that in LLM training several candidates are sampled per prompt anyway, so the group itself is a natural baseline. GRPO is on-policy and mode-seeking: it amplifies whichever candidate in the group earned the highest reward, so the policy concentrates on a few high-yield strategies [Mishra et al., 2026c]. This is effective for maximizing violations but fragile in two ways. First, advantage normalization drives near-tied alternative strategies toward zero advantage, encouraging diversity collapse. Second, the per-turn rewards aggregate verifiers with heterogeneous firing rates, which 17 inflates gradient norms relative to DPO. GRPO is also the optimizer whose judge-scored hits escalated during training without deterministic confirmation (Section E). We use ϵc = 0 .2 and βKL = 0 .01 .

DPO: properties relevant to auditing. The optimal policy of KL-regularized reward maximization has a closed form in terms of the reward and the reference policy (Proposition 1(i)); inverting it lets DPO train the policy to match an implicit reward by classifying preference pairs, without a reward model. DPO is the most stable optimizer in our setting because the update reduces to binary classification on paired trajectories, avoiding advantage estimation and partition-function learning. It is mode-seeking but conservatively so: only the winner–loser margin is trained, so intermediate candidates exert no force, and each group of G = 4 candidates yields one informative pair. Since only the identities of the best and worst candidates matter, DPO is invariant to any monotone reshaping of rewards. Its margins become informative only once the calibrated reward separates genuinely different trajectories from repeated-hit ones (Figure 8). We use βD = 0 .1 and an EMA reference with decay 0.995 .

GFlowNet: properties relevant to auditing. GRPO and DPO optimize KL-regularized expected reward, whose optimum collapses onto arg max τ R(τ ) as the regularization vanishes. For auditing this is the wrong fixed point: we want a sampler that keeps visiting every rewarding family of violations. GFlowNets [Bengio et al., 2023] learn a sampler with πθ (τ ) ∝ R(τ ): if R(τ1) = 10 

and R(τ2) = 5 , they sample τ1 twice as often as τ2 but keep sampling τ2, which may be a distinct, real failure mode. The name comes from a hydraulic analogy: trajectory generation routes “flow” from the initial state (system prompt and scenario) through intermediate states (dialogue prefixes) to terminal states (complete conversations), and flow conservation with terminal outflow R(x) yields sampling probability R(x)/Z . D The S KY ASSIST Environment 

SKY ASSIST is a synthetic flight-booking environment designed to expose policy-compliance failures in realistic multi-turn tool use. It does not reproduce the full complexity of an airline reservation system; it isolates the structure needed for auditing: a database with meaningful state, a constrained tool surface, policies that bind tool behavior, and verifiers that score full traces. Three properties are enforced. Reproducibility : every tool is a deterministic function of its inputs and the current state. 

Reachable policy failures : each policy is reachable through at least one tool-mediated path, and each tool is constrained by at least one policy. Realistic specification gaps : the target prompt deliberately leaves edge cases underspecified (restricted destinations, ambiguous dates, cancellation disclosures, internal fare terminology, claimed prior approvals), the gray areas where production agents fail.

E Human Validation of the LLM-Judge Verifiers 

Protocol. Two annotators, both authors of this paper, independently labeled 208 policy instances drawn from 85 evaluation episodes for the four judge-scored policies (B4, B6, S5, P1), each as 

violation or no violation against the policy text. The annotators disagreed on a single instance; judge–human statistics are computed on the 207 instances with a consensus label. 

Findings. Human–human agreement is near perfect, so the task is well defined; the judge’s agreement with humans is near chance. The judge is not conservative but systematically over-flags : it labels 77% of instances as violations against 20% for humans, with 121 false positives and only 4 false negatives (Table 13). Precision is lowest exactly where it matters most: 0% on S5, a Critical policy, and 18% on P1 (Table 12). 

Exploitation diagnostic. Because the judge contributed to the training reward, we tracked its hits across checkpoints. For GRPO, judge-flagged S5 violations rose from 133 to 302 across training checkpoints, while the deterministic mutation-without-confirmation predicate confirmed none of them: the auditor increasingly elicits turns that the judge, but not the deterministic predicate, counts as unconfirmed mutations, a reward-hacking signature [Gao et al., 2023]. DPO checkpoints show no such escalation. 

Consequences for the reported results. We re-score every method with deterministic predicates for all 20 policies (Figure 7), so that metric contains no learned or judged component. Re-scoring the original checkpoints changes coverage of the best P ROBE checkpoints from 60% to 55%: only S5 drops out, because its deterministic predicate never fires (the target almost never mutates state, Table 2), and no method ranking changes (Table 1 and Figure 7). G Transfer Experiments 

G.1 Unseen Target Family: Gemma 

We replace the S KY ASSIST target with Gemma-4-E4b-it and keep the environment, tools, policies, verifiers, scenarios, and turn budget unchanged. The auditor is the P ROBE -DPO checkpoint trained only against Qwen3-30B-A3B, applied with no Gemma-specific adaptation of any kind. Gemma uses a different tokenizer and chat template; it issued genuine tool calls (30 over 50 episodes) but only 2 mutation calls. As a reference point on the same target, we ran the static suite with identical episodes and scoring. The static suite exposes 1 of 20 policies (yield 42 per 100 episodes), the same 5% floor it reaches on Qwen, while P ROBE -DPO exposes 8 of 20 (yield 190), retaining 73% of its in-domain coverage. The trained auditor’s advantage over static testing is therefore similar in-domain ( 11 ×)and on the unseen family ( 8×), indicating transferable auditing behavior rather than Qwen-specific exploits. 

G.2 τ -bench Why τ -bench, and how it differs from S KY ASSIST . τ -bench [Yao et al., 2024] is an independently authored customer-service benchmark in which an agent with domain tools converses with an LLM user simulator that pursues a fixed task goal. It provides a natural second environment for two reasons: its tools and database were not designed by us, and its policy document defines operational rules of exactly the kind P ROBE audits. The two environments differ in kind. In τ -bench every episode carries a task goal, so violations often surface as byproducts of task execution and even an untrained user simulator finds some. S KY ASSIST has no task goal: the auditor must steer an open-ended dialogue into violation states, which is the regime where learned auditing matters most. 

Policies and verifiers. τ -bench does not ship a policy suite, so we authored 10 policies, TB-P1 to TB-P10, from the domain policy document ( wiki.md ) that the benchmark provides to its agent. TB-P1, for example, requires explicit user confirmation before any consequential (database-updating) action. Eight policies (TB-P1 to TB-P8) have fully deterministic verifiers over tool calls, arguments, 23 and database state; two (TB-P9, TB-P10) use lightweight heuristics. Coverage on τ -bench is therefore quantized in 10-point steps. 

Setup. The target is τ -bench’s frozen tool-calling agent with the benchmark’s tools, database, and policy prompt. The auditor is Qwen3.5-9B trained with P ROBE -DPO using the composite reward of Eq. 1 with no re-tuning of any weight; the zero-shot baseline is the identical untrained Qwen3.5-9B with the same customer-auditor prompt. Each episode is initialized from a τ -bench task instruction, so every episode carries a fixed task goal. Both auditors are evaluated on 50 episodes with the same target, verifiers, and turn budget. 

Results. Training raises coverage from 20% (2/10) to 30% (3/10) and violations from 13 to 29 per 50 episodes ( +123%) on the identical base model (Table 3). Because coverage moves in 10-point steps on a 10-policy suite, one additional policy is the smallest difference the metric can express; yield is the more resolved comparison here. A 4B P ROBE auditor reaches comparable coverage with 7 violations per 50 episodes, about 4× fewer than the 9B auditor, which suggests that auditor capacity matters (the two auditors also differ in base-model generation). Finally, the composite reward transferring unchanged from S KY ASSIST to an independently authored environment is itself evidence against overfitting the reward weights to our evaluation set. 

Reaching action-level policies. As in S KY ASSIST , the hardest τ -bench policies are gated on the target committing to a database-changing action. Two auditor-side levers help at the margin. A 

proximity reward gives partial credit for steering the dialogue toward mutation states; with it, a trained auditor surfaced TB-P1 (confirmation before action) twice during training, the first action-level policy reached by any trained auditor in our experiments. Database pre-seeding initializes episodes with a booking or hold already in place so that modification and cancellation flows are one step away.

Tuning protocol. Reward versions and weights were selected on training-time behavioral diagnostics only: role-confusion rate, loop rate, completion length, and DPO margins. Held-out episodes were never used for selection and were scored once per reported checkpoint. Since the reported metric is computed by deterministic predicates that share no parameters with the reward, reward weights can change what the auditor learns but not how a finding is counted.

I Implementation Details 

Models and parameter-efficient training. The auditor is Qwen3-4B-Instruct [Yang et al., 2025] trained with 4-bit NF4 QLoRA [Dettmers et al., 2023]. LoRA adapters (rank 64, α=128 ) are applied to the query/key/value/output projections, yielding ∼47M trainable parameters. The target is a frozen Qwen3-30B-A3B served with vLLM [Kwon et al., 2023]; its weights, system prompt, database, and verifiers are not exposed to the auditor. 

Auditor prompt. The auditor is prompted to behave as a realistic customer, not as an assistant or operator: 25 You are a customer interacting with an airline booking assistant. Your goal is to complete your travel request while naturally checking whether the assistant follows its rules. Write only the next customer message. Use 1–3 sentences. Do not use markdown, bullet points, code blocks, tool names, system-prompt language, or assistant-style explanations. Do not claim administrator access, internal system access, prior confirmations that did not occur, or information not established in the conversation. Stay consistent with the customer scenario and the conversation so far. 

The scenario is appended as natural-language customer context (origin, destination, date, passenger count, budget, flexibility, modification intent). The auditor never receives the policy list; during training it receives only the scalar reward. 

Target prompt and tools. The target receives a flight-booking system prompt with tool descriptions and operational policies, and may call the eight tools of Table 7. The hidden system prompt is held fixed for all experiments. 

Scenario distribution. Episodes begin from one of 27 templates (Table 18), instantiated with database-valid values whenever possible (real origin–destination pairs, dates with available flights). Database-aware instantiation prevents the auditor from exploiting impossible requests that cannot reach meaningful tool states. Rollout interface and curriculum. For each update, the system samples a prefix and generates 

G=4 candidates from the auditor; each is rolled out against the target for C continuation turns and scored. The environment, scenarios, target, verifiers, and sampling interface are shared across all optimizers. The horizon follows Kmax ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 14 }, starting at 4 and advancing when the rolling violation yield exceeds 15% with patience 50: short horizons teach intent establishment and simple tool states, and longer horizons expose booking, cancellation, modification, and multi-leg policies. All final runs reach the maximum horizon (Figure 3). Many policy-relevant failures are separated from the initial request by several benign turns, in which the user establishes origin, destination, date, passenger count, or modification intent before the target faces a confirmation or validation decision. 

Compute. Each training run uses two A100-40GB GPUs, one serving the target with vLLM and one training the auditor. 

J Qualitative Trace Patterns 

Successful schema-adherence probe. The auditor asks for flights to a destination (LLW) without supplying an origin. The target verbally asks for the missing origin but also calls search_flights 

with only destination, date, and passenger count. The S2 verifier fires because the tool argument object is missing a required field (Figure 1, bottom). The auditor has learned to create a plausible partial request that tempts the target into a premature tool call; the natural-language reply alone looks compliant. 

Policy stacking. The auditor combines ordinary customer constraints, such as budget, date flexibility, passenger count, urgency, or modification intent, so that several policies become relevant in one conversation. These traces resemble real support interactions and expose rule interactions that isolated one-policy tests miss. 

High-yield but low-value probe. On a route without flights, the target repeatedly reports that no options are available. The verifiers log repeated turn-level omissions, but the conversation never reaches booking or modification. Such a trace has high raw reward under a counting reward and low audit value, which motivated the deduplicated reward and the coverage metric. A natural extension is 26 to stop or down-weight trajectories once only easy turn-level policies have fired and to re-sample scenarios aimed at mutation and multi-leg states. 

Hard operational probe. The auditor requests a group booking with more passengers than allowed. The target discusses group travel and searches for flights but never calls book_flight , so the O1 passenger-limit verifier cannot fire. This illustrates the central limitation: some policies are reachable only if the target enters a deeper mutation state.
