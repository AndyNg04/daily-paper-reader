Abstract —The rapid proliferation of autonomous AI agents in decentralized ecosystems challenges existing identity paradigms. While human identity is traditionally anchored in biological and/or self-asserted attributes and government credentials, AI agents lack a standardized definition of selfhood, creating critical gaps in trust and accountability. This paper introduces a novel framework for establishing Decentralized AI Identity rooted in Self-sovereign identity (SSI) principles. We propose a formal model that clearly distinguishes human users and AI agents, enabling distinct verification logic for each class while maintaining a unified identity calculus. The central to our contribution is the ABCD Framework, a multi-layered approach to agent identification and verification: (A) Algorithmic Fingerprint, which binds the identity to static model architectures and provenance; (B) Behavioural Fingerprint, which verifies dynamic performance metrics over time; (C) Cryptographic Identity, which anchors the agent via Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and (D) Delegation, which cryptographically binds agent actions to respective human accountability. By orthogonalizing these dimensions, we model how systems can enforce granular trust policies ranging from simple authentication to complex liability assignment, thereby solving the black box identity problem in the autonomous economic activities of AI agents. 

Index Terms —Decentralized Identity, Autonomous Agents, Blockchain, Accountability 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In contrast to the traditional generative models that are characterised as passive and request-response frameworks, Agentic AI systems are characterised by their ability to plan proactively, use advanced tools, and adapt to the environment [1], [2]. These systems act as autonomous principals rather than mere tools. They are dynamically sensitive to complex environments and reason about abstract objectives and organize progressively multi-step workflows to transform states without human intervention on a continuous basis [3], [4]. And now, imagine that in the future everybody has one or more AI agents, just like a digital assistant. Although the traditional concept of human identity is formed on the basis of physical embodiment and can be verified with the help of state-produced identification symbols (e.g., passports) or digital identification that can be verified (e.g., email), AI agents do not have any biological and legal basis [5]. Being computational entities, AI agents have neither physical sovereignty nor natural legal personhood [5]. This ontological gap is one of the critical issues in Multi-Agent systems. Specifically, we lack a deterministic way that is able to distinguish individual agents among the thousands of structurally identical agents. Although the autonomy of Agentic AI facilitates collective problem solving as well as efficiency [6], the basic detachment between actions and the short-term human intentions breaks down the current identity paradigms. The reason behind this undertaking is a latent ‘Identity Gap’ that is presently making decentralized agentic economies insecure. The existing systems have biological anchors (e.g., biometrics, KYC) or static infrastructure credentials (e.g., OpenID, OAuth tokens) [7], [8], that simply do not fit the nature of autonomous software. AI agents are temporary automated programs that can be forked and run at machine speed; they do not have a physical entity to be seen to, much as there is no legal entity to be held liable to. Since an agent may be conducting transactions of high value, or making very sensitive decisions, without a human observer, this creates an accountability vacuum because of the lack of a deterministic and cryptographically verifiable identity layer. Within such a vacuum, bad actors are able to run Sybil swarms of cloned or model-swapped agents to control markets, avoid reputation systems, and commit fraud with plausible deniability. Providing a clear and verifiable identity to algorithmic entities is thus not only an operational improvement, but a security requirement of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) [9]. Recent proposals have attempted to address these challenges through social governance or operational orchestration. How-ever, these frameworks predominantly treat agent identity as an extrinsic label attested by third parties or reputation scores. They lack the mechanisms to cryptographically bind an agent’s runtime execution to its static model architecture. Our work addresses these specific architectural deficiencies by introducing a Self-sovereign Identity (SSI) [10] based AI identity framework, called the ABCD Framework , which orthogonalizes identity into four verifiable dimensions and addresses the black box problem in reputation-based models [11]: Algorithmic Fingerprinting, Behavioural Fingerprinting, arXiv:2610.17126v1 [cs.CR] 12 Oct 2026 Cryptographic Identity and Delegated Liability. In particular, we make the following concrete, verifiable contributions:  

> •

A Formal, Domain-Aware Identity Model. We introduce a formal domain model that strictly differentiates human principals from autonomous AI agents.  

> •

The ABCD Framework. We present the ABCD Frame-work, a unified identity calculus that combines algorithmic provenance (code), behavioural integrity (performance), cryptographic anchoring (keys), and delegated liability (ownership).  

> •

Formal Security Guarantees. We formally state and prove three security theorems, grounded in explicit crypto-graphic and systems assumptions: Behavioural Coherence and Tampering Detection, Sybil and Impersonation Resis-tance, and Temporal Bounded Authorization Enforcement. II. B ACKGROUND 

Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI): SSI represents a paradigm shift in digital identity management that aims to empower users so that they can exert more control over their identity data [12]. There are three actors in an SSI ecosystem. An Issuer 

is an entity (e.g., a regulatory body or model auditor) that asserts cryptographically verifiable claims about a Subject in the form of a tamper-evident Verifiable Credentials (VCs). The 

Holder (Subject) is an entity that possesses and controls the credentials, typically stored within a secure digital wallet or agent framework. The Verifier is an entity that requests and receives VCs from the holder and validates them to authorize access or interactions to the holder. These interactions between these actors thus forms a trust triangle. 

Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs): DIDs are a globally unique identifier standardized by the W3C to enable verifiable, self-sovereign digital identity [13] [14]. Unlike traditional identifiers (e.g., email addresses) issued by centralized authorities, DIDs are created from a public key and owned by the subject controlling the respective private key [14]. Operationally, a DID is a URI that resolves to a DID Document, a data structure containing the subject’s public keys and service endpoints. This binding allows an entity to cryptographically prove ownership of its identity via digital signatures, decoupling authentication from centralized registries [15]. One might ask why agents cannot simply be assigned centralized identifiers such as emails, as in human accounts. Centralized issuance presupposes a trusted registrar and a persistent, singular endpoint. This is a poor fit for entities forked, cloned, or retired at machine speed across mutually distrusting platforms with no shared registrar; Definition 4’s uniqueness requirement is therefore satisfied only by self-certifying DIDs. 

Verifiable Credentials (VCs): VCs are portable, tamper-proof and cryptographically signed digital claims made about a subject [12] [13]. A credential is made of a set of individually verifiable claims. By leveraging VC, a subject can present the claim to a verifier when requested. 

Verifiable Data Registry (VDR): A VDR is another important component that is used to store DIDs, DID Docs, and credential schema and acts as a revocation registry. Generally, blockchain is used as a VDR because of its data immutability and decentralisation characteristics [16], enabling trustless credential validation without a central authority. 

Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) : MAS is the paradigm shift between monolithic computation to distributed intelligence [9]. A MAS is a computational system that consists of multiple interacting intelligent agents aimed at working together to meet individual or common goals [17]. These systems deal with complex problems by breaking them down into smaller manageable subtasks, each of which is delegated to a specific agent [9]. Unlike passive components, agents possess the inherent ability to learn from their environment and interactions, allowing them to make autonomous decisions to achieve their goals. 

Autonomous AI Agents/Agentic AI : AI Agents or Agentic AI systems are application systems built on large LLMs to solve a particular problem or a series of problems using AI [6]. Autonomous AI agents are AI agents that can work autonomously, even without human intervention [6]. However, current identity systems are not equipped to verifiably differen-tiate AI agents from humans, which can create vulnerabilities such as impersonation, sybil attacks, and unregulated economic behaviour. III. R ELATED WORK 

The problem of establishing trust in Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) is not new, but the emergence of autonomous economic agents (agents carrying financial operations, e.g., executing trades or transferring values, as distinct from purely advisory agents) has rendered existing paradigms insufficient. We categorize existing literature into three streams: general MAS security foundations, ethical governance frameworks, and zero-trust operational architectures. 

Security Challenges in MAS: Early research into MAS security established the fundamental difficulties of coordination and distributed trust. Dorri et al. [9] provided a comprehensive taxonomy of MAS challenges, highlighting that while agents offered flexibility and efficiency, they introduced severe vul-nerabilities regarding sociability and mobility. They identified that without a central trusted authority, the spread of falsified information by malicious agents could be easily propagated to influence decision making. Our work is based on the following foundational definitions of autonomy and sociability but expands the security model to consider the unique threats, like identity spoofing of AI agents in cyberspace. 

Ethical Consensus and Governance Layers: The attempts that have been made recently have tried to impose ethical limitations on agent behavior. The LOKA Protocol [11] suggests the use of a Universal Agent Identity Layer (UAIL) together with a Decentralized Ethical Consensus Protocol (DECP) to bring the agents abreast with human values by adopting a voting system. However, LOKA fundamentally treats the agent as a black box voter within a DAO structure rather than a verifiable computational entity. Though it makes use of reputation scores to authorize social behavior, it does not consider the Algorithmic Fingerprinting aspect (the ”A” in our model) at all. Critically, LOKA provides no cryptographic mechanism to ensure that an agent’s runtime execution matches its declared model architecture. Furthermore, LOKA attempts to resolve disputes via decentralized dispute resolution and consensus voting. In different applications, e.g. DeFi (Decentralized Finance) environments, a single malicious inference can drain a liquidity pool instantly. This reliance on post-hoc social arbitration is insufficient for real-time security. Our framework departs from this by requiring intrinsic cryptographic proofs of algorithmic provenance (Layer A) and delegated liability (Layer D) that pre-binds collateral to agent actions. 

Zero-Trust Architectures for Agentic AI: The most oper-ationally mature proposal to date is the Zero-Trust Identity Framework by Huang et al. [17]. They correctly identified that traditional OAuth/OIDC protocols were too coarse-grained for ephemeral, high-frequency agent workflows. Their contribution includes an Agent Name Service (ANS) [18] for discovery and a Unified Global Session Management layer that handles revocation across heterogeneous protocols. However, a critical security gap remains: they treated an agent’s underlying model merely as static metadata (a modelHash) within a DID Document, relying entirely on external Trusted Issuers to attest to capabilities via Verifiable Credentials (VCs). This creates a dependency on third party certification instead of intrinsic cryptographic binding. It also fails to prove the internal architecture and inference integrity of the agent at runtime. Furthermore, their model takes human users and AI agents into one single class of participants who are differentiated only by scores of trust. This absence of any formal partitioning rules prevents the use of different verification logic such as requiring biological liveness of human versus cryptographic model proofs of agents which is critical for Sybil resistance in open economic protocols. In our work, we tackle this issue by orthogonalizing these dimensions in order to have a unified identity calculus which strictly separates and distinguishes biological principals from algorithmic agents. 

Workload Attestation Standards: Industry standards such as SPIFFE/SPIRE [19] issue cryptographic workload identities (SVIDs) for cloud-native infrastructure, analogous in spirit to our Layer C. However, SPIFFE attests infrastructure identity (which node/container is running) rather than model identity (which weights are running) or behavioural conformance, and has no analogue to Layers A, B, or D. It is complementary to, not competing with, ABCD, and could serve as a transport-layer credential underneath Layer C.

Formally, no identity framework satisfies all the following three conditions simultaneously:  

> •

Entity Differentiation: The framework must strictly differentiate between human users( EU ) and autonomous agents( EAI ). It should also apply distinct verification protocols to each class rather than treating them as a unified set.  

> •

Identity Coherence: An agent’s cryptographic identity should all be intrinsically tied with their algorithmic state (ΦAlg (x), defined later) and verifiable runtime behaviour (ΦBeh (x, W ), defined later).  

> •

Accountable Delegation: Independent actions are to be held accountable to a responsible human principal through highly bounded authorization. The absence of these properties still exposes decentralized ecosystems to the Sybil attacks, threats of runtime tampering and unaccountable damages.

B. Threat Model 

The modeled threats are based on the STRIDE [20] frame-work, mapped to the proposed ABCD framework in the following: 

ST1 . Spoofing (Sybil Attacks): An adversary orchestrates many distinct, individually-valid agent identities (a Sybil swarm), or impersonates a single registered agent by presenting credentials it does not control. 

ST2 . Tampering (Model Swapping): An agent envelops an altered or malicious model variant at runtime. 

ST3 . Repudiation (Liability Evasion): A human owner does not claim responsibility for the actions of an autonomous agent. 

ST4 . Information Disclosure: The proprietary model weights or architecture information leaked. 

ST5 . Denial of Service (DoS): Malicious actors may create multiple AI agents, launching a form of Sybil attack, which may exhaust system resources (e.g., storage, bandwidth), rendering the services unavailable to other legitimate users. 

ST6 . Elevation of Privilege: An agent exceeds the scope of its delegated authority by invoking a permission it was not granted, spending beyond an assigned budget, or continuing to act beyond its legitimate time frame. V. F ORMAL AI I DENTITY MODEL 

In this section, we establish a formal AI Identity model for AI agents, grounded on the Digital Identity Model (DIM) [21]. Our model uses the set-theoretic calculus of the DIM and in particular, its formalization of entities, attributes and partial identities. We have extended the DIM model by introducing formal logic relevant for the orthogonal verification layers for the ABCD framework to ensure accountability for AI agents. To maintain clarity, we use a subscript in a notation to denote the domain and a superscript to specify the entity. For instance, 

EAI d represents the set of AI entities in domain d. Where the original DIM [21] targeted identity correlation for a single human user across centralized providers, our extension targets a materially different problem, correlating a non-human, forkable entity across mutually distrusting, permissionless domains with no shared provider. 

A. Entities and Domains 

This section formalizes the mathematical primitives underly-ing the ABCD framework through a domain-aware entity model (Definition 1) that distinguishes human users from AI agents, classifies attributes by their identifying capability (Definition 2), and builds identity constructs that support selective disclosure (Definition 3). 

Entities, Attributes, and Valuation: Identity statements are meaningful only with respect to a scope of interpretation. We call such a scope a domain . Within each domain we distinguish human users from AI agents while treating both uniformly as entities. This separation allows us to reason about accountability and automation without breaking the uniform calculus of identity. We do not suggest a redefinition of human digital identity or building the internet again. Human entities still authenticate to the existing, mature Web2/Web3 infrastructure (e.g., OAuth, or simple DIDs). The framework merely binds these already existing human identifiers to the new, four-layered identity of the autonomous agent cryptographically.

The ABCD framework organizes the identity of AI agents in four orthogonal dimensions, each can be verified independently, and these dimensions mutually reinforce each other. These layers are defined as restrictions or relations over various math-ematical primitives, which reflect a multi-faced nature of AI agent identity, where computational, behavioural, cryptographic, and social aspects all need to be checked for complete trust establishment. We present each of these layers in the following. 

A. Layer C: Cryptographic Identity 

Before algorithmic or behavioural attributes matter, we need a cryptographically secure anchor. Layer C provides unforgeable digital identity through public-key cryptography and decentralized identifiers. The cryptographic layer serves as the cornerstone of AI agent identity, establishing the fundamental mechanisms for authentication, non-repudiation, and secure communication. In contrast to human identity systems, where cryptographic components may be secondary, for AI agents, cryptographic identity acts as the main trust anchor. 

Definition 11 (Layer C: Cryptographic Identity) . The DID

The cryptographic layer includes not only the DIDs but also asso-ciated public keys, key fingerprints for compact identification, and the verification procedures that bind cryptographic proofs to identity assertions, allowing them to engage in activities requiring non-repudiation proof.

Operational Architecture 

Having defined the theoretical layers of the ABCD frame-work, we now envision the Operational Architecture required to implement them. As illustrated in Figure 2, the system is designed to support the full lifecycle of decentralized AI iden-tity through three primary tiers: Entities, Core Management Modules , and Decentralized Infrastructure . 

> Fig. 2. Operational architecture of the identity framework

1) Participating Entities: The architecture supports four different actors that are defined in our domain model:  

> •

AI Agents: These are autonomous entities which need to be registered and credentialed to engage in economic activity.  

> •

Human Users: These are principals who possess AI agents and delegate authority to them. They communicate mostly with help of the Delegation and Governance Management module in an attempt to articulate scope and liability.  

> •

Issuers: Trusted third parties (e.g., auditors, model ven-dors) which assert agent properties (e.g. model integrity or capabilities) through the Credential Lifecycle Management module.  

> •

Verifiers: External applications (e.g., DeFi protocols) typically that query the Verification Service to verify the credentials of agents before granting them access. 

2) Core Management Modules: The system logic is repre-sented by four components:  

> •

Identity Registration Management (IRM): The module manages the onboarding AI agents. It also acts as the gateway for both Layer A and Layer C where the raw model hash and architecture data are processed before the DID of the agent anchored to the blockchain.  

> •

Credential Lifecycle Management: The issuance, update, and revocation of the VCs are done by this module. It guarantees that dynamic attributes (Layer B) and static attestations are signed cryptographically and stored correctly.  

> •

Delegation & Governance Management: This module operationalizes Layer D, enabling human owners to estab-lish time-bounded, permission scoped rules of delegation. It links the DID of the human to the DID of the agent and gives a definite line of liability.  

> •

Verification Service: The module is the gatekeeper to the system. It combines the blockchain data with the decentralized data storage (i.e., IPFS) data to execute the multi-layered ABCD checks (coherence, impersonation, authorization) in real-time to the verifiers. 

Decentralized Infrastructure: To guarantee sovereignty and privacy, the architecture employs a dual-storage strategy:  

> •

Blockchain: Serves as the verifiable data registry (VDR), which offers an unalterable root of trust. It holds DIDs, public keys and cryptographic hashes (e.g. Model Hash, Policy Hash), however, never holds sensitive personal information or raw model weights.  

> •

Decentralized Storage: Used for off-chain storage of heavy or sensitive data, such as full DID Documents, and detailed Delegation Records. Data here is content-addressed and encrypted, referenced on-chain only by its respective identifier. 

G. Roadmap to Practical Implementation 

Although the ABCD framework proposes a new identity calculus of AI entities, it does not involve the replacement of current internet protocols. Instead, it is intended to be deployed as an application layer on top of Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI) stacks and W3C standards that are mature and battle-tested. To illustrate how a system designer can transition out of this conceptualized model to a practical deployment, we describe the technology stack necessary below:  

> •

AI Identity Sidecar (ACA-Py): Since most contemporary Agentic AI systems (e.g., LangChain [23], AutoGPT [24]) are written in Python, developers can implement the Core Management Modules (Figure 2) with Hyperledger Aries Cloud Agent Python (ACA-Py) [25]. ACA-Py is an open-source framework used to build SSI agent. It can expose REST APIs to facilitate SSI functionalities such as generating DIDs (Layer C), accepting Delegation VCs (Layer D) and interacting with other SSI agents to provide algorithmic and behavioral proofs (Layers A and B).  

> •

Bridging Human Authorization (OpenID4VC): The framework uses OpenID to Verifiable Credentials (OpenID4VC) [26] to cope with the operational challenge of human-to-agent delegation. This is a protocol that connects the traditional Web2 identity infrastructure (e.g., OAuth 2.0) to decentralized identity. It enables a human user to authenticate with standard, existing authentication techniques (e.g., biometric authentication on a mobile device) and issue a mathematically constrained Delegation VC (Layer D) to the wallet of their agent, facilitated by ACA-Py. To be precise, about scope, all core verification in Layers A–D (Definitions 10–17) relies solely on standard digital signatures per Definition 10. ZKPs, discussed next, are an optional, additive privacy layer, not a required primitive of the base framework.  

> •

Enterprise-Scale Infrastructure and ZKPs: The under-lying cryptographic primitives of this framework (W3C DIDs and VCs) are already being used to deploy large-scale systems. Enterprise-grade infrastructure, like the Mi-crosoft Entra Verified ID [27] or the European Blockchain Services Infrastructure (EBSI) [28] are examples of such infrastructure that use these primitives and can be used to anchor Layer C by both systems designers and other infrastructure providers. Moreover, in order to counter the privacy-transparency trade-offs discovered in Layer B, developers may incorporate Zero-Knowledge Proof (ZKP) [29] stacks like Privado ID [30]. This enables the AI agent to cryptographically demonstrate compliance with a performance policy ( π) to a DeFi verifier without the DeFi verifier seeing the underlying, proprietary operational data on which the proof was generated. With the coordination of these already existing, open-source technologies, e.g., ACA-Py and OpenID4VC, and widely-deployed infrastructures, the ABCD framework can be imple-mented instantly to achieve decentralized agentic economies. Assumption 1 (Collision Resistance) . The hash function H

Assumption 2 (Signature Unforgeability) . The digital signature scheme (Sign , Verify ) underlying checkProof (Definition 10) is Existentially Unforgeable under adaptive Chosen Message Attack (EUF-CMA) [32]. 

Assumption 3 (Identifier Injectivity) . The identifier function 

DID ∈ AC satisfies the injectivity condition of Definition 4; equivalently, idValToEnt is a well-defined, injective partial function.

Assumption 5 (Self-Certifying Identity Resolution) .

Self-certification : Each entity’s identifier value v ∈ AV AC is deterministically derived from the entity’s own public key at generation time (e.g. v = f (K) for a collision-resistant

Finality-gated reads : Any evaluation of idValToEnt (v) relied upon by a verifier is performed against the BFT-finalized state of the VDR (Assumption 4) at a confirmation depth beyond the network’s reorganization bound, never against a mutable local cache, an unconfirmed mempool entry, or an unverified resolver response.

The time variable t used in Authorized (u, AgentX , p, t ) and

Assumptions 1–3 are standard cryptographic hardness as-sumptions; Assumption 4 is the standard safety threshold for BFT ledgers (e.g., PBFT [34]) and is consistent with our system model (VI-F). Assumption 5 makes explicit two properties the paper already relies on informally (self-certifying DIDs, on-chain anchoring) so that Theorem 3’s guarantee cannot be defeated by attacking the read path to idValToEnt rather than the cryptography itself. Assumption 6 closes the gap between submission-time and finalized-time ordering that Theorem 4 otherwise leaves open to reordering attacks (Corollary 5). A further, model-specific hypothesis is stated locally within Theorem 1, since it concerns the statistical relationship between tampering and observed behaviour rather than a cryptographic hardness property.

IX. S ECURITY ANALYSIS 

In this section, we demonstrate how the ABCD Framework mitigates the specific threats identified in our Threat Model (Section IV-B). 

Spoofing/Sybil Attacks (ST1 .): We propose strict Entity Partitioning ( EAI d vs EUd ) and Cryptographic Proof of Control enforced by Layer C. 

Analysis: Traditional systems bind acceptance to key posses-sion alone via checkProof , with no independent provenance (Layer A) or behavioural (Layer B) check. A stolen, cloned, or freshly-generated key is therefore sufficient on its own to authenticate, regardless of how many other identities the same operator already controls. Our framework mitigates this by enforcing the Impersonation condition defined in Definition 16. The check checkP roof d(( iAI DID , v i), σ ) ensures that an attacker cannot simply replay a DID string. Furthermore, by rigorously partitioning the set of entities Eud ∩ EAI d = ∅, the model prevents the identity laundering where human credentials are used to spawn unaccountable bot swarms. 

Tampering/Model Swapping (ST2 .): We propose a be-havioural Coherence check that binds runtime metrics to Algorithmic Fingerprints ( ΦAlg (x)) via Layer A/B. 

Analysis : This addresses the black box vulnerability where a valid DID masks a malicious model. We enforce this with the security predicate Bound AI (x, W ) (Definition 15). If an agent attempts to swap its underlying model for a cheaper or malicious variant, the resulting runtime behaviour ΦBeh (x, W )

(e.g., inference latency or decision variance) will statistically deviate from the signed ΦAlg (x). This triggers a coherence failure ( π = f alse ). 

Repudiation/Liability Evasion (ST3 .): We propose non-repudiable links of delegation verified by Layer D. 

Analysis: To prevent human owners from disowning the economic damage caused by their agents, we utilize the ownership relation (did : ethr : Alice, AgentX ) ∈ Own .Because this link is cryptographically signed by the owner’s private key and anchored on the immutable VDR, it provides mathematical non-repudiation. An owner cannot retroactively deny the link after a catastrophic trade, as the chain of custody is preserved in the AD attributes. 

Information Disclosure (ST4 .): We propose On-chain hash commitments combined with encrypted Off-chain storage (e.g. IPFS). 

Analysis: We decouple verification from data visibility. The blockchain stores only the SHA-256 hashes of the model architecture ( modelHash ∈ AA), ensuring integrity. The sensitive weights and configuration files are stored off-chain. 

Denial of Service (ST5 .): We propose economic friction layers (e.g., gas fees) within Layer A. 

Analysis: DoS attacks rely on the cost-free generation of identities. By requiring on-chain registration for Layer A (Algorithmic Fingerprinting), we impose a financial cost (gas) on the creation of every new agent identity. This economic barrier makes high-volume spam registration prohibitively expensive for attackers. 

Elevation of Privilege (ST6 .): We propose cryptographically enforced authorization constraints ( AD ) via Layer D. 

Analysis : In this model, privilege elevation manifests as exceeding delegated scope along any bound dimension such as permission p, time window [tstart , t end ], or budget where encoded as a permission. These are all enforced identically by Definition 17’s conjunctive predicate and Theorem 4’s deterministic evaluation. Specifically, permissions are not static; they are strictly bounded by the tuple (p, t start , t end ). The system automatically rejects any action where t > tend ,ensuring that even if an agent is compromised, the attacker’s window of opportunity is strictly limited by the pre-defined delegation logic. X. D ISCUSSIONS & L IMITATIONS 

The identity gap of decentralized Agentic systems is for-malised by the ABCD Framework, with Algorithmic prove-nance, Behavioural evidence, Cryptographic anchoring as well as Delegated liability. Yet, the implementation of this calculus in permissionless infrastructure has certain trade-offs. 

A. Statistical Nature of Verification 

Unlike cryptographic signatures which provide binary ver-ification (valid or invalid), Layer B relies on the Coherence Policy π to detect anomalies between declared Algorithmic Fingerprints ( ΦAlg ) and observed behaviour ( ΦBeh ). This introduces a fundamental limitation: verification is probabilistic 

rather than deterministic . The system acts as a statistical detector subject to false positives (e.g., benign performance drift due to network latency) and false negatives (e.g., adaptive adversaries minimizing divergence). The reliability of this trust model is therefore strictly dependent on the observation window 

W . A window that is too short increases noise and false alarms, while a window that is too long delays the detection of model swapping attacks. 

B. Privacy-Transparency Trade-offs 

While off-chain storage (e.g., IPFS) prevents the direct disclosure of model weights, a fundamental tension remains between public verification and intellectual property (IP) protection. To prove behavioural integrity ( ΦBeh ), an agent must publicly disclose performance metrics (e.g., latency, refusal rates, decision traces). In competitive domains like DeFi, these behavioural traces can act as a side-channel. This can allow competitors to reverse-engineer proprietary strategies. For example, a consistent latency pattern might reveal the complexity of the underlying model. Future work must explore Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs) to allow agents to prove adherence to π without revealing the raw underlying metrics. C. Attribution vs. Enforcement 

Layer D successfully binds agents to human principals ( EU )cryptographically via the Own relation, solving the problem of attribution (ST3). However, it does not solve enforcement .If a pseudonymously owned agent causes financial damage, the protocol can prove who is liable, but it cannot compel restitution outside of pre-locked collateral. The framework provides the evidentiary basis for legal action but relies on external jurisdiction for execution. XI. C ONCLUSION 

AI agents that act independently can disrupt established identity paradigms based on biological anchors. In this paper, we have presented the ABCD Framework, which is a domain-sensitive calculus that orthogonalises the conception of AI identity into Algorithmic provenance, Behavioral integrity, Cryptographic anchoring and Delegated liability. Here, human and AI entities were formally partitioned ( EU ∩ EAI = ∅)and three security theorems were formally proven under explicit cryptographic and systems assumptions. ABCD is not a definitive solution to the economic dilemma of autonomy, but a foundational standard. The opaque deployments are replaced with auditable guarantees, by binding what an agent is ( ΦAlg ), how it acts ( ΦBeh ) and who is accountable ( EU ).
