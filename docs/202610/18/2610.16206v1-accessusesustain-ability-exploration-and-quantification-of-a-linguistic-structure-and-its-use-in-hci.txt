# {Access|Use|Sustain|_}-ability: Exploration and Quantification of a Linguistic Structure and Its Use in HCI 

Uhde, Alarith Ritsumeikan University uhde@fc.ritsumei.ac.jp Haimes, Paul Ritsumeikan University haimes@fc.ritsumei.ac.jp 

Abstract 

Language plays a crucial role in research and technology design, throughout problem formulation, method descriptions, analysis of findings, and interpretation. In this paper, we focus on a particular, trending linguistic pattern in the Human-Computer Interaction literature, which we call -abilities . Examples include usability, accessibility, and explainability. These -abilities have become increasingly popular, with potential impacts on the discipline. They take a verb, turn it into an adjective, and finally nominalize it. We approach the -abilities phenomenon with a survey of the HCI literature between 1988 and 2025. All in all, we identified 349 different -abilities. Moreover, the use of -abilities notably increased since the late 2000s, both in the absolute number of different -abilities and the frequency of use of the most popular ones. The majority of -abilities we found had a positive notion, and most -abilities with a negative notion are prefixed counterparts of their positive opposite (e.g., un+sustainability ). We critically discuss implications of their increased use. First, -abilities change the literal meaning of design intentions. For example, acceptability reduces the concrete verb accepting 

into only its potential ( acceptable ), and finally nominalizes it, claiming the existence of acceptability as a somewhat stable and real, abstract characteristic of a technology. Second, the prefix-based opposite construction can weaken the design goal. 

Un+sustainability avoids clarification of the -ability’s meaning (here e.g., ecological or financial), and does not spell out the expected impact of designs that are not sustainable (e.g., the destruction of nature). Future work should compare the use of -abilities in English with other languages to better understand the ways in which they transform meaning. 

Keywords: accessibility, sustainability, usability 

# 1 Introduction 

The words we use to describe design goals can be understood as linguistic interfaces between design intentions and product im-plementations. We might for example intend to design a website with good usability , and then implement certain features so that visitors experience no major issues. Likewise, a tote bag intended to be sustainable will have features to protect the environment, and socially acceptable wearables will be built to make people feel comfortable about them in public. In other words, design goals such as usability, sustainability, and (social) acceptability constitute an important first mental step that shapes the later de-sign direction. We are in principle free to pick or create any words to define our design goals, with perhaps linguistic abilities, creativity, and grammatical rules as the only limits. In practice, community conventions can serve as additional guidance and establish com-monly used terms, as in the case of usability . These help commu-nication, as they (literally) establish a shared language, which in turn helps designers identify commonalities in their work. Over time and across many projects where people use different words to describe their goals, we can look for patterns of language use, which can tell us something about our field. For example, by an-alyzing the terms we can learn about general interests, trending topics, or shared values, but also potential blind spots or other issues expressed through the collective use of language. The motivation for this paper grew out of the observation of such a collective use of language in the Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) literature. Rather than a specific term, we noticed a gram-matical pattern that has become a trend in the late 2000s and has further risen in popularity ever since (see Figure 1). We call them

Usabil-ity [1], accessibility (e.g., [2]), and sustainability (e.g., [3]) are among the most popular. In Artificial Intelligence (AI) related research, we find explainability [4], contestability [5], and trusta-bility [6]. And in the sciences more broadly, we speak of gener-alizability, reliability, and replicability (e.g., [7, 8]). We have col-lected several hundreds of such -abilities from HCI-related papers published over the past decades. Here, we present our analysis of their use and critically discuss it. In sum, the paper makes the following three contributions. First, we confirm and quantify the widespread use of -abilities in the HCI literature, with detailed descriptions of their frequencies and developments over time. Second, we discuss potential explana-tions for their increasing popularity. And third, we reflect on the impact of -abilities on the meaning of design goals, along with the risks and opportunities that go along with that. 

# 2 Background 

We can characterize -abilities based on their linguistic structure to have a clearer definition for further analysis. Generally, -abilities are based on a root verb, which provides them with their core lexical meaning. This root verb is expanded with the -able 

or -ible affix into an adjective, and finally nominalized using the -ity affix. For example, the verb sustain is extended into the adjective sustainable , and finally nominalized into 

sustainability (see also [9]). Some -abilities also have a prefix (e.g., un+sustainability ). Depending on the con-text, the adjective form is also used, although our focus here lies primarily on the nominalized variant. Figure 2 illustrates this ba-sic structure. The -ability suffix also appears in some other words, which we treat separately here. For example, ability is a special case that has no separate root verb. 2.1 The Role of Language in Research and Design 

We found no prior work with a particular focus on -abilities, but critical reflection on the use of language in general, and its role in shaping our thinking, has been a key concern at least since the linguistic turn in philosophy [10]. It was also foundational for postmodern theory (e.g., [11, 12]). Over time, this increased attention to language use has found its way into HCI and design research, notably through the “semantic turn” [13] and in feminist and postcolonial perspectives [14]. A prominent example is the term “Artificial Intelligence” or “AI”. Over the decades, this term has undergone critical anal-ysis because of its imprecise and shifting meaning. Although initially used to describe problem-solving and expert systems, it has shifted to describe statistical and data-driven models today, sometimes also chatbots more specifically [15]. “AI” is also com-monly used to rebrand existing technologies (e.g., “Big Data”), or to hide the human labor the systems are based on [15, 16, 17]. This function as a “floating signifier” with a drifting meaning and “strategical vagueness” [15] serves today as a marketing tool in politics, business, and research. Besides “Artificial Intelligence”, similar critiques have also been voiced about intelligence as such, with its comparable vagueness and practical consequences, along with attempts to redefine it [18]. Unlike “AI”, -abilities are not a particular term but a broader morpheme structure that has become popular over the past two decades. While the use of -abilities in HCI specifically has not been studied yet, such trends in morpheme structures are not new. We can find practical examples, such as the trending company names in the late 2000s that omitted a vowel as a means of mar-keting and recognition (e.g., flickr, tumblr, or grindr) [19]. An-other example was Apple’s former product naming scheme which started with an i followed by a capital letter (e.g., iTunes, iPhone, iPod). This pattern has later been mimicked by other companies (e.g., iRobot, iHealth, or iZotope), which may have contributed to Apple’s shift away from it. A final example is the “Smart X” trend (e.g., smartphone, smartwatch, smart city), which has even sparked its own “dumb” countermovement [20]. Some research has focused on suffix use in scientific language. For example, Montero-Fleta’s work [21] compared nominaliza-tions in scientific language corpora, specifically from medicine and computer science, with more broadly used English lan-guage from the British National Corpus (BNC). The analysis revealed a high prevalence of nominalizing suffixes including 

-ion , -ness , and -ist in the scientific corpora, compared with the BNC. A second, more recent work by Zagà [22] stud-ied the use of the -logy suffix in its use to name scientific dis-ciplines. The -logy suffix implies scientific authority but is at times misused to add a scientific appearance to non-scientific or pseudoscientific contexts (e.g., astrology or phrenology). In sum, critical discussions about language use enrich the dis-course and reveal issues, as in the case of “AI” or -logy . It also helps describe linguistic trends both within and outside of academia. One takeaway is that the choice of words and their form matters. The specific choice of words influences how we think about design, how we formulate problems or possibilities, and finally how the further design process develops. To quan-tify and better characterize the -ability trend specifically, we con-ducted a systematic literature search as outlined below. 

# 3 Method 

We started by collecting a sample of -abilities from scientific pub-lications in HCI. In the further process, we quantified their use to identify which -abilities are most common, how frequently they are used, and how many we can distinguish. We also identified “misuses” in which the -ability construction was grammatically incorrect. Finally, we looked into the valence (generally posi-tive or negative meaning of the -ability) and prefix use to further characterize them. 

3.1 Literature Search 

We ran a search query in the ACM Digital Library (ACM Full Text Collection) for articles that contain any word ending in -bility in the abstract and were related to HCI 1. We included only research articles, which were published in journals and confer-ence proceedings before the end of 2025. The ACM Digital Li-brary does not allow the search for suffixes directly, because it cannot handle search terms that begin with a wildcard character. Thus, we bracketed the wildcard * manually between each letter of the English alphabet and the -bility suffix, which allows for both the -ability and -ibility affixes. This led to the following query:                               

> Abstract:(a*bility OR b*bility OR c*bility OR d*bility OR e*bility OR f*bility OR g*bility OR h*bility OR i*bility OR j*bility OR k*bility OR l*bility OR m*bility OR n*bility OR o*bility OR p*bility OR q*bility OR r*bility OR s*bility OR t*bility OR u*bility OR v*bility OR w*bility OR x*bility OR y*bility OR z*bility) AND AllField:(HCI OR "Human-Computer Interaction")

After obtaining the list of results, we applied filters based on the publication date (before 2026) and type (journal or conference proceedings) in the ACM Digital Library web interface. All in all, this resulted in 23122 hits (3043 journal papers and 20079 conference proceedings publications).

Next, we extracted all -abilities from the abstracts using an auto-mated script, which resulted in 34523 hits of 420 different words. We then looked through the 420 words and identified several is-sues, which we verified in the original abstracts before fixing them. Some of the words included typos (e.g., 1usability) and some -abilities appeared with different spellings for the same con-cept (e.g., customisability and customizability). Moreover, some words were hyphenated (e.g., [indistin]guishability). We cleaned these by correcting the typos, standardizing spelling, and com-pleting hyphenated words. After cleaning, 386 distinct words re-mained. In the next step, we filtered out words that fell outside the -abilities definition as described above. Here, we distinguished between two categories. First, we excluded 12 words that do not contain a root verb but are grammatically valid (e.g., equitabil-ity ). Second, we excluded 25 further words that we considered grammatically incorrect, but which seemed to be intentionally constructed as they are (i.e., they are not typos). One difficulty of this step is that the existence of a root verb is not always clear. The English language sometimes allows for nouns to be used as verbs. For example, one of these cases was robotability [23]. Al-though typically a noun, to robot could also be used as a verb. In such cases, we verified how they were used in the context of their original abstracts and excluded them if not used as a verb there. A counterexample was unionability , which initially seemed to use a root noun (union) but was included because it described a table operation as verified in the original context ( to union ) [24]. Fi-nally, although we excluded these grammatically incorrect cases, we want to stress that we do not see them as an issue in principle. They indicate the popularity of -abilities, even in unconventional forms. In a sense, this expresses the impact of -abilities and the range of their creative appropriation. 

3.2 Exploratory Content Analysis 

In the final step of our analysis, we explored characteristics of the -abilities themselves with a brief content analysis. Specifi-cally, we quantified a) the use of prefixes and b) the valence of the -ability. For prefixes, we considered an -ability as prefixed only if re-moving the prefix would leave a valid root verb. For exam-ple, the prefix in un+sustain+ability leaves the verb sus-tain , so it would count as prefixed. Conversely, the prefix in 

ad+just+ability , leaves only just , which on its own is not a valid verb, so this -ability was coded as not prefixed. To quantify the valence, we coded each -ability depending on whether achieving this -ability would in the general case be con-sidered positive, whether the valence was unclear, or whether it would be considered negative. The goal of this was to get a broad estimate of how many positive and negative -abilities are in use, so we based this assessment on an everyday understanding. For example, accessibility would generally be seen as a pos-itive characteristic of a technology, whereas irritability

# 4 Results 

4.1 Overall Number and Frequencies of Use 

After filtering and fixing typos, the final set consisted of 349 valid -abilities, plotted in Figure 3. Out of these, the most com-monly used -abilities included usability (6984 times), accessibil-ity (3364 times), and feasibility (1442 times). However, the dis-tribution was skewed with a long tail, and 120 -abilities were only used once. The large difference between the mean (82 .2) and me-dian (3) reflects this strong skew. 

4.2 Development Over Time 

Figure 1 already indicated a strong increase in the number of -abilities, from 4 in 2006, to 31 in 2007, to 77 in 2008, and an ongoing high level over the following years up to 147 -abilities in 2025. In the time before that, between 1988 and 2005, the most fre-quently used -ability was usability (52 times), followed by avail-ability (3 times) and a total of 14 further -abilities only used once or twice. Notably, we found no -abilities in the ACM Digital Li-brary database before 1988, and in further 9 out of the 17 years between 1988 and 2006 no -ability was mentioned in any ab-stract. The major trends for the time after the sharp increase that happened between 2006 and 2008 can be seen in Figure 4. Us-ability has remained the most frequent -ability so far. However, accessibility has also seen a sharp increase since the mid-2010s and recently reached a high level of 500 uses in 2025, close to the 529 uses of usability. The following three most frequent -abilities (feasibility, reliability, and mobility) already have a notably lower usage (in 2025, 142, 151, and 95, respectively). 

4.3 Prefix Use and Valence 

We found 88 -abilities with a prefix, and a clear majority of -abilities with positive valence (291), followed by negative va-lence (36), and unclear valence (22). We also explored the inter-section of prefix use and valence, and found that most -abilities with negative valence (29) had a prefix that negated or inverted their original meaning (e.g., un-, dis-, in-, or non-). There were only seven negative -abilities without prefix: fallibility, fatiga-bility, irritability, perishability, susceptibility, suspectibility, and vulnerability. The 22 -abilities with unclear valence could be subdivided into three different subtypes. Four -abilities were relatively objective descriptions and used neutral or technical root verbs (e.g., caus-ability, improbability). Eight used potentially ambiguous terms with different meanings, depending on the context (e.g., discrim-inability, exploitability, manipulability). And ten were prefixed, inverted -abilities that described the absence of something (e.g., intangibility, invisibility, irreducibility). 

# 5 Discussion 

This paper presented a study on the use of -abilities in the HCI and design literature. We identified 349 valid -abilities, with a sharp increase of use in the late 2000s and a steady increase after that. The most frequently used -abilities were usability, acces-sibility, and feasibility. Usability has a historically high usage, whereas accessibility has recently reached similarly high usage levels. Many other -abilities only have a low usage, with 120 -abilities only used once. Finally, the majority of -abilities has a generally positive meaning and is not prefixed. Among the -abilities with negative meaning, the majority is prefixed. A few further -abilities have neutral or ambiguous meanings. 

5.1 Why Are -abilities Increasingly Popular? 

The -abilities phenomenon is still relatively recent, with very low numbers before 2006 and a sharp increase after that. The partic-ular timing coincides with a few major shifts in the technology landscape we outline here. However, our analysis does not al-low for causal attributions, and we assume that a combination of multiple aspects may have led to the phenomenon. Moreover, the sharp increase in the late 2000s both happened in the use of us-ability as the top -ability (see Figure 4) and in the total number of -abilities (see Figure 1). The first major shift was described by Susanne Bødker in her ar-ticle on the third wave of HCI [25]. In the 1980s and 1990s, research had mostly focused on office contexts with single users (first wave) and groups (second wave). Accordingly, the design goals were mainly focused on efficiency, user errors, and group coordination. In the third wave, the interest in other domains of computer use increased, and a broader set of design goals went along with that (e.g., joyful user experiences [26]). Although this increased breadth of applications and design goals could have contributed to the number of -abilities in use, Bødker’s paper described a process that was already happening before 2006, so slightly before the effect shown in our data. The second and related shift was the increasing variety of mobile devices. The first iPhone was released in 2007, but other mobile technologies such as mp3 players were already widely in use. In the following years, more devices such as tablets, fitness track-ers, and smartwatches became widely available. Mirroring the third wave trend, these products broadened the variety of contexts and applications of interactive technology. This variety may have gone along with an increase of -abilities to describe new design goals. We have considered further explanations, but some of them seem less likely to have contributed to the sharp increase. For exam-ple, it would be possible that a new, popular measurement tool for usability was published briefly before. However, the most widely used measure for usability today, the System Usability Scale (SUS), was already published in 1996 [27] and the ISO standardized definition of usability in 1998 [1]. A further issue could be changes made to the ACM Digital Library database or a general increase in the number of publications over the years, both of which we could not verify or rule out with our data. In addition, we looked for trends within our dataset, to test whether changes over time in the frequency of -ability use per abstract, the variety of -abilities per abstract, or abstract length may have contributed to the effects. We found negligible correlations for the total number of -abilities (Spearman’s 

ρ = −.007) and the number of different -abilities per abstract (Spearman’s 

ρ = .02, 

p < . 01) over time, which seemed not to have played a major role. However, there was a small increase in abstract length over time (Spearman’s 

ρ = .22, p < . 001), which may have contributed to but not fully caused the effect. Further details can be found in the supplementary material. Finally, there is the possibility of self-reinforcing effects: Readers may notice the increasing use of -abilities in the literature, which could suggest it as a candidate for new word creations. 

5.2 What Work Do -abilities Do? 

So far, we have discussed external reasons that may have led to the increased use of -abilities over the past years. Here, we want to focus on internal reasons or ways in which the particular mor-pheme structure of -abilities shapes the meaning of the under-lying concepts, and how that might be particularly attractive for HCI researchers. We illustrate these effects below with concrete examples that link the linguistic notions to their impact on thinking about design. However, we would like to point out that the linguistic, “literal” meaning is not the only meaning of a word, and that it is not always defining for the concept it describes [9]. For example, the word usability has a precise definition [1]. This definition includes aspects such as user satisfaction and a context of use, which do not appear in the word itself. Someone unfamiliar with the usability definition cannot derive these aspects by only read-ing the word. On the other end of the precision spectrum, the word “sustain-ability” encompasses multiple, ambiguous, and partially contra-dictory meanings. It can be used in the context of environmental protection and efforts against climate change. However, it can also be used in the context of “sustainable development” to argue for economic growth, which can have opposing effects [28]. That said, a look at the literal meaning can still be insightful to critically reflect on the use of -abilities. Following our definition, -abilities take a root verb, turn it into an adjective, and finally nominalize it. These transformations on the linguistic level cor-respond to transformations on the conceptual level. 

5.2.1 -abilities Imply the Existence of a Real Phenomenon 

Take the example of a wearable device that is designed to have high social acceptability . The first and perhaps strongest claim already made at this point is that something like “social accept-ability” exists, as a potential characteristic of an object, which may be measurable and assumably a somewhat stable latent con-struct. An implication of this claim is that we can take two de-vices or forms of interaction, one with higher and one with lower social acceptability, and we would generally find one of them to be more acceptable than the other. However, this relation is less clear in practice. As Uhde and Has-senzahl [29] pointed out, the acceptability of a “phone call” varies significantly across contexts. The same phone call can have a low acceptability when performed during a classical concert, and high acceptability in a shopping mall—even if the performance itself is identical. But the form of interaction also has inconsistent im-pacts. Some early work has found that “subtle” gestures (i.e., hidden in natural movements) have high acceptability, whereas “suspenseful” gestures (i.e., visible input gestures with invisible effects) [30, 31, 32] have low acceptability. However, later work found not only that suspenseful gestures can also have high social acceptability [33], but that the experience of suspenseful gestures can even be better than for subtle gestures, for example in face-to-face interactions [34]. In other words, the notion of “accept-ability” is highly contextual and may not be usefully described as a characteristic of a technology or form of interaction alone. 

5.2.2 -abilities Transform Action Into Potential Action 

The second step made by -abilities is the addition of the able af-fix. This turns a verb into an adjective. At the same time, it trans-forms the action itself into its potential. For example, to accept 

becomes acceptable . In the verb form, we make a stronger as-sertion that people actually accept a technology. In the adjective form, the technology only needs to be potentially acceptable— not actually accepted . This frees the designer from the onus of proving that they have achieved their design goal. If we observe that users do not accept a technology, it is (objectively) not ac-cepted. However, it may still be (potentially) acceptable, even if the accepting has not happened for (perhaps external) reasons. In other words, this transformation blurs the conditions under which we claim that the technology would be accepted, and failure to meet them cannot be accounted to the designer anymore. 

5.2.3 -abilities Abstract and Recontextualize 

Another shift that goes along with the transformation of action into potential action is the omission of who or what is acting, and who or what is being acted upon. In the verb form ( to ac-cept ), we need to make explicit who is accepting what. Users may accept the technology. Other people may accept that users use the technology. However, by transforming the verb into an adjective, the (potential) accepters become abstract. This issue has led to confusions in the social acceptability literature, which now contains some work focusing on users as accepters and other work on other people who witness the interaction as accepters (e.g., [35, 36, 37]). But the same issue of abstract users affects other -abilities as well. In the case of sustainability , it is often not clear who is sustaining exactly what [28]. An alternative reading of this abstraction is a shift of focus, away from characteristics of the actors and towards the interaction it-self. Put differently, the actual “Human” and “Computer” in Human-Computer Interaction become secondary concerns, and the “Interaction” moves into the center. This position can in part be supported if we look into the specific -abilities from our col-lection. Several examples can be seen as more specific sub-forms of usability. For example, we found draggability , browsability ,and foldability , each of which defines a more specific action than 

to use . Thus, the loss of specificity of actors may go along with an increasing specificity of the interaction. The sheer number of -abilities in use today also supports that notion. 

5.2.4 -abilities Could Benefit From Clearer Opposites 

One of our findings was that most -abilities have a positive con-notation. This makes sense, given their use as design goals. How-ever, we also found several -abilities with negative connotations, which were mostly constructed by taking a positive -ability and negating it with a prefix. For instance, we found unsustainability 

as an opposite of sustainability , and inaccessibility as an opposite of accessibility .Given that our study only focused on -abilities, our corpus is somewhat incomplete. For example, we cannot see whether other papers have spelled out what they consider to be the opposite of sustainability beyond unsustainability if they used words with other endings. That said, we did find several prefix constructions in use to negate -abilities, which prompted us to reflect on this usage. Specifically, we have two concerns with prefixed negations of -abilities. First, they do not spell out what they mean. Describing a system as unsustainable tells us little about its effects. This lack of a clear opposite may contribute to its ambiguity. If we talked about environmentally unsustainable systems by instead describ-ing them as environmentally destructive or ecocidal, the environ-mental framing would be clear. This would also strengthen our case to design for sustainability, because we learn about the neg-ative alternative. Second, prefixing sometimes alters the root verb into a form that makes no sense as a standalone verb anymore. The verb 

to sustain , although abstracted away, can still be interpreted af-ter we clarify the actors. But the (non-)verb to unsustain cannot. The reason for this is that prefixes sometimes negate the verb it-self (e.g., in dis+cover ), and sometimes the adjective (e.g., in un+sustainable ). This further removes the -ability from its potential action. To avoid such issues, we recommend un-wrapping negated -abilities into the prefixed verb form and using alternatives if the verb makes no sense on its own. 

5.3 Future Work and Limitations 

This paper has provided a first critical reflection on -abilities, and we hope that this sparks a debate on their use and potential alter-natives in the future. However, it also comes with several limita-tions. First, the corpus we analyzed is limited in several ways. It only included articles with abstracts that have words ending in -ability. This is the nominalized form, and nominalization may be used differently in abstracts compared with full texts of articles (e.g., to reduce word counts). Thus, the abstract-based analysis may not fully generalize to the full articles. Likewise, because of the -ability filter, we could not conduct a comprehensive analysis of the opposite pairs, to see if they may have been constructed with different suffixes. The article database itself adds further restric-tions. We only used publications covered in the ACM Digital Library. This excluded various venues from adjacent fields, such as design research. And finally, the keyword-based approach to filter for HCI-related articles may have been imprecise in some cases, and may have added a bias to the dataset that is different from for example venue-based filters. In addition, we restricted the corpus analysis on descriptive quan-tifications. More in-depth, qualitative analyses of particular -abilities in their use contexts may reveal further issues or poten-tials. Finally, the discussion focused on the literal meaning of the -abilities [9], independent of alternative definitions that exist in some cases. For future work, we would be interested in comparative analyses with translations used in other languages. For example, the dou-ble function of nouns as verbs in English might cause language-specific issues that take different forms in other languages. 

# 6 Conclusion 

The linguistic phenomenon of -abilities shapes how we concep-tualize design intentions, and how we think about the steps to im-plement them. -abilities have become increasingly popular since the late 2000s, both in the total number of -abilities and in their frequency of use (e.g., usability or accessibility). We identified several opportunities for reflection, including the claim of real existence of a phenomenon, the transformation of actions into potential actions, their abstraction away from concrete actors and contexts, and the lack of spelled-out opposites. We invite HCI researchers and designers to reflect on their use of -abilities when formulating their design intentions. When de-signing a new tote bag, for example, rather than selecting “sus-tainability” as an abstract intention, designers could try to specify who should do the sustaining of what exactly and in what way. Likewise, the social acceptability of wearables should be speci-fied more precisely in terms of who, in which contexts, does the accepting. In that sense, we trust in the reflectibility, reconsider-ability, and finally the flexible adaptability of our field’s language use.
