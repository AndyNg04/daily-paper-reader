Abstract
AI agents require specialised retrieval across domains and tasks, but adapting
embedding models is constrained by costly manual training-data design. Here we
introduce SYNTRA, a self-improving agentic framework that turns a few retrieval
examples into reusable synthesis instructions. Given an unlabelled corpus and a
few relevant query-document pairs, SYNTRA infers implicit information needs
and relevance criteria, then uses feedback on trial outputs to refine instructions
for query generation and relevance assessment. Experiments across 24 datasets
spanning 15 domains demonstrate that the same synthesis workflow produces
effective training data, benefiting even strong embedding models with and with-
out existing labelled training sets. Self-improvement strengthens this supervision
on all six evaluated tasks, yielding better retrieval than human calibration on
four. The training value persists at larger scales: on MS MARCO, retrieval gains
continue across a 32-fold range of synthetic training-data sizes. SYNTRA thus
enables scalable embedding-model adaptation with minimal human intervention.
Keywords: self-improving, data synthesis, text embeddings, information retrieval,
large language models
Artificial intelligence (AI) agents increasingly rely on information retrieved from exter-
nal resources to guide reasoning and action. Cursor combines a specialised embedding
model with conventional search tools to locate relevant code (https://cursor.com/blo

g/semsearch). OpenScholar retrieves and integrates evidence from scientific literature
to answer research questions [1]. Across domains and tasks, agents’ use of external
knowledge depends on retrieving information that meets each task’s needs (Fig. 1a).
New domains and applications continually create demand for retrieval models tai-
lored to new corpora and information needs. During the COVID-19 pandemic, rapidly
expanding scientific literature and evolving search needs motivated the development
of TREC-COVID [2]. More recently, OpenScholar adapted its retriever to scientific
literature [1], while Cursor trained a specialised embedding model for code retrieval.
Such adaptation must account for differences in domain knowledge and task-specific
relevance criteria. Even within the same corpus, different user objectives can change
which documents are relevant [3] (Fig. 1b). Embedding models must therefore be
adapted rapidly and eﬀiciently as domains and information needs change.
A key bottleneck in this adaptation is constructing training data that reflect
both domain knowledge and task-specific relevance criteria. Suitable training data
are often scarce, even when unlabelled corpora are available [4, 5]. To learn task-
relevant distinctions, embedding models need queries paired with relevant documents
and informative negatives [6, 7]. Constructing these examples requires deciding what
users might ask, which documents satisfy their needs and which plausible alternatives
should be rejected. These decisions depend on the domain and task, making data
construction a recurring challenge as retrieval is adapted to new settings.
Generative AI offers a way to turn unlabelled corpora into retrieval supervision
[8, 9]. Large language models (LLMs) can generate queries [10–18] and assess docu-
ment relevance [19–21]. These capabilities have enabled the construction of effective
training datasets for general-purpose retrieval [19, 22–26], code retrieval [27] and
reasoning-intensive document retrieval [28, 29]. Yet constructing such data can still
depend on task-specific human preparation and design. This human work includes
defining retrieval tasks and relevance criteria, screening proposed tasks, and refining
generation and annotation instructions [11, 14, 17, 27, 29]. Repeating these activities
across retrieval tasks makes human effort a bottleneck to scaling retrieval adaptation.
The central question is: Can an AI system infer task requirements and adapt data syn-
thesis with minimal human intervention, enabling retrieval adaptation to scale across
domains and tasks?
Here we introduce SYNTRA, a self-improving agentic framework for retrieval
data synthesis. With minimal human intervention, SYNTRA enables scalable adap-
tation of embedding models across domains and tasks. Given an unlabelled corpus
and few-shot examples pairing queries with positive documents, SYNTRA infers task
requirements and develops initial synthesis instructions. Through few-shot-guided self-
improvement, SYNTRA evaluates trial outputs against these requirements and uses
the resulting feedback to refine the shared instructions. This process aligns data syn-
thesis with task-specific information needs and relevance criteria without requiring
manual refinement of synthesis instructions for each task. The refined instructions
are then reused for large-scale data production (Fig. 1c). By turning few-shot guid-
ance into reusable synthesis instructions, SYNTRA makes adaptation of embedding
models scalable across domains and tasks.
Experiments covering 24 retrieval datasets spanning 15 domains show that the
same SYNTRA workflow produces synthetic training data that improve the retrieval
performance of embedding models. Ablations across six tasks show that automatic

self-improvement enhances the training value of the generated data and provides

SYNTRA
Here we briefly introduce the conceptual framework of SYNTRA, with formal defini-
tions and implementation details deferred to Methods, and complete prompt templates
for the principal synthesis workflow provided in Supplementary Note 1.
Framework
SYNTRA uses few-shot-guided self-improvement to adapt the instructions govern-
ing a shared data synthesis workflow for retrieval (Figs. 2 and 3). Adaptation takes
place in test mode, where feedback on trial outputs guides revisions to shared instruc-
tions. In production mode, these instructions are reused to construct training data
across the corpus.
The user supplies an unlabelled corpus and a small set of few-shot examples pairing
queries with relevant documents (Fig. 2a). Corpus preprocessing selects diverse source
documents for query generation and builds a full-corpus index for candidate retrieval.
SYNTRA uses the examples to infer a task definition specifying the intended query
type, document type and relevance criteria. It checks and refines this definition against
the examples, establishing common task requirements for the synthesis modules. The
resulting definition guides the initial instructions for query synthesis and relevance-
label synthesis.
In test mode, SYNTRA evaluates whether trial outputs follow the task definition
and the patterns conveyed by the few-shot examples (Fig. 2b). Diagnostic feedback
guides revisions to the shared instructions, including how queries express information
needs and how documents are judged for relevance. The modules are tested again on
small samples with the revised instructions. Because self-improvement targets shared
synthesis rules, revisions made during testing guide later outputs across documents.
Once the test loops finish, the final instructions remain fixed throughout production.
In production mode, three synthesis modules apply these task-adapted instructions
to the selected documents. Identifier extraction and attribute construction identify
document-grounded entities or relations and establish dimensions for varying the infor-
mation need and its expression (Fig. 3a). Query synthesis combines an identifier with
attribute options to plan an information need, then expresses it as a query (Fig. 3b).
Relevance-label synthesis retrieves candidates from the full corpus and assesses them
against the task’s relevance criteria, producing positives, hard negatives and easy neg-
atives (Fig. 3c). The source document receives the same relevance assessment if it
is retrieved. The resulting (query, positive, negative) records provide the supervision
used to train embedding models after synthesis.

1 Results
1.1 One synthesis workflow improves embeddings across
domains and tasks
Our experiments covered 24 retrieval datasets spanning 15 domains (Fig. 4a).
SYNTRA generated task-specific training data using the same synthesis workflow.
Few-shot-guided self-improvement in test mode adapted the shared synthesis instruc-
tions, with human involvement limited to task setup (Section 3.2). In the main
cross-task evaluation, we compared models trained on SYNTRA-generated data with
an MS MARCO-trained baseline [30], using the same embedding backbone with LoRA
adaptation. We selected MS MARCO as a common reference because it is widely used
for supervised training of dense retrievers [31–33]. We report retrieval performance as
normalised discounted cumulative gain at rank 10 (nDCG@10), on a 0–100 scale.

1.2 Self-improvement enhances the training value of synthetic
data
Automatic self-improvement increased the training value of SYNTRA-generated data
across all six retrieval tasks we tested (Fig. 4b). Comparing synthesis using initial
and refined instructions revealed the largest gains on NFCorpus and FiQA. Their
nDCG@10 scores increased from 24.828 to 36.007 and from 28.873 to 35.529, respec-
tively. On both tasks, training on the initial synthetic data yielded retrieval scores
below the MS MARCO-trained baseline. Training on data generated with the revised
instructions exceeded that baseline. Smaller gains were observed on TheoremQA
theorems, ArguAna, SCIDOCS and Text2SQL.
Automatic self-improvement provided an effective alternative to human calibration
of synthesis instructions. It yielded higher retrieval scores on four of the six tasks.
Performance on Text2SQL was close to that obtained with human calibration (62.240
versus 62.449). Human calibration yielded a higher nDCG@10 score on TheoremQA
theorems than automatic self-improvement (26.681 versus 22.199).
A FiQA case study illustrates how feedback sharpened the task requirements
expressed in shared synthesis instructions (Fig. 5). For query generation, refinement
explicitly directed the system to express a user’s financial information need rather
than summarize the source document (Fig. 5a). For relevance assessment, a known-
positive example exposed an overly narrow interpretation of helpfulness: the annotator
rejected an answer explaining why a proposed mortgage-payment plan might fail
because it did not help implement the plan. Feedback prompted a revision clarifying
that an answer can be relevant by resolving the user’s question, including by cor-
recting its premise (Fig. 5b). The same example was then labelled positive. In both
cases, feedback was incorporated into shared instructions for subsequent data syn-
thesis. Complete instructions and example inputs for this case study are provided in
Supplementary Note 1.

1.4 Generated labels agree with human relevance judgements
We evaluated Qwen3-30B-A3B relevance labels against reference labels obtained by
voting across seven independent human annotators. The evaluation included 150
query–document pairs, with 50 pairs from each of MS MARCO, Text2SQL and Theo-
remQA (theorems). Dataset-level agreement and class-wise performance are reported
in Extended Data Tables 2 and 3.
Binary agreement was 91.3% when distinguishing relevant from non-relevant
documents (137/150; Cohen’s κ = 0.719). Three-class agreement was 68.7% when dis-
tinguishing positives, hard negatives and easy negatives (103/150; κ = 0.510). Most
three-class disagreements concerned the distinction between hard and easy negatives
(34/47; Extended Data Table 3).
We further assessed the positive and hard-negative labels used for training. Among
the 87 evaluation pairs assigned either category by the LLM, 72 matched the human
reference category, yielding a pooled label precision of 82.8% (Fig. 4d; Extended Data
Table 3).
These results support using SYNTRA-generated relevance labels as supervision
for retrieval training. The labels closely matched human binary relevance judgements,
while most three-class disagreements concerned the distinction between hard and easy
negatives.
1.5 Gains extend to widely used, strong embedding models
The cross-task experiments used a controlled backbone, leaving open whether gener-
ated data still help models already trained on large curated corpora. Two situations
matter in practice: a new task for which no labelled training set exists, and an estab-
lished task for which one does. We adapted two widely used, strong embedding models,
BGE-M3 [37] and Qwen3-Embedding-8B [38], in both situations, using three retrieval
tasks each (Fig. 6a,b).
For tasks without existing labelled training sets, SYNTRA used a small set of
few-shot examples to guide task adaptation and generated the data used for model
training. Both embedding models improved on all three tasks. On LeCaRDv2, Evi-
denceBench and SciRepEval Search Tasks, BGE-M3 gained 0.505, 4.187 and 0.820
nDCG@10 points, respectively, while Qwen3-Embedding-8B gained 3.255, 0.477 and
0.670 points. These improvements were measured relative to the corresponding
pretrained models without task-specific fine-tuning.
For tasks with existing labelled training sets, we compared fine-tuning on those
sets alone with fine-tuning on the same sets augmented by SYNTRA-generated data.
Augmentation improved both models on all three tasks. On TheoremQA questions,
SpartQA and WinoGrande, BGE-M3 gained 7.237, 1.662 and 4.163 nDCG@10 points,
respectively, while Qwen3-Embedding-8B gained 0.938, 4.278 and 0.887 points. These
improvements were measured relative to fine-tuning on the existing task-specific train-
ing sets alone. Synthetic data can therefore both supply task-specific supervision
where labelled training sets are unavailable and complement existing supervision when
adapting widely used, strong embedding models.

1.6 Scaling synthetic data yields continued retrieval gains
To evaluate the effect of scaling synthetic training data, we trained a Qwen3-8B back-
bone on 10,000 to 320,000 SYNTRA-generated examples from MS MARCO passages.
We evaluated retrieval performance on MS MARCO with a fixed training recipe and
evaluation protocol.
Retrieval performance improved at each of the six evaluated scales. nDCG@10
increased from 30.88 to 33.20 as we scaled up the training data (Fig. 6e), an overall gain
of 2.32 points. These results demonstrate the value of scaling synthetic training data,
with larger datasets yielding continued improvements in embedding performance.
1.7 Core design choices improve retrieval performance
To identify contributions from SYNTRA’s synthesis components, we evaluated task-
adapted query instructions, planning with semantic identifiers and query attributes,
relevance annotation, and multiple queries per document. For the component
ablations, we report mean nDCG@10 across 13 NanoBEIR tasks. Query-count
comparisons are reported separately for each evaluated task (Fig. 6c,f).
Removing semantic identifiers and query attributes reduced mean nDCG@10
on NanoBEIR from 54.64 to 53.96. Removing the task-adapted query-generation
instruction reduced the score to 52.56. For relevance-label synthesis, replacing rel-
evance annotation with hard-negative mining reduced the score to 52.80. Adding
relevance annotation for the source document raised it to 53.75, below the full work-
flow’s 54.64. These comparisons support the contributions of semantic identifiers and
query attributes, task-adapted query instructions and relevance annotation to the
effectiveness of the generated training data.
Generating multiple queries per document also improved retrieval on both evalu-
ated tasks (Fig. 6f). Different combinations of semantic identifiers and query attributes
allow the same document to support queries expressing different information needs.
Increasing the number of queries from one to two and four raised nDCG@10 from
37.048 to 37.961 and 38.374 on CodeTrans-DL. On MedicalQA, the corresponding
scores increased from 33.813 to 37.956 and 39.721. These results support generating
several queries per document as a way to provide additional useful supervision on
these tasks.
2 Discussion
Adapting retrieval models to new domains and tasks requires training data that
reflect their information needs and relevance criteria. SYNTRA uses few-shot-guided
self-improvement to adapt shared synthesis instructions to different retrieval require-
ments. Few-shot examples convey what users seek and which documents satisfy their
needs. Feedback on trial outputs translates that understanding into reusable guid-
ance for constructing supervision. The synthesis system can therefore take on part
of the task-specific design work needed to adapt specialised embedding models. Once
refined, the instructions are reused for large-scale production with a locally deployed
12


model, while commercial-model calls are concentrated in small-scale calibration. This
supports economical data synthesis.
The self-improvement results show why adapting synthesis instructions mat-
ters for downstream learning. On NFCorpus and FiQA, training on data generated
with refined instructions exceeded the MS MARCO-trained baseline. Training on
data generated with the initial instructions fell below that baseline. Automatic self-
improvement was competitive with human calibration on most tested tasks, although
human calibration remained advantageous on TheoremQA (theorems). SYNTRA-
generated data also benefited widely used, strong embedding models, supplying
supervision where labelled training sets were unavailable and complementing existing
supervision. On MS MARCO, retrieval gains from scaling up the training data indi-
cate that the additional synthetic data remained useful across the evaluated scales.
These findings support few-shot-guided adaptation of a shared synthesis workflow
within the retrieval settings examined here.
A natural extension is to apply self-improvement to training-data synthesis for
diverse LLM capabilities. Such systems could infer what a target capability requires
and adapt how training examples are generated and assessed, with minimal human
involvement and low production costs. Recent studies have automated question–
answer construction with assessment criteria and task construction with teacher
demonstrations, providing foundations for this direction [39, 40]. Feedback for self-
improvement would need to show whether generated examples meet the intended
training requirements. Answer checks and code execution offer scalable ways to assess
correctness in mathematical and coding tasks [41]. Within a task, these checks may
need to be combined with assessments of relevance, completeness and contextual
requirements using task-specific rubrics [42]. Such feedback could guide revisions to
the synthesis rules for the target capability.
A further direction is to use feedback from model evaluation, task execution and
user interactions to identify previously unmet training needs. These signals could also
reveal requirements that emerge during use and guide revisions to generated examples
and their assessment criteria. For retrieval and LLM training alike, this could extend
self-improvement towards identifying what models need to learn next and adapting
data synthesis accordingly.
3 Methods
We describe corpus preprocessing and task preparation, followed by self-improvement
in test mode and data synthesis in production mode. We then explain how the gener-
ated labels are converted into training data and describe the embedding models and
evaluation procedures.

Task preparation uses the few-shot examples to infer a task definition comprising

or insuﬀiciently specific requirements. Alternatively, users can supply a task definition
directly for the synthesis workflow. This step establishes the task requirements used
to construct and refine the synthesis instructions in test mode (Section 3.2).
3.2 Task adaptation through self-improvement
Test mode implements self-improvement by refining shared synthesis instructions
through generate–evaluate–refine cycles on small samples (Fig. 2b). Each synthesis
module generates trial outputs and evaluates them against the task definition and
few-shot examples. Diagnostic feedback guides revisions to its instruction or guidance
before the module runs again on the same trial inputs. These revisions update the
shared synthesis rules that guide subsequent data generation across documents.
Each loop terminates when verification passes or its iteration limit is reached.
All three loops allow up to three attempts by default, including the initial trial.
14


This limit is configurable. For the identifier–attribute and query loops, LLM verifiers
return scores from 0 to 3. Scores of at least 2 are treated as a pass in these loops.
Relevance-label verification uses the programmatic consistency check on few-shot
positive pairs described below. On termination, SYNTRA stores the last evaluated
instruction, including when the iteration limit is reached without a pass. All loops
finish before production begins, and their final shared instructions remain fixed
throughout production.
Identifier extraction and attribute construction share one refinement loop on sam-
pled documents. The initial trial uses a shared generation template conditioned on
the task definition, with no additional refinement guidance. An LLM verifier assesses
document grounding, redundancy and whether the attribute dimensions and options
support useful variation within the task. Its feedback is used to develop reusable guid-
ance for subsequent extraction and construction. After this loop finishes, SYNTRA
regenerates identifiers and attributes with the final guidance to provide fixed inputs
for query-instruction refinement.
Query-instruction refinement adapts information-need planning and query writing
to the task definition and few-shot examples. The initial instruction is induced from
these inputs and specifies the intended query producer and information need. It also
specifies how document evidence should be selected or abstracted, together with the
linguistic characteristics and constraints of the target queries. Trial synthesis applies
this instruction to both planning and query writing. An LLM verifier evaluates sam-
pled document–query pairs for style, task alignment and naturalness. It also assesses
whether queries plausibly reflect their intended producer and whether the instruction
is effective. Its feedback guides revisions to the instruction used in both stages, while
the upstream identifiers and attributes remain fixed during this loop.
Relevance-label refinement adapts the task-specific criteria for positives, hard neg-
atives and easy negatives. The initial instruction is generated from the task definition
and the required annotation format. It is tested on the known (query, positive doc-
ument) pairs supplied as few-shot examples. A program checks whether every pair
is classified as positive, treating missing predictions as mismatches. When the check
fails, the LLM analyses the errors and revises the relevance criteria. It also infers and
refines criteria distinguishing apparently relevant documents that fail a required condi-
tion from documents clearly unrelated to the information need. These criteria become
part of the shared annotation instruction used during production. The examples
directly verify positive classifications, whereas negative criteria are inferred from the
task requirements. The framework also supports calibration against human-confirmed
labels during test mode.
Human involvement in the principal experiments was confined to task setup. The
operator reviewed and occasionally revised the inferred task definition before data
production. The operator also sometimes adjusted the document-selection threshold
to obtain the intended document count. Retrospective estimates indicated that setup
typically required no more than 10 min per task.
The self-improvement ablation compared three conditions across six retrieval tasks
(Fig. 4b). Synthesis without self-improvement used the initial task-specific instructions
for data production. Automatic self-improvement used instructions revised through
15


feedback during test mode. In the human-calibration condition, human input was
used to calibrate module-level synthesis instructions during test mode. Data gener-
ated under each condition were used to train task-specific embedding models. Their
retrieval performance is compared in Results (Section 1.2).
3.3 Production-mode data synthesis
In production mode, three synthesis modules reuse the final task-adapted instructions
across the selected documents (Fig. 3). Their outputs comprise generated queries
and sets of labelled candidate documents. These outputs are converted into training
records as described in Section 3.4.
3.3.1 Identifier extraction and attribute construction
For each selected document, a single LLM call jointly extracts semantic identifiers and
constructs query attributes. This call uses the instruction and guidance established
during test mode. Semantic identifiers are document-grounded entities or relations
that provide possible targets for an information need. Query attributes define dimen-
sions along which the information need or its expression can vary, such as scope,
reasoning requirements or specificity. Both the dimensions and their task-appropriate
options are constructed for each document. The resulting identifiers and attributes
provide the components for planning distinct queries.
3.3.2 Query synthesis
Query synthesis uses two LLM stages to plan an information need and express it as
a query. The planner receives the source document, task definition, few-shot exam-
ples and task-adapted query instruction. Each proposed plan combines one semantic
identifier with one option from every query-attribute dimension. These plans specify
the information need and its intended expression for query writing. The query-writing
stage converts each plan into a query using the same task-adapted instruction and
source document.
3.3.3 Relevance-label synthesis
Each generated query retrieves its top-K candidate documents from the full corpus
using the indexing encoder and cosine similarity. If the source document is retrieved,
it receives the same relevance assessment as the other candidates. Candidates are
annotated in chunks using the task-adapted relevance-label instruction, with each
query–document pair judged independently on a four-point scale. A score of 3 indicates
strong satisfaction of the relevance criteria, whereas 2 indicates a weaker but valid
positive. A score of 1 indicates a plausible candidate that fails the criteria, and 0
indicates a clearly unrelated candidate. Scores of 3 and 2 map to positive, 1 to hard
negative and 0 to easy negative. Candidate annotations are merged into positive,
hard-negative and easy-negative sets for training-data construction. Parsing retries
and structural checks are also applied to outputs during production.
16


3.4 Training data construction and model configurations
Training records.
Training records retain queries with at least one positive and one usable negative docu-
ment. Each record contains the query and variable-length lists of positive and negative
document texts. The hard-negative-only export uses labelled hard negatives as its
negative examples. An alternative export supplements hard negatives with labelled
easy negatives when fewer than the requested number of hard negatives are available.
For Qwen3 training, the loader samples one positive and a configurable number of
negatives from each record. It repeats available negative indices when necessary to
supply the requested number. Records also contain a retrieval instruction that can be
prepended to the query during training.
Embedding models.
Embedding-model training and retrieval evaluation followed completion of data syn-
thesis. The main cross-task evaluation used a Qwen3-0.6B backbone with LoRA
adaptation, with MS MARCO-trained models providing the comparison baselines.
SYNTRA generated data separately for each task, and models trained on those data
were evaluated on the corresponding retrieval tasks. Key experimental settings are
provided in Supplementary Note 2 (Supplementary Tables 1–2).
Experiments with BGE-M3 and Qwen3-Embedding-8B evaluated synthetic data in
two settings (Fig. 6a,b). For tasks without existing labelled training sets (LeCaRDv2,
EvidenceBench and SciRepEval Search [44]), models fine-tuned on SYNTRA-
generated data were compared with their pretrained counterparts. For tasks with
existing labelled training sets (TheoremQA questions [45], SpartQA [46] and Wino-
Grande [47]), we compared two fine-tuning conditions. These used the existing sets
alone or the same sets augmented by SYNTRA-generated data.
Query-source comparisons.
The MS MARCO query-source experiment compared original human-generated
queries from Bing search logs [30] with SYNTRA-generated queries using the same
pool of 10,000 source documents. Both conditions used the same relevance-annotation
and negative-mining procedures and the same embedding-model training recipe. Valid
training-record counts are provided in Extended Data Table 1. Retrieval performance
was evaluated on both the BEIR and NanoBEIR suites.
We also compared SYNTRA-generated queries with task-specific query sources
on TheoremQA questions and theorems, CosQA and Synthetic Text2SQL. Within
each task, both query sources underwent the same candidate-retrieval and relevance-
annotation procedures and were used to train the same embedding backbone.
For the TheoremQA comparisons, we used the ReasonEmbed-Data-0928 release
[29] from the Hugging Face repository hanhainebula/reason-embed-data (config-
uration v0928, revision 8dada2e649a2). The files theoremqa_questions-formatt
ed.jsonl and theoremqa_theorems-formatted.jsonl contained 7,184 and 8,861
queries, respectively. We used the query field and paired each query with one of its
17


released positive documents as the source document for candidate retrieval and rele-
vance annotation. All usable released queries were retained, without matching their
counts to SYNTRA.
For CosQA and Synthetic Text2SQL, reference queries came from the train splits
of CoIR-Retrieval/cosqa (revision 0846fa3b963a) and CoIR-Retrieval/synth
etic-text2sql (revision 16c5efcfbda2), respectively. CosQA used all 9,020 pos-
itively labelled training pairs. For Synthetic Text2SQL, we sampled 10,000 oﬀicial
training pairs from the 13,621 whose document identifiers intersected the selected
source-document pool, using seed 20260627. SYNTRA generated queries from those
same 10,000 documents.
The SYNTRA-trained CosQA model used seed 42 and a maximum passage length
of 1,024 tokens. The oﬀicial-query reference used seed 42 and 512 tokens. Other
training settings followed the same recipe.
For these four tasks, training records required at least one annotated positive
and one hard negative. Two positive-selection variants were supplied together to the
training loader: one retained all annotated positives, whereas the other retained only
the source document when it was among those positives and otherwise kept the original
positive list. The variants used the same negatives and were concatenated without
deduplication. Query counts, valid-record counts and the four tasks’ absolute retrieval
scores are reported in Extended Data Table 4.
Data-scaling experiment.
The MS MARCO data-scaling experiment used a Qwen3-8B backbone with 10,000,
20,000, 40,000, 80,000, 160,000 and 320,000 SYNTRA-generated training examples.
The training recipe and evaluation protocol were held fixed across data scales. We
performed one training run at each data scale using the settings in Supplementary
Table 2.
Computational resources and production times.
Data production used Qwen3-30B-A3B on four NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPUs
within a six-GPU system. The other two GPUs in the system were used for embedding-
model operations. Extended Data Table 1 reports selected production batches across
24 retrieval tasks used in the experiments. The reported times cover production stages
and exclude calibration, prebuilt indexing and embedding-model training and evalua-
tion. The table caption specifies the batch-selection procedure and the stages included
in the timings.
3.5 Human evaluation of relevance labels
Seven annotators independently rated the 150 query–document pairs using the rele-
vance scale and label mapping described above (Section 3.3.3). Human reference labels
were determined by plurality voting over the three categories. Four pairs with tied
highest vote counts were assigned hard negative. For binary evaluation, each anno-
tator’s two negative categories were merged before majority voting. We measured
agreement between LLM and human reference labels using exact matches and Cohen’s
κ. Human inter-annotator agreement was measured using Fleiss’ κ on the mapped
18


labels. Training-label precision was the fraction of LLM-labelled positives and hard
negatives whose exact category matched the human reference. We estimated 95%
percentile confidence intervals using 10,000 bootstrap resamples, each comprising 50
query–document pairs sampled with replacement from each dataset. Human reference
labels were held fixed, and training-label precision was recalculated for each resample.
Human inter-annotator agreement, measured by Fleiss’ κ, was 0.483 for three-class
labels and 0.606 for binary labels. To assess whether the four tied pairs influenced
LLM–human agreement, we repeated the three-class comparison after excluding those
pairs. Agreement was 69.2% (101/146; Cohen’s κ = 0.520), compared with 68.7%
(103/150) when all pairs were included.
3.6 Consistency between NanoBEIR and BEIR
Evaluating every model variant on the complete BEIR suite [31] requires substantial
computation and time. We therefore examined whether NanoBEIR could serve as a
lower-cost proxy for repeated model screening and ablation studies during iterative
development. We evaluated 18 publicly available embedding models on both BEIR and
NanoBEIR and computed paired suite-level mean nDCG@10 scores (Extended Data
Table 5). These comprised nine encoder-only models and nine decoder-only models.
Only the models listed in the paired-score table were included in the correlation
analysis.
NanoBEIR evaluation used the MTEB implementation [48] and comprised 13
retrieval tasks: ArguAna, Climate-FEVER, DBpedia, FEVER, FiQA2018, Hot-
potQA, MS MARCO, NFCorpus, Natural Questions, Quora, SCIDOCS, SciFact
and Touche2020. We evaluated the split named train in the corresponding
zeta-alpha-ai/Nano* dataset releases. Each task contained 50 evaluation queries
except Touche2020, which contained 49, giving 649 queries in total. For each
task, nDCG@10 was averaged over its evaluation queries. The suite score was the
unweighted arithmetic mean of the 13 task scores, multiplied by 100; tasks therefore
contributed equally rather than in proportion to their query counts.
We evaluated component ablations on NanoBEIR and the MS MARCO query-
source comparison on both BEIR and NanoBEIR. NanoBEIR runs for these exper-
iments used MTEB version 1.39.7. The 18-model consistency analysis used versions
1.39.7 and 2.12.30. The dataset identifiers and pinned dataset revisions were identical
across these evaluations and across the two software versions.
The decoder-only models generally occupied a higher score range than the encoder-
only models. Pooling the two families could therefore yield a high correlation partly
driven by between-family differences. We computed Spearman’s rank correlation sep-
arately for each family to assess whether NanoBEIR preserved the ordering of models
within each group. NanoBEIR closely reproduced the BEIR ordering for encoder-only
models (ρ = 0.933, two-sided P = 2.0 × 10−4). Decoder-only models also showed a
strong positive association (ρ = 0.817, two-sided P = 7.0 × 10−3; Extended Data
Table 6). The median absolute rank change was one position in both groups. Eight of
nine encoder-only models and six of nine decoder-only models remained within one
position of their BEIR rank.
19


These results indicate that NanoBEIR preserves the broad relative ordering of
models within both architecture families. They support using NanoBEIR as a lower-
cost proxy for rapid model screening, ablation studies and hyperparameter selection
during iterative experimentation. The absolute nDCG@10 scores on the two suites
are not directly interchangeable, and several local rank reversals remain.

Supplementary Note 1: Prompts and task-specific instructions
Source and input conventions
Prompts are user messages unless a system role is specified. Braced lower-case fields denote runtime
inputs; upper-case fields denote shared display blocks that must be expanded before a prompt is
sent. Each block contains the complete text of its branch, rather than an excerpt. The repository
at https://github.com/zucanlv/SYNTRA contains the prompt builders and input formatters. The
accompanying docs/supplementary/ files provide fully expanded templates, optional branches, the
complete selected instruction records and registry descriptors.
Prompt wording and branch-specific schemas are preserved. Line wrapping and blank-line spacing
are typographic.
The accompanying machine-readable files provide fully expanded templates; the
source builders specify exact formatting. Pictographs, arrows and non-breaking hyphens use reversible
Unicode escape notation; decode these to recover the original symbols. Python JSON expressions
denote the serialized runtime value, using ensure_ascii=False and indent=2, rather than literal
code passed to the model.
The principal flow infers task requirements, adapts identifier/attribute and query-generation guidance,
synthesizes queries, and refines a relevance-annotation instruction. The refined annotation instruction
is subsequently reused as the system message for production annotation. Generating several queries
from one source document and annotating several candidate documents for one query are separate
operations.
Input fields. query_type, doc_type and relevance_def describe the task. doc supplies a source
document. task_instruction is the task-specific query instruction. annotation_instruction
or evaluation_instruction is the relevance-annotation instruction. feedback supplies evaluation
feedback; samples_json and samples_brief supply trial outputs. refinement_guidance and
current_guidance hold the identifier/attribute addendum.
identifiers_brief, attributes,
plans and already_selected supply planning inputs. num_plans specifies the requested number of
new plans; query generation produces one query for each supplied plan.
Few-shot and demonstration inputs.
target_examples_text and examples_text format
labelled target-task examples. few_shot_section contains query-only or query–positive examples
according to the caller. Labelled examples include a hard-negative field only when available. demo_blo
cks pairs the MS MARCO and ArguAna reference examples with their task definitions; anchor_block
contains their definitions alone. References equal to the target task are omitted. The task-definition
caller uses up to five examples from each reference task in stored order. The complete fixed inputs
and formatting functions are available in Main_Pipeline/Few_Shot_Example.py, Few_Shot_Forma
tter.py and HighLevel_Generator.py.
Trial and candidate inputs. Identifier/attribute validation serializes document identifiers, docu-
ment text, identifiers and attributes; refinement omits document text. Query-instruction validation
and refinement sample up to five and eight trial query–document pairs, respectively.
Annotation
candidates retain their order. Multi-document calls use zero-based local doc_id values, which are
mapped back to original identifiers after parsing.

ANALYSIS with one of the following complete lines, according to whether the target-task examples
include hard negatives.

its labeled examples.

expenses), a "what’s the difference" comparison (trading vs. flipping), or a "can I do this" permission-based

*
**Adopt a Direct, Natural Voice:** Frame the query using everyday financial vocabulary; avoid overly
formal or academic language in favor of the terminology a non-expert would likely use.
*
**Abstract Specifics into Scenarios:** If the document provides a detailed numerical example or specific
case study, generalize it into a broader situational query while retaining the essential financial variables.
*
**Focus on the Underpinning Intent:** Ensure the query strikes at the heart of the document’s unique
insight rather than just matching keywords; if the document debunks a myth or offers a psychological tip,
frame the query to elicit that specific guidance.
*
**Maintain Concision:** Limit the query to a single sentence or a brief phrase that captures the primary
information need, omitting any extraneous details from the passage that the user would not yet know.
### Linguistic DNA
*
**Varied Intent Syntax:** Queries should range from direct "How-to" questions and "What is"
definitions to complex situational scenarios and mechanical "Is it possible" inquiries.
*
**Pragmatic Tone:** The tone must be inquisitive and utilitarian, reflecting an individual seeking
actionable financial advice or clarification on technical market rules.
*
**Variable Length:** Synthesize queries across a spectrum of brevity, from short keyword-based phrases
(4-6 words) to detailed multi-part scenarios (15-25 words).
*
**Domain-Specific Vernacular:** Use common financial terminology naturally (e.g., "401k," "shorting,"
"tax implications," "long position," "initial payment").
*
**Informal Polish:** Maintain a style that mimics real-world user behavior—grammatically functional
but often lacking perfect capitalization or formal academic structure.
### Relevance Heuristics
*
**Scenario-Action Coupling:** Focus on queries that pair a specific financial situation (e.g., being in
your early 20s) with a specific decision point (e.g., 401k investment choices).
*
**Mechanism Inquiries:** Emphasize the "how" of financial movement, such as the liquidity of funds
between trades or the timing of down payments.
*
**Comparative Analysis:** Generate queries that require the document to distinguish between two
related but distinct concepts (e.g., day trading vs. flipping).
*
**Outcome Orientation:** Ensure the query seeks a material benefit, such as spending less, reducing tax
liability, or accelerating a home purchase timeline.
*
**Constraint-Based Questions:** Frame queries around limitations or permissions within financial
regulations and account types.
### Negative Constraints
*
**No Generic Financial Literacy:** Avoid overly broad "What is money?" or "What is the stock
market?" queries that lack a specific user intent or scenario.
*
**No Hyper-Dense Jargon:** Avoid queries that read like textbook headings; they should feel like a
human asking for help, not a search engine index.
*
**No Opinion-Seeking:** Avoid subjective prompts like "What is the best stock?" or "Do you like
Vanguard?"; focus on factual, procedural, or strategic information needs.
*
**No Excessive Preamble:** Eliminate "filler" phrases like "I was wondering if you could tell me..." Start
directly with the core question or scenario.
*
**No Unrelated Personal Anecdotes:** Keep scenarios concise; do not include irrelevant biographical
details that do not impact the financial logic of the question.
