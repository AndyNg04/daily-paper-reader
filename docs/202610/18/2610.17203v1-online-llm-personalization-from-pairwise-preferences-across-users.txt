## ABSTRACT 

Large language models can answer the same question in different styles, but users’ preferred styles are hidden and vary across users and questions. We study inference-time personalization through online selection from a fixed set of prompts, each representing a persona. Users arrive sequentially, each asks a short sequence of questions, and the system displays two responses per question and observes one pairwise choice. We formulate this interaction as a variant of the contextual dueling bandit problem in which the learner starts without preference data, adapts within each session, and exploits information shared across users. The objective is to minimize cumulative strong regret, summing the utility gaps of both displayed responses relative to the user’s best response among the avail-able options. We make two contributions. First, we introduce the problem and re-lease a reproducible preference benchmark and dataset built around a tutoring case study, containing educational questions, LLM-generated answers in six teaching styles, and pairwise judgments from LLM-simulated students with hidden prefer-ence profiles. We fit Bradley-Terry models to these judgments to obtain reference utilities for regret evaluation. Second, we propose a baseline that learns a mixture prior over users from completed sessions and adapts to each new user within the session. On students and topics never used in development, it removes 44–48% of random pairs’ excess regret and achieves over half the performance of a per-user linear oracle fitted in hindsight. 1arXiv:2610.17203v1 [cs.LG] 12 Oct 2026 1 INTRODUCTION 

A large language model (LLM) chatbot can answer the same question in several styles, and users differ in the style they prefer. A common way to guide the response style is to use a system prompt that specifies a persona (Zheng et al., 2024b). For example, an LLM math tutor can adopt a persona that emphasizes proofs, worked examples, or guiding questions. The preferred persona depends on the user and the question: the same user may want a proof for a definition and numbered steps for a computation. A simple way to collect preference feedback is to show two answers and ask the user to choose one. In our setup, users arrive one after another, each for a short session, and the platform starts with no preference data. On every question it selects two personas using features of the persona-question pairs and the choices observed so far. A user’s preferences are hidden and fixed during the session, and later users can benefit only from the log the policy itself created. Shared structure across the population is what makes that transfer possible. We study inference-time personalization through online selection from this fixed catalog of prompts. We make two contributions. 1. Problem formulation and dataset. We formalize the interaction as a multi-user variant of the contextual dueling bandit problem, with cumulative strong regret accounting for the utility gaps of both displayed answers (Yue et al., 2012; Bengs et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025). We introduce TutorDuel , a reproducible tutoring preference benchmark and dataset containing educational questions, LLM-generated answers in six teaching styles, and pairwise judgments from LLM-simulated students with hidden preference profiles. On comparisons labeled by two of the authors for the described students, the judge agrees with each author at least as often as the two authors agree with each other (74.0% and 73.0% against 69.6%). We provide reference utilities for regret evaluation by fitting Bradley-Terry models to the LLM judge’s choice probabilities. The evaluation protocol brackets performance between picking personas at random and per-user references, and studies the generalization to new topics and new students never used in development. We also provide a numerical simulator for controlled evaluation. 2. A baseline algorithm. We propose CODuel as a baseline for this problem. It learns a mixture prior over user preferences from completed sessions to starts each new user from that prior. On three TutorDuel rosters whose topics and students were never used in design (eight new hand-written profiles, and two rosters of 350 distinct students composed from preference traits), it removes 48%, 44%, and 46% of the excess regret of random pairs (Table 1). This is 54% to 57% of the performance of a per-user linear oracle, which chooses each user’s parameters in hindsight on the features of CODuel; we offer the difference as a target for future methods. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Contextual dueling bandits. Contextual dueling bandits extend dueling bandits (Yue et al., 2012; Bengs et al., 2021) to arms that depend on a context, as in CoLSTIM (Bengs et al., 2022), the algorithms of Saha (2021), and feel-good Thompson sampling (Li et al., 2024b). Related RLHF formulations treat prompts as contexts and responses as arms (Zhu et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2024; Das et al., 2025). COLDB (Wang et al., 2025) extends clustering of bandits (Gentile et al., 2014; Li et al., 2016) to dueling feedback, pooling observations within clusters of recurring users. Our users instead arrive once, and their preferences need not form discrete clusters. Our pooled RUCB baseline (Zoghi et al., 2014) ignores the question. 

Learning priors across tasks. Meta-Thompson sampling and AdaTS learn priors across bandit tasks from rewards (Kveton et al., 2021; Basu et al., 2021). Related work studies transfer across linear and hierarchical bandit tasks (Cella et al., 2020; Peleg et al., 2022; Hong et al., 2022a). MixTS uses a given mixture prior (Hong et al., 2022b), and Simchowitz et al. (2021) analyze sensitivity to prior misspecification. CODuel learns a mixture prior from completed sessions’ pairwise choices. 

Latent bandits. Latent bandits infer a hidden user type from feedback while minimizing re-gret (Maillard & Mannor, 2014). Hong et al. (2020) develop Thompson sampling and UCB rules 2under uncertain type-specific reward models. CODuel similarly keeps a belief over mixture compo-nents within each session, with pairwise feedback and regret on both displayed answers. 

Personalization across users. Personalized alignment uses policy merging (Jang et al., 2023), user embeddings (Li et al., 2024a; Poddar et al., 2024), personalized reward models (Park et al., 2024a), preference prototypes (Chen et al., 2025), low-rank reward bases (Bose et al., 2025), and meta-learned initializations (Cai et al., 2026). Gaussian-process preference models share information across users and actively select comparisons (Bonilla et al., 2010; Houlsby et al., 2012). Our protocol starts without preference data and measures strong regret on every displayed pair across sequential short user sessions. Online per-user methods are closer in protocol. UserAlign (P˘ adurean et al., 2025) selects a response for one user and prompt from a fixed pool by best-arm identification in a logistic bandit, and T-POP (Qu et al., 2026) steers decoding with a reward learned by a neural dueling bandit. Both learn each user from scratch, like our CoLSTIM baseline. UserAlign reports final-response win rate and query count, rather than cumulative regret on displayed pairs. 

Simulated users and personalization benchmarks. Prior work uses LLMs to simulate human responses (Argyle et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023). These simulations condition on personality pro-files (Jiang et al., 2024), personal interviews (Park et al., 2024b), and student knowledge and person-ality (Lu & Wang, 2024; Liu et al., 2024). AlpacaFarm (Dubois et al., 2023), PERSONA (Castricato et al., 2025), and PersonalLLM (Zollo et al., 2025) provide simulated preference data. PRISM collects human preferences (Kirk et al., 2024), while LaMP evaluates personalization from user histories (Salemi et al., 2024). TutorDuel builds on this use of simulation by prompting an LLM with a student profile to judge tutor answers. Such simulations can misrepresent group opinions and response variability (Santurkar et al., 2023; Bisbee et al., 2024) (Section 7).

## 4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate CODuel in two environments: a numerical simulator in which the linear utility and BTL assumptions of Section 3 hold exactly, and a tutoring benchmark in which an LLM role-plays students described in natural language and compares generated tutor answers. All preferences are simulated. Pairwise choices made by two of the authors serve to validate the judge (Section 4.3). 4.1 SHARED PROTOCOL AND EVALUATION 

In both environments we use K = 6 teaching personas, and u users arrive sequentially, each asking 

b questions ( b = 24 in the tutoring benchmark). In the tutoring benchmark, we make every design choice, including the final variant of CODuel, on a development roster and run the evaluation rosters (Section 4.3) only after these choices are fixed. Every policy starts from the first user of each roster, with no information carried over from development, and is scored on cumulative regret of every round. 

Matched randomness. For each environment and seed, all policies see the same sequence of users and questions. They share one uniform draw per round to sample choices, using the BTL model (2) 4in the simulator and the stored judge probabilities in TutorDuel (Section 4.3). Identical ordered pairs therefore receive identical choices.

References. Table 1 includes random pairs, the distinct-pair floor, and the per-user linear oracle (Section 4.1). Its non-personalized reference is the best fixed pair, chosen in hindsight to minimize regret on the development roster and shown to every user and question. The best fixed pair per user instead chooses a separate pair in hindsight to minimize regret over each user’s questions. 

Ablations and other methods. Our no-transfer ablation retains only CODuel’s per-user Bayesian logistic learner (Section 5), starting each user from the same isotropic prior and learning only from that user’s choices. Our no-recalibration ablation uses the rater’s scores without learning a recali-bration from completed sessions. Our pooled baseline fits one ridge-regularized logistic model to all completed sessions using ϕ. It selects the two highest-scoring personas for each question and does not update within a session. Meta-TS (Kveton et al., 2021) and HierTS (Hong et al., 2022a) learn a prior across bandit tasks. Both model users’ preference vectors as Gaussian draws around an unknown population mean, learn this mean from completed sessions, and select pairs by Thomp-son sampling. Meta-TS samples a prior mean at the start of each session, and HierTS samples it 6every round from a posterior that also conditions on the current session’s choices. We adapt both to pairwise choices by showing the two personas with the highest sampled utilities, and we tune their prior variances like every comparison method (Appendix E). Drawing the second persona from a second posterior sample instead, as in double Thompson sampling, moves their excess regret by at most 2.1% in either direction on each evaluation roster, a small fraction of their distance to CODuel (Appendix E). Appendix E also reports AdaTS (Basu et al., 2021). Our CoLSTIM baseline (Bengs et al., 2022) learns each user independently from that user’s choices. Before each question it adds random noise to every persona’s estimated utility, with more noise for personas whose utility it knows less well, and shows the persona that ranks first after the noise. The noise has the distribu-tion that generates choices in the Bradley–Terry model, so this first pick imitates how a user would choose. The second persona is the one with the best optimistic chance of beating the first, judged by an upper confidence bound on their utility difference. We tune the noise scale together with its other constants. COLDB (Wang et al., 2025) groups users by estimated preferences and pools each group’s comparisons, and our variant COLDB-LR assigns a user to a cluster by a likelihood-ratio test. RUCB (Zoghi et al., 2014) is a non-contextual dueling bandit with one preference matrix pooled over all users and questions. Random pairs select uniformly among distinct persona pairs. 

## 5 METHOD 

We propose CODuel (collaborative dueling), which learns from completed sessions to adapt faster to each new user. A population model describes users’ preference vectors as a mixture of Gaussians fitted to the completed sessions. For a new user, the population model is the prior of a population learner , which updates its posterior after every choice and selects pairs with a lookahead pair rule :it accepts a pair with higher regret now when the user’s choice is expected to lower regret later in the session. An evidence gate lets the population learner select pairs only while it predicts completed users’ choices better than a no-transfer learner , which learns each user alone and selects pairs oth-erwise. Both learners observe every comparison and, in TutorDuel, use features ϕ(p, q ) whose rater score is recalibrated from completed sessions, as described in a paragraph below. Evidence gate. A population fitted to users who share little with the next one can pull that user’s estimate in the wrong direction. The gate therefore checks, on completed sessions, whether the pop-ulation learner predicts choices better than the no-transfer learner. Before each choice is revealed, both learners predict it. For user i, let ℓi be the log-likelihood ratio of the population learner’s pre-dictions to the no-transfer learner’s, summed over the user’s choices. After each session, the gate opens if at least nmin sessions are complete and the mean of ℓ1, . . . , ℓ n exceeds z standard errors, and closes otherwise. The population learner’s advantage can be small when the displayed answers have high but similar utilities, so we use a permissive gate, z = 0 .5 and nmin = 8 , chosen on the development roster. All main comparisons use α = 20 , at most Mmax = 16 learned components, and a question bank of 16 questions (sensitivity in Appendix F).

A population model can increase regret when its learned patterns do not apply to a new user. We test CODuel on two negative controls (Appendix F, Table 24): the simulator’s isotropic null population and a scrambled development roster. In the latter, we independently permute each user’s persona labels in the choice probabilities and utilities, keeping the permutation fixed throughout the session and leaving features unchanged. This disrupts shared persona preferences while preserving each 10 user’s preference patterns under relabeling. On both controls, CODuel’s excess regret stays within one percent of its no-transfer ablation’s, with 95% intervals that include zero. Without its gate, the same policy raises excess regret by 13% on the simulator’s null population and stays within noise on the scrambled roster (Appendix F, Table 25). We set the gate’s threshold on the development roster, before running either control.

## 7 LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION 

Evaluation and generalization. In TutorDuel, reference utilities are fitted from the same LLM judge’s pairwise probabilities that generate feedback. Human judgments are limited to two authors’ choices on 204 comparisons (Appendix C.5); the judge agrees with them at least as often as they agree with each other. On the three evaluation rosters, Jev and gpt-6-luna favor the same answer in 80.7% of comparisons excluding ties, and CODuel ranks first under both judges (Appendix C.6). We have not measured human satisfaction or educational outcomes. What students prefer need not be what best supports learning (Bjork & Bjork, 2011; Pashler et al., 2008; Puech et al., 2025). The benefits of transfer vary across held-out profiles, so average gains do not imply improvements for every profile (Table 9). Reported intervals do not account for the adaptive development process, and component studies on the development roster are exploratory (Appendix F). 

Modeling assumptions and guarantees. We assume a stationary population and fixed preference vectors within each session. Linear utilities and BTL feedback approximate the judge’s preferences, and Laplace inference and lookahead are approximate (Section 5). We provide no regret bound or guarantee against harmful transfer, including under preference drift, misspecified population models, or shifts in the question distribution. 

Conclusion. We introduced TutorDuel, a benchmark for online LLM personalization from pairwise choices, and CODuel, a baseline that learns a population prior from completed sessions. On students and topics held out from development, CODuel removes 44–48% of random pairs’ excess regret, more than the other evaluated methods, and achieves a little over half the performance of a per-user linear oracle. Future work should narrow this gap and evaluate the approach with human users. 

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work involves no human participants other than the authors. The student preferences in Tu-torDuel are simulated: an LLM role-plays students described by short written profiles, and the only human judgments are choices made by two authors to check the judge (Appendix C.5). We collected no personal data. Simulated preferences can differ from those of real students, and an LLM judge can carry biases from its training data, for example by associating a profile’s stated background with a teaching style. We therefore present TutorDuel as a testbed for learning algorithms rather than as evidence of what real students prefer. The system we study adapts the style of answers to the choices a student makes, and the style a student prefers need not be the one that best supports learning (Bjork & Bjork, 2011; Pashler et al., 2008). A deployed tutor should combine preference feedback with measures of learning and let students see and change the persona serving them. The questions and answers in the dataset were generated by LLMs and may contain errors, so we release them for research on personalization algorithms and not as teaching material. 

## REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT 

We release the TutorDuel dataset on Hugging Face and, on GitHub, the code and commands that rerun every experiment in the paper, the tuning grids and selection rule of the comparison meth-ods, and a script that computes the benchmark scores and confidence intervals from the run results. The dataset stores the questions, the tutor answers, the persona-question features, and the judges’ sampled choices and probabilities, including those of the second judge (Appendix C.6), so every experiment runs on CPUs without calling an LLM API. Rebuilding the dataset itself would require 11 the LLMs named in Section 4. That section describes how the dataset was built, and Appendices C and D give the tutor prompts, the question cues, the student profiles and templates, the judge’s proba-bility calculation, and the fit of the evaluation utilities. Section 5 and Appendix F.6 specify CODuel, with pseudocode in Algorithm 1. Appendix A defines the simulated environments, Appendix E lists the grids used to tune the comparison methods, and Appendix B describes the confidence intervals. All policies share the same users, questions, and random draws for a given seed, and every setting of CODuel was fixed on the development roster.

Length. Brevity is part of the concise persona’s style. The answer instruction asks it for 40 to 90 

words and the other personas for 100 to 180 , and lets every tutor leave that range when the question calls for it (Appendix D.4); on the held-out questions its answers have a median of 107 words, against 147 to 169 for the other personas. LLM judges can favor longer answers (Dubois et al., 2024). Holding the persona pair and the student profile fixed, our judge instead slightly prefers the shorter answer (-0.161 to -0.090 logits per standard deviation of the log length ratio). Across all comparisons, which mix length with the personas’ styles, the longer answer is preferred in 44% to 52% of them. 

Consequences for the benchmark. All policies face the same fixed table of choice probabilities, including its display-order effects and capped log-odds. Some question-specific variation may be difficult to predict from our features ϕ(p, q ), so excess regret can reflect limitations of the represen-tation as well as learning. C.3 RATER MODEL 

We send the rater one query per question. The query contains the question text and a short descrip-tion of each persona’s answer style, written from the persona’s system prompt (Appendix D.5). It contains no student profile, tutor answer, or choice. The query asks which style of answer would serve the student’s request worst, and the rater returns a probability π(p | q) for each of the six styles, rounded to two decimals. We set g(p, q ) = − log max {π(p | q), 10 −3}, so a style the rater considers unlikely to be the worst receives a high score, and the floor 10 −3 handles probabilities re-ported as zero. We then center g over the six personas of each question and divide it by its standard 20 deviation over all persona-question pairs of the development questions. The test questions use the same constant. We chose this query on the development roster among three variants: the one above, a positively phrased one asking which style would serve the request best, and one asking, for each persona, whether the student wants what that persona offers. The worst-style query best captured how each user’s preferences change from one question to the next. C.4 EVALUATION UTILITIES

Labeled comparisons. We drew 300 comparisons: 75 (student, question) cells, one question per arrival, spread over the five rosters and, within the development, held-out-topic and held-out-profile rosters, balanced over profiles; in each cell 4 of the 15 persona pairs, chosen so that the sample covers the judge’s range of confidence evenly. Both authors labeled every comparison of 51 cells, 204 comparisons in total. The interface showed the student profile, the question, and the two answers, exactly as in the judge’s prompt, in an independently randomized display order for each author, and asked which answer this student would rather receive; a choice was required. It never showed the judge’s output, the persona names, or the other author’s choice. The authors made 408 choices, 204 each. Throughout, a comparison counts once, with the two authors’ choices averaged, and we report 95% intervals from a bootstrap over (student, question) cells, since comparisons within a cell share a student and a question. 

The judge agrees with each author at least as often as the authors agree with each other. The fair reference for the judge is a second human who sees the same comparison. The two authors agree with each other on 69.6% of the comparisons (Cohen’s κ = 0 .39 ; Table 4a). The judge agrees with them on 74.0% and 73.0% ( κ = 0 .48 and 0.46), +3.9 points more often than the authors agree with each other (-2.5 to +10.0). Where the two authors agree, the judge picks their answer in 119 of 142 comparisons. The judge’s log-odds also rank the authors’ choices well, with an AUC of 0.79 (0.73 to 0.84). Disagreement with the judge is thus of the same size as disagreement between two careful readers of the same profile: which of two good answers a described student prefers is partly a matter of judgment. The second judge of Appendix C.6, TypeSafe Jev, agrees with the authors just as often (73.5%, against 73.5% for gpt-6-luna ), and the two judges agree with each other on 81.4% of the comparisons, more often than the two authors do.

Last sitting a word you had never seen made a crib useless until you had one example of the object. If you already know the drill you just want the shortest working. Stories and being asked to discover the answer make you anxious about the clock. You are trying to survive the exam, not become a mathematician. 

25 Student profile 2. mathematics major (development) 

You are a mathematics major, but last year a formal lecture on generating functions left you blank until a friend computed three small examples on a napkin. You still take pride in a real argument, and notation does not scare you once you know what the object is. Vague cheerleading does. When a result feels like a trick you want to see why anyone would believe it before you will read the write-up. You have stayed up finishing problem sets with friends who write every algebra step, and on those nights a careful calculation feels more honest than a sweeping claim. You still dislike being encouraged. You want to know what is actually true. A warm explanation that never says why it is allowed still feels empty. 

Student profile 3. anxious student (development) 

You are eighteen, first term at university, and you still half-expect someone to tell you the offer was a mistake. You reread the same paragraph four times and then close the laptop because your chest is tight. You do not want a pretty story and you do not want to be left alone with a riddle. You want a voice that stays with you, names what just happened in ordinary words, and does not make a joke of your panic. If they skip a line you assume it is because you are slow. Silence in a tutorial feels like drowning. You will take a slightly long, careful walkthrough from someone who sounds kind. If they ask why a rule is allowed you still want those ordinary words for what just happened, not a flourish. You will not take a flourish, and you will not take being put on the spot before you have even copied the heading. 

Student profile 4. self-reconstructing student (development) 

You are the kind of student who pauses lectures to try the next step yourself. Being handed a finished solution feels like someone spoiling a puzzle, and you do not remember things you did not reconstruct. Dense crib sheets go in a drawer. Sunday night before a deadline you have a short fuse: if you already understand the idea and you only need a number, being quizzed feels like contempt, and you want the shortest working on the page so you can move. You will sit through a careful calculation when you are truly stuck and the hints have gone in a circle. Reconstruction is the default, not a religion. 

Student profile 5. student who reads for the ideas (development) 

You are taking this because the ideas are beautiful, not because you need the credit. You read popular maths books for fun. A tutor who only pushes symbols feels like they missed why anyone would care. You like metaphors, history, and a sense of what is actually going on. You still want to leave with a picture of why the idea is beautiful. You once trusted a pretty story that turned out to be wrong, and you still wince. When you need to know whether a claim is actually true, or why a rule is allowed, a metaphor is not enough to bet on — you want the real argument, even if it is dry. Formal write-ups are welcome when they illuminate. You have little patience for classroom cheerleading or for notes that look like an exam crib. A pretty story is no substitute for the working either. 

Student profile 6. busy student (development) 

You are thirty-four, you work full time, and you are watching a lecture on your phone between meetings. You already have a degree in something else, so a lot of this is foreign vocabulary sitting on top of skills you do have. You are not fragile and you are not here for pedagogy experiments. If it looks like something you already use at work you want the usable bit and then you close the laptop. If it is a word you have never said in a meeting, a cheat sheet you cannot interpret is a waste; you want one picture that tells you what you are looking at, then the formula. You have sat through a proof of a word you could not yet picture, and you will not do that again. Warmth still reads as padding. You will not do extra exercises for the vibe.

You would rather a slightly long explanation you can follow than a short one you have to decode. Being quizzed before you have a path still panics you. 

Student profile 8. software engineer (development)

Student profile 9. student retaking the course (test) 

You are retaking this course after failing it last year. You are not stupid, you froze. What helps you now is being told exactly what to write and why it is right, in that order; anything that wanders makes you feel the clock again. You will read a longer answer if every line earns its place, and you have stopped trusting encouragement that is not attached to a concrete step. 

Student profile 10. self-taught programmer (test) 

You taught yourself to program from documentation and forum answers, and you are filling in the mathematics that your work now needs. You read quickly, skip what you already know, and get irritated by explanations that start from zero. What convinces you is a small case you can run in your head, followed by the general statement. You do not need reassurance; you need the idea to compile. You would rather see one line of the general rule after the case than three paragraphs before it. 

Student profile 11. adult returning to study (test) 

You are forty-one, back in a classroom after fifteen years in a job that used none of this. Vocabulary is the wall: a term you have never heard turns the rest of the paragraph to noise. You want one picture or comparison that tells you what kind of thing you are looking at, then the formula, then a plain example. You are patient and you will reread, but you resent being talked down to. 

Student profile 12. olympiad student (test) 

You are seventeen and you do competition mathematics for fun. You want the real reason a thing is true, stated cleanly, and you are bored by procedures you could have found yourself. A clever short argument delights you; a numbered recipe with reassuring remarks feels like being handed the answer key. You do not mind notation, and you notice when a hypothesis is quietly missing. Being told to try it yourself is fine when the hint is sharp; a vague nudge just wastes your evening. 

27 Student profile 13. humanities graduate student (test) 

You are a graduate student in history who needs this material for a methods requirement. You learn by being asked what an idea means, and by answering in a sentence of your own before anyone writes a formula. A metaphor you cannot rephrase is decoration. A finished write-up you only read does not stay with you. You like a question you can actually answer, and you shut down if they ask you to compute before anyone has shown you how. You are not looking for a numbered recipe or a picture. You are looking for the moment you can say the point yourself. 

Student profile 14. engineer with a deadline (test) 

You are an engineer with a deadline. You will not touch a cheat sheet, and you will not sit through a pile of examples. Student profile 15. teacher preparing a class (test) 

You are a secondary-school teacher preparing to explain this to a class next week. The traits take the following values.

28 • Calculation wish: “one fully worked numerical example”; “careful algebraic steps”; “a compressed procedure”; “numbered steps you can imitate”. • Why-question wish: “a real proof or proof sketch”; “the idea in plain words and a picture”; “one sentence of justification”; “questions that let you reconstruct the argument”.

D.3 QUESTION CONSTRUCTION 

Each session covers four topics with two chains of three questions each: a definition question, a computation or numerical example, and a why-question. The second chain of a topic asks about a different aspect of it. The session asks the four first chains, then the four second chains. Every question ends with one of the following 13 statements about the student’s situation or needs. We draw one uniformly from the statements for that question type, using a fixed seed.              

> Statements in definition questions
> •“This vocabulary is new to me and a formula with no picture is not landing.”
> •“I have never seen one written out with actual numbers.”
> •“I need a line I can quote in an exam.”
> •“I have heard the story version already; I want the real statement.”
> Statements in computation questions
> •“I already know the rule exists; I just need the working.”
> •“Show me one so I can copy the method.”
> •“I need a write-up I can hand in.”
> •“I need a number I can use, not a lecture.”
> •“I got a different result twice and I cannot see where I go wrong.”
> Statements in why-questions
> •“I can use it but I do not see why it is allowed.”
> •“I believed this in school; I want to know why it is true.”
> •“I want the actual argument, not another walkthrough.”
> •“I already believe the simple case; I want to know why it holds in general.”

D.4 TUTOR GENERATION PROMPTS 

For each question, the system message contains one persona prompt below, then a blank line, then the shared answer instruction. The user message contains the question. We sample one answer per persona and question at temperature 1. These are the full tutor prompts used for every roster. Each question is answered independently, without earlier questions or answers in the generation context. 29 Shared answer instruction 

Answer the student’s question below. Write between 100 and 180 words (between 40 and 90 words if your style is the compact cheat-sheet). Plain text with light LaTeX-free notation; no headings. The student’s message may say what they already know or what they need. Take it into account, including for the length of your answer if needed (you may then leave the word range above), while keeping your own teaching style. 

Rigorous prover 

You are a mathematics tutor who teaches through rigor. Always start from precise definitions, state results as claims and prove them or sketch the proof, use standard notation, and point out where hypotheses are needed. Avoid analogies and avoid chatty language. Do not ask the student questions. 

Worked-examples tutor 

You are a tutor who teaches through concrete worked examples. Answer by immediately working through one or two specific numerical or concrete examples in full detail, then state the general pattern briefly at the end. Keep notation light. Do not ask the student questions. 

Socratic guide 

You are a Socratic tutor. Do not give the full answer directly. Instead, guide the student with a short sequence of leading questions and small hints, each building on the previous, so that they can reach the answer themselves. Give the key idea only at the end, in one sentence. 

Intuition-and-analogy tutor 

You are a tutor who teaches through intuition, analogies and big-picture explanations. Explain the idea in plain language with a vivid analogy or mental picture, say why the idea matters, and keep formulas to a minimum. Do not ask the student questions. 

Concise cheat-sheet 

You are a tutor who answers in the most compact way possible: a short bullet-point summary with the key definition, the key formula or rule, and one line on when it applies. No analogies, no questions, no encouragement, no examples beyond one line. Aim for under 90 words. 

Patient step-by-step coach 

You are a patient, encouraging tutor. Answer with a clearly numbered step-by-step procedure the student can follow, explain the purpose of each step in simple words, warn about common mistakes, and end with a short encouraging remark. Do not ask the student questions.

The rater receives the student’s question as its only input, under the key student message , and answers the query below. Its six options are the persona style descriptions, keyed by persona. 

Rater query 

Which style of answer would serve what the student asks for in `student message ` worst?

We compare learning policies on the same user and question sequences, with one binary choice per round and the same number of rounds. Each policy observes choices only on the pairs it selects. In TutorDuel, contextual methods receive the same persona indicators and rater scores; CODuel learns its recalibration from completed sessions. Policies start each roster without learned user preferences and do not receive student profiles or evaluation utilities. The subsections below describe their adaptations, tuning, and additional checks. E.3.1 COLDB AND COLDB-LR COLDB (Wang et al., 2025) pools comparisons within groups of users. Our sequential implementa-tion connects each new user to all earlier users and removes links when their individual preference estimates differ by more than a distance threshold, which decreases as they make more choices. To support grouping from short sessions, we also evaluate COLDB-LR. It keeps COLDB’s pooled logistic model and pair-selection rule but changes group membership using a likelihood-ratio cri-terion. Each user starts alone and, before its first choice, uses the model pooled over earlier users. After user i has made ni choices, it joins the group that best predicts those choices, provided that the 36 group’s log likelihood is no more than tol ( d/ 2)[1 + log(1 + ni)] below that of its own fitted model. Group estimates exclude that user’s data. If no group qualifies, the user is isolated. We tune tol as in Table 17. In the numerical simulator, COLDB’s excess regret at b = 20 is close to Pooled’s (Table 16). We therefore also checked longer sessions. On the two-type, 90 ◦ population with β = 4 (16 seeds), COLDB with distance and exploration scales both set to 0.03 produced an average of 2.0 clusters at b = 80 , with excess regret 865 versus 2489 for Pooled. With both scales set to 1, it retained one cluster at b = 160 . COLDB-LR with tolerance 0.5 and exploration scale 0.3 produced an average of 3.2 clusters at b = 40 .E.3.2 HYPERPARAMETER TUNING 

Table 17 lists the grids searched for CoLSTIM, COLDB, COLDB-LR, RUCB, Meta-TS, HierTS, and AdaTS, and for the margin of the latent-bandit UCB ablation of CODuel, with the chosen configurations. Each configuration is run on 16 tuning seeds separate from those used for benchmark evaluation, on the development roster for the tutoring benchmark and on the 16 structured b =20 anchors for the simulator, and scored by its excess regret relative to no transfer averaged over worlds. The chosen configuration of each method is then used for every reported comparison in that environment. When the best configuration lies on the edge of a grid, the grid is extended along that axis and the selection is redone before any evaluation result of the method is read: Meta-TS’s grid covers 63 configurations after this step. Three selections stay on an edge: RUCB’s α = 0 .51 , since its analysis requires α > 1/2; AdaTS’s smallest prior scale, which approaches pooling all users into one model; and CoLSTIM’s smallest noise scale on the simulator, where the perturbation does not help and smaller scales approach the unperturbed leader (we extended this axis once, from 0.1 down to 0.01 , and the ratio kept falling). E.3.3 SETTINGS CHOSEN ON THE EVALUATION ROSTERS 

The comparison methods were tuned on the development roster, so their settings may suit the de-velopment students better than the new ones. To measure how much this costs them, we reran every configuration of the grids of Table 17 on the three evaluation rosters and kept, for each method and roster, the configuration with the lowest excess regret on that roster (Table 18). These configurations are chosen on the evaluation data, so their scores are optimistic. Even so, the best configuration of any comparison method removes 34% of random pairs’ excess regret on the held-out profiles, where CODuel removes 48%. On Composite and Quirky the best comparison methods reach 39% and 40%, against 44% and 46% for CODuel. COLDB gains most from the second chance: its de-velopment setting removes 22% on the held-out profiles and its best setting there removes 32%. The three methods that learn a population mean, Meta-TS, HierTS, and AdaTS, keep more excess regret than the no-transfer ablation on the held-out profiles whatever their prior variances. We also looked beyond the edge of RUCB’s grid. A larger α makes RUCB explore more, and the extra exploration raised its excess regret on every roster, so the smallest admissible value remains its best setting. We change one component of CODuel at a time on the development roster ( b = 24 ), keeping every other parameter of the reported configuration. All policies see the same users, questions and clicks for a given seed. Regret values are excess strong regret RT − GT on the common evaluation scale. These studies are descriptive: the development roster was also used for design. F.1 ONE -COMPONENT CHANGES 

Table 21 reports how much the excess regret of CODuel rises when a component is changed. Its first row removes every component that transfers information across users, leaving the no-transfer learner (+449.7). Removing only the population, so that the same learner, with the recalibrated scores, restarts for every user, costs the most (+386.2). One tight type instead of the mixture costs +157.7, while one Gaussian with learned covariance is within noise of the mixture (-2.5, 95% interval -14.3 to +9.3). Without recalibration, excess regret rises by +41.2 (95% interval +30.2 to +52.2), and a smaller fallback concentration raises it by +22.1 (+3.8 to +40.3). Latent-bandit Thompson sampling and UCB over the same components (Hong et al., 2020) cost +213.0 and +128.5, because a sampled or optimistic component’s favorite pair is paid in strong regret whenever that component is wrong. The greedy pair rule (+5.2, 95% interval -7.9 to +18.2), the moment M-step (+3.7) and removing the evidence gate (+8.1) are each within noise of the reported configuration on this roster. Posterior updates and predictions. The no-transfer learner and each component of the population learner use a Laplace approximation: a Gaussian centered at the posterior mode, with covariance equal to the inverse Hessian of the negative log posterior there, recomputed after each choice from all of the current session’s comparisons. where σ is the logistic function of (2); we evaluate the expectation with a probit approximation. The EM procedure holds the isotropic component’s mean and covariance fixed. Its M-step pools the weighted comparisons instead of averaging the users’ posterior means, because a narrow com-ponent prior can keep users’ posterior means near its current mean and so limit updates based on their average (the moment M-step of Table 21 averages them). We run three EM-style iterations after each completed session. The first pass starts from a single learned Gaussian with the mean and covariance of the first session’s Laplace posterior under the isotropic prior. Each pooled ridge logistic fit uses prior precision Id/v 0. Learned component covariances receive a 10 −3Id ridge for numerical stability. We remove learned components whose total responsibility across users is below one, retaining the component with the largest total if all fall below this threshold. We normalize the retained totals to obtain πk.After each pass, we select the session with the largest positive difference between its approximate log marginal likelihood under the isotropic component and the maximum over learned components. If such a session exists, we initialize a new component from its Laplace posterior under the isotropic prior and run three more EM-style iterations. We repeat this process, with at most three such ad-ditions per session and at most 16 learned components. While no learned component exists, the lookahead reduces to the greedy rule. The recalibration fits a logistic model of each completed comparison: the log-odds that pt, 1 is chosen equal (ept, 1 − ept, 2 )⊤(ξ + ξit ) + ( A g (qt)) pt, 1 − (A g (qt)) pt, 2 , where ξ ∈ RK is the persona offset shared by all users and ξi the offset of user i. Besides the penalty on A′ (Section 5), the ridge penalties are 10 −3 on ξ and a and 1 on each ξi, and each refit starts from the previous solution.
