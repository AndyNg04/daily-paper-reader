Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong zero-shot reasoning across diverse domains, but their
potential for Human-Object Interaction (HOI) remains unexplored. Unlike traditional HOI approaches that rely on extensive
labeled datasets and fixed interaction taxonomies, MLLMs offer flexible reasoning, making them well-suited for dynamic, real-
world scenarios. In this work, we present the first systematic study of MLLMs for HOI recognition and detection within a
pipeline that combines an external object detector for entity localization with zero-shot multimodal reasoning for interaction
understanding. We introduce a benchmark framework for principled zero-shot evaluation, consisting of (i) a unified taxonomy
of zero-shot prompting strategies, and (ii) a methodology that transforms token-level logits into interaction scores, enabling
fair comparison with specialized HOI models under standard metrics. To support prompt-based evaluation, we additionally
construct new question–answer annotations for standard HOI datasets, transforming them into a format suitable for MLLMs.
Building on this, we propose two visual prompting strategies, namely Objects as Context and Black Other Objects, that guide
MLLMs to target objects while preserving scene context. Experiments on HICO, HICO-DET, and V-COCO demonstrate state-
of-the-art zero-shot performance, achieving 53.50 mAP on HICO and 23.69 mAP on HICO-DET. Overall, our work provides
a principled benchmarking framework for MLLMs in HOI, opening the door to new models and datasets and enabling flexible
open-world interaction understanding. Code and adapted datasets are publicly available1.

In this work, we investigate MLLMs for Human-Object Interaction (HOI), a core problem in scene under-

Traditional HOI methods (Yuan et al. (2023), Yang et al. (2023a), Kim et al. (2023)) rely on large labeled
datasets and pre-defined sets of interaction classes. However, such closed-world assumptions limit their ability
to generalize: real-world scenarios often contain unseen interactions, novel objects, or evolving vocabularies.
For HOI to be broadly useful in practice models must adapt beyond fixed taxonomies.
Prior work on HOI generalization has attempted to address this challenge by withholding certain classes
during training and evaluating transfer performance (Shen et al. (2018)). However, these approaches remain
constrained by labeled training sets and by evaluation protocols that only partially capture open-world con-
ditions. In contrast, MLLMs can operate without task-specific training: by combining multimodal reasoning
with natural-language instructions, they enable flexible and interactive zero-shot recognition (Zhang et al.

Despite the promise of MLLMs for open-world HOI, a major obstacle is the absence of benchmarks that
can fairly evaluate their capabilities. To close this gap, we design the first evaluation framework for MLLMs
tailored to HOI recognition and detection in a zero-shot setting.
These benchmarks enable systematic
comparison with prior HOI methods and provide a foundation for studying MLLMs in realistic open-world
conditions.
A key challenge in applying MLLMs to HOI is how to formulate prompts and measure their predictions.
To address the prompt design issue, we propose a unified taxonomy of zero-shot prompting strategies for
classification and adapt public datasets into a question-answering format suitable for MLLMs. To measure
the predictions, we design a method that converts token-level logits into classification scores, allowing direct
use of standard metrics such as mAP and ensuring rigorous and fair comparison with specialized HOI models.

several lightweight MLLMs. In addition, we introduce two visual prompting methods, namely Objects as

preserving scene context. Overall, we establish strong zero-shot baselines, opening a path toward flexible
open-world interaction understanding.

(1) A benchmark framework for evaluating MLLMs in HOI, including (i) a unified taxonomy of
zero-shot prompting strategies for classification, and (ii) a method that derives classification scores
from token logits, enabling the first direct comparison with specialized HOI models.

2
Related work
2.1
Multimodal Large Language Models
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) extend Large Language Models (LLMs) to process multimodal
inputs, typically text and images. Unlike earlier Vision–Language Models (VLMs), which commonly use fixed
encoders and shallow fusion mechanisms for text–image alignment, MLLMs integrate an LLM backbone into
the architecture (Yang et al. (2024), Cai et al. (2024)). This enables conversational interaction, instruction
following, and interleaved multimodal reasoning (Lin et al. (2024)). Advances in high-resolution encoding
(Dong et al. (2024), Dehghani et al. (2023)) and efficient backbones have led to competitive models (Yao
et al. (2024), Li et al. (2024), Wang et al. (2024)).
These models have shown broad versatility across domains, including document parsing (Liu et al. (2024),
Hu et al. (2024)), medical image interpretation (Li et al. (2023), Moor et al. (2023)), and visual reasoning
(Liu et al. (2023), Zhu et al. (2023)). Their extensive generalist pretraining makes them effective in zero-shot
and open-world settings.
Despite these strengths, MLLMs have not yet been systematically explored for Human–Object Interaction
(HOI), a vision task that requires reasoning about localized entities and interaction vocabularies. Existing
attempts with vision–language backbones use fixed matching paradigms and lack instruction-following capa-
bilities (Ning et al. (2023), Mao et al. (2023)), leaving open the question of how MLLMs perform in zero-shot
HOI recognition and detection.
2.2
Zero-shot prompting strategies for MLLMs
Early zero-shot work in vision–language models began with CLIP (Radford et al. (2021)), which showed that
classification could be achieved without task-specific training. However, CLIP’s inability to follow detailed
instructions or perform multi-step reasoning limited its applicability to complex tasks. These limitations
motivated the use of MLLMs for zero-shot inference, particularly in Visual Question Answering (VQA).
Prompting strategies are central to unlocking zero-shot performance and fairly benchmarking MLLMs. They
can be broadly divided into textual and visual approaches. Textual strategies aim to improve reasoning
and reliability, for instance by generating diverse candidate prompts or constructing them automatically
(Lan et al. (2023)), enriching them with external knowledge (Cao and Jiang (2024), Lin et al. (2022)), or
adopting chain-of-thought templates for step-by-step reasoning (Zhang et al. (2023b)). Complementarily,
visual prompting modifies the visual input to guide attention, using techniques such as cropping, masking,
or altering background context (Shtedritski et al. (2023)).
Despite their promise, most evaluations of these strategies remain confined to VQA benchmarks (Marino
et al. (2019), Lu et al. (2022), Goyal et al. (2017)). Although extensions to classification tasks are scarce,
studies such as Gupta et al. (2024) have explored action recognition with MLLMs, but these efforts typically
lack classification score distributions. Without such outputs, standard metrics like mean Average Precision
(mAP) cannot be computed, hindering direct comparison with specialized baselines in HOI.

Finally, while several taxonomies of prompting strategies have been proposed (Chen et al. (2023), Wu et al.
(2024b)), they primarily target textual reasoning or VQA. No prior work systematically unifies textual and
visual prompting for classification settings. Our work addresses this gap by introducing a unified taxonomy
and evaluation framework tailored to zero-shot HOI.
2.3
Zero-shot Human-Object Interaction recognition and detection
Human–Object Interaction (HOI) tasks study how humans and objects interact in images. While recognition
focuses on classifying interactions from a fixed set, detection additionally requires localizing both humans
and objects. These tasks are particularly challenging due to the large number of possible interactions and
the long-tailed, skewed class distributions found in real-world data.
Early HOI recognition and detection methods have advanced through benchmarks such as HICO (Chao et al.
(2015)) and HICO-DET/V-COCO (Chao et al. (2018), Gupta and Malik (2015)), driving progress in both
fully supervised and partially zero-shot settings. Partially zero-shot protocols withhold subsets of HOI classes
during training (Shen et al. (2018), Ma et al. (2022)), testing limited generalization to unseen interactions.
More recently, CLIP-based approaches (Liao et al. (2022), Mao et al. (2023)) leverage vision–language
pretraining for transfer to unseen categories, demonstrating the potential of multimodal representations for
open-world HOI. However, these models still depend on task-specific training subsets and struggle with
complex scene-level reasoning.
HOI detection methods are commonly two-stage or one-stage. Two-stage approaches detect humans and
objects first, then classify interactions for candidate pairs (Liu et al. (2020), Zhang et al. (2022b)), whereas
one-stage methods predict interactions and detect objects jointly in an end-to-end model (Liao et al. (2022),
Zou et al. (2021)).
In contrast, our work applies instruction-following MLLMs to HOI in a pure zero-shot setting. By removing
reliance on labeled HOI training data, our benchmarking setup directly tests models under open-world
conditions.

3.1
Textual prompting methods
Textual prompting focuses on applying specific techniques to the textual part of the multimodal input, so
that the MLLM can improve classification by making use of the proper inherent knowledge or the necessary
additional information. The prompting methods are categorized into:
• Templates: Textual templates consist of reusing specific patterns or sentences that have been deemed
useful in similar tasks. Specific templates include role prompting, use of quotes to separate parts of
the prompt, and Chain-of-Thought (Kojima et al. (2022)) prompts. Recent methods offer adaptability
to multiple tasks, such as pattern catalog (White et al. (2023)) and meta prompting (Zhang et al.
(2023a)). Pattern catalog provides an organized collection of prompt templates, while meta prompting
focuses on structuring problem formulation.
• External knowledge: Concatenating updated or specialized data into the prompt as context is
crucial for tasks where the model lacks the required knowledge or information. Providing or retrieving
information from a corpus, the Internet or specialized tools fall into this category.
• Ensemble: Methods that query the LLM in a parallel way belong to this category. Widely used
methods include resampling and parallel chain-of-thought-like methods. To fuse the different answers
obtained voting strategies and frequency metrics are commonly used.
• Multi-step: Multi-step methods query the MLLM several times in a sequential way. We distinguish
two strategies: Generated Knowledge and Task Decomposition. Generated Knowledge takes advantage
of the knowledge of the MLLM obtained through previous queries and inputs it as context for the final
query (e.g. image captioning or reasoning from Zhang et al. (2023b)). Task Decomposition splits the
problem into sub-tasks and merges all the sub-tasks solutions to solve the main task.

3.2
Visual prompting methods
Visual prompting methods manipulate images and videos to provide additional information, enhancing the
visual perception capabilities of the MLLM. Visual prompting strategies are categorized into Markers, Pixel-
level Representations, Saliency methods and Contextual Information.
• Markers: Overlay markers such as bounding boxes, circles, arrows, and dots to highlight, identify,
or draw attention to particular regions in the visual input. Markers help the MLLM understand the
image content and correlate it with the corresponding text.
• Pixel-level representations: Overlay fine-grained pixel-level information on the visual input, in
opposition to coarse markers or boxes. Object contour highlighting and semantic segmentation masking
are examples of this category.
• Saliency: Modify or delete non-relevant parts of the visual input. Compared to other categories,
saliency removes potentially misleading information instead of emphasizing relevant regions. Back-
ground adjustments fall into this category (e.g. blurring the background).
• Contextual Information: Provide the MLLM with additional visual inputs as context, encapsulating
relevant information from the original visual input. In this category, the original visual input is not
modified; instead, additional information is fed to the MLLM, such as crops of relevant objects.
3.3
Hybrid methods
Combinations of textual and/or visual prompting methods are possible, as most methods are not mutually
exclusive. As an example of a hybrid method, Zhang et al. (2023b) proposes Multimodal Chain-of-thought,
a two-stage method that uses chain-of-thought templates for the generation of reasoning and inputs it as
generated knowledge.
4
Methodology
In this section, we present our methodology for leveraging MLLMs in zero-shot HOI recognition and de-
tection. Section 4.1 presents our HOI recognition method, including object recognition, MLLM querying,
and probability computation. Section 4.2 describes a pipeline that incorporates object detection and object
specification strategies. We also introduce two visual prompting methods (Objects as Context and Black
Other Objects) designed to guide the model’s attention while maintaining scene context.

Interaction queries are constructed by expressing each interaction in gerund form (e.g., holding, carrying)
and inserting it into the template: “Is there anybody {gerund} a {object}? Answer with yes or no.”. Given
this instruction, the MLLM is prompted to produce a single-word answer, either affirmative or negative. This
formulation yields one independent prediction for each object–interaction pair, allowing multiple interactions
to be recognized simultaneously for the same object class.
Although the MLLM produces a textual output, we do not use the actual generated text to compute clas-
sification scores. This is because the textual answer only provides a single binary label, which does not
produce a probability distribution reflecting the underlying confidence of the model. Instead, we extract the
pre-softmax logits of the first generated token, which is sufficient because this token determines whether the
answer is affirmative or negative.
We then constrain the vocabulary to a set of affirmative tokens A and negative tokens N and compute
probabilities over this restricted set using a standard softmax. This effectively treats the restricted logits as
a complete vocabulary and allows us to produce classification scores from the likelihood of an affirmative
answer versus a negative answer. The specific contents of the sets A and N are discussed in Section 5.2.1.

overloading the input with less relevant detections, only the k1 most confidently detected target objects and
the k2 largest persons (foreground) are included, where k1 and k2 are hyperparameters. Figure 4 illustrates
the resulting architecture.
4.2
HOI Detection
HOI Detection extends HOI Recognition by requiring not only the classification of which interactions occur,
but also the localization of the human–object pairs involved. In recognition, the object detector is used only
for filtering and does not constrain the MLLM from handling the task end-to-end. In detection, however, it
becomes an integral component of the pipeline, providing bounding boxes for both humans and objects.
Formally, given an input image x ∈X, let H = {h1, ..., hNh} denote the set of detected human bounding
boxes and B = {o1, ..., oNo} the set of detected object bounding boxes provided by the external detector.
The task is to predict, for each human–object pair ⟨hn, om⟩and each interaction j ∈I′
om, a probability
indicating whether the interaction occurs. Since multiple interactions can co-occur for the same pair (e.g
holding and carrying a bicycle), the task is inherently multi-label at the pair level.

Our method follows a two-stage approach: the object detector first provides bounding boxes for humans
and objects, after which the MLLM evaluates candidate pairs ⟨hn, om⟩. To reduce inference cost and avoid
including less relevant detections, we do not evaluate all possible pairs. Instead, we first discard humans
and objects whose detection confidence falls below the threshold τ at instance level. Drawing inspiration
from the strategy Objects as Context, we use the tresholds k1 and k2, retaining only the k1 most confidently
detected objects and the k2 largest detected persons.
The interaction classification stage mirrors the procedure described for HOI Recognition: queries are ex-
pressed in gerund form and appended with the instruction “Answer with yes or no.”, logits of the first token
are extracted, and probabilities are computed over the affirmative (A) and negative (N) token sets. The
score for no interaction is computed following the same formulation as in Equation 2, where applicable.
The main challenge is then to specify to the MLLM which human and object are being queried. To address
this, we explore different visual and textual prompting strategies for object specification.
Among these strategies, we propose two visual prompting methods. Objects as Context is adapted from
recognition to only filter the specified object and person. Black Other Objects (a Saliency method) masks all
detected objects other than the queried human and object, aiming to reduce distractions while preserving
the scene background.
In addition, we also test established object-specification strategies adapted from
Shtedritski et al. (2023). A complete list of all strategies along with their taxonomy in Section 3 and their
use in detection appears in Table 2.

5
Experiments
5.1
Implementation details
The object detector used is the extra-large YOLO11 model (Khanam and Hussain (2024)), pre-trained on
the MS-COCO dataset (Lin et al. (2014)).
For HOI recognition, the hyperparameters are set to τ = 0.1, k1 = 10, and k2 = 3 (see Section 5.5). For HOI
detection, k1 and k2 remain unchanged, while τ is increased to 0.5 to reduce the number of MLLM queries.
Inference uses greedy decoding with a single output token and is run on three NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090
GPUs. Additional inference hyperparameters are listed in the Appendix.
5.2
HOI recognition
We construct a HICO Q&A dataset by adding question–answer annotations for each object–interaction pair,
with an illustrative example shown in Figure 3. The dataset (Chao et al. (2015)) contains 47,776 images
annotated with 600 HOI categories (combinations of 117 action verbs and 80 object categories from MS-
COCO), and the original split provides 38,118 training and 9,658 test images. In the pure zero-shot evaluation
protocol, only the test set is used, with no human–object interactions for fine-tuning. This setting effectively
mimics an open-world environment and, to our knowledge, has not previously been applied to the HICO
dataset.
Performance is measured by the mean Average Precision (mAP) over HOI classes, as proposed in the original
paper, because HOI categories are not mutually exclusive.
5.2.1
Baseline and score soundness
We evaluate the lightweight versions of state-of-the-art MLLM families, namely MiniCPM-V (Yao et al.
(2024)), LLaVA-OneVision (Li et al. (2024)), Phi-3.5-Vision (Abdin et al. (2024)), InternVL2.5 (Chen et al.
(2024d;c;b)), and Qwen2-VL (Bai et al. (2023), Wang et al. (2024)). These models vary in how they answer
the prompt “Answer with yes or no”, with differences mainly in capitalization (Yes” vs. yes” or No” vs.
no”). To ensure consistency and fair comparison across models, we standardize evaluation by defining the
affirmative and negative token sets as A = {“yes”, “Yes”} and N = {“no”, “No”}.
Although the classification score in Equation (1) is computed from the logits restricted to A ∪N, it is
important that the tokens in these sets also receive the highest probability under the model’s distribution.
This condition ensures that the restricted softmax faithfully reflects the model’s intended prediction. To

5.3
HOI detection
We conduct experiments on two datasets for HOI detection: HICO-DET (Chao et al. (2018)) and V-COCO
(Gupta and Malik (2015)). Based on these datasets, we construct HICO-DET Q&A and V-COCO Q&A
12


O. Rabasseda et al.

versions by adding question–answer annotations for each object–interaction pair, enabling evaluation with
MLLMs in a prompt-based format (an illustrative example is shown in Figure 4).
HICO-DET extends the HICO dataset with 151,276 annotated human–object pairs across 47,776 images,
providing bounding boxes for both humans and objects together with interaction labels. The split contains
38,118 training and 9,658 test images.
Evaluation follows the standard mean Average Precision (mAP)
metric: a detection is considered correct when both the human and object boxes have an Intersection-over-
Union (IoU) above 0.5 with the ground truths. Results are reported for three category sets: Full (all 600
HOI categories), Rare (138 categories with fewer than ten training instances), and Non-Rare (462 categories
with at least ten instances). Evaluations are conducted in a pure zero-shot setting, where no human–object
interactions from HICO-DET are used for fine-tuning. Pure zero-shot results on HICO-DET have previously
been reported (Yuan et al. (2022)), providing a useful reference point for our benchmark.
V-COCO is a subset of MS-COCO containing 10,346 images (2,533 train, 2,867 validation, 4,946 test)
annotated with 26 action categories. Each action is associated with one or two object roles (e.g., “cut” may
involve both knife and food). As in HICO-DET, Average Precision is computed for each action–role pair
and the mean is reported. The official evaluation code (Gupta and Malik (2015)) defines two variants for
handling missing object-role annotations: (1) AP #1
role, where the object is considered occluded and a dummy
bounding box must be predicted, and (2) AP #2
role, where the object role is treated as out of scope and no
bounding box is required. In our pipeline, the MLLM is evaluated without using any V-COCO supervision,
while the object detector is pre-trained on MS-COCO. As a result, the pure zero-shot setting strictly applies
to the MLLM, while the overall pipeline benefits from an object detector trained on data from the same
distribution.
To enable MLLM evaluation, we generate a question–answer version of each dataset. For HICO-DET, we
create a natural language question for each human–object pair and interaction, formatted to produce a
yes/no answer. Because the pipeline requires a predefined set of possible HOI categories with specific object
classes and interactions to prompt the MLLM and V-COCO defines only action–role pairs, we construct the
set of HOI categories from the action–object combinations found in the trainval split. This adaptation does
not alter the official evaluation metrics and ensures fair comparison with other methods.
Table 6 summarizes the performance of the prompting strategies listed in Table 2. Across both HICO-DET
and V-COCO the trends are consistent: (1) passing object detections as text (Textual Center Coordinates and
Boundary Coordinates) or overlaying bounding boxes/elliptic markers degrades performance; (2) saliency-
based modifications have mixed effects: Gray the Background and Crop are neutral or negative, whereas Blur
the Background and Black Other Objects consistently improve results; and (3) adding Objects as Context
provides a modest boost. Overall, these three strategies are the only ones that show positive impact across
all models, with Qwen2-VL 2B + Blur the Background emerging as the best configuration on both datasets.
Although the qualitative trends are similar across datasets, the magnitude of the improvements is substan-
tially larger on V-COCO. We attribute this difference to the higher interaction density of V-COCO: on
average, its images contain 6.04 detected objects and 4.46 interactions, compared with 5.25 detected objects
and 2.10 interactions in HICO-DET. Isolating the target human–object region may be particularly helpful
in scenes containing many simultaneous interactions, as it reduces ambiguity among multiple candidate in-
teractions. Apart from the larger magnitude of the changes in V-COCO, some methods also behave slightly
differently across the two datasets. Crop improves over the corresponding baseline on V-COCO, whereas it
is neutral or detrimental on HICO-DET. Black Other Objects consistently improves performance by remov-
ing potentially distracting visual information. However, in interaction-dense scenes, masking several objects
may introduce sharp artificial boundaries. One possible interpretation is that the vision encoders of different
MLLMs respond differently to these artifacts. Although our experiments do not isolate this factor, it could
explain the model-dependent performance differences observed on V-COCO for Black Other Objects.

5.4
HOI detection with ground truth objects
To further isolate the effect of the benchmarked prompting strategies, we introduce a pure zero-shot eval-
uation protocol that leverages ground-truth bounding boxes on HICO-DET. This protocol disentangles the
contribution of object detection errors from interaction classification performance, allowing a more direct
assessment of object specification strategies. While this evaluation setup has previously been applied to
supervised HOI methods (Gao et al. (2018), Zhang et al. (2022b)), our work is the first to explore it in a
pure zero-shot regime. In addition to reporting the quantitative results, we provide a qualitative analysis of
the proposed visual prompting methods to better understand their behavior.

5.5
Ablation studies
To better understand the effects and robustness of the different components of the proposed HOI recognition
pipeline, we perform a series of ablation studies on the HICO dataset using mainly Qwen2-VL 2B. These
experiments analyze how different components and hyperparameters influence HOI recognition performance
and evaluation computational cost. Specifically, we study the computation of the no interaction score, the
impact of the object detector model, the effect of object-class filtering through the threshold τ, and the role
of the context-selection parameters k1 and k2.
5.5.1
No-interaction score
We analyze the behavior of the no interaction computation by splitting the 600 HICO classes into 520
interacting classes and 80 no interaction classes. The interacting subset reaches 57.87 mAP, whereas the
no interaction subset obtains 9.32 mAP. This gap indicates that the product-based score in Equation 2
accumulates uncertainty for this class: although it makes the score sounder, ambiguous positive scores

Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Work
We presented the first benchmark and prompting taxonomy for pure zero-shot HOI recognition and detection
using MLLMs and proposed two effective visual prompting strategies, Objects as Context and Black Other
Objects, which improve performance by guiding model attention and reducing distractions. Evaluations on
the constructed Q&A datasets established a strong pure zero-shot baseline, achieving zero-shot state-of-the-
art results (53.50 mAP on HICO, 23.69 mAP on HICO-DET, 32.27 mAP on V-COCO) without HOI-specific
fine-tuning.
While these results are promising, several limitations should be considered. First, the reported evaluation
performance inherits the biases of the underlying HOI datasets, including their annotation errors and fixed
interaction categories. These biases may affect the evaluation protocol and the interpretation of the reported
metrics. However, this limitation does not affect the model’s generalization capabilities, since it is evaluated
in a pure zero-shot setting.
Second, our pipeline relies on pre-trained models, namely the external object detector and the MLLMs
used for interaction reasoning, whose capabilities and errors may limit the overall performance. Because
the detector is restricted to the object categories learned during training, in our case COCO classes, the
complete pipeline is not fully open-world and may miss objects outside this vocabulary. This restriction
does not limit the underlying HOI recognition capabilities of the MLLM, since the detector is used only to
filter the questions evaluated for efficiency and can be removed at deployment. For HOI detection, future
work could instead leverage the grounding capabilities of recent MLLMs to localize open-vocabulary objects
directly.
Third, although the proposed pipeline incorporates several practical optimizations to reduce computational
and memory requirements, such as object class filtering, evaluating MLLMs for HOI datasets remains com-
putationally demanding due to the large number of image–question pairs that must be processed. These
design choices introduce an inherent trade-off between computational efficiency and evaluation complete-
ness. Developing more efficient evaluation strategies that preserve performance while reducing inference cost
remains an important direction for future research.
Fourth, no interaction labels remain challenging. Future work should improve their scoring while balancing
output consistency with accurate estimation of interaction absence.
Overall, this benchmark enables systematic evaluation of MLLMs on open-world HOI and provides a basis

for extending the proposed methodology to other multi-label problems, particularly those characterized by
long-tail class distributions, where pure zero-shot inference may reduce reliance on imbalanced annotations.
Ethical considerations
HOI datasets such as HICO, HICO-DET, and V-COCO often include images sourced from the internet that
depict people in everyday activities. While our work focuses on technical benchmarking, methods developed
here could potentially be applied in sensitive domains such as surveillance or security. We emphasize that
such applications raise privacy and ethical concerns, and we encourage future work to carefully consider
safeguards and responsible deployment.

D.1
Thresholded accuracy analysis
One of the objectives of this work is to provide a benchmark for pure zero-shot HOI recognition that is
directly comparable with existing methods. Therefore, the main paper reports results using mean Average
Precision (mAP), the standard evaluation metric for HOI recognition. Since some prompting strategies tend
to produce more confident predictions, we additionally analyze their binary classification performance after
thresholding.
For each HOI query, the predicted probability is converted into a binary prediction using a threshold of 0.5.
Predictions with a score greater than or equal to 0.5 are assigned the positive label, while the remaining
predictions are assigned the negative label. The final accuracy is then computed as the percentage of correctly
classified HOI queries over the entire evaluation set.

F
Object detector size
Table F.1 shows the results of using different model sizes within the YOLO11 family. Performance scales
smoothly with detector size up to the large variant. In addition, we report an approach that uses MLLMs
as object recognizers based on the proposed classification score, thus removing the necessity of a pre-trained
object detector.
This approach directly queries the presence or absence of objects without localization,
prompting one question per object class (e.g., 80 per image for MS-COCO). However, this approach results
in a very low performance for Qwen2-VL 2B, possibly because leveraging the same MLLM for both object
recognition and HOI recognition propagates errors between tasks, highlighting the necessity of a dedicated
detector.
