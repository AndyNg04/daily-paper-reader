# GARDEN: Generalist Autonomous Reward Design Through Evidence Navigation for Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 

Dogan Urgun 

Abstract —Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) enables teams of agents, such as robots and autonomous vehicles, to learn coordinated behavior, but what such teams learn is largely determined by their reward function. Because task success alone provides too little guidance for learning, in-termediate rewards are usually designed by hand, and this effort must often be repeated whenever the environment or the team changes. Large language models (LLMs) can automate the effort by writing reward functions as code, yet the resulting rewards are typically designed and tested in a single setting. To enable their reuse across settings, GARDEN (Generalist Autonomous Reward Design through Evidence Navigation) is proposed, in which one reward program is developed for a family of related scenarios and transferred, unchanged, to scenarios that were not available during its design. During development, environment code, training records, and agent behavior are investigated by an LLM reward engineer, and draft rewards are tested in trial training runs, while the learning algorithm and the rule for selecting the final reward are held fixed. On unseen scenarios of the Overcooked and SMAX benchmarks, task returns were increased by 58.2% and 52.5% over the default rewards on two kitchen layouts, and the mean win rate in two combat scenarios was raised from 51.0% to 89.9%. Under matched candidate and probe budgets, this exceeded the 84.2% and 79.2% obtained with fixed diagnostic feedback and Eureka-inspired reward reflection. These results show that a single autonomously engineered reward can be reused effectively across related cooperative scenarios. 

Index Terms —Cooperative systems, large language models, multi-agent systems, reinforcement learning, transfer learning. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# MULTI-AGENT reinforcement learning (MARL) is used to train teams of agents that cooperate toward a shared objective [1], with applications such as multi-robot coordina-tion [2] and vehicle platooning [3]. In such systems, the reward function specifies what the team should achieve and, implicitly, how credit for a joint outcome is assigned to individual agents and decisions [4]. When only task completion is rewarded, this assignment is difficult, because a successful outcome may result from a long sequence of interdependent actions by several agents. This difficulty is commonly addressed by reward shaping, in which intermediate learning signals are added to the task re-ward [5]. Effective shaping, however, is obtained only through domain expertise and repeated trial and error, and unintended

For cooperative systems that must operate under varying environments and team compositions, reward design therefore becomes a recurring engineering cost. Large language models (LLMs) have recently been used to automate reward design by writing reward functions as executable code. Reward code has been generated from en-vironment abstractions and refined with human feedback [7], improved automatically through reflection on training statistics [8], and optimized through agentic search over evaluation records [9]. Two limitations of current approaches motivate this work. First, a generated reward is typically designed and evaluated within a single task or scenario. Whether it remains useful when conditions change, for example under a different spatial layout or team composition, is rarely examined, so each new configuration may require a new search. Second, the evidence used to refine a reward is usually defined in advance by the method designer, typically as summary statistics of training. Coordination failures that are not reflected in these statistics remain undiagnosed, because the underlying behavior and learned policies are not examined. To address these limitations, GARDEN (Generalist Au-tonomous Reward Design through Evidence Navigation) is proposed. An overview of the method is given in Fig. 1. One executable reward program is developed for a family of related scenarios from several development scenarios. The choice of evidence, the construction of analyses, and the testing of draft rewards through learning probes are delegated to a tool-using LLM reward engineer, whereas the learning algorithm and the selection rule are fixed. The selected program is then frozen and used, without modification, to train new policies on held-out scenarios that were concealed during its design. The main contributions of this paper are as follows. 1) Reward design is formulated at the level of a cooper-ative task family. A development-to-transfer protocol is introduced, in which a single executable reward program is developed on several development scenarios, frozen, and evaluated by training new policies on scenarios concealed during its design. The generalization of the reward is thereby measured separately from that of a trained policy. 2) Autonomous evidence navigation is introduced for LLM-based reward engineering. Work related to GARDEN spans reward shaping, LLM-based reward engineering, and learning signals for cooperative MARL. Across these lines of work, a reward is generally designed for and assessed on its target task, and the evidence available for its design is fixed by the method. The evidence used by representative LLM-based approaches is compared in Table I. 

A. Reward Shaping and Transfer 

Potential-based shaping preserves optimal policies under specific conditions [5]. Rewards can also be inferred from expert demonstrations [13] or learned from human preferences [14]. Shaping knowledge has been transferred across tasks by meta-learning a potential function over a task distribution [15]. In cooperative MARL, credit assignment has instead been built into the learner through counterfactual baselines [16] or value factorization [17]. These approaches depend on demonstra-tions, human judgments, a predefined reward form, or changes to the learning algorithm. In GARDEN, the learner is left unchanged, and an executable reward program is synthesized from source code and empirical evidence and reused across scenarios with different geometry or team structure. 

B. LLM-Based Reward Engineering 

Text2Reward generated dense reward code from environ-ment abstractions and refined it with human feedback [7]. Eureka evolved reward code through reflection on reward-component statistics collected during training [8]. The search was subsequently improved through reward-structure evolution and parameter alignment [18], language-agent tree search [11], self-alignment from execution results [10], and trajectory eval-uation by a vision-language model [12]. In these methods, the form of the feedback is fixed by the method design, and each reward is evaluated on the task for which it was designed. In ARBO, the closest approach, reward discovery is organized as an agentic black-box optimization loop with source inspection, tools, and persistent evaluation records [9]. The choice of evidence is thus already delegated to an agent, but the records are limited to candidate scores, per-term training curves, and error tracebacks, and each reward is evaluated within the task for which it was searched. In GARDEN, raw trajectories and checkpoints are additionally examined, draft rewards are tested through private learning probes, one program is selected jointly across development scenarios, and its unchanged source is evaluated on scenarios withheld from the search.

B. Autonomous Evidence Navigation 

The objective, information boundary, reward interface, and learner are fixed by the protocol, and the investigation within this boundary is delegated to a tool-using LLM. Environment and learner source, native reward logic, training configura-tions, trajectories, losses, learning curves, checkpoints, and completed design records are accessible through a constrained shell, in which files can be inspected, commands executed, and custom analysis programs constructed. No diagnostic checklist or fixed set of statistics is prescribed. A bounded number of learning probes can also be requested, in which a draft reward is trained on a chosen development scenario with the learner, budget, and seed of the formal assessment. Each committed program is accompanied by a short design report of the evidence examined, the proposed mechanism, and its limitations.

IV. E XPERIMENTAL DESIGN 

A. Environments and Rewards 

All experiments were conducted in JaxMARL, a library of multi-agent environments and algorithms implemented in JAX for fast, parallel training [26]. Two task families of increas-ing complexity were used. Overcooked [27] is a two-agent cooking task, in which ingredients must be collected, cooked, and delivered, and in which the kitchen layout determines how the two agents must coordinate. Each agent observes the full kitchen. SMAX [26] is a JAX reimplementation of the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge [28], a widely used cooperative combat benchmark, in which a team of allied units must defeat enemy units controlled by built-in heuristics. Its scenarios differ in team size, unit types, and numerical balance, and each unit observes only nearby units. The development and held-out scenarios of both families are listed in Table II, and a separate reward program was developed for each family. The four held-out scenarios are shown in Fig. 2. They differ from the development scenarios in spatial layout, in team size, or in the composition of the opposing teams. The native reward of each environment is its default reward, which combines a sparse task reward with built-in shaping. In Overcooked, the task reward grants 20 points to the team for each delivered dish, and the built-in shaping rewards the acting agent for intermediate steps: 3 for placing an ingredient into a pot, 3 for picking up a dish when one is needed, and 5 for picking up a cooked soup. In SMAX, the task reward is 1 when all enemies are eliminated while at least one ally survives, so its mean over episodes equals the win rate, and the built-in shaping rewards reductions in enemy health. Performance was always measured with the task reward alone. In the generated condition, the built-in shaping was replaced by the generated program, weighted by λF = 0 .1 in Overcooked and 0.01 in SMAX. Both native and generated shaping were applied with constant weights throughout training. The JaxMARL reference configuration for Overcooked instead decays its shaping during training, so the native baseline used here is the default reward under the same constant schedule as the generated reward. 

B. Policy Learning 

Policies were trained with multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO) [29], an actor–critic method for co-operative MARL, under centralized training and decentralized execution. Each agent selects its actions from its own obser-vation through an actor network whose parameters are shared by all agents. A centralized critic, used only during training, estimates per-agent values from joint information and thereby supports credit assignment within the team. The network architectures are shown in Fig. 3. In Over-cooked, the full-grid observation of each agent is encoded by three convolutional layers followed by two dense layers, and the critic receives the concatenated observations of both agents. In SMAX, where each unit observes only its surround-ings, recurrent networks with gated recurrent units (GRUs) are used to retain information over time. C. Reward Search and Controls 

Each reward search consisted of G = 3 generations with 

P = 2 candidates per generation, so six candidate programs were developed and assessed for each family. Within each candidate session, at most two learning probes could be run, each being a complete training run with the fixed learner and budget. Probes and development assessments used a single training seed, and each session was limited by a fixed time and interaction allowance. The reward engineer was the model 

gpt-6.1-sol at maximum reasoning effort. To examine the role of autonomous evidence access, two SMAX controls were evaluated with the same permitted initial source context, candidate and probe budgets, learner, and selection rule. Only the evidence available to the reward engineer and the use of reward history differed. 

Fixed diagnostics. Native-baseline diagnostics and com-pleted earlier-generation designs were retained, but training evidence was restricted to standardized reports of returns, learning curves, episode lengths, losses, entropy, and shaping statistics. 

Eureka-inspired reflection [8]. The best reward of the previ-ous generation was refined with standardized task and reward-component feedback, without initial native diagnostics. In both controls, probe feedback was restricted in the same way, and shell access and custom analyses were disabled. Evidence access, history, and inference effort were therefore compared jointly rather than as isolated factors. One search was executed per condition, and all SMAX rewards were frozen before held-out training began. 

D. Evaluation Protocol 

Each reward was developed once, and every held-out con-dition was then trained with 10 matched policy seeds, so that the same seeds were used for every reward. Performance is reported as the mean and standard error of the per-seed evalua-tion means, with standard error defined as the sample standard deviation divided by √10 . Overcooked returns are expressed as percentages of the observed native mean. Variation in policy training under one selected reward is captured by these seeds, whereas variation across reward searches is not. Gains are reported as point estimates of the difference between the GARDEN mean and the reference mean on each held-out scenario. The gains over native shaping are given in Table IV, and those over the reward-design controls are given in Section V-B. The scenario split and training budget were fixed after pilot calibration, and one held-out SMAX scenario had been examined in pilots, so the study is a pilot-informed rather than a researcher-blind evaluation. All runs were executed on a single NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU, and the software configuration is provided in the repository. V. E XPERIMENTAL RESULTS

B. Comparison With Diagnostic and Reflection Controls 

The SMAX comparison is reported in Table V. The highest win rate was achieved by GARDEN in both held-out scenarios and on average. On 10m vs 11m, all generated rewards exceeded native shaping by a wide margin. On the randomized SMACv2 10 units scenario, the Eureka-inspired reward fell below native shaping (70.8% versus 74.6%), whereas the largest improvement was obtained with GARDEN (83.6%). Its mean held-out win rate of 89.9% exceeded those of fixed diagnostics (84.2%) and Eureka-inspired reflection (79.2%) by 5.7 and 10.7 pp, respectively. Relative to fixed diagnostics, gains of 4.9 pp on 10m vs 11m and 6.5 pp on SMACv2 10 units were obtained. These comparisons hold the candidate and probe budgets, learner, and selection rule fixed. In the evaluated searches, the reward developed with autonomous access to source code, raw training artifacts, and custom anal-yses achieved higher held-out performance, with the additional model calls listed in Table V. 

C. Evidence-Guided Reward Development 

Two development investigations illustrate how training evi-dence was used to construct and revise reward programs. These cases concern development drafts rather than the programs ultimately selected for transfer. 

Overcooked: unused production capacity. A custom tra-jectory analysis revealed that the second pot in Bottleneck remained empty throughout eight recorded 400-step native-policy episodes. Demand-aware shaping, wall-respecting rout-ing, and partly shared production credits were subsequently incorporated into a reward program. Both pots were used in the recorded trajectories of this candidate, and the mean number of deliveries increased from nine to twelve. In the separate 64-episode evaluation, the mean task return increased by 33.5%. These observations support increased use of the available ca-pacity under the combined reward, without identifying which component produced the change.

GARDEN was presented as an approach to autonomous reward engineering for cooperative task families. Rather than being designed separately for each scenario, one executable reward program was developed from several development scenarios. The investigation of environment source, training artifacts, and behavioral evidence was delegated to a tool-using LLM, and draft rewards were tested through learning probes. The learner and the selection rule were held fixed, and the selected source was frozen before it was used to train new policies on held-out scenarios. Successful transfer of the frozen rewards was demonstrated in Overcooked and SMAX. Held-out task returns were in-creased by 58.2% and 52.5% in Overcooked, and the mean held-out win rate in SMAX was raised from 51.0% to 89.9%. Because the policies were trained from scratch on scenarios that were concealed during design, these gains reflect the generalization of the reward itself. Under matched candi-date and probe budgets, GARDEN also outperformed fixed-diagnostic and Eureka-inspired reward design by 5.7 and 10.7 pp, supporting the effectiveness of the evaluated reward-design process. The recorded development cases further showed how source inspection, custom analyses, and learning probes were used to diagnose coordination problems and to revise reward pro-grams. Together, these results indicate that task-family reward engineering with autonomous evidence navigation is an effec-tive route toward reusable reward design for cooperative multi-agent systems, and they motivate its extension to further task families and larger teams.
