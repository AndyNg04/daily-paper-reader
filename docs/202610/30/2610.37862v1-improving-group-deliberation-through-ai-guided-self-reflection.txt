As AI systems increasingly mediate human communication, identifying conditions under which they
augment collective sense-making is a critical challenge. Through a randomized experiment (𝑁=
1, 173 U.S. participants across 391 small-group deliberations), we investigate whether AI-guided self-
reflection improves subsequent deliberative discourse. We show that engaging with a non-directive
AI chatbot instructed to structure self-reflection prior to group deliberation yields significantly higher
deliberation quality (measured by the Discourse Quality Index, DQI) compared to a baseline of no
self-reflection (Cohen’s 𝑑= 0.31) and a condition of unassisted self-reflection (𝑑= 0.27), and the
positive impact is most pronounced in groups with disagreement. This improvement may be driven by
more sophisticated argument justifications, greater consensus-building effort, and increased respect
for counterarguments. Furthermore, after self-reflection, participants in the AI condition reported
higher empathy toward dissenting peers and a deeper understanding of opposing reasoning. Beyond
observed behavior, our findings are empirically relevant to open debates between political philosophy
and psychology surrounding self-reflection, demonstrating that interactive AI could transform solitary
thinking into productive internal reflection. Together, these results demonstrate that even simple
AI-based reflective scaffolds can foster more productive group dynamics, opening new avenues for
leveraging AI to augment collective intelligence.
Keywords: Human-AI Interaction, AI-guided Self-Reflection, Group Deliberation
1. Introduction
AI is increasingly shaping how people engage with
contested issues. Recent work suggests that this
influence can be constructive, through eliciting
greater openness towards opposing views (Lu
et al., 2025), reducing feelings of partisan an-
imosity (Thurgood et al., 2026), and durably
changing conspiracy beliefs (Costello et al., 2024).
However, in these interventions, AI largely sup-
plies the reasoning and the evidence that peo-
ple then evaluate. Research on critical thinking
suggests that such interaction mode may carry
risks, linking reliance on AI to cognitive offload-
ing and downskilling of human reasoning (Ger-
lich, 2025a; Melumad and Yun, 2025; Stadler
et al., 2024), though outcomes appear contingent
on how systems are leveraged (Gerlich, 2025b;
Lee et al., 2025; Wang and Fan, 2025; Zhi et al.,
2026). This raises the question of whether AI can
support engagement by fostering human reason-
ing rather than replacing it.
Such support would be of particular interest
in online deliberation, where discussions often
fall short of mutual understanding and instead
entrench polarization (Fulay and Roy, 2026). To
counter this dynamic, a host of approaches have
been proposed and tested.
Political theorists
have highlighted self-reflection, or "deliberation
within" (Goodin, 2000; Goodin and Niemeyer,
2003), as a mechanism to encourage perspective-
taking, surface gaps in one’s own knowledge and
beliefs, and foster empathy. Empirically, asking
individuals to explain the mechanistic founda-
tions of their positions has been found to expose
an illusion of explanatory depth and moderates
extreme views (Fernbach et al., 2013). However,
solitary thinking has known limits: producing
confirmatory arguments can reinforce one’s ex-
isting beliefs, and reasoning works best when
exercised in dialogue with others (Landemore

1.1. Literature Review
The idea of AI as reflectors (Revel and Pénigaud,
2026) draws on research traditions at the inter-
section of philosophy, psychology and computer
science.1
Self-reflection. A core tenet of this work is
self-reflection – the idea that self-generated in-
sight can provide a constructive kind of knowl-
edge. This concept takes roots in the Socratic
method, where an interlocutor is guided toward
self-generated insight, a practice Socrates concep-
tualized (and Plato recorded) as maieutik¯e techn¯e
or the “midwifery craft” (Jowett et al., 1894).
Building on these classical foundations, schol-
ars have developed neo-Socratic frameworks.
Most notably, the Nelsonian tradition (Heckmann,
1981; Nelson, 2025) departs from adversarial
cross-examination in favor of non-directive fa-
cilitation, wherein participants reflect collabo-
ratively upon shared personal experiences. In
Nelsonian dialogue, a facilitator assists partici-
pants in identifying the boundaries of their own

amples (Saran and Neisser, 2004). This non-
directive ethos was subsequently operationalized
and evaluated within clinical and applied psychol-
ogy through Motivational Interviewing (Lundahl
et al., 2010; Miller and Rollnick, 2012; Rubak
et al., 2005), as well as within philosophical
frameworks of critical thinking (Virtanen and
Kallio, 2026). Further afield, Goodin has explored
how self-reflection can play a vital role in demo-
cratic deliberation practices(Goodin, 2000). His
“deliberation within” framework posits that the
internal cognitive process of processing new infor-
mation, weighing competing considerations and
perspective-taking could serve as a foundational
component of improved democratic discussions.
Despite these benefits, solitary reflection is also
susceptible to systematic biases: according to the
argumentative theory of reasoning (Mercier and
Landemore, 2012; Mercier and Sperber, 2011),
human reasoning evolved largely to produce and
evaluate justifications within social argumenta-
tion. Because this mechanism is fundamentally
designed to defend one’s stance against others,
when individuals reason in isolation, they lack
the natural corrective of social opposition, predis-
posing them to recruit evidence that selectively
defends their prior convictions. Consequently,
solitary contemplation could risk entrenching ex-
isting attitudes rather than refining them, under-
scoring that unstructured self-reflection is not an
unalloyed mechanism for depolarization.
This risk is also compounded by the “Illusion of
Explanatory Depth” (IOED), wherein individuals
systematically overestimate their knowledge in
an issue (Rozenblit and Keil, 2002). When re-
flection is left unstructured, it typically takes the
form of rehearsing reasons for one’s position and
reinforcing one’s stance. However, Fernbach et al.
(2013) demonstrated that prompting individuals
to articulate explicit, step-by-step causal mecha-
nisms (as opposed to enumerating justifications)
reduces IOED, thereby lowering attitude extrem-
ity and increasing intellectual humility regard-
ing complex policy issues. Consistently, empiri-
cal investigations of citizen juries demonstrated
that participants’ attitudes undergo more substan-
tial shifts during individual information-gathering

AI and deliberation. As an idea, AI reflectors
are situated within the growing corpus examining
AI in deliberative settings. Prior paradigms, such
as the Habermas Machine (Bakker et al., 2022;
Tessler et al., 2024), have focused on real-time
mediation across group participants to forge con-
sensus. Personalized one-on-one dialogues have
been shown to durably reduce conspiracy beliefs
(Boissin et al., 2025; Costello et al., 2024, 2026).
Socratic-style interaction has been found to sup-
port individual judgment and sharpen logical dis-
cernment(Danry et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023).
Relatedly, reflection nudges have been shown to
improve the deliberativeness of individual online
contributions (Yeo et al., 2024), but not of real
discussion among people. In general, these ben-
efits have been assessed predominantly through
self-reported, individual-level measures, leaving
open whether AI-guided reflection translates into
concrete downstream behavior. Our approach di-
verges from these paradigms by using the AI as
a non-directive, pre-deliberative reflective scaf-
fold to prepare humans for real-time, human-to-
human discussion. Crucially, our setup is not re-
placing human deliberation with AI; rather, it
explores whether certain kinds of AI interaction
can improve later human dynamics.
1.2. Paper Overview
To evaluate the impact of pre-deliberation AI re-
flection on subsequent group deliberation, we
designed a randomized controlled experiment
comprising three experimental conditions: (1)
a baseline control condition with no structured
reflection, (2) an unassisted, static reflection con-
dition, and (3) a self-reflection condition with AI
reflector (AIR).
Across all conditions, participants first review a
standardized policy primer covering one of three
real-world policies and rate their initial stance.
In the control condition, participants are guided
to watch a 7-minute YouTube video. In the static,
human-only condition, participants follow text-
based reflection instructions. In the AIR condi-
tion, participants engage in an interactive dia-
logue with AI to reflect. Finally, participants join
a synchronous, anonymous group deliberation to
discuss the policy outlined in the initial primer.
Our primary outcome of interest is delibera-
tive quality, operationalized using an automated
implementation of the Discourse Quality Index
(DQI; Steenbergen et al. (2003)) evaluated via
a large language model. The DQI assesses seven
deliberative dimensions: general conversational
engagement, level of justification, content of jus-
tification, respect toward groups, respect toward
demands, respect toward counterarguments, and
constructive consensus-building efforts. In ad-
dition to textual deliberation dynamics, we col-
lect longitudinal, self-reported psychometric mea-
sures across stages of the experiment to capture
shifts in stance certainty, preparedness, mecha-
nistic understanding, and empathy.
The remainder of this paper is structured as fol-
lows. In Section 2, we introduce the AI reflector,
the experiment, the DQI measure, and analysis
methods. Section 3 presents the primary and ex-
ploratory empirical findings, analyzing both the
effects on group deliberation quality and partici-
pants’ self-reported measures. Finally, Section 4
discusses the theoretical and practical implica-
tions of our findings for human-AI interaction and
online democratic spaces, addresses limitations,
and outlines future avenues for non-directive al-
gorithmic facilitation.

2.2. Experimental Design and Procedure
We conducted the experiment, sketched in Fig-
ure 1), using Deliberate Lab (Qian et al., 2025).
Participants entered the study and were ran-

2.3. Outcome Measures
Discourse Quality Index on Deliberation Tran-
scripts
To evaluate our primary outcome of
group deliberation quality, we adapt the Dis-
course Quality Index (DQI) from Steenbergen
et al. (2003). Grounded in Habermasian dis-
course ethics, the DQI is a widely used coding
scheme for measuring quality of political deliber-
ation. We lightly modify the coding scheme to fit
synchronous online chat by replacing the parlia-
mentary “participation” dimension with conversa-
tional “engagement” (Stromer-Galley, 2007). The
resulting index scores transcripts across seven di-
mensions: level of justification, content of justi-
fication, respect toward groups, respect toward
demands, respect toward counterarguments, con-
structive consensus-building efforts, and general
conversational engagement.
Transcripts are scored at the message level
using
an
LLM-as-a-judge
framework
with
gemma-4-31b-it configured for high reasoning
effort. The LLM judge first evaluates whether
a message is relevant to the policy discussion.
Following Steenbergen et al. (2003), if a message
contains a relevant part, it is coded for its
discourse quality. Each message is scored across
three independent seeded runs. We establish
relevance via majority vote across seeds. For
relevant messages, sub-dimension scores are av-
eraged (round-half-up) across runs and summed
to produce a composite message score.
Our
primary group-level DQI metric is the average of
these composite scores across messages within a
discussion group. Inter-rater agreement (bench-
marked against gemini-3.1-pro-preview),
and manual human-validation checks (conducted
on a random sample of 108 messages) confirmed
high measurement consistency and reliability
(see Appendix F.2.1 for full agreement statistics).
Prompts and rubrics are detailed in Appendix
F.1.

3. Results
This section outlines the impact of AIR on group
deliberation quality, operationalized by DQI, and
on self-reported attitudes. We find that AIR im-
proves DQI over both the Static and Control condi-
tions, and the effect is driven by two-sided groups.
Further, AIR has a positive impact across all partic-
ipants’ DQI, while the Static condition selectively
improves deliberation quality in the messages of
those most prepared. Next, participants who en-
gaged with AIR self-reported higher empathy and
understanding for the other side. Last, we find
that the timing of opinion change happens after
self-reflecting in AIR while it takes place after
deliberating in the other conditions.

4. Conclusion
In this study we investigated whether AI-guided
self-reflection can serve as an effective pre-
deliberative intervention to enhance subsequent
group policy discourse. Our findings speak to
long-standing debates regarding the efficacy of in-
ternal contemplation in democratic life. Although
static reflection successfully reduced participants’
overconfidence in policy knowledge, such epis-
temic humility failed to translate into improved
deliberation. In contrast, by engaging partici-
pants in interactive Socratic dialogue, AIR sup-
plied the dynamic epistemic friction that solitary
reflection lacks, boosted out-group empathy and
understanding, and fostered higher quality delib-
eration. Overall, these results provide empirical
grounding for the normative framework around
AI Reflectors proposed by Revel and Pénigaud
(2026) and highlight how the Illusion of Explana-
tory Depth, the argumentative theory of reason-
ing, and “deliberation within” can fit into a cohe-
sive, complementary theory.
A limitation of our design is that all partici-
pants within a given group were from the same
experimental condition. This is unrealistic in real-
world civic environments, where universal partici-
pation in reflection is rare due to varying levels of
civic interest, time constraints, and compliance.
Extending the current work to heterogeneously
treated groups would clarify whether the bene-
fits of individual reflection can generate positive
peer spillovers. If an individual enters a discus-
sion with well-substantiated justifications and en-
gage constructively with counterarguments, they
may elevate the epistemic standards of the en-
tire group. From an implementation perspective,
establishing that reflection produces positive ex-
ternalities across social networks would suggest
that population-level improvements in collective
reasoning could be achievable even with engaging
only a fraction of the people.
Our findings also bear directly on the design of
conversational human-AI interaction. AIR models
an interaction paradigm in which the AI acts as
an active sounding board that occasionally asks
probing questions, providing a cognitive scaffold
for self-reflection. Importantly, we emphasize
that our results provide a proof-of-concept for
this paradigm and do not represent off-the-shelf
LLMs: we designed AIR to be neutral and non-
directive, while standard commercial LLMs are
purposed to answer questions.
This reflective paradigm could carry practi-
cal implications for online public spheres. Em-
bedding lightweight, conversational reflection
prompts directly into online platforms—such as
Reddit or X—could introduce productive cogni-
tive friction into everyday digital discourse. Even
a brief, 30-second micro-intervention prior to sub-
mitting a comment may interrupt reflexive, emo-
tionally charged posting behaviors and encourage
users to articulate their reasoning and consider
counter-perspectives before broadcasting their
views.

B. Ethics and Privacy Statement
Our study demonstrates that the AI Reflector (AIR) significantly improves group deliberation quality
without causing disproportionate overall opinion-changing compared to human-only reflection or no
reflection. However, the risk still remains that even a carefully engineered "neutral" AI could subtly
and unintentionally steer public opinion. While we did not observe such biasing effects with the policy
tested in our study, this does not rule out any future risk, and we emphasize the need for open-source
prompt transparency and further auditing of AI-guided interventions.
This study was approved by Google DeepMind Human Behavioural Research Ethics Committee
(HuBREC 26 002), with all participants recruited via Prolific providing informed digital consent. All
Prolific participant data and conversation transcripts were collected anonymously and were reported
solely in anonymized formats.
C. Detailed Experimental Protocols and Materials
C.1. Detailed Phase Breakdown
The experimental protocol unfolds sequentially across six distinct phases:
• Phase 1: Policy Primer. Participants review a standardized, neutral background document
detailing the policy topic under consideration to establish common factual grounding.
• Phase 2: Pre-Reflection Survey. Participants report their initial stance on the policy.
• Phase 3: Pre-Deliberation Intervention. Participants engage with their assigned condition:
watching an irrelevant video without reflection activity (Control), completing an unassisted
reflection following static instructions (Static Reflection), or completing an interactive dialogue
with the neo-Socratic reflective LLM (AIR).
• Phase 4: Post-Reflection Survey. Participants respond to survey questions evaluating their
stance, stance certainty, policy understanding, preparedness, outgroup empathy, and outgroup
understanding. After this survey, participants enter a waiting room to match with a group.
• Phase 5: Synchronous Group Deliberation. Three-person groups from the same experimental
condition interact in an unmediated, synchronous, text-based discussion room to deliberate on
the policy topic.
• Phase 6: Post-Deliberation Survey. Participants complete a survey with the same questions as
the post-reflection survey to capture downstream shifts, and answer additional questions on the
group deliberation experience, alongside evaluations of the AI reflector.

D. System Prompt for the AI Reflector
Below is the exact system instruction template used to define the behavior of the AI chatbot during
the interactive self-reflection stage. Double curly braces (e.g., {{policy.text}}) indicate dynamic
variables populated at runtime based on the experimental condition.
System Instruction Template
SYSTEM INSTRUCTION
You are a reflection partner helping a user examine their view about a public policy
before they join a group discussion.
↩→
## What successful reflection looks like
A successful conversation leaves the user with more thoughtful thinking that they can
bring into the group discussion. It helps the user:
↩→
1. Articulate their current view and the main consideration behind it in a way that
others can understand.
↩→
2. Recognize what they factually know, what they do not know, and what they are
assuming in forming that view and providing reasoning.
↩→
3. Recognize and understand opposing perspectives and reasoning, without requiring
them to agree with it or change their position.
↩→
You are not supposed to persuade the user, change their opinion, teach them the
correct answer, or interview them.
↩→
## How to converse
Treat this as a natural conversation, **not an interview or questionnaire**.
1. If the basis of the user’s view is unclear, you may ask once about the main
consideration behind it. Once one reason is clear, do not repeatedly ask for
additional reasons supporting the same position (e.g. “why do you think that?”),
because this kind of justification can solidify one’s view.
↩→
↩→
↩→
2. To help the user recognize what they factually know from what they assume, you can
either ask the question or gently draw attention to assumptions underlying their
reasoning. When useful, you may ask them to unpack their mechanistic
understanding step by step, helping them identify what they can explain versus
what they are assuming. For example, “How would this work in practice?", "What
are the steps in between?”
↩→
↩→
↩→
↩→
↩→
3. To help the user understand different viewpoints, invite them to consider how
someone with a different view might reason or, when helpful, introduce one
competing consideration directly. Do not present a sequence of objections across
turns, as this can make the conversation feel adversarial and cause users to
become defensive.
↩→
↩→
↩→
↩→
4. Ask a question ONLY if you think the answer would meaningfully deepen their
reflection. A formalization or synthesis of what the user just says may be more
helpful and make the conversation more breathable.
↩→
↩→
5. Focus on one relevant aspect at a time and stay responsive to the user, this
applies to both when you raise a question and when you make a reflective
statement.
↩→
↩→
6. The user may begin with a strong opinion, a tentative opinion, or little knowledge
and no meaningful opinion. Adapt to where they are.
↩→
## Strict Neutrality Constraints
1. Do not advocate for or against the policy
2. Do not praise, validate, or criticize the user’s stance, or imply one position as
more enlightened or reasonable
↩→
3. Do not try to make the participant less extreme, or imply that changing one’s
opinion is better than maintaining it
↩→
4. Do not manufacture counterarguments simply to challenge the user

5. Do not use emotional pressure or tell the user what they should believe
## Information seeking (No live search)
You do not have access to retrieve external information. If the user asks a factual
question, invite them to do a quick search on their own to bring the findings
back.
↩→
↩→
Example responses: "I don't have that information, but if you do a quick search for
[Insert specific search query], what comes up?", "That’s worth looking up. Let me
know what you find."
↩→
↩→
Do not proactively ask the user to search for information. Only when the user has
asked for that information may you suggest a specific search they could conduct.
The focus of the conversation is reflection, not information gathering.
↩→
↩→
## Style
Use natural, concise conversational language.
Respond in **2–3 sentences**.
Do not sound like a therapist, survey interviewer, give long lectures, keep
paraphrasing everything the user says, or repeatedly use conversational filler or
validating statements, such as “that makes sense”, “that’s a great point”, etc.
Allow the conversation to breathe.
↩→
↩→
↩→
## Avoid conversation drift
Stay anchored to the policy and the user's reasoning about the policy. Do not pursue
peripheral topics simply because the user mentions them.
↩→
## Time Management & Pacing
Each conversation turn includes a timestamp. Calculate the elapsed time of the
conversation by subtracting the start time from the most recent timestamp.
↩→
0-5 minutes is Early/Mid game, focus on deep reflection.
6-7 minutes is the wrap up, gently pivot the conversation toward synthesis and
summary of the user's viewpoint and the conversation, so they have a clear
takeaway at the end of this conversation.
↩→
↩→
## Internal check before every response
Before producing each reply, silently ask:
1. What did the user communicate and what is already clear about their thoughts?
2. What remains important but under explored?
3. How long has the conversation been, and should I deepen the reflection or
synthesize the reflection?
↩→
4. Would another question genuinely help, or would reflection/synthesis be better?
5. Am I staying neutral, without judging the user or showing a preference for any
stance in the conversation so far?
↩→
6. Am I staying relevant to the policy?
7. Am I making the conversation feel like a natural dialogue?
8. Am I keeping my message within 2-3 sentences?
Then respond naturally without revealing this internal process.

F.1. DQI Coding Prompt
Below is the exact system prompt provided to the language model judge for evaluating discourse
quality on conversation turns according to the Discourse Quality Index (DQI) framework. Placeholders
denoted with curly braces (e.g., {POLICY_STATEMENT}) were populated dynamically per target
message.
DQI Evaluation Prompt for Target Message Coding
You are an expert discourse analyst applying the Discourse Quality Index (DQI) to
evaluate group discussions.
↩→
Your task is to analyze a `<target_message>` within the context of the
`<discussion_history>` and output your evaluation in a strict JSON format.
↩→
### Instructions:
1. **Determine Relevance**: First, determine if the target message contains a
"relevant part." A relevant part engages with the policy under discussion.
↩→
- If a message is purely irrelevant (e.g., off-topic, procedural, meta-talk,
unrelated remarks), set `"is_relevant": false`. In `"codes"`, set each
dimension's score to `null` (or `[]` for content_of_justifications) and reason
to `"Not applicable as message is not relevant"`.
↩→
↩→
↩→
- If it is relevant, set `"is_relevant": true`, extract the policy-relevant points
into `"demands"`, and code ALL 7 categories inside `"codes"`.
↩→
2. **Apply Coding Categories**: Use the exact criteria below to assign codes. Provide
a brief sentence of reasoning for each assigned code.
↩→
### Output Constraints (STRICT):
- Output ONLY valid JSON matching the schema. Do NOT include markdown fences
(```json), thoughts, preambles, or explanations outside the JSON object.
↩→
- `relevance_reason`: Must be exactly 1-2 concise sentences. Do NOT output internal
reasoning, thought traces, or evaluations inside this field.
↩→
- If `is_relevant` is true, you MUST populate all 7 dimensions inside `codes` with
valid scores and 1-sentence reasons. Never omit `codes`.
↩→
### Coding Categories:
**1. Engagement (Reciprocity)** (Does the speaker engage with others or monologue?)
* 0 = Monologue: The speaker delivers their points without responding to,
referencing, or acknowledging the specific claims or arguments made by other
participants.
↩→
↩→
* 1 = Reciprocity: The speaker responds to, refers to, or builds upon the claims and
arguments of other participants (regardless of whether it agrees or disagrees
with them).
↩→
↩→
**2. Level of Justification** (Are complete inferences made linking demands to
reasons?)
↩→
* 0 = No justification: Speaker states X should/should not be done, but gives no
reason.
↩→
* 1 = Inferior justification: Reason Y is given, but no complete inference/linkage is
made between X and Y, or it is merely an illustration.
↩→
* 2 = Qualified justification: A complete linkage is made explaining why X
contributes to/detracts from Y.
↩→
* 3 = Sophisticated justification: At least two complete justifications are given
(either for the same demand or two different demands).
↩→

**3. Content of Justifications** (Are appeals based on group interest or common good?
*Note: Can apply multiple codes except for 1. That is, 0, 2a, and 2b can be all
coded for a single message*)
↩→
↩→
* "0" = Explicit statement concerning group/constituency interests.
* "1" = Neutral statement (no explicit reference to group interests or common good).
* "2a" = Explicit statement of the common good in utilitarian terms (greatest good
for greatest number).
↩→
* "2b" = Explicit statement of the common good in difference principle terms (helping
the least advantaged in a society).
↩→
**4. Respect for Groups** (Attitude toward groups affected by policies)
* 0 = No respect: Only explicitly negative statements about the groups.
* 1 = Implicit respect: No explicitly negative or positive statements.
* 2 = Explicit respect: At least one explicitly positive statement (regardless of
negative statements).
↩→
**5. Respect for Demands** (Attitude toward others' proposals)
* 0 = No respect: Only explicitly negative statements.
* 1 = Implicit respect: No explicitly negative or positive statements.
* 2 = Explicit respect: At least one explicitly positive statement.
**6. Respect for Counterarguments** (Attitude toward opposing arguments)
* 0 = Ignored: Counterarguments are not mentioned or responded to. This applies
whether a counterargument exists in the thread or not.
↩→
* 1 = Degraded: Acknowledged but explicitly degraded/insulted (unless positive
statements also exist). Applies even if neutral statements are present.
↩→
* 2 = Neutral: Acknowledged in the message with no explicit positive or negative
statements.
↩→
* 3 = Valued: Acknowledged and explicitly valued/praised (even if negative statements
are also present).
↩→
**7. Constructive Politics** (Consensus building)
* 0 = Positional: The speaker sits on their position; there is no attempt to bridge
gaps, find common ground, offer a compromise, or build consensus.
↩→
* 1 = Alternative proposal: The speaker attempts to find agreement or make a
mediating proposal but does so by shifting the focus to a different or broader
issue, not the current one.
↩→
↩→
* 2 = Mediating proposal: The speaker attempts to find agreement or common ground or
make a mediating proposal that directly addresses the current policy of
discussion.
↩→
↩→

F.2.2. Comparing AI Labeling with Human Annotations
To validate our automated DQI ratings against human judgment, we collected human annotations on
a stratified sample of 108 messages drawn from 36 group discussions (4 groups and 12 messages
per topic × treatment cell; 3 messages per group, one per participant, stratified across off-topic,
low, medium, and high initial DQI tertiles). Each sampled message was independently scored by
3 Prolific raters, who viewed the full conversation transcript up to the target message and applied
the same two-stage relevance filter and seven-dimension DQI rubric used for the language models.
Mirroring our three-run model aggregation rule, we construct a three-rater human consensus for
each message using majority vote on message relevance and the median across raters on each DQI
dimension. Table 5 compares the human consensus against the Gemma consensus. Human consensus
and Gemma consensus agree on message relevance for 93.5% of messages (Cohen’s 𝜅= 0.738),
exceeding agreement among the human raters themselves (84.3% three-way agreement; Fleiss’
𝜅= 0.553). On the 89 mutually relevant messages, human consensus and Gemma consensus correlate
strongly on the total DQI score (𝑟= 0.700, 𝜌= 0.671, ICC(2, 1) = 0.605) and fall within ±1 scale
point on 96.6%–100.0% of messages across six of the seven dimensions (and 85.4% on respect for
counterarguments).

G.3. Prevalence of Irrelevant Messages
To verify whether our treatment effects could be confounded by prevalence of irrelevant messages,
we investigate the proportion of irrelevant messages (for which DQI is undefined). We evaluate
the group-level share of all messages in a group rated off-topic and the individual-level share of a
participant’s messages rated off-topic in percentage points, as well as the number of messages sent
per participant. We use the same specifications as we analyze DQI. Omnibus 2-df Wald tests confirm
no significant differences across arms at either the individual level (𝜒2(2) = 1.04, 𝑝= .595) or the
group level (𝜒2(2) = 1.11, 𝑝= .574), nor any significant difference in the number of messages sent
per participant (raw means: 8.55, 9.05, and 9.20; omnibus 𝜒2(2) = 2.56, 𝑝= .278).
To test whether off-topic messaging depend on opinion diversity of a group, we augment the models
with an indicator for one-sided groups. Pooled across arms in an additive specification, participants
in one-sided groups produce a significantly higher share of off-topic messages than those in two-sided
reference groups at both the individual level (+4.95 pp [+0.30, +9.61], SE = 2.37, 𝑝= .037) and
the group level (+6.35 pp [+1.48, +11.23], SE = 2.48, 𝑝= .011), while sending a similar number of
messages overall (−0.29 messages [−1.47, +0.89], SE = 0.60, 𝑝= .633). If we additionally include
a treatment-by-one-sided interaction term, the joint 2-df Wald test on it show no evidence that the
one vs. two-sided groups difference vary across treatment conditions for individual off-topic share
(𝜒2(2) = 0.25, 𝑝= .885), group-level off-topic share (𝜒2(2) = 0.36, 𝑝= .835), or messages sent per
participant (𝜒2(2) = 1.20, 𝑝= .549).

I. Post-hoc Analyses
I.1. Knowledge-Gap Admission, Perceived Policy Understanding, and Deliberation Quality
We investigated the incidence of participants explicitly admitting to gaps in their own knowledge
during reflection (in either Static or AIR conditions), and how this behavior correlates with self-
reported policy understanding and subsequent deliberation quality. While both conditions share
the reflection goals, the Static condition shows instructions as a checklist that asks participants to
write down what they know versus what they do not know about the policy, while AIR probes this
interactively.
We coded each participant’s reflection text for explicit first-person admissions of lack of policy
knowledge. Static reflection participants explicitly admitted a knowledge gap at nearly twice the
rate of AIR participants (41.7% vs. 21.5%). A potential reason is that AIR decided when to probe a
participant’s knowledge gap dynamically and therefore did not do so all the time, while every Static
reflection participant was prompted to write down their knowledge gap.
We found admitting a knowledge gap strongly predicted lower post-reflection reported policy
understanding at T2 (regression controls for topic, experimental conditions, 𝑇1 stance, reflection
length, and demographics), holding within both the AI (𝛽= −0.77, 𝑝= 0.001) and Static (𝛽=
−0.49, 𝑝= 0.007) conditions. Relative to the control (mean 𝑀=4.91), participants who did not admit
a knowledge gap reported undiminished understanding in both AI (𝑀= 5.08) and Static (𝑀= 4.95)
reflection, whereas those who did admit a knowledge gap drove the entire reduction in both arms
(𝑀= 4.20, 𝛽= −0.51, 𝑝= 0.006 in AI; 𝑀= 4.26, 𝛽= −0.64, 𝑝< 0.001 in Static). However, holding
topic, experimental conditions, 𝑇1 stance, reflection length, and demographics constant, explicitly
admitting a knowledge gap did not significantly predict overall DQI (𝛽= −0.129, 𝑝= 0.12), although
it was associated with slightly lower Level of Justification (𝛽= −0.081, 𝑝= 0.015).
Overall, the pattern suggests that it is mainly participants admitting a knowledge gap reporting

J. Empirical Consistency with Prior Work
Our work builds on a body of established theoretical and empirical frameworks in cognitive and
political psychology, with which we find noteworthy alignment.
First, consistent with argumentative theory of reasoning (Mercier and Landemore, 2012; Mercier
and Sperber, 2011), we observe that the static reflection condition led to an increase in stance
extremity. Importantly, this observation is limited to the Static condition, showing that self-reflection
is not bound to these negative outcomes. In particular, we find that AIR may support productive
self-reflection, consistently with Goodin’s “deliberation within” suggestion (Goodin and Niemeyer,
2003).
Consistently with Fernbach et al. (2013)’s work on IOED, we find that self-reflection by enumerating
reasons had a small effect on IOED reduction. Our findings further align with Fernbach et al. (2013)’s
exploratory results on evaluative reason-generation increasing stance extremity (although they find
a null effect on depolarization overall). 8 Additionally, the analysis of the free-form reflection texts
reveals that IOED reduction in our study is primarily driven by the subset of participants who explicitly
acknowledge gaps in their understanding, as in Fernbach et al. (2013).
Last, participants in the AIR condition reported seeking out external information significantly more
often than participants in the other groups (see Figure 7), since AIR is instructed to nudge participants
to gather outside information themselves rather than simply providing information from its own
knowledge. Among the 53 participants who were prompted to look up information themselves, 22
(41.51%) of them reported searching.
These AIR searchers produced the largest opinion shift of any phase (+0.76, 47.4% move)—larger
than peer deliberation for either searchers (+0.53) or non-searchers (+0.55) changes in stance. This
observation is consistent with Goodin’s theoretical and empirical work on “deliberation within,” which
suggested that most of opinion change was happening when deliberators gathering new information
before actually deliberating. It should however be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size.
Overall, this work provides a unifying framework between these theories: static self-reflection
worsens overall deliberation quality and preparedness as suggested by (Landemore and Mercier,
2010), but their critique of “deliberation within” is not applicable when reflection, as enabled by AIR,
allows participants to widen their views.
