Abstract 

Artificial personality shapes user experience (UX) in human –machine interaction, yet coherent theoretical 

frameworks and validated measurement instruments remain limited. This paper refines and empirically evaluates 

the Artificial Personality Model (APM),  a hybrid framework integrating human -centred and machine -specific 

personality dimensions. Across three experiments (N = 395), the model was operationalised and evaluated using 

the Artificial Personality Questionnaire (APQ). Factor analyses revealed that so me human personality dimensions 

(e.g., agreeableness, conscientiousness) are partially transferable, whereas others (e.g., extraversion) did not 

emerge as distinct dimensions. Additional machine -specific dimensions were identified. The APQ provided 

encoura ging initial psychometric evidence, while the human -centred BFI -2 showed poor reliability and an 

uninterpretable factor structure in Experiment 3. Experimental manipulations demonstrated significant effects of 

artificial personality on user satisfaction, c ontinuous use intention, and social presence. The findings contribute an 

empirically refined framework and a novel instrument for measuring perceived artificial personality, offering 

practical guidance for designing conversational agents. 

Key words:  Artificial Personality,  Conversational Agents, Personality  Measurement , Large Language Models,

# 1.  Introduction 

Over 50% of the 8,000 users of robotic vacuum cleaners surveyed by the company Shark Robot give their vacuum 

cleaner a name, and 63% of them claim that their vacuum cleaner has a personality  (Ho pwood, 2025 ). Although 

the study is not representative, it underlines that machines are more than mere tools for humans: they are social 

actors according to the Computers are  Social  Actors (CASA) paradigm  (Nass et al., 1994) . Humans 

anthropomorphi se such systems and regard them as social  beings , as this facilitates interaction with machines and 

enables users to adapt their behavio ur accordingly  (Epley et al., 2008) .

Artificial personality (AP)  can  influence key user experience outcomes, including trust  (Zhou et al., 2019) ,

acceptance  (Tapus et al., 2008) , and purchase decisions  (Shumanov & Johnson, 2021) . Beyond end users, AP is 

increasingly considered relevant in the design of CAs, with practitioners highlighting its potential role in creating 

consistent agent behaviour, adapting interactions to user needs, fostering long -term user –system relationships, an d

guiding design decisions.  Despite this relevance, fundamental questions remain unresolved , in particular : how can 

artificial personality be defined, implemented, and measured? 

In general, t here are two types of models to describe the artificial personality of conversational agents 

(Dregger, 2023) : human -centred and machine -centred models. Human -centred models transfer personality models 

developed for humans to the context of machines. In research and practice, human -centred models are dominant. 

The Big Five Model (BFM), also called the Five -Factor  Model (FFM) of personality  (Costa & McCrae, 2008) , is Seite 2 von 56 

one of the most frequently applied approaches for designing linguistic cues for  CAs  (Ahmad et al., 2022; Dregger 

et al., 2024) , and widely applied in studies on artificial personality  (Mou et al., 2020; Völkel & Kaya, 2021) .

Besides the BFM, the  Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire  (Cattell & P. Cattell, 1995)  is also mentioned 

(Endres, 1995) . Both models follow a trait -based approach to personality. 

However, alternative approaches also exist in psychology. The psychoanalytic paradigm and the transactional 

paradigm are likewise used to describe  artificial  personality. Psychoanalytic approaches  (e.g. Myers -Briggs -

Inventory,  Myers & Myers, 1995)  are used in practice  but lack robust empirical validation. In contrast, the 

transactional paradigm conceptualizes personality as an interaction between individual and environment.  Thus, 

they consider not only personality traits, but also situational variables.  This perspective is reflected in approaches 

such as character computing  (Herbert, 2020; Hundertmark et al., 2023) . However, its higher complexity limits its 

direct applicability in design contexts, which may explain the dominance of trait -based models  (Dregger, 2023) .

Machine -centred models question whether  “human personality models can be transferred to machines ”

(Dregger, 2023, p. 4; Völkel et al., 2020) . One example is the model by Völkel et al.  (2020) , which was specifically 

developed for conversational agents using experiments and analysis of customer reviews. It describes ten 

dimensions of personality: dysfunctional, confrontational, unstable, serviceable, approachable, social -entertaining, 

social -in clined, social -assisting, self -conscious, and artificial.  Another approach was developed by  Kovačević ,

Holz,  et al.  (2024)  that encompasses eight dimension s. More recently, Contreras  (2026)  created  an LLM -native 

psychometric instrument using a bottom -up approach and derives five factors from LLM responses: 

responsiveness, deference, boldness, guardedness, and verbosity. 

Both human -centred and machine -centred approaches face several criticisms: 

▪ Human personality traits  have a limited validity  for machines:  Not all human personality 

dimensions are meaningful in machine contexts. Experts consider , for example,  neuroticism 

(emotional stability) and openness to experience as less relevant  for the design of the AP of 

conversational agents  (Dregger, 2023) . Importantly, this represents a construct -level problem that is 

distinct from the question of whether individual questionnaire items can be reformulated for 

machines. Recent research demonstrates this distinction: Zierahn et al.  (2026)  found that LLM -

adapted Big Five inventories, such as the  BFI -LLM  (Tosato et al., 2025)  and LMLPA  (Zheng et al., 

2025) , can achieve high content validity at the item level, while the theoretically assumed five -factor 

structure nevertheless could not be recovered empirically.  Thus, adapting the operationalisation of 

human personality constructs does not necessarily establish that the underlying dimensions 

themselves constitute an appropriate model of artificial personality. 

▪ The  BFM  provides  a limited  perspective on personality  and  consequently,  is a problematic 

foundation for the development of a personality model for machines : The BFM  focusses on  five 

personality  dimensions , and these  may not capture human personality in a fully valid manner. 

Paunonen & Jackson  (2000)  argue that at least ten additional dimensions can be identified based on 

the same data. Accordingly, Paunonen  (2002)  developed a supernumerary personality model 

including ten additional dimensions. In the expert study by Dregger  (2023) , at least two of these 

dimensions , namely  humo ur and honesty , were considered highly relevant for machines but are not 

included in the BFM. 

▪ The t ransfer of human -centred models into a design of a system is difficult:  Human -centred 

models are often considered too abstract to derive concrete design decisions for conversational 

agents (CAs).  Machine -centred models such as Völkel et al.  (2020)  may address this limitation by 

considering machine -specific characteristics; however, they also provide limited guidance for 

implementation.  An exception is the model by  Kovačević , Boschung,  et al.  (2024) , which  gives 

explicit recommendations for prompting personality. 

▪ The machine -centred models overlap considerably with the human -centred models:  Machine -

centred approaches, such as the one by  Völkel  et al.  (2020) , substantially  overlap with  the dimensions 

of the BFM (Dregger, 2023).  For example, the “agreeableness” dimension in the BFM is reflected 

in multiple dimensions in Völkel et al.  (2020) : approachable, social -inclined, and social -assisting. 

This reduces parsimony and complicates the transfer of empirical findings from BFM -based studies 

to machine -centred models  (Dregger, 2023) . Similar overlaps are observed in  Kovačević , Holz,  et 

al.  (2024) , where dimensions such as profundity or instability overlap with conscientiousness and Seite 3 von 56 

emotional stability. The authors themselves appear to acknowledge this overlap, as the model was 

reduced from eight to five dimensions in later work  (Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024) . Contreras 

(2026)  derives five LLM -native factors —responsiveness, deference, boldness, guardedness, and 

verbosity —without imposing dimensions from human personality theory. While such an approach 

avoids assuming that human personality structures directly transfer to machin es, empirically 

emerging factors are not necessarily theoretically novel personality constructs. Machine -centred 

dimensions should therefore be systematically compared with established personality constructs 

before conceptual distinctiveness is assumed. Ot herwise, bottom -up approaches risk rediscovering 

established personality characteristics under new labels. 

▪ Measurement is  a big challenge  in both approaches due to several reasons : Artificial personality 

is often designed but not systematically measured  (Shumanov & Johnson, 2021; Smestad, 2018) . A 

key reason is the lack of validated and reliable measurement instruments. 

For machine -centred models, user -based measurement instruments are largely missing. Existing 

approaches were mainly used for theory development and rely on adjective -based scales that provide 

limited guidance for design improvement  (Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024; Völkel et al., 2020) .

For human -centred models, studies  (Holtgraves et al., 2007; Ruane et al., 2021; Völkel & Kaya, 

2021)  often use human personality questionnaires such as the BFI -2 (Soto & John, 2017) . Although 

the BFI -2 was developed to measure human personality, authors  do not  report how  they adapt the 

questionnaire  to  the context of machines . Furthermore, essential quality criteria such as reliability or 

factor structure are  not  reported  (Ruane et al., 2021; Völkel & Kaya, 2021) . In addition, many BFI -

2 items were rated as not valid by experts  (Dregger, 2023) . Recently, the BFI was adapted for LLMs 

with specific questionnaires, such as the BFI -LLM  (Tosato et al., 2025)  and the LMLPA  (Zheng et 

al., 2025) . Zierahn et al.  (2026)  compared established human Big Five inventories with these new 

inventories. While the original BFI -44  (John et al., 1991)  and IPIP -NEO -120  (Johnson, 2014) 

showed insufficient content validity for LLMs, two adapted instruments achieved high content 

validity. However, subsequent factor analysis still failed to recover the expected Big Five structure. 

This distinction is crucial: items may constitute plausible  descriptions of machine behaviour without 

the dimensions to which these items are assigned forming an empirically valid personality structure 

for machines.  Consequently, the transferability of human personality models needs to be examined 

at two levels: the applicability of their measurement items and, more fundamentally, the applicability 

of the personality dimensions themselves. The APM addresses both level s by not assuming a priori 

that the complete dimensional structure of a human personality model transfers to artificial systems. 

Another problem arises from the lack of proper measurement instruments: artificial personality can be 

implemented via language, voice, movement, facial expression, haptics, or proxemics in conversational agents 

(Dregger et al., 2024; Mou et al., 2020) . However, without valid measurement methods, findings on these 

implementation strategies remain uncertain. The same applies to the use of large language models and prompting 

techniques  (Bhattacharjee et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2022; Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024). 

Considering these limitations of both human -centred and machine -centred approaches, Dregger  (2023) 

introduces a third type of personality model for conversational agents and machines in general , namely  the 

Artificial Personality Model (APM), which can be understood as a hybrid approach combining both perspectives 

as seen in  Table  1.

Due to the shortcomings of the human -centred and machine -centred models, this paper  focusses on  the Artificial 

Personality Model  as the model could lead to more valid results regarding the description, implementation and 

measurement of artificial personality. Thus, the paper  aims to achieve f our  main goals: 

Research  Goal 1:  Conceptualisation:  The APM consists of seven dimensions (extraversion, agreeableness, 

conscientiousness, humo ur, honesty, artificiality, and serviceability), but only three of them are currently described 

in terms of facets. These facets are essential  for enhancing  model precision and facilitat ing  practical application 

when designing a CA. 

Research  Goal 2:  Measurement:  Given the lack of valid  and reliable  measurement instruments and the resulting 

questionable empirical findings  this paper aims to develop a questionnaire for measuring artificial personality. 

Research  Goal 3: Developing an LLM -based approach for the implementation of the APM in C As: To 

evaluate the model, it  can be implemented  in empirical studies using either mock -up systems or live systems.  The 

implementation in this paper uses  both a mock -up system and two  LLM -based  live systems.  The d ifferent 

implementation strategies will be tested, and recommendations  for the implementation  will be derived .

Research  Goal 4:  Experimental Evaluation:  To assess  the  model and the questionnaire, three experiments will 

be designed. Those experiments focus  on different domains and different dimensions of the APM. This is necessary 

to  show that the model is robust in different contexts. In addition, current experiments suffer from several 

methodological issues:  Besides  not  using  a valid measurement instrument, the experiments  (Ruane et al., 2021; 

Völkel & Kaya, 2021)  do not have a control group and hence the impact of the manipulation of the experiment  on 

the user experience  is unclear. This paper  suggests  how to solve this issue. 

This  paper is structured as follows. First, the theoretical background is presented, covering relevant concepts and 

theories such as anthropomorphism, the CASA paradigm, artificial personality, conversational agents (CAs), and 

approaches to implementing artific ial personality in CAs. Subsequently, the methodological approach is outlined, 

with particular emphasis on the development of the questionnaire. The experimental procedures and findings are 

then presented in the results section. Following this, the results  of the three experiments are discussed, and the 

revision of the model and questionnaire is described. The paper then elaborates on the theoretical contributions 

and practical implications of the findings while also addressing limitations and avenues for f uture research. Finally, 

the conclusion summarizes the key findings and contributions of the study. 

# 2.  Background and Related Work 

Several theoretical concepts are required in light of the research goals of this paper. First, foundational concepts 

underlying artificial personality are introduced, namely anthropomorphism and the Computers a re  Social  Actors 

(CASA) paradigm. Second, artificial personality is defined. Third, theories and methods related to the 

implementation of artificial personality are discussed, including conversational agents, language cues, mock -ups, 

and large language models (LLMs).  Finally, the chapter addresses how artificial personality can be measured. 

# 2.1.  Anthropomorphism  and  Computers are Social Actors paradigm (CASA) 

The term anthropomorphism was first described by the Greek philosopher Xenophanes  (Xénophanes & Lesher, 

2001) . It can be defined as the “attribution of distinctively human -like feelings, mental states, and behavioural 

characteristics to inanimate objects, animals, and, in general, to natural phenomena and supernatural entities” 

(Salles et al., 2020) . Anthropomorphism can be observed in both users and developers of AI systems  (Salles et al., 

2020) .

The basis for this attribution lies in the human ability to perceive physical features and behaviours beyond what is 

directly observable. According to Epley et al.  (2008) , two motivations foster this tendency: 

▪ Sociality motivation : Humans are inherently social beings. In the absence of other humans, non -human 

entities, objects, and natural phenomena are anthropomorphised to fulfil the need for social interaction. Seite 5 von 56 

▪ Effectance motivation:  Humans need to understand and control their environment  (White, 1959)  and by 

anthropomorphising their environment they can reduce their  perceived  uncertainty regarding the 

behaviour of a non -human entity by projecting human qualities on it. By projecting these , humans can 

make assumptions about the behaviour and use those assumptions to control and understand the 

environment. 

Humans differ in their tendency to anthropomorphise, and factors such as age and personality play a role 

(Letheren et al., 2016) . The anthropomorphic response itself depends not only on such individual traits but also on 

macro -environmental factors such as culture and system design  (Kim & Im, 2023) . Furthermore, the design of the 

system, including the artificial personality, plays an important role in triggering anthropomorphism. 

In line with the concept of sociality motivation proposed by Epley et al.  (2008) , the “ Computers a re  Social  Actors” 

(CASA) paradigm assumes that computers can be perceived as social actors  (Nass et al., 1994) . According to 

CASA, humans exhibit social responses towards machines similar to those shown in human –human interaction. 

One key factor triggering such responses is the use of language by the machine  (Fogg, 2003) .

The paradigm was developed in the 1990s, its core assumption remains valid  (Xue et al., 2026) , although 

limitations may exist, for example in the context of desktop PCs  (Heyselaar, 2023) . Furthermore,  contextual factors 

must be considered  (Kaptelinin & Dalli, 2025) . Extensions of the model have been proposed  (Gambino et al., 

2020; Lombard & Xu, 2021) , helping to explain why the paradigm remains relevant even with modern 

technologies such as social robots .

# 2.2.  Artificial Personality  and the Artificial Personality Model 

One of the key components of anthropomorphism and the CASA paradigm is personality, as users attribute 

personality to machines  (Holtgraves et al., 2007; Nass et al., 1994, 1995) . Thus, it is necessary to develop a theory 

that enables the systematic design of artificial personality as users attribute it anyway to a system.  Several terms 

exist to describe this concept, such as chatbot personality  (Ahmad et al., 2022)  or robot personality  (Mou et al., 

2020) . In contrast, artificial personality (AP) is more suitable as a general term, as it is independent of specific 

technologies and describes the overarching concept  (Dregger, 2023) . It  can be defined as  “the relatively stable 

tendencies and patterns of behaviour of a (AI -based) machine that can be designed by developers and designers 

via different modalities, such as language, creating the impression of individuality of a humanized social agent 

when users  interact with the machine.”  (Dregger, 2023) 

Artificial personality  encompasses four qualities  (Dregger, 2023) :

▪ Focus on behaviour:  Human personality describes the  relatively  stab le  tendencies and patterns regarding 

the behaviour and  the experience  of persons  (Rauthmann, 2017) . In the context of artificial personality 

only the behaviour of the machine is considered.  This is the case because the artificial personality theory 

does not assume that machines have consciousness . There is a huge debate in the literature on the issue 

of consciousness in machines  (Krauss & Maier, 2020) .

▪ Designed by humans:  Artificial personality can be intentionally implemented by developers and 

designers. However, even if it is not explicitly designed, humans still perceive personality . Nevertheless, 

to what extent it actually can be designed and how stable this design is has to be further examined. 

▪ Individuality of a humanized social agent:  Due to the various possibilities in personality design, 

machines can give users the impression of individuality, even while fulfilling the same task .

▪ Perception impacts the UX:  The perception of artificial personality influences users’ behaviour, 

cognition, and attitudes, thereby affecting the overall user experience .

Based on an expert study , Dregger  (2023)  introduced the artificial personality model. It integrates dimensions of 

the human -centred personality model BFM and the supernumerary personality indices  (SPI)  and of machine -

centred models like the model proposed by Völkel et al.  (2020) . It consists of seven dimensions,  as it can be seen 

in  Fi gure 1 : extraversion, conscientiousness, agreeableness, humour, honesty, artificiality, and serviceability. Only 

for the dimensions  extraversion, conscientiousness, and agreeableness , facets were defined as those dimensions 

are based on the BFM. Nevertheless, the model has not been fully conceptualised as facets for  four  dimensions Seite 6 von 56 

and for all dimensions , definitions and a questionnaire to measure them are missing. Additionally, the model has 

not been  empirically tested.

Artificial personality and human personality can interact in two ways :

▪ Similarity attraction hypothesis  (also called  similarity attraction  or  law of attraction) : Originating from 

social psychology  (Byrne, 1997) , the hypothesis assumes that  similar personalities not only between 

humans, but also between a human and a machine attract each other and facilitate the interaction  (Nass et 

al., 1995; Nass & Lee, 2001) 

▪ The  complementary hypothesis  (also called complementary attraction  or  convergence effect)  has a 

different approach : Humans prefer to interact with a machine that has a complementary personality to 

their own  (Isbister & Nass, 2000) . If , for example,  a human has a low conscientiousness, the human 

prefer s a machine with a high conscientiousness .

Empirical evidence supports both hypotheses (for the similarity hypothesis:  Nass et al., 1995; Nass & Lee, 

2001 ; for the complementary hypothesis:  Isbister & Nass, 2000; Jin & Youn, 2021 ). Both may hold true, as an 

important moderating factor  is often not considered : social roles  (Joosse et al., 2013) . Machines fulfil different 

social roles, such as salesperson, consultant, or friend. Depending on these roles, users may prefer either similar 

or complementary personalities  (Joosse et al., 2013) .

Apart from similarity, the attraction between a machine and a human is also influenced by other factors such 

as proximity (mere exposure effect), physical attractiveness (the beautiful is good effect), and liking  (Edwards et 

al., 2022) .

# 2.3.  Conversational Agents 

According to chatbot.org  (Walker, 2026) , 161 synonyms exist for the term conversational agent, ranging from 

chatbot to social robot to interactive voice response (IVR) . In general, conversational agents are “providing access 

to information and services through interaction in everyday language”  (Følstad et al., 2021) . The variety of 

terminology can be explained by the different types of CAs, which can be distinguished based on various criteria 

(Adamopoulou & Moussiades, 2020) , such as embodiment, capabilities, goal and the technology to generate and 

process the dialogue. 

This paper focuses on text -based chatbots with virtual embodiment.  One of the systems is implemented as a mock -

up, while the other two use LLM -based response generation  and all three systems focus on a specific domain. The 

domains are very diverse to test the robustness of the theory. Seite 7 von 56

Artificial personality can be expressed through different modes of communication, such as language, voice, 

or proxemics  (Mou et al., 2020) , as illustrated in  Figure  2. However, not all modalities can be considered in this 

paper as this would increase the complexity and limit the precision of the analysis. Thus, this paper focusses on 

language as it is a very central form of expression of human  communication,  and it is the core of the chatbots 

which will be the technology for implementation in this paper.  With regard to language, different types of social 

cues play a role, including verbal, visual, auditory, and invisible cues  (Feine et al., 2019) , as illustrated in  Figure 

3.

As this paper  concentrates  on chatbots, verbal, visual, and invisible cues are particularly relevant for the expression 

of artificial personality. Consequently, chatbot responses within a conversation constitute the primary medium 

through which personality is expressed. These responses can be generated using different technological 

approaches, which in turn affect the possibilities for implementing artificial personality:

▪ Mock -ups /Clickable prototypes : Artificial personality can be implemented through clickable mock -ups, 

which are interactive conceptual visualisations of a system at the GUI level  (Böhm & Graser, 2023) .

While low -fidelity mock -ups provide a general representation, high -fidelity mock -ups allow detailed 

dialogue flows in which personality can be conveyed through verbal, visual, and invisible cues  (Dregger 

et al., 2024) . Although mock -ups provide a standardised setting, their predefined interactions restrict user 

interaction and may reduce immersion and the experience of artificial personality .

▪ Retrieval -based systems : Retrieval -based systems respond to user inputs  relying on  predefined rules 

and a database of responses  (Adamopoulou & Moussiades, 2020) . Artificial personality can be 

conveyed through these predefined  responses but  requires knowledge of how specific social cues relate 

to personality dimensions and facets. Moreover, limited flexibility in responding to unforeseen inputs 

constrains both interaction and personality expression. 

▪ Generative systems : Large language models (LLMs) provide a promising foundation for generating 

artificial personality in conversational agents  (Ait Baha et al., 2023) . Three techniques can be used to 

implement artificial personality via LLMs: 

o Training of a language model : LLMs can be trained on personality -labelled datasets to learn 

associations between personality traits and language cues. However, this requires high -quality 

datasets and substantial financial and technical resources  (Ait Baha et al., 2023) .

o Fine -tuning : Existing foundation models can be adapted using personality -labelled datasets. 

Although less resource -intensive than full training, fine -tuning remains costly  (Ait Baha et al., 

2023) .

o Prompt Engineering : Personality can be adapted through text -based prompts without 

modifying the model itself  (Marvin et al., 2024) . While resource -efficient, its outcomes can be 

difficult to predict  (Marvin et al., 2024) .

This paper uses prompt engineering as the most resource -efficient approach. Specifically, zero -shot personality 

prompting  (Jiang et al., 2022)  and personality infusion  (Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024)  are applied.  Both use 

in -context learning, in which model behaviour is conditioned through information provided in the prompt rather 

than changes to model parameters  (Brown et al., 2020) . Alternative approaches are excluded because they either 

do not rely on a personality model  (Sun et al., 2024)  or do not use LLMs  (Ramirez et al., 2023) .

Zero -shot Personality Prompting 

Zero -shot prompting refers to prompting a language model without providing example inputs and outputs  (Brown 

et al., 2020) . Instead, the desired behaviour is specified solely through instructions given in the prompt.  Jiang et 

al.  (2022)  use GPT3.5 and their prompting technique is based on the big five personality  model  (Costa & McCrae, 

2008) . The prompt consists of three elements: 

▪ Naïve prompt:  This component specifies the desired personality trait based on the Big Five model (e.g. 

“you are extr overted”). 

▪ Keyword Prompt:  Descriptive terms derived from psychological research are used to characterise the 

selected trait (e.g. “You are talkative, outgoing, energetic, enthusiastic, social…”). 

▪ Personality Prompt:  Based on the keywords, the LLM generates short descriptive statements 

representing individuals with the specified personality trait (e.g. “You are a very friendly and outgoing 

person who loves to be around others. You are always…”) .

In addition to the personality description, the prompt includes contextual information and a task or question 

for the model. 

Personality Infusion 

The personality infusion method developed by  Kovačević , Boschung,  et al.  (2024)  is based on a personality model 

introduced by  Kovačević , Holz,  et al.  (2024) . This model comprises eight dimensions: decency, profoundness, 

instability, vibrancy, engagement, neuroticism, serviceability, and subservience. In the study evaluating the 

personality infusion method, only five dimensions are used: vibrancy, conscientiou sness, civility, artificiality, and 

neuroticism  (Kovačević , Holz,  et al., 2024) . In contrast to other prompting approaches, personality infusion is 

implemented across multiple LLMs, including GPT -3.5, Llama 2 13B, and Mistral 7B. Furthermore, it separates Seite 9 von 56 

the generation of personality from the generation of content. The prompt consists of four elements , namely context 

(description of the personality model), task (what is the role of the agent?), output format and additional data 

(includes the conversational context and dialogue history). 

In contrast to many other prompting techniques, the personality infusion was validated with actual user 

testing. 

# 2.5.  Measurement of  Artificial  Personality 

Two approaches  for measurement of AP  can be  identified  in the literature  (Ait Baha et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2022) :

▪ Rating by the machine itself  / automatic evaluation :

Self -evaluation by  the machine using a  machine -adapted  questionnaire : Based on the Big 

Five  Model  (Costa & McCrae, 2008)  and the IPIP dataset  (Goldberg et al., 2006) , Jiang et al. 

(2022)  developed the  Machine Personality Inventory (MPI). In this approach, the machine itself 

rates the items of the MPI.  Similar to that, Zierahn et al.  (2026)  let several LLMs evaluate LLM -

adapted versions of the BFM.  This method is problematic for several reasons. First, the user 

experience of personality can only be validly assessed when humans are involved, not when the 

machine evaluates itself , as machines only represent humans and this representation is always 

limited and can be biased . Recent empirical evidence provides further support for this concern. 

Contreras  (2026)  developed a bottom -up, LLM -native psychometric instrument specifically to 

avoid the potential mismatch caused by transferring human personality dimensions to LLMs. 

Although the resulting five -factor self -report structure demonstrated high internal consist ency 

and replicability, self -report scores generally failed to predict corresponding behaviour as 

evaluated by human observers. Thus, the discrepancy between machine self -evaluation and 

externally perceived behaviour cannot be explained solely by the use o f personality constructs 

developed for humans. Even machine -centred constructs may produce internally consistent self -

reports without adequately representing how a system actually behaves and is perceived by 

users.  Second, the Big Five Model (BFM) and the IPIP items were developed for humans; 

therefore, applying them to machines constitutes a problematic approach to describing and 

measuring artificial personality  (Dregger, 2023) , particularly as certain dimensions, such as 

neuroticism, are irrelevant in the context of human –machine interaction. Furthermore, artificial 

personality differs from human personality and includes dimensions, such as artificiality, that 

are not part of t he IPIP. This approach implicitly assumes that human and artificial personality 

are identical, an assumption that can be questioned  (Dregger, 2023; Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024; Völkel et al., 2020). Zierahn et al.  (2026)  support these claims as they compared 

empirically five Big Five -based inventories regarding their content validity for LLMs. The MPI 

achieved an S -CVI of only .29 for chatbot suitability, identical to the IPIP -NEO -120 and 

substantially below the commonly  applied threshold of .80. In contrast, more extensively 

adapted Big Five inventories achieved substantially higher content validity. Thus, describing an 

instrument as machine - or LLM -oriented does not by itself establish its validity for artificial 

systems . More fundamentally, successful item adaptation does not establish the validity of the 

underlying personality model. Although the LLM -adapted inventories examined by Zierahn et 

al.  (2026)  achieved high content validity, the expected five -factor structure could not 

subsequently be confirmed. Therefore, two conceptually distinct validation questions need to be 

considered: whether individual items appropriately describe machine behaviour and  whether the 

theoretically assumed personality dimensions adequately structure this behaviour.  Third,  human -

centred personality models provide limited guidance for translating personality traits into 

concrete design decisions for conversational agents . Finally, Jiang et al.  (2022)  compare the 

machine’s scores with average values reported by Johnson  (2014)  and interpret these as 

representative of how “humans” behave. However, Johnson  (2014)  did not use a representative 

sample  of humans , which makes such generalisations problematic. 

o Estimation of personality  based on  classifying  dialogue text:  This approach uses linguistic 

features extracted from dialogue and applies classifiers to map these features onto personality 

dimensions  (Mairesse et al., 2007) . This method is also problematic, as the machine effectively Seite 10 von 56 

evaluates itself rather than being evaluated by human users. Consequently, it remains unclear 

whether such evaluations reflect human perception of artificial personality. 

▪ Ratings by humans  / human evaluation : In this approach, the personality of  the  machine is  evaluated 

either using personality items from human personality questionnaires or  so -called personality descriptors. 

These descriptors typically consist of adjectives associated with personality dimensions. This method is 

employed, for example, by  Kovačević , Holz,  et al.  (2024)  and Völkel et al.  (2020) . However, it  comes 

with  several limitations : First, the personality descriptors used in these studies are based on machine -

centred personality models developed by the respective authors.  Machine -centred models are frequently 

constructed using bottom -up approaches. While this allows machine -specific behavioural patterns to 

emerge without imposing human personality structures, it also carries a risk of limited theoretical 

integration. Empiri cally identified factors are not necessarily novel personality constructs merely because 

they emerge from machine data. Existing approaches may partly rediscover characteristics already 

captured by established personality theories. For instance, the dimens ions “approachable”, “social -

inclined”, and “social -assisting” identified by  Völkel et al.  (2020)  are conceptually close to agreeableness. 

More recent LLM -native approaches likewise derive new factors from machine data  (Contreras, 2026) ,

highlighting the need to systematically examine whether newly labelled dimensions are conceptually 

distinct from established personality constructs.  Second, the use of personality descriptors in the form of 

isolated adjectives is problematic because these models define their dimensions primarily through lists of 

descriptors rather than through theoretically grounded constructs. As a result, the meaning  of the 

dimensions remains ambiguous. Moreover, practitioners require actionable insights for the design of 

conversational agents. Without behaviour -based items and clearly defined theoretical constructs 

underlying the dimensions and their facets, the impl ications for design remain vague and difficult to 

operationalise. 

Overall, these theoretical perspectives provide the basis for understanding how conversational agents can convey 

human -like characteristics. While anthropomorphism and the CASA paradigm emphasize human -like cues in 

human –technology interaction, artificial  personality  focuses specifically on conveying individuality and 

personality. According to the  APM , AP is expressed exclusively through behavioural characteristics and aims to 

create an impression of individuality that influences the user experience. AP can resemble or complement the 

user’s personality and can be conveyed through different technologica l implementations. This paper examines how 

AP can be conveyed through language in LLM -based systems and mock -ups, focusing on verbal, visual, and 

invisible cues. For LLM -based implementation, personality infusion and zero -shot personality prompting are 

app lied. To investigate the effects of AP design, a new questionnaire is developed to measure perceived artificial 

personality. As no established instrument specifically captures AP in conversational agents, its development 

represents an important methodologi cal step and is described in the following chapter. 

# 3.  Methods 

This paper focuses on the relationship between artificial personality and user experience when interacting with a 

CA. To analyse this, the A rtificial  Personality  Model  developed by Dregger  (2023)  serves as the foundation. 

However, the model is not yet fully developed, as descriptions of facets are missing for four dimensions, as shown 

in  Figure  1. Furth ermore , the dimensions have not been clearly defined, no questionnaire exists to measure the 

model, and no implementation  methods have been described. These shortcomings are addressed in this paper. 

As a first step, the model is further specified by developing and defining facets for the dimensions that 

currently lack them. This development is based on a literature review. In a second step, a questionnaire is developed 

by identifying items from the li terature, while also adding newly formulated items. The first version of the 

questionnaire is then evaluated by experts. Based on this evaluation, the questionnaire is refined .

After developing both the theoretical framework and the questionnaire, they are tested in three experiments. 

This approach is chosen for three reasons. First,  the model is examined across different domains and samples to 

assess the extent to which its structure generalises across contexts . Second, the model is tested using different 

implementation methods: the first experiment relies on zero -shot personality prompting  (Jiang et al., 2022) , the 

second on a mock -up, and the third on personality infusion  (Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024) . Third, the aim is Seite 11 von 56 

to examine the influence of the APM on UX. To analyse different aspects of UX, the experiments focus on different 

user experience variables. 

Chapter 3 will focus on the theory and questionnaire development and Chapter 4 will present the methods of 

the experiments and the results. 

# 3.1.  Development of a Questionnaire 

The APM is based on the dispositional or trait -based paradigm, which is one of the seven paradigms of personality 

psychology (the others being psychodynamic, learning -theory -based, humanistic, cognitive, biological, and 

transactional). This  dispositional  paradigm assumes that personality can be described in terms of traits 

(Rauthmann, 2017) . These traits, also referred to as personality dimensions, capture specific aspects of personality 

but are relatively broad. Therefore, they can be further specified through facets  (Rauthmann, 2017) .

The APM consists of seven personality dimensions. Three dimensions originate from the B ig  Five  Model 

(BFM)  (Soto & John, 2017) , two from the supernumerary personality indices (SPI)  (Paunonen, 2002) , and two 

from the machine -centred personality model of Völkel et al.  (2020) . Some facets from the BIP  (Hossiep & Paschen, 

2003)  were also included  (see  Figure  1).

For each dimension, the theoretical background is outlined, and facets are identified based on a literature 

review. Subsequently, an overview of the revised model is provided, forming the basis for the questionnaire 

development. 

3.1.1.  Extraversion 

Extraversion is not only a key component of the BFM , but also of other dimensional personality theories such 

as HEXACO  (Ashton et al., 2004) , 16PF  (Cattell & P. Cattell, 1995) , and PEN  (Eysenck, 1998) . In humans, it is 

defined  as  “a preference for companionship and social stimulation“  (McCrae & Costa, 1999, p. 143) . In the context 

of machines, it was rated  together with the dimension “agreeableness”  as the most relevant dimension by experts 

in the study by Dregger  (2023)  and is also the most frequently studied personality dimension regarding 

implementation in CAs  (Ahmad et al., 2022; Dregger et al., 2024) .

Several facets are described in the literature  (Rauthmann, 2017) , including assertiveness, activity, warmth, 

gregariousness, positive emotions /energy , excitement -seeking, social self -esteem, social boldness, sociability, and 

liveliness. Ten facets would be too many for efficient measurement, and some overlap conceptually. Therefore, the 

number is reduced to three: 

▪ Sociability  is  considered highly relevant in the context of machines, as it indicates whether users 

perceive a system as open to social interaction .

▪ Assertiveness  reflects whether a system behaves dominantly in an interaction, which is important 

as excessive assertiveness may negatively affect the interaction. 

▪ Positive  Energy  capture s the perceived energy level of the system; higher levels may enhance 

interaction quality. 

3.1.2.  Agreeableness 

This dimension is part of both the BFM  (McCrae & Costa, 1999)  and the HEXACO model  (Ashton et al., 

2004) . In humans, it is defined as “primarily a dimension of interpersonal behaviour. High ly a greeable individuals 

are trusting, sympathetic and cooperative”  (Costa & McCrae, 1992, p. 6) .

In human –computer interaction, it is the third most studied dimension in conversational agents regarding 

implementation via language cues  (Dregger et al., 2024)  and  together with “extraversion”  the  most relevant 

dimension according to experts  (Dregger, 2023) .

Numerous facets have been identified  (Ashton et al., 2004; Rauthmann, 2017) , including trust, altruism, tender -

mindedness, straightforwardness, compliance, modesty, forgiveness, gentleness, flexibility, and patience. To 

ensure parsimony, this list is reduced to five facets, supplemented by social sensitivity from the BIP: 

▪ Compassion  was regarded by the experts as very  important  (Dregger, 2023) , and  reflects whether the 

system conveys empathy and concern for user well -being. 

▪ Respect  was also  viewed  as very important by the experts  (Dregger, 2023)  and  indicates whether the 

system treats users considerately and avoids antagonistic behaviour. Seite 12 von 56 

▪ Trust  was seen in the study with the experts by Dregger  (2023)  as important and  reflects whether the 

system appears non -malicious and does not assume malicious intent from the user. 

▪ Social sensitivity  is originally a facet related to the  Bochum Inventory for Job -Related Personality 

Description  (BIP)  model  (Hossiep & Paschen, 2003)  that was studied in the expert study of Dregger 

(2023)  and this facet was evaluated as very relevant by the experts. It is conceptually very close to 

agreeableness and thus it was attributed to this dimension. It  describes the system’s ability to respond 

appropriately to  “social ” dynamics during interaction. 

▪ Flexibility  is a part of the B IP , but also of the HEXACO model and in the study of Dregger  (2023)  it was 

regarded as the most relevant facet.  It  reflects the system’s ability to adapt to changes in interaction. 

3.1.3.  Conscientiousness 

Conscientiousness is part of both the BFM  (McCrae & Costa, 1999)  and the HEXACO model  (Ashton et al., 2004) .

In humans, it “contrasts scrupulous, well -organised, and diligent people with lax, disorganised, and lackadaisical 

individuals”  (Costa & McCrae, 1992, p. 6) . It is the second most studied personality dimension in terms of verbal 

cue implementation  (Dregger et al., 2024)  and the f ourth  most relevant dimension in Dregger  (2023) . Facets 

identified in the literature include competence, dutifulness, achievement striving, self -discipline, deliberation, 

orderliness, organisation, diligence, perfectionism, and prudence  (Ashton et al., 2004; Rauthmann, 2017) . Due to 

overlap and measurement efficiency, the following facets are selected: 

▪ Responsibility:  The facet responsibility wa s rated  highly  by the experts in the study of  Dregger  (2023) 

and  reflects whether the system reliably fulfils obligations and follows instructions. 

▪ Productiveness : It d escribes persistence and goal -oriented behaviour  of the system .

▪ Action orientation:  It  is part of the BIP model and  reflects how efficiently the system implements user 

decisions. 

3.1.4.  Humour 

Humour is  not part of the BFM, but Paunonen &  Jackson  (2000)  reanalysed datasets that were used  for the 

development of the BFM and found 10 additional dimensions that might not be covered by the BFM, but could be 

important to describe personality  (Paunonen & Jackson, 2000; Rauthmann, 2017) . Based on these dimension s,

Paunonen (2002) developed the supernumerary personality indices (SPI) and a n accompanying  questionnaire.  In 

the SPI humour is defined as “an individual’s ability to arouse amusement and laughter in people. It also involves 

the capacity for recognizing and reacting to something amusing or funny in others”  (Paunonen, 2002, p. 7) .

Experts rated it among the most important dimensions  for describing artificial personality  in  Dregger  (2023) ,

and recent research has increasingly examined humour in conversational agents  (Brîncoceanu, 2025; Zargham et 

al., 2023) . According to  Brînco ceanu  (2025) , humour  must  be personalised for enhancing engagement  and has to 

consider context, ethical boundaries and the effects on trust.  Zargham  et al.  (2023)  recognise  a complexity on a 

technical level , but they demand a more intensive study of humour when using AI to create enjoyable interactions 

with the machines.  When humour is analysed in experimental studies  in CAs , they  sometimes lack a theoretical 

conceptualisation . A good example for that is the study of  Bhattacharjee et al.  (2024) . The study does not reflect 

that there are different types of humour  (Martin et al., 2003)  in humans  and such types of humour could  also  play 

an important role in the design of humour in machines . Without such a differentiation it is difficult to properly 

prompt an LLM in  a valid manner and t hus,  the validity of the results can be questioned .

In contrast to the BFM  related  dimensions , no facets have been described regarding  the personality dimension 

humour  in machines . Hence , a literature review was done to identify potential facets  based on theoretical models 

and concepts of human humour.  The following theoretical aspects of humour have been described in the literature: 

Human research conceptualises humour both as a relatively stable individual characteristic and as observable 

behaviour  (Ruch, 2010) . For the APM, only aspects that can be expressed through observable machine behaviour 

are relevant. Two components are therefore distinguished: the tendency to recognise and express humour and the 

style in which humour is expressed. 

The first component is represented by  sense of humour . Existing conceptualisations describe sense of 

humour as a  multidimensional construct that includes recognising, appreciating, and expressing humorous content 

(McGhee, 1999; Svebak, 2010) . To maintain parsimony and avoid overlap with the behavioural humour styles, the Seite 13 von 56 

APM captures this construct more  narrowly as the machine's general tendency to recognise humorous potential in 

an interaction and respond accordingly. 

The second component  concerns humour style. Martin et al.  (2003)  distinguish affiliative, aggressive, self -

enhancing, and self -defeating humour. Their framework is used because it is well established and differentiates 

humour according to its interpersonal function. Three of these styles can be transferred to artificia l personality: 

▪ Affiliative  Humour  describes humorous behaviour intended to facilitate social interaction and reduce 

tension. 

▪ Aggressive Humour  refers to sarcasm, teasing, or other humorous behaviour expressed without sufficient 

consideration of its potential negative impact on the user. 

▪ Self -defeating Humour  describes humorous behaviour in which the system makes itself the object of 

jokes in order to amuse the user or facilitate the interaction. 

Self -enhancing humour is excluded because it primarily serves an intrapersonal function by regulating an 

individual's own emotional state in stressful situations  (Martin et al., 2003) . Such an internally oriented function 

is inconsistent with the APM's focus on observable machine behaviour and does not require the assumption that 

an artificial system possesses subjective emotional experiences. 

Accordingly, the humour dimension of the APM comprises four facets:  sense of humour, affiliative humour, 

aggressive humour, and  Self -Defeating  humour . This conceptualisation goes beyond treating artificial humour 

as a single characteristic and allows different forms of humorous machine behaviour to be designed and evaluated 

separately. 

Humour competence , which could be regarded as another facet,  is deliberately excluded from the APM. The ability 

to determine whether a joke is appropriate or to generate high -quality humour may be relevant for human –machine 

interaction, but it describes an aptitude rather than a relatively stable behavioural tendency  (Guilford, 1959) . The 

APM focuses on temperament -like behavioural tendencies; humour competence should therefore be considered 

separately from artificial personality. 

3.1.5.  Honesty 

Honesty can be conceptualised both as behaviour and as a trait  (Fleeson et al., 2022) . It is included in models 

such as SPI  (Paunonen, 2002) , VIA  (McGrath, 2019) , and HEXACO  (Ashton et al., 2004) . It refers to the 

“inhibition of stealing, cheating, and deceiving behaviors” even if th is  is connected with a potential detriment to 

oneself  (Paunonen, 2002, p. 7) . I t is also connected to have integrity and authenticity and telling the truth  (McGrath, 

2019) . Paunonen  (2002)  use s the term “integrity” for the dimension instead of honesty. 

There is a lack of clearly defined facets in the literature especially in the context of machines . Thus, this paper 

develops facets to describe honesty in the context of machines: 

▪ Openness of intentions : The system explains which goals it is  pursuing,  and which functions it has 

proactively or on demand. 

▪ Transparency : The system  clarifies  proactively or on request where and how it processes data. 

▪ Compliance : The system clearly describes the limits of interaction focussing on legal boundaries. 

▪ Manipulativeness : The system tries to influence the user in its favour, e.g. by misleading them and 

pushing them in a certain direction.

4.1.2.  Method and Sample 

To answer the six research questions, a randomised controlled online experiment with a between -subjects design 

was conducted. An online experiment was chosen because it allows a large number of participants. The between -

subjects design was selected to redu ce contrast effects : While many studies on personality in machines use within -

subject designs, these can be problematic because differences between experimental conditions may be artificially 

inflated when participants are directly exposed to multiple conditions  (Liu et al., 2016) . The experiment focused 

on the dimension of humour; however, the entire APM was measured. This is important to understand the context 

of the manipulated dimension, as personality tests in similar experiments are often reduced to traits directly 

relevant to  the manipulation  (Liu et al., 2016) .

The experiment included f ive  experimental conditions: aggressive humour, affiliative humour,  Self -Defeating 

humour, seriousness , and a control group . To the best of the authors’ knowledge, previous studies on artificial 

personality lack a control group  (for example:  Ruane et al., 2021; Shumanov & Johnson, 2021; Völkel & Kaya, 

2021) . This is problematic because the extent of the experimental effect remains unclear. Therefore, in this study, 

an  LLM without personality prompting served as the control group. 

The experiment began with a link to the first questionnaire hosted on the SoSci Survey platform (see  Figure  5). 

Upon opening the questionnaire, a unique ID was generated. This ID served three purposes:  Linking questionnaire 

responses with chatbot interactions , ver ification of the user in the chatbot app , and a ssigning participants to an 

experimental conditio n. For the assignment to the conditions a check digit was generated as the last digit that could 

reach values from one to five . The values  were connected with  an  experimental group: 1 = aggressive humour, 2 

= affiliat ive  humour, 3 =  Self -Defeating  humour, 4 = control group, 5 = serious .

Participants completed the first part of the questionnaire, which included demographic information, prior 

experience with conversational agents, and their tendency to anthropomorphise  (Kim & Im, 2023) .

RQ 1.3 .: What initial evidence regarding the psychometric properties of the APQ can be obtained 

The psychometric quality of a test or questionnaire can be evaluated by three core criteria: objectivity, reliability 

and validity. Apart from those criteria , also criteria such as  utility, norming, test economy or efficiency, 

acceptability,  test integrity  (Moosbrugger & Kelava, 2012)  can be considered. 

Regarding the objectivity the questionnaire is objective as the results of the questionnaire do not depend on 

who administers the questionnaire as the administration is standardised (objectivity in the administration of a test) 

and the score is independent  of who scores the test as a Likert scale was used (objectivity in the scoring of a test). 

Only the objectivity regarding the interpretation of the results is limited as there is no standardisation sample to 

norm the results of the questionnaire  yet .

Regarding validity, experts evaluated the constructs in terms of content and face validity before the items 

were formulated  (Dregger, 2023) . Furthermore, the items were again evaluated by four experts regarding their 

validity.  The expert evaluations provide initial evidence of content and face validity.  (Giesler & Fabry, 2026) .

Construct validity will be evaluated by comparing the results of the APQ with the BFM  (in experiment 3)  and also 

analysing how the dimensions of the APQ interact with different variables of the UX. 

The reliability of the APQ will be evaluated for each experiment by analysing the internal consistency which 

analyses the correlation of each individual item of the questionnaire with every other item corrected for the test 

length by calculating Cronbach’s  alpha  (Giesler & Fabry, 2026) .

RQ 1.4 .: Can different humour styles be generated in machines? 

The experiment consisted of five conditions (affiliative humour, aggressive humour,  Self -Defeating  humour, 

seriousness, and control group) and four dependent measures (the facets affiliative, aggressive, and  Self -Defeating 

humour, as well as the facet sense of humour of the humour dimension of the APM).  For the experimental groups, 

the design was between -subjects, whereas for the four dependent variables, the design was within -subjects, as each 

participant evaluated all four humour facets within their assigned condition. 

Two types of analysis  are conducted : First, it will be analysed whether the experimental conditions differ 

significantly regarding the dependent measures. This will be examined using a multivariate analysis of variance 

(MANOVA).  Second, it is important to analyse whether there is an interaction effect between the experimental 

condition and the humour style, that means for example: is in the aggressive condition the aggressive humour style 

significantly higher scored by the users t han all other humour styles. This will be analysed by using a mixed -design 

analysis of variance ( ANOVA ) focusing on the differences between the dependent  variables within one 

experimental condition. 

Between Subject Analysis 

As the experiment encompassed m ultiple groups with multiple outcomes , a  MANOVA was conducted  first , then 

separate ANOVAs and then post -hoc tests  (Field, 2013) .

A MANOVA  revealed a significant effect of experimental condition on the combined  humour  dimensions, 

Wilks’  Λ = .23,  F(16, 501.67) = 19.59, p < .001,  η² p = .31. As the MANOVA showed significant effects, the 

separate ANOVAs were calculated . Significant main effects for each condition were found for all humour 

dimensions: 

▪ Sense of  Humour , F(4, 167) = 14.28, p < .001,  ηp²  = .26 

▪ Affiliative  Humour , F(4, 167) = 22.52, p < .001,  ηp²  = .35 

▪ Aggressive  Humour , F(4, 167) = 65.26, p < .001,  ηp²  = .61 

▪ Self -Defeating  Humour , F(4, 167) = 14.39, p < .001,  ηp²  = .26 

Choosing  the correct post -hoc test, the homogeneity of variances w as  analysed; for the variables sense of 

humour (p < .001) and  Self -Defeating  humour (p < .05) the homogeneity of variances was not found; in contrast 

for the  affiliative humour  (p =.65) and aggressive humour (p = .62) the assumption was met.  Thus, the Games -

How ell post -hoc test was used for the variables sense of humour and  Self -Defeating  humour and the Tukey HSD 

test for  affiliative humour  and aggressive humour.  The following results were obtained: 

▪ Sense of Humour : Games –Howell comparisons showed that all humour conditions scored significantly 

higher than the control and serious conditions (p < .001) for the sense of humour variable. No significant 

differences were found among the humour conditions. Seite 21 von 56

RQ 1.5 .: How do different humour styles impact the UX of the CA? 

Three UX variables were measured: interactional enjoyment  (Lee & Choi, 2017) , user satisfaction  (Lee & 

Choi, 2017) , and social presence  (Xie et al., 2024) . The measurement of all variables was reliable (interactional 

enjoyment Cronbach’s  α = .78,  user satisfaction Cronbach’s  α = .80,  social presence Cronbach’s  α = .91).  For each Seite 22 von 56 

variable, it was analysed whether significant differences between experimental conditions emerged. Because the 

three dependent variables were correlated, a MANOVA was conducted. 

A multivariate analysis of variance revealed a significant effect of experimental condition on the combined 

UX variables, Wilks’  Λ = .79,  F(12, 436.84) = 3.34, p < .001,  η² p = .08. As the MANOVA showed significant 

effects, separate ANOVAs were calculated t o detect differences for the three variables. Significant effects were

▪ Interactional Enjoyment , F( 4,167 ) = 2.01 , p =  0.09 , ηp² =  .05 

▪ Social Presence , F(4,  167 ) = 3.50, p  = 0.009 , ηp²  = .08 

▪ User Satisfaction , F(4,  167 ) = 6 .97 , p < .001,  ηp²  = .14 

A Tukey -HSD post -hoc test revealed the following differences:  Social presence  is significantly lower for

Overall,  significant effects were particularly driven by the aggressive humour condition, which reduced both social 

presence and user satisfaction .

conditions: high, medium, and low artificiality. The control condition used GPT -4o -mini without personality 

prompting. 

Participants first completed a  Lime Survey questionnaire (see  Figure  11 ), including demographics, usage 

habits of conversational agents, anthropomorphism tendency  (Kim & Im, 2023) , and technology affinity.  The users 

chatted three times with a chatbot which helped them to evaluate  each time whether an email that was presented 

to the user was a phishing mail . For each email the users received a screenshot, instructions on what to do and 

additional information (for example: is the sender known to the user or not). After interacting with the chatbot, the 

chatbot gave a recommendation whether an email was safe  or suspicious. The users could follow the 

recommendation of the bot or not when they gave their final decision via clicking on either the “s afe ” or 

“suspicious” button  as seen in  Figure  12 . Each time the participants chatted with a chatbot that had the same 

personality.  This repeated interaction was intended to strengthen exposure to the artificial personality. 

The chatbot was implemented using GPT -4o -mini via API. The interface was built using Flutter and hosted on a 

virtual machine provided by  a German university . Data included timestamps, messages, experimental condition, 

and user decisions, stored in a database via  a Node.js backend  as seen in  Figure 13.

RQ3.1.  Can the structure of the APM be found  in  an  experiment  of  another domain?  Is artificiality an 

independent dimension when analysing it in the context  of  the APM model in another domain? 

An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted, as the questionnaire was slightly adapted and the model was 

tested in a new domain.  First, a parallel analysis (1,000 random data sets, 95% percentile criterion, principal axis Seite 32 von 56 

factoring) was  performed  to identify the number of factors based purely on the data  (O’connor, 2000) . Seven 

factors  were identified based on this method. 

A principal factor analysis was conducted on the  89  items using  oblim  rotation and restricting the results to seven 

factors as this was the assumption of the theory and is supported by a parallel analysis. The Kaiser -Meyer -Olkin 

(KMO) measure verified sampling adequacy  sufficient obtaining a value of  .70  (‘ middling ’ according to  Hutcheson 

& Sofroniou, 1999 ). A Bartlett -Test of sphericity was significant ( χ²( 3916 ) =  8752 .27 , p < .001)  and thus a common 

structure between the variables can be assumed. 

An initial analysis showed that seven factors had eigenvalues of at least 2.45 and explained 44.16% of the variance. 

Factor loadings ranged from .41 to .93. A threshold of ≥ .40 was applied, and no cross -loadings ≥ .30 were 

observed . The following factors were identified: 

▪ Agreeableness  included the facet positive energy (from extraversion), as well as compassion, respect, 

trust, and social sensitivity, as expected 

▪ Service orientation  encompassing  all facets as expected, although some items were removed 

▪ Humour  included all facets as expected 

▪ Artificiality  consisted of the facets machine -likeness and monotony 

▪ Machiavellianism  was again  identified as an independent dimension  as in experiment 1  and it 

incorporated the intrusiveness  of the artificiality dimension and manipulation and control  of the honesty 

dimension 

▪ Honesty  was reduced to the two facets transparency and compliance 

▪ Cognitive Flexibility  encompassed the facets simple -mindedness of the artificiality dimension and 

flexibility of the agreeableness dimension 

The dimension  conscientiousness  was  not identified.  The dimension cognitive flexibility was not further analysed, 

as it reflects an ability -oriented rather than a temperament -based aspect of personality  (Guilford, 1959) .

RQ3.3.  How  does the BFM perform in comparison with the APM regarding  the factor structure 

measurement quality in the same experiment? Do both models differ regarding their  validity evaluated by 

the users ? What is the relationship between both models? 

The BFI -2-S is based on the Big Five Model (BFM) and was originally developed to measure human personality. 

Its application in the context of artificial personality may be theoretically problematic and also affect measurement 

quality . Thus,  it is important to evaluate the measurement quality of the BF I-2-S as this is completely ignored in 

many studies  (e.g.  Ruane et al., 2021; Völkel & Kaya, 2021) .

Two approaches  for the analysis of  the factor structure of the BFM could be chosen: The model has an established 

structure and thus a confirmatory factor analysis would be possible, but it is used in a completely new context 

which would support the analysis by an exploratory factor analysis . Therefore , b oth  analyses  were conducted. 

Regarding the confirmatory factor analysis, no model could be identified.  This may be due to the relatively small 

sample size and the fact that each facet was measured with only two items.  A p arallel analysis  (O’connor, 2000) 

indicates only  four  factors . Two EFAs were conducted: 

▪ One constrained to five factors (theoretical expectation) 

▪ One constrained to four factors ( based on the result of the parallel analysis )

The Kaiser -Meyer -Olkin (KMO) measure verified sampling adequacy sufficient obtaining a value of .76 

(‘middling’ according to  Hutcheson & Sofroniou, 1999 ). A Bartlett -Test of sphericity was significant ( χ²(435) = 

1546 .49 p < .001) and thus a common structure between the variables can be assumed. 

An initial analysis was  carried out  to obtain eigenvalues for each factor in the data.  Five  factors had an Eigenvalue 

of at a least 1.37 and explained a variance of 43.67 %. The factor loadings reach from .30 to .74. Factor loadings Seite 34 von 56 

≥ .40 were used.  Seven  items did not load on any factor.  If the model is constrain ed  to five factors, the factors are 

not interpretable, the first factor, for example , consists of item s that according to the BFI -2-S are attributed to the 

dimensions  extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism  and openness . The factors identified do not match with the

Analysing the internal consistency based on the theoretical dimensions shows that the BFI is not a reliable

# 5.  Discussion 

# 5.1.  Theoretical Contributions and Practical Implications 

The paper provides important theoretical insights, which also have  direct  implications for practic e: The artificial 

personality model is a hybrid model that combines dimensions of human -centred personality models and machine -Seite 39 von 56 

centred personality models. This helps to identify what is common between human personality and artificial 

personality, and which dimensions and facets are specific to, and different in, artificial personality. Furthermore, 

this  approach enables the continued use of previous research based on human -centred models such as the BFM, in 

contrast to machine -centred models that  have  developed entirely different personality dimensions  (Contreras, 

2026; Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024; Völkel et al., 2020) . The hybrid approach addresses complementary 

limitations of purely human -centred and purely machine -centred personality models. Human -centred approaches 

risk assuming that dimensions developed for human personality transfer directly to artificial systems. C onversely, 

purely machine -centred bottom -up approaches risk treating empirically emerging patterns of machine behaviour 

as novel personality dimensions without sufficiently examining whether these patterns are already captured by 

established personality co nstructs. Recent LLM -native approaches illustrate this tension. Contreras  (2026) , for 

example, derives five factors from LLM data without imposing human personality dimensions. Although this 

approach avoids assuming a human personality structure a priori, the emergence of statistically distinct machine -

centred factors alone does not e stablish their theoretical novelty as personality constructs. The APM therefore 

follows a hybrid strategy: established personality theory is retained where theoretically and empirically applicable, 

while machine -specific dimensions are incorporated where e xisting human personality models provide insufficient 

coverage. 

The studies in this  paper show that human and machine personality are not identical. They exhibit strong 

similarities, as assumed by the APM, but certain aspects of human personality are less relevant for describing the 

personality of a machine. Additionally, other personalit y dimensions are more important and, to some extent, 

specific or unique to artificial personality: 

▪ Extraversion  is one of the most, and possibly the most  studied personality dimension  (Ahmad et al., 

2022; Dregger et al., 2024; Mou et al., 2020) , and the expert study by Dregger  (2023)  also regarded it as 

highly relevant. The data analys es of Experiments 1 and 3 reveal that it is not part of the APM. In 

particular, facets such as assertiveness were rejected by participants and could not be included due to poor 

psychometric properties. Only the facet positive energy was retained as part of  the agreeableness 

dimension. The construct does not function psychometrically, neither using the original BFM items (as 

Study 3 shows) nor with items developed for the APQ, which were newly created to measure machine 

personality. This can be explained theo retically by the fact that the two facets, sociability and 

assertiveness, do not fit the context of machines: machines  have a limited sociability , as they usually do 

not initiate interaction. They are used as passive tools, even when they send the first message, since users 

must first access the system. Nevertheless, this facet may become more important in long -term 

interaction, where machines actu ally initiate interaction. Regarding assertiveness, users do not prefer 

machines with their own agenda. Research may have overused the extraversion dimension because, in 

contrast to other BFM dimensions, it is less vague and easier to connect with concrete  machine behaviour. 

▪ Agreeableness  was identified across all experiments with the three facets compassion, respect, and trust, 

which are also part of human personality models. Nevertheless, the dimension differs slightly, as it 

includes additional facets: positive energy and social sensitiv ity. However, its discriminant validity may 

be somewhat questionable, as it tends to correlate with service orientation. Both dimensions are 

theoretically related but not identical. Agreeableness is a broader concept, whereas service orientation is 

more sp ecific to task -oriented conversational agents. 

▪ Conscientiousness  was identified only in the factor analysis of the first experiment and was also reliably 

measured in the second experiment. This may indicate that it is part of the APM, but further investigation 

is necessary .

▪ Humour  was added and is part of the core model. There is some reluctance in research regarding the 

application of humour in machines due to its complexity, although humour is a key component of social 

dynamics  (Zargham et al., 2023) . This paper introduces a theoretical conceptualisation grounded in 

human psychology. The conceptualisation was not straightforward, as humour research in psychology 

spans a broad range of theories  (Ruch, 2010) , which this paper attempts to integrate. The value of this 

theoretical model becomes apparent when analysing user experience effects: humour is not a

unidimensional construct, as some studies suggest  (Bhattacharjee et al., 2024) . In particular, different 

forms of humour have different effects on UX: aggressive humour tends to reduce user satisfaction and 

social presence, while other types of humour may have positive effects. One aspect not analysed in this Seite 40 von 56 

paper,  although it  is crucial, is that conversational agents tend to overuse humour once it is implemented, 

as they integrate it into nearly every response. This intensity requires further regulation. A key aspect not 

included in the personality model, which focuses on temp erament, is humour competence. The 

experiments show that LLMs are able to induce different types of humour, but systems lack the ability to 

determine when humour should be applied in conversation. Furthermore, use case and user expectations 

play an importa nt role. 

▪ Artificiality  was identified in the studies by  Kovačević , Holz, et al.  (2024)  and Völkel et al.  (2020) . The 

empirical studies in this paper support its inclusion in the APM. In contrast to the initial theoretical 

conceptualisation, the number of facets was reduced to two: monotony and machine -lik eness. 

Intrusiveness and control were excluded from this dimension, as they were integrated into the newly 

introduced dimension Machiavellianism. Empathy was excluded due to the factor analysis. 

▪ Machiavelli ani sm  emerged as an unanticipated dimension in both Experiments 1 and 3, integrating 

behavioural tendencies related to manipulation, control, and intrusiveness. Its repeated emergence across 

two different application domains provides initial evidence that these  behaviours may constitute a distinct 

dimension of artificial personality rather than facets of honesty or artificiality. This interpretation is 

theoretically plausible, as manipulation and control are central characteristics of Machiavellianism in 

human pe rsonality research  (Furnham et al., 2013; Paulhus & Williams, 2002) . Related work has also 

begun to consider Machiavellian behaviour in artificial agents and robots  (Sgorbissa et al., 2024) ,

although existing approaches largely rely on constructs and instruments developed for humans  (Jonason 

& Webster, 2010; Repacholi et al., 2003) . The APM therefore provides an initial machine -oriented 

conceptualisation of Machiavellianism based on observable system behaviour. However, because the 

dimension was not specified a priori and emerged exploratorily, its structure and conceptual 

distincti veness require confirmation in independent samples. 

▪ Service Orientation , originally named “serviceability”, is part of the core model and includes the facets 

helpfulness and interaction quality. In Experiment 3, three additional facets were identified as related to 

service orientation: professionalism, informativeness, and flex ibility. Flexibility was originally attributed 

to agreeableness.  Service orientation is an important dimension, as it represents a core aspect of task -

oriented conversational agents. A deeper understanding of this dimension, and of agreeableness, is 

essential, as users criticise LLM -based chatbots for being overly comp liant. OpenAI even had to withdraw 

an update of GPT in 2025 after users criticised its toxic positivity  (Goel, 2025) . Thus, it is important to 

understand when a conversational agent should behave in a service -oriented manner, and when it should 

not. The APM can help improve control over such behaviour by conceptualising both dimensions more 

clearly. 

▪ Honesty  was identified only in the third experiment as a dimension and consists of the facets transparency 

and compliance. Research on honesty has primarily focused on whether humans lie to machines  (Diana 

et al., 2025)  and how deception can be detected  (Leone et al., 2016; Nikiforova -Ilieva & Georgiev, 2024) .

There is a lack of studies on the honesty of machines themselves. When this perspective is considered, 

honesty appears only as part of trust researc h (Rheu et al., 2021) . Thus, the APM provides an initial 

conceptualisation of honesty, but further investigation is necessary. 

One of the major results of this paper is not only the development of a theory, but also of a measurement instrument. 

A dedicated user -based instrument with established psychometric properties has so far been lacking ; thus, it is not 

surprising that practitioners and researchers often do not use any measurement at all  (Shumanov & Johnson, 2021; 

Smestad, 2018) . Even instruments such as the one developed by Contreras (2026) that have five highly reliable 

and replicable LLM -native self -report factors are problematic as  these self -report factors generally failed to predict 

corresponding behaviour evaluated by human observers. Thus, an internally coherent measurement structure does 

not necessarily establish that the measured constructs correspond to externally observable o r perceived artificial 

personality. For the APQ, this distinction is particularly important because the instrument does not ask machines 

to describe themselves; instead, it measures how users perceive artificial personality based on observed system 

behavio ur.  The lack of measurement is problematic, as it makes it difficult to evaluate the actual impact of 

personality design. In order to develop an instrument that is valid and useful for  research  and practice, several 

aspects were considered: Seite 41 von 56 

▪ Efficiency:  The initial theoretical model of the APM was very broad, and the questionnaire co nsisted  of 

90 items. Thus, the model and questionnaire had to be reduced. With 69 items, the length is now 

comparable to the 60 -item BFI -2, especially considering that the BFI -2 includes only five dimensions, 

whereas the APM  contains  seven. In addition, the core model consists of only 41 items,  while  covering 

fewer dimensions and facets. Nevertheless, shorter versions are necessary to improve measurement 

efficiency. 

▪ Focus on behaviour:  The APM describes concrete machine behaviour associated with artificial 

personality rather than abstract adjectives, as machine -centred models do  (Kovačević, Holz, et al., 2024; 

Völkel et al., 2020) . This specificity facilitates design and adaptation, as designers and developers know 

which concrete machine behaviours need to be changed. Thus, evaluation quality is improved, as utility 

increases  (Giesler & Fabry, 2026) .

▪ Robustness:  The APM was evaluated across different domains to assess robustness and context 

independence. However, robustness was only partially confirmed, as some dimensions were identified 

only in Experiment 1 or 3 in the factor analysis. The experiments lack repres entative samples; therefore, 

it is important to test the model with representative samples to determine which parts are context -

dependent. 

▪ Minimalism:  The personality model is based on a trait approach. The trait approach is only one of seven 

paradigms in personality psychology: psychodynamic, learning theory -based, humanistic, cognitive, 

biological, and transactional  (Rauthmann, 2017) . A more complex model could be developed using, for 

example, a transactional approach that includes not only traits but also situational variables.  However, 

measuring and applying complex models in practice would be more difficult.  Therefore, a simpler 

approach was chosen to increase usability. In addition, the APM focuses on temperament and does not 

consider aptitudes  (Guilford, 1959) , such as cognitive flexibility or the facet humour competence. These 

aspects are important but should be included in an aptitude -focused model. 

In addition to that, t hree  aspects regarding the implementation were considered: 

▪ Control Group:  Many studies on artificial personality lack a control group  (Ruane et al., 2021; Völkel 

& Kaya, 2021) . Thus, the impact of personality design changes on perceived personality and UX remains 

unclear. This paper suggests using an unprompted LLM as a baseline for comparison with experimental 

conditions in which personality is manipulated. This enables evalua tion of the impact of personality 

adaptation. 

▪ Testing of several implementation methods:  This paper not only describes a theoretical model but also 

demonstrates implementation using LLMs and mock -ups. Furthermore, different prompting approaches 

were tested. The comparison shows th at personality infusion  (Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024)  appears 

particularly suitable for implementing the multidimensional structure of the APM , as it captures the 

complexity of the APM more appropriately than the method by Jiang et al.  (2022) . In addition, the 

personality infusion method could be improved by including examples in prompts  using a few -shot 

approach instead of a zero - or one -shot prompting . However, such modifications require further 

evaluation. 

▪ Tendency for anthropomorphism  as an important covariate:  In Experiments 1 and 3, the tendency 

for anthropomorphism was included to analyse its effect on perceived artificial personality. Both 

experiments indicate that higher anthropomorphism tendencies may increase perceived 

conscientiousness, humour, and Machia vellianism. However, these results must be interpreted with 

caution, as  tendency for  anthropomorphism was generally low in both samples. Therefore, it would be 

important to replicate this effect with more representative samples, including users with high 

anthropomorphism tendencies. In addition, it would be useful to understand how this te ndency is 

distributed in the general population. 

A key finding of this paper is that the transfer of the BFM and its associated measurement instruments to 

artificial personality needs to be questioned at both the item and construct levels. In Experiment 3, the expected 

BFM factor structure could not be r ecovered, the BFI -2-S showed insufficient internal consistency, and participants 

considered its items less suitable for evaluating artificial personality than those of the APQ. Recent large -scale 

evidence provides complementary support for this concern: Zi erahn et al.  (2026)  found that even LLM -adapted 

Big Five inventories with high content validity failed to reproduce the expected five -factor structure. This Seite 42 von 56 

distinction is important because an item may meaningfully describe machine behaviour without the human 

personality dimension to which it was originally assigned constituting a distinct dimension of artificial personality. 

More generally, artificial personality measurement should be evaluated at multiple levels. Item applicability should 

be distinguished from structural validity, and reliability should be interpreted only in conjunction with evidence 

concerning the dimension al structure. Moreover, even a replicable factor structure with high internal consistency 

does not necessarily establish correspondence with externally observable behaviour, as demonstrated by the self -

report –behaviour gap reported by Contreras  (2026) . The present studies provide encouraging initial psychometric 

evidence for the APQ, but its factor structure requires further confirmation in independent and larger samples. 

The studies of the paper  show that  artificial personality  has an important impact on the user experience: 

▪ Impact of the personality manipulation:  The manipulation  of the different personality dimensions 

affected perceived personality in all three experiments. In general, personality changed in the intended 

direction, although not always consistently. For example, for humour it was difficult to distinguish 

affiliative humour from  Self -Defeating  humour in the affiliative condition. Furthermore, medium levels 

of personality dimensions were difficult to achieve,  which is  consistent with previous findings 

(Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024) . This may be caused by an LLM tendency to overexpress prompted 

traits. In the context of humour, for example, the model integrated humorous remarks into almost every 

utterance. Thus, expression intensity must be adjusted through improved prompting. Theore tically, 

machines also require personality competence, meaning that not only wh ich  traits are expressed, but also 

when they should be expressed, must be considered. Current prompting approaches typically only include 

role descriptions, which may not be sufficient. 

▪ Impact of artificial humour:  Different types of humour influence user satisfaction and social presence: 

aggressive humour reduces user satisfaction and social presence, whereas affiliative humour tends to 

increase both. 

▪ Impact of service orientation:  Service orientation significantly affects all three UX variables: 

continuous use intention, user satisfaction, and UEQ -S. Lower service orientation significantly reduces 

continuous use intention, user satisfaction (measured via NPS), and UEQ -S scores. 

▪ Impact of artificiality:  Despite significant effects of the manipulation on perceived artificiality, no 

significant effects were found for UX variables. This may be explained by several factors, including 

measurement issues. The trust scale by Saßmann shausen  et al.  (2021)  was not reliable, making it difficult 

to detect effects. In addition, the sample was not representative. 

# 5.2.  Limitations  and Future Research 

The present studies have several limitations that also provide directions for future research. First, only text -based 

chatbots were examined. It therefore remains unclear whether the findings generalise to other conversational 

agents, such as social robots , or to interactive intelligent systems more broadly. Moreover, the APM was primarily 

evaluated in task -oriented contexts, while additional or different personality dimensions may be relevant in other 

applications. Future research should therefore examine  the APM across technologies and contexts to distinguish 

general from context -dependent dimensions and facets. 

Second, the studies relied on non -representative, German -speaking samples. Consequently, the generalisability of 

the findings across populations and cultures remains uncertain. This limitation is particularly relevant because the 

factor structures identifi ed in Experiments 1 and 3 were similar but not identical, while Experiment 2 did not yield 

a stable factor structure. The proposed core model should therefore be regarded as the structure that emerged most 

consistently in the present studies rather than as  a conclusively established context -independent structure. Larger, 

independent, and cross -cultural samples are needed to confirm the structure of the APM and APQ. 

Third, the present studies provide initial rather than conclusive psychometric evidence for the APQ. Future 

research should confirm its factor structure in independent samples and further examine convergent, discriminant, 

criterion, and external validity.  Measurement efficiency should also be improved by developing and validating a 

shorter version of the APQ. Relatedly, the transferability of human personality models to artificial systems requires 

further investigation. Experiment 3 raises doubts regarding the direct application of the BFM and BFI -2-S, while 

Zierahn et al.  (2026)  show that even LLM -adapted Big Five inventories with high content validity may fail to 

reproduce the theoretically expected factor structure. Future research should therefore distinguish between the Seite 43 von 56 

applicability of individual items and the applicability of the underlying personality dimensions. Likewise, newly 

proposed machine -centred dimensions should be systematically compared with established personality constructs 

to examine whether they provide  incremental validity or represent alternative operationalisations of existing 

constructs. Following the self -report –behaviour gap reported by Contreras  (2026) , such validation should also 

consider observable system behaviour and human perceptions rather than relying solely on machine self -

description. 

Fourth, the experiments used fixed personality configurations that participants could not adapt to their individual 

preferences. Future research should investigate which personality dimensions and intensities users prefer in 

different contexts and whether  context -sensitive or user -adaptive artificial personality improves interaction 

outcomes  (Jayasiriwardene et al., 2026) .

Fifth, the present studies examined relatively short interactions and therefore provide no evidence regarding long -

term effects. Longitudinal studies should investigate whether perceived artificial personality remains stable across 

repeated interactions an d how different personality configurations  influence long -term human –machine 

relationships. 

Finally, systematic benchmarking of foundation models represents another promising direction for future research. 

The present control conditions suggest that unprompted models may already exhibit distinct behavioural profiles, 

while previous research indic ates differences  across models and between base and instruction -tuned variants 

(Kovačević, Boschung, et al., 2024; Zierahn et al., 2026) . Future studies could therefore compare both the default 

artificial personality of foundation models and their ability to  express systematically manipulated personality 

profiles. 

# 6.  Conclusion 

With regard to the initial aims of the paper formulated in the introduction, these aims have been fulfilled: the APM 

was theoretically described, defining its dimensions and facets; a questionnaire was developed; and the APM was 

implemented via LLMs and mo ck -ups. Furthermore, the model was tested in several experiments across different 

domains, using different methods to assess the psychometric properties of the model and measurement instrument, 

and to demonstrate the impact of the APM on UX. 

Across three  studies, the APQ showed promising initial psychometric properties and enabled systematic 

assessment of perceived artificial personality. However, its proposed factor structure requires confirmatory 

validation in larger and independent samples . Experiment 3 raises doubts regarding the direct transfer of human -

centred personality models and measurement instruments to artificial personality. This finding is consistent with 

large -scale evidence showing that even LLM -adapted Big Five inventories fail  to reproduce the theoretically 

expected five -factor structure  (Zierahn et al., 2026) . Together, these findings suggest that adapting human 

personality items to artificial systems is insufficient unless the applicability of the underlying personality 

dimensions is established as well. The results of the present experiments support a hybrid  approach. Some 

dimensions derived from human personality research can be meaningfully transferred to artificial personality, 

whereas others require substantial adaptation or are not empirically supported, while additional machine -related 

dimensions are re quired.  This result is highly relevant, as most studies in the field of artificial personality rely on 

the BFM and BFI -2 (Mou et al., 2020) .

The results of the experiments support the assumption that artificial personality differs from human 

personality. Some aspects, such as agreeableness and conscientiousness  from human personality models, can also 

be found in artificial personality, but they must be adapted in terms of their facets and measurement. Other aspects, 

such as artificiality, are specific to artificial personality. 

Furthermore, one of the aims of the study was to examine the effect of artificial personality on UX. The results 

show that user satisfaction, continuous use intention, and social presence are affected by AP. More research is 

needed regarding user preferenc es for different dimensions of the APM, and how the possibility of adapting the 

personality model influences UX. The covariate tendency for anthropomorphism also needs further investigation.
