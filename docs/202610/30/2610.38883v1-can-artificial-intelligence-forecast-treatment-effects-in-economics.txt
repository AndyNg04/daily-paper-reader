# Can Artificial Intelligence Forecast Treatment Effects in Economics? 

Eva Vivalt ∗

October 28, 2026 

Abstract 

I use LLMs to forecast treatment effects of research studies pre-registered on the AEA RCT Registry and the Open Science Framework. I focus on studies whose results were first publicly released in 2026 and elicit forecasts from models whose training or knowledge cut-off dates were before the relevant set of papers was released. First, LLM models extract forecasting questions from the pre-registrations or pre-analysis plans. These questions elicit forecasted treatment effects; not all resolve in the subsequent papers, but those that do can be used to consider forecast accuracy. Forecasts were elicited for 889 treatment effects from 39 papers with results disclosed in January 2026 or later, or 673 from 33 papers after screening. Forecasts were elicited in raw units but I focus on those that could be standardized and therefore compared across papers. Currently, the median absolute forecast error is 0.054 SD for the best model, the average absolute error is 0.093 SD, and the MSE is 0.0218. The AI forecasts perform substantially better than benchmarks forecasting 0 effect or forecasting the mean effect of other papers’ results in that time period. Overall, these results suggest considerable scope for forecasts to be of value in informing policy and research.

1arXiv:2610.38883v1 [econ.GN] 28 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

How well can we predict the results of a study before its findings are known? Decision-makers regularly draw on existing evidence to anticipate the effects of a treatment, but often high-quality evidence does not exist and they need to make their best guess. Large language models offer a way to generate predictions from descriptions of research projects before they resolve, however, the accuracy of these models is not known. Research on human forecasts documents they contain predictive power as well as systematic errors (DellaVigna and Vivalt, 2025; Dreber et al., 2015; Camerer et al., 2016), and recent evidence establishes that AI can also predict effects in social science experiments (Ashokkumar et al., 2026). This paper shows that LLMs can forecast the magnitudes of treatment effects across a broad range of economic interventions using pre-registrations and pre-analysis plans. Their forecasts are informative not only about expected effects, but also about uncertainty: predictive intervals have approximately their stated coverage, and wider intervals identify effects that are harder to predict. This paper considers recent projects registered on the AEA RCT Registry and the Open Science Framework (OSF). The pre-registrations provide descriptions of planned trials in economics. I link these registrations to their earliest identified public results. I restrict attention to those with papers first shared in 2026, in order to use results released after the reported knowledge cutoff of GPT-5.5 in December 2025 and look at subsets from February 2026 and May 2026 in order to include still more recent models like Opus 4.8, Fable 5, and GPT-6 Astra. More capable models, as measured by the Epoch Capability Index (ECI), seem to do a better job forecasting than older models, though the sample sizes available after their knowledge cut-off dates are correspondingly smaller. In total, there were 889 forecasting targets for standardized treatment effects based on results for 39 papers. 673 of these, from 33 papers, passed some screening quality checks, and these form the main analysis sample. The median absolute forecast error for the most recent model (GPT-6 Astra, on a sample of 339 forecasts from 20 papers released since May 2026) is 0.054 SD, with a mean of 0.093 and a MSE of 0.0218. For comparison, using the leave-one-paper-out mean within a time period to forecast effects in the remaining paper would produce a median absolute error of 0.092 SD, a mean of 0.126, and a MSE of 0.0463. Another possible point of comparison is Vivalt (2020), which collected results 2from 635 impact evaluations across 20 development interventions. Across 47 intervention-outcome combinations in that paper with at least 3 papers, a leave-one-out meta-analysis would predict the results of another study with a median absolute forecast error of 0.112 SD, mean of 0.182, and MSE of 0.102. 1 While this sample of papers is different, and in principle a mixed model that included useful explanatory variables could do better at forecasting, this is nonetheless a large gap in forecasting performance. AI has been doing better than humans on other types of forecasting tasks, such as forecasting geopolitical events (Forecasting Research Institute, 2026), and the models are only improving, so there seems to be considerable scope for AI forecasts to usefully inform decisions that would benefit from estimates of treatment effects. 2

A growing literature uses LLMs to simulate human responses and economic behavior (Argyle et al., 2023; Horton, 2023). Ashokkumar et al. (2026) use simulated responses to predict experimen-tal treatment effects, studying primarily survey experiments in psychology and political science, alongside a set of megastudies. This paper examines forecasts across a broad range of economic interventions, asking models to predict treatment effect magnitudes from pre-registration materials. This paper makes four main contributions. First, it shows that LLMs can directly forecast economic treatment effects with small errors across a range of interventions and settings. Second, it compares these forecasts against simple prediction benchmarks. Third, it illustrates that AI forecasts are not only good at estimating the mean effect, but are also pretty well-calibrated in their 10th–90th percentile predictive intervals and in “knowing when they do not know”: wider intervals identify effects that are harder to predict. Finally, it shows that more accurate forecasts are obtained for more detailed protocols. These results imply that AI forecasts can be useful for informing priors in policy and research. They also suggest that to be most useful, more information should be shared. Finally, while this work suggests a role for forecasts, it also indirectly provides a perhaps sobering assessment of current economics research: if results can already be forecast so well, what is gained from research that only modestly overturns priors? Should we not be pursuing more ambitious projects?

I construct a dataset linking public descriptions of planned research with resultant reported treat-ment effects. Figure A.1 summarizes the steps from identifying papers to constructing the evalua-tion sample. 3 The following sections describe each of these stages in more detail. 

2.1 Study identification and source materials 

I draw on three sources of study descriptions: public pre-analysis plans posted on the AEA RCT Registry, public pre-registrations without public pre-analysis plans posted on the AEA RCT Reg-istry, and public pre-registrations on OSF. Registrations without a public pre-analysis plan are considered usable when their descriptions provide enough information for a LLM to formulate a numerical treatment effect forecasting question. 4

I restrict attention to outcome families whose earliest documented public findings are in 2026, based on systematic web searches, and record the date of first public release. 5 To search for results, registries are searched for linked results papers; targeted searches of working paper repositories are implemented, such as searches of NBER papers that first came out in 2026; and public materials on the internet are searched. This approach identified 89 candidate records, of which 49 papers satisfied the paper-level requirements concerning when results were seemingly first made publicly available and whether pre-registration information was sufficiently detailed to enable forecasting treatment effects that could later be standardized. 6 The 49 candidates at this stage comprise 21 papers with public AEA pre-analysis plans, 25 with AEA registrations without public plans, and three with OSF pre-registrations. 2.2 Constructing forecasting questions 

The pre-registration and pre-analysis plan materials were used to construct forecasting questions without access to the resultant papers. Claude Opus 5 was used to initially propose questions, while GPT-6 Astra was used to review Claude’s outputs for coverage, consistency, duplicate questions, units, and clarity. Explicit conventions were used to determine how to treat questions that were almost fully specified but for some detail. The research package retains the prompts, submitted materials, responses and dispositions. Questions specify the intervention, treatment arm, outcome, sample, time period, unit, and specification (if provided in the pre-registration documents). Questions are not restricted to primary outcomes but also include heterogeneous treatment effects and results reported at different time periods. If a pre-registration is unclear on some dimensions, such as not reporting the time period, it is not excluded on that basis, however, each question requires an identifiable intervention or treatment contrast, substantive outcome, relevant population, and numerical scale; any permitted conventions for unspecified details are stated explicitly. Using these broad criteria, 9,922 questions were extracted from 44 papers. Most, however, could not be connected to unique numerical results in the papers, as described in the next section. 

2.3 Extracting and matching reported results 

A separate numerical extraction step extracted values from the identified papers. The interven-tion, treatment arm, outcome, sample, time period, unit and specification, where available, were 5logged and the closest match selected between the extracted results and each forecasting question. Forecasting questions and results were matched 1:1, with duplicates removed. Exact matches in the content between a forecasting question and a result were distinguished from close but inex-act matches. If a result was reported in a way that flipped the sign from the original forecasting question, that was also identified and results flipped before analysis. Results were extracted in the way they were reported (e.g., a treatment coefficient and standard error vs. a treatment group mean, standard deviation, and number of observations, along with a control group mean, standard deviation, and number of observations). Results were also read off of figures, where applicable, and a flag was added to indicate if a result was extracted in this way. Additional review rounds checked the identified results against the supplied papers and assessed correspondence to the forecasting question. A “match score”, on a 0–100 scale, provides a contin-uous measure of the degree of match between the description in the pre-registration and the paper for each specific question. Overall, 1,866 matched question–result pairs were produced from 43 papers. 

2.4 Reporting units and standardization 

Forecasts were elicited in “raw” units where feasible and converted to comparable standardized effects. A compatible baseline outcome standard deviation was preferred for standardizing where available; otherwise, a documented compatible control-group, pooled, follow-up, or other outcome SD was used. For binary outcomes, the SD was constructed from reference probabilities. Of the 1,866 matched pairs from 43 papers, 889 questions from 39 papers yielded usable stan-dardized results. 

2.5 Forecast elicitation and eligibility 

Forecasting requests contain the study protocols, forecasting questions and reporting instructions, but no results, and are implemented through the API as a closed-book exercise without any web search/browsing/retrieval/or coding tools enabled. The prompts request a mean forecast and dis-tributional quantiles. The main analyses use xhigh reasoning effort for GPT-5.5 and GPT-6 Astra and max effort for the Anthropic models, with a 64,000-token output cap. GPT-5.1 uses high, its 6maximum available effort, with a 32,000-token cap (Appendix Table B.1). Additional checks verify information such as units and time periods. This paper uses GPT-5.1, GPT-5.5, Opus 4.6, Opus 4.8, Fable 5 and GPT-6 Astra. Papers from January onwards are considered for GPT-5.1, GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.6, papers from February onwards are used for Opus 4.8 and Fable 5, and papers from May for Astra, per provider-reported model cutoffs (Table A.1). For the analysis, a question must have a correspondence score of at least 60 and must not have a demonstrated major mismatch according to two eligibility screens. A major mismatch requires evidence that the result answers a substantively different question regarding the intervention, treat-ment arm, outcome, sample, time period, or unit. The correspondence screen excludes 193 of the 889 targets. I also exclude an outcome family if numerical or qualitative findings on it were publicly documented before the start of the model’s evaluation window. 7 Applying these criteria leaves 673 questions from 33 papers in Jan+, 629 from 30 in Feb+, and 339 from 20 in May+, as shown in Figure A.1. Table A.2 provides further details on the resultant set of papers and forecasting questions and their sources. The 33 papers in the January-onward screened sample include 16 with public AEA RCT Registry pre-analysis plans, 14 with AEA RCT Registry pre-registrations without public pre-analysis plans, and 3 with OSF pre-registrations. The papers retained in the screened analysis sample are listed in Appendix C. To assess accuracy, I calculate squared errors in SD units, average within each paper, and then weight papers equally. Neither results nor forecasts are winsorized. The 10th and 90th percentiles were also elicited, defining an 80% predictive interval.

12 3.3 Distributional results 

Figure 3 evaluates the 10th–90th percentile intervals against the reported treatment effects. The treatment effects fall within this 80% interval 80.5–82.3% of the time for five models, and 72.7% for GPT-5.1. The second panel of Figure 3 suggests these forecasts of different quantiles could be meaningful in decision-making: the questions with the largest estimates at the 90th percentile are not the same as the questions with the largest estimates at the mean. In particular, questions’ rank according to their 90th percentile estimate has a Spearman correlation of 0.62–0.84 with their rank at the mean, and for a decision-maker trying to select the top 10%, judging by the 90th percentile rather than the mean would change 23.5–55.6% of selections. 10 

Figure 4 asks whether wider forecast intervals identify less predictable results. Wider intervals do appear to generally accompany larger absolute forecast errors. This implies that models can be used to gauge which results are less predictable, beyond achieving coverage on average.

15 4 Discussion and Limitations 

The results describe how accurately LLMs can predict treatment effects from registrations and pre-analysis plans and seem to suggest substantial scope for these forecasts to improve decision-making. Several limitations remain. First, despite best efforts to ensure that models are not contam-inated with results, the exercise would be more credible if done prospectively. Some work is in progress by the author and others to do exactly this. Second, the leave-one-paper-out benchmark uses a simple prediction rule based on other papers’ realized results. Future work could compare AI forecasts with alternative benchmarks. Third, the matching of results to forecasting questions could introduce noise. The findings also apply only to outcomes with reported results that can be standardized and matched sufficiently closely to the pre-registered questions. Importantly, the sample is selected in ways that could affect results: the focus is on studies with well-documented pre-registrations or pre-analysis plans, but these may be precisely the easiest to forecast. Indeed, studies with longer descriptions (as measured by number of words) tend to be forecast better within both AEA RCT Registry source categories (Figure A.7). 11 Figure A.8 illustrates. Rather than this being a drawback, however, it highlights that forecasts could do even better in the future if researchers wrote fuller descriptions of their studies. With LLMs being used more in research, writing more complete descriptions may become easier. LLMs might both write scientific protocols and assess their treatments’ likely effects. Despite these limitations, it seems clear that forecasting can already serve as a useful tool -not just for forecasting states of the world like who wins an election or sports game, but also for forecasting causal treatment effects. Forecasting treatment effects may at first glance seem like a step backwards: the credibility revolution occurred in large part to mitigate the risks of biases that can result in the absence of rigorous evidence. However, given we do not always have results from a RCT, and sometimes one cannot be done, high-quality forecasts can at least improve over the counterfactual of less well-informed and biased individual beliefs. B.1 Sensitivity to forecasting prompts 

The experiments in this section use the same model-specific effort settings and output limits as the main analysis. Figure B.1 compares five alternative prompts with the original prompt. These alternative prompts include three that make relatively small changes: prompting the LLM to act more as an independent expert, consider a reference class of results, and consider a balanced set of mechanisms. Two other alternative prompts introduce more structured reasoning: one again asking the LLM to consider relevant literature in the same reference class and one which asks the LLM to explicitly consider a mixture of scenarios. No prompt systematically improves accuracy across models.
