# ABSTRACT 

Conversational interfaces have been proposed as a more accessible alternative to dashboard-based explainable AI (XAI) tools for users without machine learning expertise. In healthcare, where predictive models are increasingly deployed for clinical decision support and end-users span a wide range of technical backgrounds, the practical benefits of conversational explanation remain insufficiently characterised. Existing healthcare conversational XAI systems have typically been evaluated in isolation rather than against the dashboard interfaces they aim to displace, leaving open the question of whether the conversational modality itself contributes to interpretability beyond the underlying explanation methods. This paper reports a within-subjects user study comparing the Explainability Assistant, a conversa-tional XAI system based on LLM function-calling, with a traditional dashboard interface for the explanation of a heart disease classification model trained on the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset. Both interfaces provide access to the same Decision Tree classifier and the same post-hoc explanation methods (SHAP feature attribution, DiCE counterfactuals, and what-if analysis), so that observed differences can be attributed to the interaction modality rather than to differences in explanation content. Five participants with healthcare backgrounds, ranging from medical students to practising clinicians and public health researchers, completed parallel task blocks on each interface; three also took part in follow-up interviews. Quantitative measures (task accuracy and Likert ratings) and qualitative analyses (free-text questionnaire responses and interview transcripts) converge on a nuanced finding: rather than clearly preferring one modality over the other, participants identi-fied complementary strengths and weaknesses on both sides, and recommended an integrated tool combining conversational and dashboard elements in a single clinical workflow.

# 1 Introduction 

Machine learning models are increasingly deployed in healthcare for clinical decision support, from triage and risk prediction to diagnostic assistance and treatment planning. Predictive accuracy alone, however, is rarely sufficient for adoption in a clinical workflow: practitioners must understand why a model has produced a given prediction in order to assess its plausibility against clinical evidence, communicate it to colleagues and patients, and intervene when the model is wrong. The post-hoc explanation methods that have become standard in this setting (SHAP attribution [1], counterfactual generation [2], and what-if exploration) address part of this requirement by attaching interpretable structure to individual predictions, but they leave a separate question open: in what form should these explanations be delivered to clinical end-users with widely varying levels of machine learning expertise? The dominant interface paradigm has been the explanation dashboard, a graphical environment that consolidates the available explanation methods into a single point-and-click application. Dashboards are information-dense and well-suited to systematic inspection, but they presuppose that the user can map a clinical question onto the appropriate dashboard view, which is a non-trivial cost for clinical end-users without machine learning training. Conversational interfaces driven by large language models (LLMs) have recently been proposed as a more accessible alternative [3, 4], on the premise that allowing users to express explanation needs in natural language removes the mapping step and lowers the cognitive cost of using the underlying methods. Evaluations of conversational XAI in healthcare, however, have typically reported on the conversational system in isolation rather than against the dashboard interfaces it is meant to displace, leaving open the question of whether the conversational modality itself contributes to interpretability beyond the contribution of the explanation methods themselves. This paper reports a within-subjects user study designed to isolate that contribution. The Explainability Assistant [5], a conversational XAI system based on LLM function-calling, is compared against a traditional dashboard interface for the explanation of a Decision Tree classifier trained on the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset. Both interfaces expose the same classifier and the same set of post-hoc explanation methods (SHAP, DiCE, and what-if analysis), so that observed differences in task accuracy, subjective ratings, and qualitative response patterns can be attributed to the modality of interaction rather than to differences in explanation content. Five participants with mixed healthcare backgrounds, spanning practising clinicians, medical and nursing students, and public health researchers, completed parallel task blocks on both interfaces, and three of these participants took part in follow-up interviews. The analysis combines descriptive quantitative comparison with two layers of qualitative evidence: the free-text responses participants gave alongside the task questions and in the final open-ended item, and the recorded interview discussions. The principal finding of the study cuts against the prevailing expectation from earlier conversational XAI work. Rather than selecting one modality over the other, participants identified complementary strengths and weaknesses on both sides: the conversational assistant offered easier entry and clearer scoping of responses to individual patients, while the dashboard offered simultaneous visibility of global and local information and the visible separation of the underlying model from the conversational layer. All three interview participants explicitly recommended an integrated tool combining the two modalities in a single clinical workflow. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on clinical XAI, conversational interfaces, and trust calibration. Section 3 describes the Explainability Assistant architecture, and Section 4 the prediction task and the explanation methods shared by the two interfaces. Section 5 reports a benchmark evaluation of six candidate LLMs against the tool-calling requirements imposed by the architecture. Section 6 describes the user study, Section 7 reports the quantitative and qualitative results, and Sections 8 and 9 discuss the findings and conclude. 

# 2 Related Work 

The work presented here connects three strands of prior research: explanation accessibility for non-technical users in clinical decision support, conversational interfaces for model interpretability, and the empirical literature on trust calibration in human-AI collaboration. Post-hoc explainability methods such as SHAP [1], LIME [6], and counterfactual approaches [2] are standard tools for attributing predictions in clinical machine learning models, but their direct presentation to clinical end-users is a known source of friction. A controlled study with 63 surgeons and physicians on pre-operative blood product prescription found that clinician-friendly explanation formats produced higher acceptance, trust, and usability scores than raw SHAP visualisations, with LLM-generated narrative explanations emerging as a candidate solution to the clarity gap [7]. Critical perspectives on medical XAI argue that current methods are calibrated to the needs of model developers rather than clinical end-users and that individual feature attributions may not constitute clinically meaningful explanations [8, 9]. Interactive dashboards such as the What-If Tool [10], the Language Interpretability Tool [11], and the open-source Explainer Dashboard [12] consolidate multiple explanation methods into a single graphical interface, 2A PREPRINT - 28 O CTOBER 2026 but presuppose that the user can map a clinical question onto the appropriate dashboard view, a non-trivial cost for users without machine learning training that often shifts the interaction from active inquiry to passive inspection. Natural-language dialogue lowers this accessibility barrier by allowing users to express explanation needs directly. TalkToModel [3] demonstrated the paradigm using fine-tuned T5 models with custom semantic grammars, reporting maximum utterance parsing accuracy of 76.8%. More recent designs leverage the function-calling capabilities of frontier models to obtain structured tool invocations without task-specific fine-tuning, while approaches such as LLMCheckup [13] rely on prompt chaining over general-purpose LLMs. A systematic review of dialogue-based XAI [14] notes the relative scarcity of comparative evaluations against non-conversational baselines. The most direct healthcare-domain comparator is the visual-conversational interface for diabetes risk prediction of Samimi et al. [4], which combines a fine-tuned T5 parser with a general-purpose LLM and grounds explanations in scientific evidence. A mixed-methods study with 30 healthcare professionals reported that conversational interaction supported clearer understanding of model assessments and that evidence grounding contributed to trust calibration. The system retains a fine-tuned analytical component, and the evaluation does not include a head-to-head comparison against a dashboard interface providing the same underlying explanation methods. Explanation interfaces in clinical settings should also support appropriate reliance: users should follow the model’s recommendation when it is correct and override it when it is not [15]. Empirical evidence on whether explanations achieve this is mixed. A study with 223 dermatologists on lesion classification reported that self-reported trust accounted for up to 24% of the variance in reliance behaviour, with both over-reliance and under-reliance observed at the extremes of the trust distribution [16]. Reviews of human-AI collaboration in high-stakes domains have documented that explanation provision can promote over-reliance through false-confirmation effects [17], and the risk that conversational interfaces may amplify over-reliance through the persuasive fluency of natural-language responses has been raised in recent dialogue-based XAI work [18]. These findings motivate evaluation designs that measure objective task accuracy alongside subjective trust ratings. Against this background, the present paper compares the Explainability Assistant, a conversational XAI system that uses LLM function-calling for intent parsing and analytical tool invocation, against a dashboard baseline using the same classifier and the same post-hoc explanation methods, so that observed differences can be attributed to the interaction modality itself rather than to differences in explanation content. The participant pool spans practising clinicians, medical and nursing students, and public health researchers, providing a broader test of accessibility than evaluations restricted to a single professional category. Both quantitative measures and qualitative data are collected, the latter including free-text questionnaire responses and a subset of follow-up interviews. 

# 3 System Overview 

The Explainability Assistant [5] is a conversational interface to a configured set of post-hoc explanation methods over a fixed predictive model. Its application logic is generic across deployment domains: a deployment is specified through configuration files that fix the dataset, the trained model artefact, the JSON function specifications exposing the explanation methods, and the system prompt under which the LLM operates. The heart disease deployment evaluated in this paper is obtained through configuration alone, as detailed in Section 4 and Section 3.4. The remainder of this section describes the architecture, the function-calling pipeline, and the response contract that governs information flow between the back-end and the LLM provider. 

3.1 Architecture 

The system comprises a Next.js front-end and a FastAPI back-end, with a separation between presentation and computational layers (Figure 1). The front-end (Figure 2) manages the conversation history visible to the user, allows selection among supported LLM models, and renders both the assistant’s free-form reasoning and the structured outputs of executed functions. The back-end orchestrates model inference, executes the configured explanation, prediction and data inspection methods, and communicates with external LLM providers through respective APIs. No long-lived server-side session state is required: the conversation history is supplied with each request, and the back-end is otherwise stateless. The current deployment supports several LLM providers behind a uniform internal interface, including instances from the Gemini, GPT, and open-weight families through OpenRouter. 3.2 Function-Calling Pipeline 

For every user message, the back-end constructs a request to the LLM containing a subset of conversation history, a system prompt describing the agent’s role, and a JSON specification of the available functions. Each specification entry lists the function name, a natural-language description of when it should be used, and the schema of its arguments. Modern LLMs with function-calling support map the user’s request onto one or more of these functions and populate the arguments from the conversation context, without task-specific fine-tuning. The functions exposed by the back-end implement the post-hoc explanation methods described in Section 4 (SHAP feature attribution, DiCE counterfactual generation, what-if analysis), together with auxiliary operations supporting typical conversational queries: retrieval of an individual patient record, computation of model performance metrics, identification of misclassified cases, retrieval of dataset and model summaries, and counts of patients in clinically defined subgroups. The full catalogue used for the heart deployment contains twenty functions and is published with the source code. This design contrasts with prompt-chaining approaches such as LLMCheckup [13], in which the LLM mediates the orchestration of multiple intermediate prompts, and with grammar-based parsers such as TalkToModel [3], in which a fine-tuned analytical model emits a custom intermediate representation of the query. Here, the function specifications are the only contract between the LLM and the back-end, and the LLM is treated as an off-the-shelf component restricted to intent parsing and argument selection. 

3.3 Zero-Leakage Response Contract 

A clinical deployment must operate under tighter constraints on the flow of patient information than a deployment in a less sensitive domain. Patient-level records and the outputs of explanation methods over those records may contain or reconstruct protected health information, and once such information enters an LLM context window operated by an external provider, the controls available to the deploying institution are limited to whatever the provider’s data handling policy and contractual terms allow. The design adopted here removes the question by eliminating the channel: tool outputs are not passed back to the LLM at any point in the interaction, and any information the user sees about a tool’s result is rendered through pre-authored templates over the structured output of the function rather than through a generative summary. The response the LLM is required to produce on each turn is constrained by the schema illustrated in Figure 3 to two fields. The function_calls field lists the function invocations the assistant proposes to execute, expressed as ordinary call strings rather than as native provider tool-call objects so that the schema is uniform across providers. The 

freeform_response field contains the agent’s natural-language description of what it is about to do, prepared without sight of any tool output and shown to the user alongside, but separately from, those outputs once they are computed. The back-end parses the function calls, executes each function locally against the trained model artefact and the dataset, and dispatches the structured results to the front-end for rendering. Suggested prompts are generated by a separate agent based on the state of the conversation. This contract has three properties relevant to a clinical deployment. First, data minimisation at the LLM boundary: the only material transmitted to the provider is the user’s natural-language request, the limited conversation history including user requests and corresponding function call strings, along with the function specifications; patient records, computed feature attributions, and counterfactual examples remain on the back-end. Second, non-fabrication of tool output: the assistant cannot paraphrase or alter the result of an explanation method because it does not see it, and the user sees the deterministic output of the method as rendered by the front-end. Third, interpretability of intent: the user sees the listed function calls and the free-form description together, and a misinterpretation of the request is recoverable by reformulating the question rather than by inspecting opaque output. The term Zero-Leakage is used in the remainder of the paper to refer to the configuration in which this contract is enforced; it is taken as the architectural baseline against which the evaluations in Sections 5 and 6 are conducted.

The heart disease deployment of Explainability Assistant is obtained from the system configuration through changes to a small number of files, with no modification to the core application logic. # 4 Healthcare Prediction Task 

The prediction task selected for this study is the diagnosis of coronary artery disease from a small set of routinely collected clinical attributes. The task is well-known in the medical machine learning literature, the underlying dataset is publicly available, and the clinical features have established interpretations familiar to participants with healthcare backgrounds, making it a suitable testbed for an evaluation focused on the interpretability of model outputs rather than on the modelling problem itself. 

4.1 Dataset 

The Cleveland Heart Disease dataset [19] is used in its standard form as distributed by the UCI Machine Learning Repository 2. It contains records for 303 patients evaluated at the Cleveland Clinic Foundation, with 14 attributes per record: thirteen predictors and one target. The predictor variables include patient demographics, symptoms reported during clinical interview, and the results of standard non-invasive examinations such as resting electrocardiography and exercise stress testing (Table 1). The original target variable encodes coronary artery disease severity on a five-point ordinal scale (0-4) corresponding to the proportion of major vessels exhibiting more than 50% diameter narrowing on angiography. 4.2 Classifier and Explanation Methods 

A Decision Tree classifier is used as the predictive model. The choice is motivated by fit between model and study purpose rather than maximisation of predictive performance: decision trees are intrinsically interpretable, providing a clear ground truth against which the plausibility of the post-hoc explanations can be assessed informally, while the explanation methods themselves (SHAP, DiCE, and what-if analysis) are model-agnostic and apply unchanged as they would to a more opaque classifier. The classifier is implemented using scikit-learn [20] with maximum depth 4, minimum five samples per leaf, and a fixed random seed; all other hyperparameters retain library defaults. Performance is evaluated on a stratified 80/20 holdout split (237 training, 60 test). The data preparation script, the trained model artefact, and the evaluation outputs are available in a public repository 3. On the held-out test partition the classifier achieves an accuracy of 80% and a weighted F1 score of 0.80 (Table 2). Class-conditional performance is moderately asymmetric: precision and recall for the negative class are 0.79 and 0.84, whereas for the positive class they are 0.81 and 0.75. The lower recall for the positive class indicates that the model is somewhat more likely to miss disease cases than to flag healthy patients incorrectly, an asymmetry visible in the misclassified cases the model can be asked about through either interface. The performance level is sufficient to support the explanation study, in which predictive accuracy is not the variable of interest. Both the Explainability Assistant and the dashboard baseline expose the same set of post-hoc explanation methods, configured identically: SHAP [1] for local and global feature attribution; DiCE [2] for counterfactual generation, configured to respect training-data feature ranges so that suggested modifications correspond to clinically plausible patient profiles; and user-directed what-if analysis in which the user specifies feature modifications and observes the resulting change in prediction. This identity of explanation methods is essential to the study design: any difference in observed outcomes can be attributed to the modality of interaction rather than to differences in explanation content. 

# 5 LLM Tool-Calling Evaluation 

The Zero-Leakage contract imposes a strict requirement on the LLM: the model must produce a JSON object containing the list of function calls and a free-form description of the planned actions, with both fields valid for the back-end to proceed. The model never receives tool outputs and cannot recover from an error in argument selection by reading the result of a call. The architecture is therefore feasible only to the extent that available LLMs can produce the required object reliably under the request distribution generated by clinical end-users. This section reports an evaluation of six LLMs on a held-out tool-calling benchmark over the heart deployment, together with a complementary evaluation of free-form response quality on cases where the correct behaviour is to refrain from calling a tool. 

5.1 Evaluation Setup

5.3 Free-Form Response Quality 

The benchmark scores tool-calling behaviour but not free-form text. Under the Zero-Leakage contract, the free-form field is shown to the user verbatim alongside the structured tool outputs, and its quality matters most precisely on cases where the model produces no tool call: there is no template-rendered output to fall back on, so the user receives only what the model writes. The 97 no-call cases in the reviewed dataset were therefore the subject of a complementary evaluation of free-form response quality. Each free-form response was scored on a three-point rubric: a score of two for a response that correctly identifies the situation, asks for the specific information that is missing, or explains the limitation that prevents the request from being satisfied; a score of one for a response that points in the right direction but is incomplete, for instance offering the catalogue of available functions in place of a specific clarifying question; and a score of zero for an off-topic, fabricated, or clinically inappropriate response, or for a tool call when none was expected. Scores were normalised to the unit interval. Scoring was carried out by Claude Opus 4.7 acting as LLM judge over the full set of 576 valid responses (six models against 97 cases, with three of Kimi’s responses excluded because they failed the response schema and could not be evaluated). Aggregate results are reported in Table 5. The free-form ranking partially mirrors the tool-calling ranking but is not redundant with it. Gemma MoE leads on both metrics. Gemma dense ranks second on free-form quality despite having the lowest no-call accuracy of the leading models: when it does abstain its explanations are competitive, but it abstains less often than Gemma MoE in conflicting-context cases. Gemini exhibits the inverse pattern: a respectable no-call accuracy paired with the lowest score on conflicting-context text, because the model overcalls on these cases almost universally and the few abstentions it produces are not of high quality. Three failure modes recur across the no-call cases. The first is arbitrary argument substitution, in which a model defaults to patient_id=0 or patient_id=1 without disclosure when the patient identifier is not specified; GPT and DeepSeek exhibit this behaviour, the two Gemma variants do not. The second is deflection to the catalogue of available functions in place of a clarifying question. The third is helpful overreach on out-of-scope clinical requests, in which a model treats a question about treatment, prognosis, or referral as a data-retrieval task and chains together patient-level explanation calls in response. 5.4 Latency and Selection 

Latency was measured on the same evaluation runs. Gemma MoE has a median response time of 2.0 s with a 90th-percentile of 4.0 s and a maximum of 32.7 s. DeepSeek V4 Flash has a median of 3.5 s, a 90th-percentile of 12.2 s, and a maximum of 134.2 s. The Gemma MoE latency profile is the more suitable of the two for an interactive deployment. Taken together, the tool-calling and free-form evaluations identify Gemma 4 Mixture of Experts as the leading default for the heart deployment under the Zero-Leakage contract: the model leads on overall joint goal accuracy, holds a substantial advantage on no-call discipline and on free-form response quality, and has the most favourable latency profile among the candidates with latency reported. Gemma 4 dense and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite are competitive alternatives in deployments that prioritise stateful supported-tool execution over conservative ambiguity handling. The user study reported in Section 6 was conducted with Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite rather than Gemma 4 MoE. The choice was driven by deployment constraints during the study period: Gemini provided greater API stability, lower operational latency under sustained interactive use, and simpler integration within the existing production environment used for participant access. 

# 6 User Study Design 

The user study is designed as a within-subjects comparison between the Explainability Assistant and the dashboard, with each participant completing parallel task blocks on both interfaces. 

6.1 Participants 

Of the ten participants recruited, five completed the study appropriately and contribute to the analyses reported below; the remaining five submissions were excluded during quality control (see Section 10 for the exclusion criteria and their justification). The pool spans healthcare professionals and trainees with mixed clinical and public health backgrounds, including a radiotherapist, a final-year medical student, public health and physiotherapy students at master’s level, and an MPH student with an epidemiology focus. Self-reported familiarity with machine learning ranges from minimal exposure to intermediate working knowledge; no participant identified as a machine learning specialist. 

6.2 Compared Interfaces 

Two interfaces are compared, both providing access to the same Decision Tree classifier and the same set of post-hoc explanation methods (SHAP, DiCE, what-if analysis) described in Section 4. The first is the Explainability Assistant, which exposes the explanation methods through natural-language dialogue. The second is the Explainer Dashboard [12], an open-source dashboard widely used as a reference point for interactive explanation interfaces, providing tabbed access to SHAP feature importance plots, individual prediction inspection, global feature importance rankings, and interactive sliders for what-if analysis. 

6.3 Tasks and Subjective Evaluation 

Each interface is paired with a parallel task block of six questions: retrieving information about the dataset or model artefact, retrieving an individual patient’s prediction, identifying the feature that contributed most to the prediction, evaluating the feature’s meaningfulness to the user, evaluating a hypothetical change to one feature, finding additional scenario of model prediction change. The blocks are matched in structure and difficulty but use different patient records to prevent memorised answers from one block carrying over to the other. Participants could not leave a question blank and were instructed to write explicit “I cannot tell” if the interface does not allow them to find an answer within reasonable effort, so that the task accuracy measure reflects what the interface enabled the participant to discover rather than what the participant could guess. After each task block, participants complete a five-item subjective evaluation on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) covering ease of use, trust in the outputs, clarity of the explanations provided, self-reported understanding of how the system operates, and likelihood of using the interface in future work. A final open-ended item invites free-text feedback on the interfaces used. 

6.4 Procedure 

The study was administered online and asynchronously through Google Forms, allowing participants to complete the survey at their own convenience and without researcher supervision. Each participant received a unique access code linking their responses to the appropriate conversation for analysis. The questionnaire was organised into six sequential 10 A PREPRINT - 28 O CTOBER 2026 sections: background information, Task Block A, post-task evaluation A, Task Block B, post-task evaluation B, and open-ended final feedback. Interface order was counterbalanced across participants, partially mitigating the ordering effect in which familiarity acquired during the first block can inflate performance and usability ratings for the second. Participants received a 25 EUR gift card for the questionnaire on the condition that the task was completed properly, and the criteria used to judge proper completion (and the exclusions that resulted) are described in Section 10. Participants who indicated willingness to take part in a follow-up interview received a separate 25 EUR payment by bank transfer after the interview was completed. The two compensation streams are kept distinct so that the questionnaire payment is not contingent on subsequent interview availability, and the interview payment is not used as leverage on questionnaire content. 

6.5 Follow-up Interviews 

Three of the five valid participants additionally took part in follow-up interviews of approximately twenty minutes each, conducted by one of the authors over Zoom. An interview script guides the conversation, but in practice the sessions take the form of open discussions, with the script serving as a checklist of topics rather than a rigid sequence. Topics addressed included the participant’s reasoning strategies during the task blocks, points of friction with each interface, perceived limits of either system, and which interface the participant would prefer to use in clinical or research practice and why. With the participant’s explicit consent, each session was recorded in audio and video, and the Zoom AI Companion note-taking feature was used to produce a written summary. The recordings served as the primary record from which transcripts could be produced; the automatically generated summaries supported quicker inspection during analysis. The interview component was designed primarily to inform the future development of both interfaces, with conversations focused on what either system would need to offer to be usable in clinical practice and on the design changes participants believe would close the gap between a research prototype and a tool fit for routine use. 

# 7 Results 

The results are reported in three parts. Section 7.1 summarises the quantitative findings from the questionnaire. Section 7.2 reports the analysis of the free-text responses provided alongside the task questions and in the final open-ended item. Section 7.3 reports the findings from the follow-up interviews. Across all three layers a single convergent finding emerges: participants did not regard the two interfaces as substitutes for one another and recommended their integration into a single clinical tool. 

7.1 Quantitative Findings 

This subsection reports objective task accuracy and the Likert ratings collected after each task block, for the participants whose responses were retained after the validity checks described in Section 6. Given the size of the sample, the analysis is confined to descriptive comparison and median paired differences, with inferential claims avoided in favour of effect-pattern observations. 

Task accuracy. On the questions with an unambiguous ground-truth answer, the conversational assistant produced more uniformly correct responses than the dashboard. The patient-level prediction question and the hypothetical single-feature-change question were answered correctly by every participant on both interfaces, indicating that point predictions and simple counterfactual sensitivities were communicated reliably by either modality. Differences between interfaces appeared on the model-description, feature-importance, and exploration questions. On the chat-side model-description question, four of the five participants returned a correct description of the underlying Decision Tree classifier; the fifth identified the language model providing the conversational interface itself in place of the classifier, a failure mode discussed further in Section 7.2. On the dashboard the parallel dataset-description question elicited two “can’t find” responses and one fragmentary answer, with the remaining two participants returning a substantive description. The clearest accuracy contrast appeared on the feature-importance question. For the chat-side query identifying the strongest contributor to the prediction for patient ID 39, four of the five participants returned the local SHAP attribution (trestbps ) that the conversational assistant emits directly. For the dashboard-side query on patient 55 all five participants instead returned the top global feature ( thal ), with two of them flagging the local-versus-global ambiguity explicitly. The contrast is consistent with the conversational interface scoping its response to the entity under discussion and the dashboard juxtaposing global and local views in adjacent panels without disambiguating them (Section 7.2). 11 A PREPRINT - 28 O CTOBER 2026 The dashboard scenario question, which asks participants to identify a feature modification that flips the prediction, drew one explicit abandonment of the partial dependence plot and four substantive responses, ranging from a single feature change to a sequence of modifications that drove the predicted probability of disease to its maximum. The corresponding chat-side modification question produced substantive responses in every case, including responses that used the counterfactual function to generate ranked suggestions automatically. 

Likert ratings. Each interface received a five-item subjective evaluation on a five-point scale covering ease of use, trust in the outputs, clarity of the explanations provided, self-reported understanding of how the system operates, and likelihood of future use. Individual participant ratings are reported in Figure 4. 

Figure 4: Subjective evaluation of the two interfaces ( n = 5 ; 1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Individual participant ratings comparing the Explainer Dashboard (left) and the Explainability Assistant (right) across five dimensions. The conversational assistant carries a substantial advantage on ease of use, clarity, and self-reported understanding, with a median paired difference of two Likert points on each of these dimensions. Trust in outputs and likelihood of future use are at parity at the median. Within-participant comparisons preserve the same direction: every participant rated the conversational assistant at least as easy to use as the dashboard and at least as understandable, with the largest within-participant difference on ease of use reaching three Likert points. The single participant with the most substantive clinical experience rated the two interfaces equivalently on every dimension shown in Figure 4, suggesting that the consistent ease-of-use and understanding advantages observed across the rest of the pool are concentrated among participants with less prior exposure to dashboard-style explanation tools. The sample is too small to support inferential claims with confidence, and the descriptive pattern reported here should be read as an effect-direction signal rather than as a measurement of population-level preferences. The pattern is consistent with the interpretation that the conversational modality reduces interaction cost for the kinds of clinical inquiry the task block exercises, without yet establishing a corresponding trust or adoption advantage. The reasons participants gave for their reactions are taken up in Section 7.2 and Section 7.3. 

7.2 Free-Text Feedback and Task Engagement 

The questionnaire generated qualitative data on two further layers: the textual answers participants gave to the task questions themselves, several of which include written explanations of how the answer was reached, and the final open-ended item asking for general feedback. Five recurring patterns emerged across these responses. 

Honest abandonment in place of guessing. Several participants opted out of questions when the interface did not let them locate the answer within reasonable effort, rather than producing a confident-sounding response without grounds. 12 A PREPRINT - 28 O CTOBER 2026 Two participants returned “I can’t find” in place of a description of the dataset on the dashboard, and one wrote that they were going to “give up” on the dashboard’s partial dependence plot, which they had found “too inconvenient” for the question. The instruction that blanks were preferred over guesses functioned as intended: the resulting blank rate is itself a measure of how often each interface failed to support a question rather than of participant capability. 

Confusion of the conversational layer with the classifier. A failure mode specific to the Explainability Assistant emerged in answers to the model-description question. One participant identified the underlying predictive model as “gemini 3.1 flash lite”, which is the language model providing the conversational interface rather than the Decision Tree classifier on which all predictions are computed. The conflation is consistent with the surface presentation of the assistant: the LLM is the most visible component, the classifier is invoked by tools the user does not directly observe, and the distinction is never explicitly surfaced in the chat. The implication for clinical deployment is that an interface relying on conversational mediation should be transparent about which component performs the inference and which mediates the conversation, since a user who attributes the prediction to the LLM is in a poor position to reason about the model’s reliability. 

Local versus global feature importance. The dashboard’s response to the question of which feature contributed most to a specific patient’s prediction was characterised by a recurring uncertainty about whether the answer should be drawn from the local explanation for that patient or from the global feature ranking for the model as a whole. Two participants recorded the ambiguity directly. One wrote “Thalassemia? Or average of population? That’s unclear for me”, and another flagged “thal = 3.0 (average of population should probably be excluded as the starting point)”. The convergence of independent responses on the same uncertainty suggests that the dashboard’s juxtaposition of global and local feature plots does not reliably communicate which view applies to a question phrased at the level of an individual patient. Responses through the conversational assistant to the parallel question on a different patient did not exhibit the same pattern, with most participants returning the local SHAP attribution directly. The contrast is consistent with the conversational modality scoping its response to the entity under discussion, while the dashboard presents both views in adjacent panels and leaves disambiguation to the user. 

Local clinical context and unit mismatch. The final open-ended item drew substantive responses on the integration of the tools into specific clinical workflows. One participant working in primary care in Estonia observed that general practitioners in that setting do not have access to the ca feature, which limits the practical relevance of the model in that context, and added that Estonian clinical practice records cholesterol in mmol/L rather than the mg/dl displayed by the interface, so that the figures shown required mental conversion before they could be interpreted at a clinical glance. A second participant phrased a related concern in writing that a “medical practitioner would want to make min amount of klicks”. These responses point to a class of design issues that originate not in the explanation methods but in the assumed clinical context: the input feature set, the display units, and the interaction cost are calibrated to the dataset’s source population and to a particular professional category, and neither interface currently exposes configuration affordances for adapting them. 

Volunteered design suggestions. The free-text responses included concrete design proposals from three participants, clustering around the dashboard’s presentation rather than its explanation content: plain-language tooltips for abbreviated feature labels, clinical units and reference ranges next to feature inputs, a reset-patient control, ranges rather than single values for categorically presented features, and clearer feedback when a what-if modification changes the underlying prediction. The convergence of these suggestions on tighter coupling between the user’s modification action and the visible model output indicates that the dashboard’s strongest perceived weakness in this sample is not the explanation methods themselves but the rendering of state changes in the explanation interface. 

7.3 Interview Findings 

The follow-up interviews extend several patterns observed in the questionnaire responses and reinforce the convergent finding that participants do not regard the two interfaces as substitutes. Findings are derived from analysis of the audio and video recordings, supported by the AI-generated Zoom meeting summaries as a cross-reference; the recordings served as the authoritative record where summaries were ambiguous or incomplete. The three interview participants are referred to as P1 (physiotherapy MSc graduate), P2 (public health MSc student), and P3 (radiotherapy MSc student). 

Integration over selection. All three participants, when asked to express a preference, recommended integrating the conversational and dashboard modalities rather than adopting one to the exclusion of the other. P1 proposed embedding the conversational assistant as a chatbot panel within the dashboard interface; P2 framed the integration in the opposite direction, suggesting that dashboard elements be embedded into the conversational assistant; P3 endorsed the integration position more agnostically, treating both modalities as legitimate and complementary. The convergent point across the 13 A PREPRINT - 28 O CTOBER 2026 three formulations is the same: a clinical XAI tool should support both interaction modalities together. P1 additionally observed that the conversational assistant tended to return ranked tables rather than direct answers when asked for the single most important factor in a prediction, a calibration choice consistent with the participants’ preference for visual dashboard elements that present information in structured rather than enumerated form. This finding contrasts with patterns reported in the prior conversational XAI literature, in which evaluations of natural-language interfaces have generally found that conversational modalities are preferred over dashboards by users with no expertise in machine learning [3, 4]. In the clinical setting examined here, the conversational modality’s ergonomic advantages are recognised but do not translate into a recommendation to displace dashboards. 

Conversation continuity and question structuring. P1 and P2 independently identified the absence of conversation history and question categorisation as the sharpest usability gaps in the conversational assistant. Both explicitly drew analogies to consumer LLM interfaces (ChatGPT, Claude) when describing what they wanted: a visible record of prior questions, the ability to copy and modify previous prompts rather than retype them, and a structuring of questions that distinguishes patient-specific queries from broader inquiries about the model or dataset. P1 added that question categorisation should be performed automatically rather than by requiring users to select a category before asking, since explicit categorisation steps were perceived as friction. The convergence on these specific UI features suggests that contemporary user expectations of conversational interfaces are shaped strongly by the consumer LLM market, and that clinical tools failing to meet these expectations will read as unfinished regardless of their analytical capabilities. 

Onboarding friction is uniform but locally specific. All three participants reported difficulty during initial use, but the location of the difficulty differed and tracked participant background. P1, whose training is in physiotherapy and includes no formal statistics curriculum, identified statistical terminology as the central barrier and recommended interactive tutorials and clearer plain-language explanations of metrics. P2, with a public health background including statistical training, did not raise this concern but identified instead the chat interface’s lack of conversation overview and a confusing dropdown control on the dashboard. P3 reported difficulty with graph generation in the dashboard, particularly pie chart creation, while finding the prediction content easy to understand. The implication is that onboarding effort cannot be reduced to a single intervention: clinical users with different training backgrounds encounter friction in different layers of the same interface. 

The dashboard signals professional expertise. A distinctive observation from P3 is that a dashboard interface with graphical elements is more appropriate for professional clinical settings than a pure conversational interface, on the grounds that the dashboard demonstrates the operator’s knowledge and expertise. This observation is sociological rather than ergonomic: it concerns what the tool communicates about the person using it, not whether the tool is easy to use. Conversational interfaces, however accessible, may carry connotations of low-stakes consumer software that conflict with professional clinical identity, and integrated tools may need to preserve dashboard-style visualisation not only for information communication but for the professional framing of the clinical interaction itself. This observation was raised by only one participant and is reported as a hypothesis worth testing in larger samples rather than as a generalised finding. 

# 8 Discussion 

The most consistent finding across the quantitative measures, the free-text questionnaire responses, and the follow-up interviews is that the comparison did not yield a clear preference for either modality. The study should be interpreted as an exploratory formative evaluation rather than as a statistically powered comparative trial. With five valid participants and three follow-up interviews, the aim was not population-level generalisation or significance testing, but the identification of robust design patterns, recurring usability frictions, and convergent preferences across quantitative and qualitative evidence. For this reason, descriptive comparisons and qualitative convergence are treated as the primary source of evidence, while Likert differences and task accuracy are interpreted as effect-direction signals rather than inferential results. The value of the study lies in clarifying what participants consider necessary for clinical usability and in informing the design of the next prototype iteration. The Likert ratings and task accuracy figures reported in Section 7.1 do not separate the two interfaces along a single dimension, and the qualitative analyses (Sections 7.2 and 7.3) converge on a more substantive observation: participants treated the conversational and dashboard modalities as complementary rather than competing, and all three interview participants explicitly recommended an integrated tool combining the two. This pattern contrasts with earlier conversational XAI evaluations, where users with less exposure to machine learning have generally preferred natural-language interfaces over dashboards [3,4], and is consistent with the more recent observation that conversational 14 A PREPRINT - 28 O CTOBER 2026 modalities are recognised for their ergonomic advantages but do not, on their own, displace the dashboard view in settings where both modalities are available together. The qualitative analyses are informative about what an integrated tool would have to preserve from each modality. From the conversational side, participants valued the natural-language entry point and the fact that the assistant scoped its response to the entity under discussion, which avoided the local-versus-global ambiguity that recurred in the dashboard responses to questions about individual patient predictions. From the dashboard side, participants valued the simultaneous availability of global and local feature views, the visual reference values, and (in one participant’s framing) the professional appearance of a graphical interface in a clinical setting. The two sets of strengths are complementary rather than redundant: conversational scoping addresses a specific weakness of the dashboard, while the dashboard’s visual reference frame addresses a specific weakness of the conversational assistant when participants asked questions that the LLM answered with ranked tables rather than with direct answers. The free-text responses surfaced a related set of design implications that an integrated tool would need to address. The conflation of the conversational layer with the underlying classifier, observed in at least one participant’s answer to the model-description question, indicates that an interface relying on LLM mediation should be transparent about which component performs the inference and which mediates the conversation. The clinical context observations, including the absence of the ca feature in Estonian primary care and the cholesterol unit mismatch between mg/dl and mmol/L, point to a class of issues that originate not in the explanation methods but in the assumed clinical context, and that would need to be addressed through configuration affordances that neither interface currently exposes. The dashboard-specific suggestions volunteered by participants, including plain-language tooltips for abbreviated feature labels, clinical units and reference ranges next to feature inputs, a reset-patient control, and a clearer coupling between what-if modifications and the visible prediction state, all point to the same underlying issue: the dashboard’s strongest perceived weakness in this sample is not the explanation methods themselves but the rendering of state changes in the explanation interface. The trust calibration concerns reviewed in Section 2 bear on these findings in a specific way. The empirical literature has documented that explanation provision can promote over-reliance through false-confirmation effects [17], and the conversational modality has been identified as a particular risk because the persuasive fluency of natural-language responses can mask the limits of the underlying model [18]. The conflation of the LLM with the classifier in the free-text responses is consistent with this risk and suggests that the visual separation of model and interface in the dashboard contributes something to trust calibration that the conversational modality does not currently match. A natural design response is an integrated tool that combines a conversational entry point with a clear visual separation between the LLM and the underlying classifier, so that users trust (or distrust) the right component. Whether such an integration would in fact produce better alignment between user trust and model reliability is an open empirical question, and not one that can be settled by the fact that each modality has some desirable property on its own. 

# 9 Conclusion 

This paper reported a within-subjects user study comparing the Explainability Assistant, a conversational XAI system based on LLM function-calling, with a traditional dashboard interface for the explanation of a heart disease classification model. Both interfaces were configured to expose the same Decision Tree classifier and the same set of post-hoc explanation methods (SHAP feature attribution, DiCE counterfactuals, and what-if analysis), so that the comparison isolated the contribution of the interaction modality rather than confounding it with differences in explanation content. Five participants with mixed healthcare backgrounds completed the study, and three additionally took part in follow-up interviews. The quantitative measures (task accuracy and Likert ratings) did not produce a clear preference for either modality. The qualitative analyses, drawing on free-text questionnaire responses and the interview transcripts, converged on a more substantive finding: participants treated the conversational and dashboard modalities as complementary rather than competing, and all three interview participants explicitly recommended an integrated tool combining the two. This pattern is at odds with earlier conversational XAI evaluations, where non-expert users have generally preferred natural-language interfaces over dashboards. In the clinical setting examined here, the conversational modality’s accessibility advantages were recognised but did not translate into a recommendation to displace the dashboard. The natural direction for future work is a prototype of the integrated tool implied by these findings: a single clinical interface that combines a conversational entry point with the dashboard’s visual representation of the underlying model and its explanations. Such a prototype would convert the qualitative recommendation into a concrete artefact that can itself be evaluated, and would make it possible to test empirically whether the integration of two modalities inherits the strengths of each or introduces new trade-offs of its own. Findings suggest that conversational XAI should not be evaluated as a replacement for dashboards, but as a complementary modality whose value emerges through integration. 15 A PREPRINT - 28 O CTOBER 2026 

# 10 Limitations 

Four limitations constrain the interpretation of the present study. First, the participant pool is small: of ten recruited participants, five responses were excluded during quality control because the questionnaires showed indications of malicious AI assistance or other unethical participation motivated by the financial compensation, leaving five valid questionnaire responses and three follow-up interviews. Inferential statistical analysis is therefore not attempted, and the quantitative findings are reported as descriptive effect patterns. Second, the study was conducted asynchronously and without supervision, which improved accessibility but required retrospective detection of low-quality responses; supervised or observational protocols would raise the effective response rate in future replications. Third, the evaluation covers a single clinical task, dataset, and interpretable classifier, and the findings may not transfer directly to higher-dimensional data or less inspectable models. Fourth, the dashboard baseline is one implementation among several, and the conversational interface was evaluated with a single deployed LLM configuration; alternative choices on either side may shift the specific pattern of strengths and weaknesses, though the integration finding is at the level of modality rather than of implementation.
