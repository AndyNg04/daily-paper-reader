Learning task progress from robot demonstrations provides a practical way to construct dense shaping
rewards for robotic reinforcement learning. A common approach uses normalized demonstration
time as progress labels, implicitly assuming monotonically increasing task progress. During policy
exploration, however, actual task progress can stall or even regress. Such models may mistake
regressions for progress, assigning positive shaping rewards to actions that cause progress loss. Real
suboptimal and failed executions offer corrective experience, but assigning absolute progress values to
their states is difficult. Our key observation is that stalls, regressions, and recoveries establish local
relative progress relations even when absolute progress is unknown. We introduce Event2Reward,
which uses normalized temporal labels to anchor the progress scale and these within-trajectory
relations to constrain local changes, combining absolute and relative progress supervision. Its progress
predictions serve as potentials for reward shaping. We curate an event-annotated dataset from
approximately 100K raw real-robot execution records, covering successful, suboptimal, and failed
executions, without requiring framewise absolute progress labels. Under the same training budgets,
Event2Reward improves average policy success by 18.16 percentage points over a baseline using only
sparse task rewards across 40 tasks in four LIBERO suites. On real robots, average success after
30 minutes of wall-clock training reaches 60.0% on three precise insertion tasks and 90.0% on three
object manipulation tasks, exceeding the sparse-reward baseline under the same budget by 36.7 and
23.3 percentage points, respectively. Ablation studies further show that absolute progress supervision
and relative progress relations are complementary, with joint supervision yielding better downstream
policy learning.

Introduction
Reinforcement learning (RL) enables robots to improve manipulation skills through trial and error [17].
However, learning on real robots often requires extensive and costly interaction. Reward shaping offers a
way to accelerate policy learning by improving credit assignment with dense rewards. A common approach
learns task progress from successful demonstrations, using normalized elapsed time as a proxy [14, 30].
This temporal supervision encodes monotonically increasing task progress. Progress estimates can serve
as potentials for reward shaping, with the shaping term computed from discounted differences between
successive predictions [21]. During policy exploration, however, robots routinely produce suboptimal and
failed executions, in which progress can stall or even regress. The reward model may still predict rising
progress during such non-ideal behavior, assigning positive shaping rewards even when progress stalls or
regresses. Instead of improving credit assignment, such rewards credit the actions that caused the loss,
hindering rather than accelerating policy improvement.
A natural remedy is to train the reward model directly on suboptimal and failed executions. However,
normalized time continues to assign increasing progress values during stalls and regressions, so it cannot

demonstration frames to synthesize stalls and regressions with labels known by construction [14, 30], but
these edits cannot introduce failure states absent from the original demonstrations. Another workaround uses
outcome labels or trajectory-level preferences, which do enable reward learning from real suboptimal and
failed executions [13, 14]. These signals describe overall execution quality, whereas progress-based shaping
rewards depend on progress changes between adjacent observations. Such overall judgments therefore leave the
timing and direction of these changes underdetermined: a failed execution can retain partial progress, while a
successful one can include regressions and recoveries. How, then, can we obtain local progress supervision
from real executions without assigning an absolute progress value to every state?
Our key observation is that task events in real executions can establish local progress relations even when
absolute progress values are unknown. For example, consider a robot moving a cup from a table onto a
shelf. If the cup slips from the gripper and falls back onto the table, how much progress remains is difficult
to quantify, yet this state clearly holds less task progress than the state just before the drop. We directly
supervise the progress predictions used for reward shaping with these within-trajectory progress relations,
which encode the direction of change between temporally anchored states. We combine these relations
with normalized temporal labels from successful demonstrations to train our reward model, Event2Reward
(Figure 1). The temporal labels anchor the numerical scale of predicted progress, while the relations constrain
the direction of local progress changes in real suboptimal and failed executions.
We also contribute an event-annotated dataset curated from approximately 100K raw real-robot execution
records across multiple sources, spanning successful, suboptimal, and failed executions. The annotations
identify where task progress stalls, regresses, or recovers, and link these events to temporal regions and
reference states. They turn observed execution events into local progress comparisons beyond terminal
outcomes, without requiring framewise completion percentages. We combine two-pass VLM annotation with
2


independent human annotation and review (Appendix A.3). We will release the structured annotations, data
splits, and tools for constructing progress comparisons, providing a reusable resource for reward learning and
execution analysis. We train Event2Reward through pretraining and rollout joint training, then adapt it
using a small set of target-task executions.
We evaluate whether Event2Reward improves policy learning on 40 LIBERO [15] tasks and six real-world tasks
on Franka robots. The real-world experiments cover three precise insertion tasks trained with SERL [16, 17]
and three manipulation tasks trained with DSRL [28] using a π0 [4] base policy. Across the four LIBERO
suites, Event2Reward improves average policy success by 9.96–13.76 percentage points over the strongest
baseline in each suite under matched interaction budgets. In real-world experiments with a matched 30-minute
training budget, Event2Reward increases average success from 37.8% to 60.0% on precise insertion tasks and
from 81.7% to 90.0% on manipulation tasks, compared with Robometer, the strongest learned reward baseline
in each setting. Our ablations show that combining temporal labels with within-trajectory progress relations
yields larger gains in downstream policy learning than combining them with trajectory-level preferences.
Combining temporal and relational supervision outperforms either alone in downstream policy learning.

Related Work
Visual reward and value models.
Learning rewards from visual observations reduces the need for
task-specific reward engineering in robot learning. VIP [19] learns goal-directed visual representations through
value-based pretraining, and LIV [18] extends this approach to language and image goals. For vision-language
reasoning, GVL [20] estimates progress in context without parameter updates, while ProgressLM [41] learns
to assess progress relative to visual or textual demonstrations. WVM [30] instead uses video world-model
representations to predict distributions over progress sequences. Our work focuses on the supervision of
progress estimates: how real suboptimal and failed executions can provide evidence about local progress
changes.
Progress supervision from demonstrations.
Demonstrations provide temporal order and semantic task
structure for progress supervision. Temporal order supports ranking-based objectives: Rank2Reward [35]
learns rankings on expert videos and combines them with an expert–policy discriminator, while Video-Language
Critic [2] combines sequential ranking with video–text matching and uses failed executions as contrastive
negatives. VLAC [39] derives signed progress differences from temporal offsets. RynnValue [10] learns
absolute temporal distance to task completion and relative temporal displacement from timestamps, using
temporal-order shuffling and value-isolation attention to reduce shortcut learning. Task-stage annotations
provide semantic structure: SARM [6] aligns numerical progress targets with annotated stages, and Robo-
Dopamine [26] derives signed progress differences from stage-based progress coordinates. Augmentation
extends this supervision to nonmonotonic sequences. ReWiND [40] reverses demonstration segments to
synthesize regressions, while WVM [30] constructs increasing, stable, and decreasing profiles through frame
preservation, repetition, and reversal. Our approach complements these signals with progress relations
grounded in stalls, regressions, and recoveries in real suboptimal and failed executions.
Reward learning from nonideal experience.
Preference-based reward learning uses relative judgments
without requiring absolute numerical ratings [7]. At the observation level, RL-VLM-F [29] asks a VLM
to compare images, and Venkataraman et al. [27] apply this approach to reward labeling for real-world
offline RL. At the trajectory level, RoboReward [13] combines instruction relabeling and temporal clipping of
successful demonstrations with naturally successful and failed rollouts carrying episode-level completion scores.
Robometer [14] combines normalized temporal progress labels from expert demonstrations with success
prediction and trajectory preferences, allowing real failures to contribute supervision while also producing
per-frame progress estimates. Beyond these judgments, execution events provide structure for datasets
and evaluation. WVM’s Suboptimal-Value-Bench [30] converts annotated hesitation and retry intervals
into reference progress curves, while REBOOT [22] pairs precision-assembly demonstrations with phase
boundaries, failure and recovery timestamps, and a phase-level evaluation protocol. We use event-grounded
within-trajectory relations to supervise the progress estimates used for reward shaping.

Method
Event2Reward learns task progress from temporal labels and within-trajectory progress relations. After
adaptation to downstream tasks, the model’s scalar progress predictions serve as the potential for reward
shaping. Figure 2 illustrates these complementary supervision signals.
3.1
Task Progress Model
Event2Reward is a task-conditioned model that estimates task progress from visual observations. At time t,
its input is xt = (ℓ, o0, Ht, g), where ℓis the task instruction, o0 is a reference start observation, Ht is a short
visual history ending at the current observation, and g is an optional goal image depicting task completion.
It outputs a scalar progress estimate pθ(xt) ∈[0, 1], with higher values indicating greater progress toward
completing the instructed task. The visual history provides context for interpreting local changes: progress
may remain stable during stalled execution or decrease when previously achieved task states are lost.
We implement Event2Reward with a Qwen3.5-4B vision-language backbone, a progress head, and an auxiliary
trend head. The progress head predicts a distribution over discretized progress values in [0, 1], whose
expectation gives pθ(xt). As detailed in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, temporal labels supervise this distribution,
while within-trajectory progress relations constrain its scalar expectation. The same scalar serves as the
potential for downstream reward shaping. The trend head predicts whether progress increases, remains stable,
or decreases within the visual history as an auxiliary training task. Input construction and prediction-head
details are given in Appendix B.1.
3.2
Progress Supervision from Robot Executions
We curate an event-annotated dataset from approximately 100K raw real-robot execution records. The
annotations connect observable execution events to temporal anchors and reference states, providing local
progress comparisons beyond terminal success or failure. Normal successful trajectories supply normalized-
time labels as a progress proxy over their task-relevant execution intervals. Trajectories containing events
supply within-trajectory relations that capture stalled execution, loss of achieved task states, and recovery.
4


For each execution event, we record its category, a reference state, and the relevant temporal interval;
regression-and-recovery events additionally identify a recovery state. Together with the supporting visual
observations, these temporal anchors identify the within-trajectory progress comparisons used for supervision.
Annotation therefore consists of identifying task events and their temporal anchors, without assigning
framewise completion percentages.
Our annotation protocol organizes events by execution outcome, while their progress relations are grounded
in observable task-state changes. For successful executions, we annotate progress stall, an interval with
approximately unchanged task progress, and regression and recovery, a decrease in task progress followed
by recovery to the pre-regression reference level. For failed executions, we distinguish no further progress,
where no advancement beyond a reference state is observed before recording ends, and unrecovered regression,
where task progress falls below its pre-regression reference level and does not return to that level before
the recording ends. This organization does not imply that stalls or local recoveries occur only in successful
executions. Event judgments depend on the task instruction and observable changes relative to the reference
states. An unsuccessful grasp attempt is not regression unless a grasp had already been established, and
renewed motion is not recovery unless the relevant task state is restored. A failed execution may retain
partial progress, so failure alone does not establish unrecovered regression.
We construct event annotations through two-pass annotation with GPT-5.6 Sol and independent human
annotation. GPT-5.6 Sol first identifies candidate events and temporal anchors across each execution, then
refines them using denser local observations and later context when needed. Human inspection covers the
raw real-robot collection, and each retained trajectory is independently annotated without access to the
machine labels. Disagreements and unresolved cases undergo third-party review, while stratified samples
of agreement cases are independently audited. Only visually supported progress comparisons are used for
supervision. On the 8,146 retained RoboArena trajectories, machine and independent human annotations
agree on the primary category for 87.56% of trajectories. Full annotation procedures and audit statistics are
provided in Appendix A.3.
We convert the reviewed event annotations into within-trajectory progress comparisons. For each event e,
its temporal interval locates candidate observations, and only those supported by the annotated task-state
relation enter the comparison set Me. For example, when a dropped cup is regrasped and lifted, observations
showing the loss of the established grasp should have lower progress than both the pre-drop reference and
the recovered state. The annotations specify the observations to compare and the direction of their progress
relation; the model learns the numerical scores through the relation loss in Section 3.3. Additional definitions
and examples are provided in Appendix A.2.
3.3
Learning Objectives
Temporal supervision.
For successful trajectories without annotated events, temporal labels are yt =
(t −t0)/(tS −t0) over the task-relevant interval from t0 to completion tS. We train the progress distribution
with cross-entropy against a soft encoding with mean yt, yielding Lprogress. During pretraining and replay,
successful histories and their temporal transformations provide labels for the auxiliary trend cross-entropy
loss Ltrend (Appendix B.2). Reference and comparison observations used for relation supervision receive
neither temporal nor trend labels.
Progress relations.
Event categories select constraints on the scalar progress prediction pt = pθ(xt),
rather than serving as classification targets. Reference and comparison scores use the same model and task
conditions; reference scores are recomputed as the model changes, with gradients stopped through them in
the relation loss. For a regression-and-recovery event e, let ¯pa and ¯pr be the reference and recovery scores.

Joint objective.
We jointly optimize L = λpLprogress + λrelLrel + λtLtrend, with weights controlling the

local changes in real executions without assigning absolute targets to event observations. This distinction
matters for reward shaping, whose magnitude depends on the prediction scale at a fixed shaping coefficient.
Section 4.1 evaluates the complementary contributions of these two supervision signals.
Training and adaptation.
Training proceeds through pretraining, rollout joint training, and target-task
adaptation. We pretrain Event2Reward on approximately 20,000 hours of robot expert demonstrations and
human manipulation videos using temporal labels and auxiliary trend supervision. Rollout joint training
then combines temporal supervision from normal successful rollouts, relation supervision from event-bearing
rollouts, and replay of pretraining data. Target-task adaptation uses demonstrations and event-bearing
executions under the same joint objective. Training settings are provided in Appendix B.4, and adaptation
protocols are given in Section 4.
3.4
Progress-Based Reward Shaping
Event2Reward converts learned task progress into dense reward signals for downstream policy learning. After
target-task adaptation, we freeze the reward model and predict task progress pt during execution. Following
potential-based reward shaping [21], we add the discounted difference between successive progress predictions

Progress
predictions should therefore reflect stalls, regressions, and recoveries. In particular, they should decrease when
previously achieved task states are lost, to avoid assigning misleading positive shaping rewards to actions
that cause progress loss.
Event annotations are used to train the progress model. During policy learning, rewards are computed
directly from its predictions, without requiring online event categories or recovery annotations. We use this
reward construction in both simulation and real-robot experiments. Action-chunk reward computation and
episode-boundary handling are detailed in Appendices C.1 and D.3.
4
Experiments
We first use controlled ablations to compare supervision from within-trajectory progress relations and
trajectory-level preferences, and to examine the complementary roles of progress relations and temporal
labels. We then compare Event2Reward with baseline methods for policy learning in simulation. To assess
the contribution of rollout-based joint training, we compare models under the same target-task adaptation
budget. Finally, we evaluate whether Event2Reward improves policy success and learning efficiency on real
robots.

Supervision type.
We compare temporal labels alone (Progress-only) with temporal labels combined
with trajectory-level preferences (Progress + Preference) or within-trajectory progress relations (Progress +
Relation). The latter two use the same additional trajectory pool and identical temporal supervision. We
measure downstream policy learning using normalized evaluation AUC, which summarizes average evaluation
success over a common interaction interval. Progress + Relation achieves the highest mean AUC on all four
tasks, with a four-task average of 36.7%, compared with 19.6% for Progress-only and 19.4% for Progress
+ Preference (Figure 3). To distinguish the effect of relation supervision from that of mixed sampling, we
also evaluate Relation-loss-off, which retains the mixed-sampling scheme of Progress + Relation but disables
the relation loss. Its four-task mean AUC is 19.1%, close to Progress-only and below Progress + Relation
(Table 11 in Appendix C.1). These results support a contribution from relation supervision beyond mixed
sampling and show that adding progress relations to temporal labels yields larger downstream gains than
adding trajectory-level preferences in this setting.
Joint supervision.
To examine the complementary roles of temporal labels and progress relations, we
compare Progress + Relation with Progress-only and Relation-only. The latter uses only progress relations
during continued training. Joint supervision achieves higher mean evaluation AUC than either alternative on
all four tasks (Figure 3).
We diagnose this gap through relation satisfaction and within-trajectory prediction range, without rescaling
model outputs (Table 1; protocols in Appendix C.1.2). Relation-only has the highest relation satisfaction
(58.86%) but near-zero outputs with a mean range of only 0.0021, consistent with weak shaping at a fixed
coefficient. Joint supervision improves relation satisfaction over Progress-only while retaining prediction
variation. Prediction range alone does not establish accuracy. Together with the RL results, these findings
support temporal labels for scale anchoring and relations for local constraints in this setting.

4.2
Policy Learning in Simulation
We compare Event2Reward with Sparse (environment rewards only), Robometer [14], and Robo-Dopamine [26]
on four LIBERO [15] suites—Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10—covering 40 tasks. Event2Reward’s
pretraining and rollout joint training exclude LIBERO data. For each suite, Event2Reward and Robometer
are adapted using the same 200 target-suite trajectories, while Robo-Dopamine follows its one-shot adaptation
protocol with one expert demonstration per task. All reward models remain frozen during policy learning.
Within each task, all conditions share the same frozen π0.5 [24] base policy and use the residual SAC framework
from Section 4.1 under matched interaction budgets. Adaptation, policy-training, and evaluation details are
provided in Appendix C.2.
Event2Reward achieves the highest mean policy success on all four LIBERO suites (Table 2). Compared with
the strongest baseline in each suite, it improves success by 10.12, 11.60, 9.96, and 13.76 percentage points on
Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10, respectively. Figure 4 shows learning curves for eight of these tasks,
which are listed in Table 17; Appendix C.2.3 reports curves for all 40 tasks.

Conclusion
We introduce Event2Reward, which combines temporal labels from demonstrations with progress relations
grounded in real execution events to learn a task progress model for reward shaping in robotic reinforcement
learning. We curate an event-annotated dataset covering successful, suboptimal, and failed executions to
supervise local progress changes. Experiments on 40 LIBERO tasks and six real-robot tasks show improved
policy success under the same training budgets. Controlled ablations further support the complementary
roles of temporal labels in anchoring the numerical scale and relative progress relations in constraining local
changes. These results show that event-grounded relations can provide effective supervision for robotic reward
learning even when absolute progress values are difficult to assign to states in real suboptimal and failed
executions.

A
Event-Annotated Robot Rollouts
A.1
Data Sources, Composition, and Splits
Data sources and scope.
We curate approximately 100K raw real-robot execution records from multiple
sources. Public sources include RoboChallenge [34], RW-RL [5], RoboArena [3], ViFailback [38], MolmoAct2 [8],
ArmNetBench [25], the real-world subset of RoboFAC [37], and AutoEval [42]. We organize visual observations,
task instructions, and outcomes in a common format for within-trajectory event annotation.
Execution quality and dataset composition.
Normal successes complete the task without events
identified by our protocol; suboptimal successes complete it with events, and failures remain incomplete
when recording ends. Retention requires a task instruction, usable visual observations, and either a valid
normal-success completion endpoint or at least one supported progress relation. Some retained trajectories
have uncertain outcomes but supported local relations. Table 4 gives retained counts by source, execution
quality, and split.
Data splits.
Each rollout and its derived clips and event annotations remain in one split; source records
identified as the same execution are grouped first. Table 4 reports split sizes. Validation and test trajectories
are excluded from training, and training goal images come only from the training split.
A.2
Event Annotations and Examples
Annotation contents.
For each event, records contain its category, reference state, and temporal region;
regression-and-recovery also supplies a recovery state. These anchors and supporting observations define
justified within-trajectory comparisons without framewise completion percentages; available descriptions
explain attained states and their changes. Table 5 summarizes the four categories, interpreted against the
complete task instruction and observable states; a failed outcome alone does not imply lost progress.
These distinctions depend on task-state evidence rather than motion alone. Task-required waiting is not
automatically a progress stall; an unsuccessful grasp attempt is not regression unless a grasp had been

A.3
Annotation Procedure and Quality Control
Data scope and temporal alignment.
We manually inspected the full collection of approximately
100K raw real-robot execution records. Records were organized by task instruction, source outcome, and
observation timeline, with available camera views retained. Source success and failure outcomes were preserved.
For successful rollouts used for progress supervision, we identified the task-relevant completion endpoint,
retaining release and stabilization needed for completion while excluding unrelated post-task motion or waiting.
RoboChallenge success videos additionally used a robot-state-derived execution timeline that compresses
long static intervals while retaining context around motion boundaries. Annotations were aligned with the
corresponding observation timeline, with mappings back to the source recordings.
Machine annotation.
We used GPT-5.6 Sol with medium reasoning effort to generate initial annotations
(V1) for all real-robot data sources from task instructions and indexed visual observations in two passes.
A coarse pass identified candidate events, reference states, and temporal regions across the execution. A
refinement pass used denser observations around these regions and later context when needed to refine event
interpretation and boundary localization, including whether a lost task state was subsequently recovered. The

Each retained trajectory had an independent human

real-robot examples in this paper use the final annotation records.

A.4
Release Contents and Usage
We will release event annotations, data splits, source-media references, and documented tools for loading
records and constructing supervision. Records link task instructions, execution outcomes, event regions,
and reference and recovery states, where applicable, to their source trajectories. They retain annotation
provenance and human-review status. Accompanying semantic descriptions support interpretation and
retrieval; they are not an additional training objective. Event definitions and training objectives are specified
in Appendices A.2 and B, respectively.

B
Model and Training Details
B.1
Model Inputs and Prediction Heads
Inputs and visual history.
Using the input components defined in Section 3.1, we process images at
224 × 224 resolution and take the start observation from the beginning of the trajectory or training segment.
The instruction and images form a single multimodal input, with text markers indicating image roles and
temporal order. The visual history contains five observations. For chronological inputs, it ends at the queried
observation and contains no later observations. Videos in the event-annotated dataset use approximately
0.1-second sampling intervals, with each requested timestamp mapped to the latest available frame at or
before it. Stage-1 pretraining and replay use 0.25-second intervals. AutoEval uses the latest five logged
observations without assuming a fixed frame rate. When history is insufficient at the beginning of a trajectory
or segment, we repeat the first eligible observation. Temporal transformations for trend supervision are
described in Appendix B.2.
Goal conditioning.
For the event-annotated dataset, goal images are selected from accepted completion
frames of successful training trajectories with compatible task, scene, and camera context.
Successful
trajectories containing events may also supply goal images. When a compatible goal image is unavailable
or omitted during training, we use a dedicated no-goal token. Goal-image dropout settings are given in
Appendix B.4.
Backbone and prediction heads.
With Qwen3.5-4B as the backbone, we append separate progress
and trend query tokens to the multimodal input and apply a prediction head to each token’s final-layer
hidden state. Each head applies layer normalization followed by a two-layer MLP with hidden width d/2,

Experimental Details and Additional Results
C.1
Ablation Study
Supervision conditions.
We compare five reward-model variants on LIBERO-10 Tasks 1, 3, 7, and 9.
All variants use Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct and start from the same checkpoint trained for 2,000 updates using
temporal labels from normal successful rollouts. Each variant then undergoes 2,000 additional updates with
the supervision specified in Table 9. We preserve chronological input histories and disable auxiliary trend
supervision throughout these ablations. Variant names refer only to the supervision used during continued
training.
Training data.
For the controlled supervision ablations, we use π0 rollouts covering all ten LIBERO-
10 tasks. We select 2,000 normal successful rollouts. This pool supplies temporal labels for the shared
initialization and for continued training in variants that retain temporal supervision. An additional training
pool contains 2,559 rollouts, comprising 839 event-containing successes and 1,720 failures. Relation and
trajectory-preference supervision use this same additional pool.
The two training pools and the held-out validation and test sets, containing 128 and 139 rollouts respectively,
are mutually disjoint at the trajectory level. Relation and preference supervision use the same trajectory
splits. The held-out sets cover the same ten tasks as training, while downstream reinforcement learning
evaluates Tasks 1, 3, 7, and 9.
Sampling and supervision.
Temporal labels are constructed by normalizing time within normal successful
segments. Progress-only uses clips from the normal-success pool throughout continued training, while Progress
+ Relation allocates half of its clip budget to this pool and half to event data. Event clips receive relation
supervision and no temporal labels. Relation-loss-off retains the same 50/50 sampling recipe but sets the
relation-loss weight to zero, controlling for the sampling change relative to Progress-only. Relation-only uses
event data without temporal supervision; any padding clips carry no supervision. Under the fixed training
budget, this condition therefore increases event exposure as well as removing temporal supervision.
Trajectory preference supervision.
Progress + Preference uses the same normal-success pool and
additional rollout pool as Progress + Relation. Preference supervision is derived from trajectory outcomes,
without using event categories or boundaries. For preference supervision, we pair a successful and a failed
trajectory from the same task and balance their presentation order. Each trajectory is represented by eight
uniformly sampled frames, together with its start and goal images. The model jointly processes the two
trajectories, and a separate preference head is trained with binary cross-entropy to favor the successful
trajectory. Each update uses 40 normal-success clips and 14 trajectory pairs, matching the normal-success
clip count and total image and visual-token exposure of Progress + Relation. Over 2,000 updates, this
gives 80,000 normal-success clip exposures and 28,000 pair exposures. Relation supervision samples local
event groups, whereas preference supervision samples whole-trajectory pairs. During downstream RL, the
preference-trained model provides rewards through its progress head. Held-out evaluation of the comparison
head confirms high accuracy on same-task success–failure preferences (Appendix C.1.1).

Optimization.
We use AdamW with weight decay 0.01 and 100 warmup updates. Learning rates are
2 × 10−6 for the language backbone, 5 × 10−7 for the trainable vision parameters, and 10−5 for the prediction

the heads used by each variant. The progress, relation, and preference losses have weights 1, 10, and 1,

auxiliary trend supervision is disabled here.

Metrics and uncertainty.
Following the preference prediction metric in Robometer [14], accuracy is the
fraction of comparisons whose predicted ordering matches the ground truth; here, a correct prediction selects
the successful trajectory. We additionally report binary cross-entropy (BCE) and order-swap diagnostics.
Macro averages weight tasks equally, whereas pooled results weight trajectory pairs equally. We compute
percentile 95% intervals using 10,000 bootstrap replicates, independently resampling successful and failed
trajectories within each task and weighting each pair by the product of its endpoints’ resampling multiplicities.
The two orders of a pair remain together. Thus, shared trajectories and mirrored comparisons are not treated
as independent observations. The intervals are conditional on the fixed checkpoint and task set and do
not capture reward-model training variability. This is a same-task success–failure evaluation, rather than a
reproduction of Robometer’s out-of-distribution benchmark.

The preference head achieves high held-out accuracy in
comparing successful and failed trajectories from the same task. Our RL rewards are shaped using progress
predictions at individual time steps, which the preference head’s pairwise outputs do not directly provide.
Although preference training updates the shared backbone, accurately ranking whole trajectories does not by
itself ensure that the progress head provides an effective shaping potential. On the four RL tasks, Progress
+ Preference achieves a mean evaluation AUC of 19.4%, compared with 36.7% for Progress + Relation
(Table 11).
C.1.2

To diagnose Relation-only’s poor RL performance, we evaluate progress decreases and output compression on

both outcomes; the RL ablations cover four tasks. We use the fixed reward-model checkpoints from those
ablations, identical query frames and goals across conditions, and original [0, 1] predictions without per-model
or per-trajectory rescaling. Relation-loss-off retains the joint-training sampling recipe with the relation loss
disabled.
Relation satisfaction rate.
We compute this metric on the unrecovered regression subset of the 103
held-out trajectories: 46 trajectories spanning nine tasks. These annotations identify achieved progress that
is lost and remains unrecovered at the end of the execution. For each trajectory, we compare the prediction

reference to the end of the trajectory. A comparison satisfies the relation when the event-frame prediction is
strictly lower than the reference prediction; ties count as unsatisfied comparisons. We average the relation
satisfaction rate within each trajectory, then within each task, and finally equally across the nine tasks.

Policy Learning in Simulation
C.2.1
Reward-Model Adaptation
We adapt reward models for four LIBERO suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10, each containing
ten tasks. Event2Reward starts from the model obtained after rollout joint training described in Section 3.3.
Robometer [14] and Robo-Dopamine [26] start from their publicly released pretrained checkpoints.
Within each suite, Event2Reward and Robometer use the same 200 adaptation trajectories. The Spatial set
contains 114 expert demonstrations, 58 suboptimal successful rollouts, and 28 failed rollouts; the Object, Goal,
and LIBERO-10 sets each contain 75, 64, and 61 trajectories of these categories, respectively. Suboptimal
and failed rollouts are collected using pi0 libero and manually annotated and checked. Each method
follows its own adaptation recipe consistently across suites for 500 updates, and the final checkpoint is
used for policy learning. Event2Reward uses temporal labels and auxiliary trend supervision on expert
demonstrations, together with progress relations derived from annotated events in suboptimal and failed
executions. Robometer retains its progress, preference, and success objectives. Table 14 summarizes the
optimization settings used consistently across the four suites.
Robo-Dopamine uses task-specific one-shot adaptation. For each task, we select one expert demonstration
from the official LIBERO data and adapt the pretrained model for two epochs using its released training
objective. The final frame of the demonstration serves as the goal image. This produces one reward model
per task, whereas Event2Reward and Robometer each use one adapted model per suite. Sparse uses only
the environment reward. All reward models remain fixed during policy learning, and each adapted model is
reused across environment seeds.

Policy Learning and Evaluation
For each suite, we jointly fine-tune π0.5 [24] on one expert demonstration from each of its ten tasks. Within
each task, all reward conditions share the frozen base policy, while task-specific residual policies are trained

We use the residual SAC framework and online-update protocol in Appendix C.1, with action-chunk length
H = 5. LIBERO-Goal and LIBERO-Object use residual scale α = 0.2 and discount γ = 0.998; LIBERO-
Spatial uses α = 0.5 and γ = 0.99; LIBERO-10 uses α = 0.1 and γ = 0.998. Reward construction and all
other SAC settings are unchanged. Reward-model adaptation trajectories are excluded from policy replay.
For online reward inference, Event2Reward uses the task instruction, the episode’s initial main-camera image,
a fixed goal image from the same view, and the latest five consecutive main-camera observations. Robometer

the current-frame progress clipped to [0, 1]. Robo-Dopamine uses forward inference with fixed demonstration
reference and goal images, together with main- and wrist-camera observations at the episode start and current
step. Its percentage score is divided by 100 and clipped to [0, 1].
The environment provides a sparse reward of 1 upon success and 0 otherwise, with no additional failure
penalty. Episodes terminate upon success or are truncated after 220, 280, 300, and 520 environment steps for
Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10, respectively, in both training and independent evaluation.
Each method is evaluated with five environment seeds per task, following the seeds and independent-evaluation
protocol in Appendix C.1. Quantitative budgets are 200k training environment steps per run for Spatial,
Object, and Goal, and 1.2M for LIBERO-10. The first three suites’ curves extend to 240k steps, but
quantitative statistics exclude evaluations beyond 200k. Evaluation interactions are excluded from both the

C.2.4
Rollout Joint-Training Ablation
Training controls.
Event2Reward w/o rollout joint training uses the 50,000-update pretraining checkpoint;
Event2Reward uses the 10,000-update joint-training checkpoint initialized from it. Both share Qwen3.5-4B
and the target-adaptation objective. All conditions use 500 adaptation updates, 80 queries per update, and
training seed 42. With 200 trajectories, the ablated model uses eight devices and one accumulation step; all
other conditions use two devices and four accumulation steps, preserving the global batch. Joint training
combines real rollouts, 5,452 simulated RoboFAC rollouts, and pretraining-data replay, adding 10,000 updates
(800,000 queries; Appendix B.4). The comparison tests this stage as a whole, without separately controlling
data source, scale, supervision, or additional compute.
Fixed adaptation subset.
The 40-trajectory subset contains 15 normal successful trajectories and 25
event-bearing trajectories, sampled from the same 200-trajectory Object adaptation set used in Section 4.2.
Normal successes cover all ten tasks, with at least one per task. The four event categories retain 7, 5, 11,
and 2 trajectories for no further progress, unrecovered regression, progress stall, and recoverable regression,
respectively. Event counts follow the original category proportions, with sampling stratified by task. All
variants use the same subset, fixed before training without consulting model predictions or RL outcomes, and
share ten fixed goal images; nine are sourced outside the 40-trajectory subset. The reduced-data condition
therefore uses 40 adaptation trajectories plus these shared goal images.
RL and evaluation.
Each condition uses one reward-model checkpoint and 50 RL runs on ten Object tasks
with environment seeds {7, 8, 9, 17, 27}. All share the frozen Object one-shot π0.5 checkpoint at step 49,999
and Object residual SAC settings from Appendix C.2: residual scale 0.2, discount 0.998, and chunk horizon
five. Runs train for 300k environment steps, while Table 3 retains the 200k comparison budget. Independent
evaluation and last-periodic success follow Appendix C.2.2. AUC follows that section’s fixed-budget rule: it
includes each run’s initial evaluation, interpolates between in-budget observations, and holds the last value to
the budget endpoint; no out-of-budget observation contributes.
Uncertainty and scope.
Pairing the task-averaged results by environment seed, the 200k AUC gain from
rollout joint training is +3.39 percentage points with 200 adaptation trajectories, with a nominal 95% paired
t interval of [−2.46, 9.24]; with 40 trajectories, it is +4.13 points with interval [−0.63, 8.89] (four degrees of
freedom). These intervals reflect environment-seed variation conditional on one reward-model training seed
and one fixed 40-trajectory subset. The positive mean differences do not establish statistical significance
or equivalent performance after reducing adaptation data by 80%. Both methods already include the same
pretraining and relation-supervised target adaptation; this comparison addresses the incremental rollout
joint-training stage.
Full training budget.
Table 18 reports the same metrics at the actual 300k-step training budget. Rollout
joint training increases mean AUC by 3.23 points with 200 trajectories and 4.77 points with 40 trajectories;
last periodic evaluation success increases by 4.44 and 6.20 points, respectively. The final periodic evaluations
occur between 290,617 and 300,000 environment steps. Benefits vary across tasks: Task02 and Task06 account
for 5.68 points of the 6.20-point mean success gain in the 40-trajectory comparison. These results complement
the fixed 200k comparison rather than changing its reporting budget.

Real-World Tasks and Evaluation Protocols
D.1
Insertion Tasks
The three SERL tasks use the insertion setups shown in Figure 5 (top). Insertion success is judged by a
human operator according to the task-specific criteria below.
Plug Insert.
The robot starts with the plug already held in its gripper, aligns it with the target socket,
and inserts it fully. An episode succeeds when the plug is fully seated in the socket. The maximum episode
length is 190 steps.
RAM Insert.
The robot starts with the RAM module already held in its gripper, aligns it with the target
memory slot, and presses it into place. An episode succeeds when the module is fully inserted and seated;
engagement of the retaining clips is not required. The task has an insertion tolerance of approximately 2 mm.
The maximum episode length is 400 steps.
Ethernet Insert.
The connector initially rests on the tabletop. The robot must grasp it, lift the connector
and the attached cable, and insert it into the target Ethernet port. An episode succeeds when the connector
is fully seated in the port. The maximum episode length is 300 steps.
D.2
Object-Manipulation Tasks
The three DSRL tasks use a π0 base policy on Franka robots. Success is assessed at the episode level according
to the task-specific criteria below.
Stack Bowls.
Three bowls are placed on the tabletop, with one bowl already at the central target position.
The robot must place the other two bowls into the central bowl to form a stack of three. An episode succeeds
only when all three bowls form a stable stack after the gripper releases them. The maximum episode length
is 800 environment steps.
Press Buttons.
Blue, pink, and yellow buttons are placed on the tabletop. The robot must press them
downward in the order blue, pink, then yellow. An episode is successful only when all three buttons are
pressed in this order. The maximum episode length is 500 environment steps.
43


Plate Vegetables.
The robot must pick up a cabbage, an ear of corn, and a pepper from the tabletop and
place all three on the target plate. An episode succeeds only when all three objects remain stably on the
plate after the gripper releases them. The maximum episode length is 800 environment steps.
D.3
Training and Evaluation Protocols
SERL offline demonstrations.
Each SERL insertion task uses an offline buffer of 20 demonstrations,
shared by all four reward conditions for policy learning. We do not perform behavior-cloning pretraining.
SERL initialization and autonomous rollouts.
Each SERL run initializes its online replay buffer
with 10 newly collected human-assisted trajectories, using the same protocol across reward conditions.
Subsequent training and evaluation are autonomous. Initialization time counts toward the training budget,
but initialization trajectories are excluded from rollout-success statistics.
DSRL initialization and online learning.
For each task, we use the 20 policy demonstrations to fine-tune
π0 with LoRA. All reward conditions for that task start from the same fine-tuned checkpoint. During online
reinforcement learning, π0 remains frozen and only the latent-noise control policy is trained. DSRL uses
action chunks of 25 environment steps. Online exploration and evaluation are autonomous.
Reward-model adaptation.
Robo-Dopamine follows its official one-shot adaptation protocol. Robome-

For
Event2Reward, we follow Appendix A.3 to annotate suboptimal executions with event types, reference states,
event intervals, and recovery states where applicable, without framewise absolute progress scores. With our

simulation-adaptation learning rates, batch sizes, and trainable parameter groups (Appendix C.2.1). Final
adapted checkpoints remain frozen throughout online policy learning.
Reward annotation and computation.
The sparse task reward is 1 upon human-judged task success and
0 otherwise, with no additional failure penalty. Learned-reward conditions use potential-based reward shaping
(PBRS), with predicted progress as the potential, following Equation 2. Shaping rewards are computed at
action-chunk boundaries with β = 1 and γ = 0.999. The potential at absorbing terminal states is set to zero.
For SERL with learned rewards, rewards for offline demonstration trajectories are precomputed with the
corresponding reward model before the transitions enter the offline buffer. During online collection, the actor
completes an episode without waiting for reward inference. Rewards for the collected trajectory are computed
during the subsequent reset, and the transitions enter the replay buffer once their rewards are available.
Reset randomization and episode termination.
Each insertion task randomizes the starting position
at every reset with independent perturbations of approximately 1 cm along each of the X, Y , and Z axes.
Episodes end upon success, declared failure, or the applicable step limit. Declared failure triggers a reset, with
no further attempt in that episode. During SERL training, a human operator may terminate substantially
deviating, potentially unsafe motion; this ends the episode without providing corrective actions.
Our
experiments evaluate task performance in the reported settings and do not establish safety for deployment
beyond these settings.
Training budget and independent evaluation.
Each reward condition uses three independent runs per
task, each with 30 minutes of online wall-clock training, including initial buffer warm-up where applicable,
resets, and reward computation. Each 30-minute checkpoint is evaluated over 20 autonomous episodes (60 per
task and condition across the three policies). We report the mean and sample standard deviation of per-policy
success rates across runs; setting-level means weight the three tasks equally before rounding. Human-aborted
episodes count as failures and remain in the denominator.
Plug-insertion training-time comparison.
One additional run per method uses the same SERL
framework and measures wall-clock time to the first ten consecutive successful autonomous training episodes:
15 minutes for Event2Reward and 45 minutes for Sparse. Only post-warm-up autonomous episodes enter
the window; both times include warm-up collection and resets. This training-rollout metric differs from the
