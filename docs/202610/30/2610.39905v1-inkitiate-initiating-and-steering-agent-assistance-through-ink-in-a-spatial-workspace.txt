Abstract
Handwriting and sketching help people develop ideas around in-
formation. As this work unfolds, users may seek assistance with
finding, understanding, and organizing materials. We investigate
how ink can initiate and steer agent assistance in this process.
An eight-participant formative study explored desired assistance
and interaction expectations, informing three connected activities:
bringing materials in, thinking with materials, and shaping the
space. We present Inkitiate, a spatial workspace for ink-based agent
assistance in knowledge work. Through ink, users retrieve external
materials, build on accumulated content and results, and reorganize
the canvas, while inspecting and guiding agent actions. In a study
with eighteen participants, users reported favorable usability and
low perceived workload, and used retained ink and materials to
develop further ink queries. An ink-query vocabulary and cases
reveal how users combine linguistic and graphical expressions to
direct agents. These findings inform ink-based agent interaction
design for expressive requests and continued work with materials.

Introduction
Handwriting and sketching help people externalize and develop
ideas that are still taking shape [35]. In digital interfaces, these
handwritten and sketched marks—digital ink—can serve not only
as notes or illustrations but also as input for computational tasks.
Generative AI systems use sketches to guide the creation of visual
content, including images and animations [18, 24]. Ink can also be
interpreted in context to support code editing, document feedback,
and tasks across applications [11, 17, 56]. These examples illustrate
how ink can express both what users want to create and what they
want a system to do with existing content.
Knowledge work often involves sensemaking: an iterative pro-
cess of gathering information and developing and revising interpre-
tations [33, 38]. Digital spatial workspaces support sensemaking
by enabling users to retain, group, and rearrange documents and
notes, keeping relevant information available and expressing re-
lationships as their understanding develops [2, 3, 15]. With large
language models, these workspaces also support AI-assisted ex-
ploration, synthesis, and organization, whose results can become
materials for subsequent tasks [6, 14, 44].
Within these workspaces, users seek agent assistance with ques-
tions and ideas expressed in their notes, sketches, and annotations,
using relevant materials as context. The results become part of the
workspace for users to examine, reorganize, and build upon as their
understanding develops. However, ink rarely specifies a complete
task on its own: its linguistic, visual, spatial, and relational infor-
mation must be interpreted together with selected materials and
prior results to determine the task’s referents, scope, and intended
action. As an agent interprets and executes such a situated request,
users also need to understand which materials and interpretations
are shaping the task and be able to clarify, correct, or stop it when
execution diverges from their intent. We therefore ask: How can
ink interaction enable users to seek and steer agent assistance within
a spatial workspace to advance ongoing knowledge work?
We began with a formative study involving 8 participants and an
initial pen-first workspace prototype. The prototype served as a de-
sign probe for exploring where participants wanted agent assistance
and how they expected to interact with it. The study highlighted
a need for cumulative agent assistance, in which users revisit and
reorganize existing materials and use prior results to develop subse-
quent inquiries, rather than treating each ink request as an isolated
question–answer exchange. Participants expected their selections
and spatial marks to delimit the content the agent should use and
indicate where its results should appear. When the prototype failed
to meet these expectations, participants wanted ways to inspect
and correct its interpretation and stop unwanted actions. Drawing
on these insights, we derived key design implications that guided
our system development.
We present Inkitiate1, a spatial workspace in which users initi-
ate and guide agent assistance through ink to bring materials in,
think with them, and shape the space. Each task begins with an
explicit submission of ink, optionally with selected canvas objects,
which the system interprets using bounded spatial context and
relevant history from prior ink queries. During execution, the in-
terface exposes the agent’s understanding, included materials, and
progress. Users can respond to clarification questions through sug-
gested options or additional ink, and abort execution when needed.
The system writes results back to the canvas by creating or modi-
fying persistent, editable objects. These objects remain available
for reorganization and reuse in subsequent ink queries, allowing
agent-assisted work to build on what is already in the workspace.
We examined Inkitiate with 18 participants through prescribed
knowledge-work activities (Reproduction) and self-directed explo-
ration (Freeform Creation). Participants reported favorable usability
and low perceived workload. Their experiences highlighted the
reuse of retained ink and materials, difficulties addressing local
targets, and the importance of output form for further work. An
ink query vocabulary characterizes how participants combined lin-
guistic, referential, and spatial marks to express their intentions,
while qualitative cases illustrate ink’s potential for guiding iterative
image creation and precise revision, as well as the organization of
materials into meaningful visual structures. Together, these obser-
vations inform our discussion of ink’s value for directing agents
and of designs that support continued work with materials while
preserving user direction. Our contributions are:
• A formative account of three connected activities for ink-
initiated agent assistance–bringing materials in, thinking
with materials, and shaping the space–along with expecting
interaction and the resulting design goals.
• Inkitiate, a spatial workspace that connects explicit ink
submission with contextual task interpretation, user inter-
vention during execution, and persistent, reusable results.
• An empirical account of how people use ink to direct agents,
characterizing an ink-query vocabulary and illustrating
interaction possibilities through cases.

Related Work
To contextualize Inkitiate’s design and the challenges of ink-initiated
agent assistance, we review related work across three areas.
2.1
Ink as Content and Instruction
Handwriting and sketching provide a flexible medium for exter-
nalizing ideas and annotating existing materials [16, 35]. In digital
systems, ink can serve both as content to interpret and augment
and as a means of indicating what users want the system to do,
with its meaning shaped by its form, location, and relationship to
surrounding materials [40, 56].
Ink externalizes users’ ideas, making them available for later
review and computational interpretation. Research on data analysis
found that free-form inking supported graphical externalizations
and that explicit links between ink marks and data helped users

revisit their work [16]. AmIWrite [21] interprets handwritten math-
ematical work to provide feedback. Inkeraction [40] recognizes how
ink is grouped and connected, adjusting layout and preserving con-
nections as users rearrange content. Sketches also provide visual
content for generation: ImaginationVellum [24] uses sketches as
spatial prompting for image generation, Inkspire [19] supports iter-
ative product design, and SketchDynamics [18] interprets sketches
as dynamic behavior for animation.
Ink can also express instructions, with its content and con-
text helping systems determine the intended actions and their tar-
gets. texSketch [43] uses ink annotations made while reading to
guide causal diagram construction, and ActiveInk [36] lets users
activate existing ink annotations as predefined analytic opera-
tions. InkSight [20] uses sketch-based selections to identify chart
data for generating findings and documentation. SketchingRelat-
edWork [23] maps hand-drawn boxes, handwriting, and node-link
structures onto paper and citation queries. For free-form requests,
Code Shaping [56] and Notational Animating [41] interpret con-
textual sketches to guide code edits and animation keyframes, re-
spectively; SketchGPT [11] combines sketch and speech to express
task intentions across existing application interfaces. Thinkink [31]
supports iterative ideation around handwritten notes, sketches, and
model responses, allowing users to formulate requests using can-
vas content and revise generated content through ink annotations.
These studies demonstrate how ink can both externalize ideas and
communicate intended actions. They motivate us to explore how
users can draw on these roles to initiate and guide agent assistance
in knowledge work.
2.2
Evolving Workspaces for Knowledge Work
Sensemaking in knowledge work involves repeatedly gathering in-
formation, developing interpretations, and revising understanding
as new materials emerge. Foundational work frame sensemaking as
constructing and reusing external representations: Russell et al. [38]
analyze its cost structure, and Pirolli and Card [33] distinguish an
information-foraging loop from a sensemaking loop with evidence,
schema, and hypothesis stages. Analytic workspaces support this
process by keeping documents and evidence available for inspec-
tion, organization, and manipulation [4, 42, 50]. In such workspaces,
large displays can further support working memory and externalize
evolving analytic structures through spatial layout [2, 3].
Recent systems integrate LLM assistance into workspaces where
users can revisit and build on earlier materials and results. Grapho-
logue [13] turns model responses into interactive node-link dia-
grams, while Sensecape [44] supports exploration across levels of
abstraction. Memolet [55] addresses context reuse by turning con-
versational memories into manipulable objects that users curate,
reference, and adjust to guide subsequent generations. IntentTag-
ger [9] externalizes generation intent as small, composable tags,
including references to existing content, which users can refine
to steer deck-level generation and subsequent slide-level edits. In
scholarly and qualitative knowledge work, Garden of Papers [22]
connects paper finding, reading, and organization, while Schol-
arMate [54] explores mixed-initiative sensemaking. CrossLit [6]
further links the spatial organization of papers and notes with
editable textual structures, allowing users to revise an emerging
review across visual and textual representations. Contemporary
products such as Heptabase [10], Miro [27], tldraw [47], and Mi-
crosoft’s Huabu [26] also provide persistent spatial workspaces for
organizing, connecting, and revisiting heterogeneous materials.
Prior work therefore shows how spatial workspaces can preserve
and reorganize materials and intermediate results as users’ under-
standing develops. Complementing these systems, Thinkink [31]
supports iterative ideation through user ink and LLM-generated
responses on a shared canvas. However, it remains underexplored
how situated ink can initiate and steer agents across a broader
knowledge-work process: acquiring new information, reasoning
over selected materials, and creating or reorganizing workspace con-
tent. As these actions operate over heterogeneous, source-bearing
materials and prior results, users must communicate not only what
they want but also which objects the agent should act on and how.
We investigate how situated ink can support this form of agent
assistance in an evolving workspace.
2.3
Interaction-Augmented Instructions and
User Control
Users can communicate their intentions to AI systems through in-
teractions with visible materials as well as language. Earlier work on
semantic interaction explored how users’ manipulation of analytic
representations can reveal their intent and guide updates to un-
derlying computational models [7]. For generative AI, interaction-
augmented instructions combine GUI actions, language, and artifact
context to form the instruction received by the model [39].
When systems infer part of a request from these interactions,
users need ways to inspect and correct the resulting interpreta-
tion and its effects. Guidelines for human–AI interaction similarly
emphasize helping users understand system behavior and support-
ing efficient correction [1]. S-PRISM [45] interprets spatial inter-
actions as report-refinement intentions, presents these interpre-
tations through interface bubbles, and highlights changes to the
report. DirectGPT [25] lets users select visible objects for model
operations and undo edits, while VizTA [49] connects references
to chart elements with conversational explanations. AI Chains [51]
exposes model calls and intermediate outputs as editable modules,
allowing users to inspect and debug individual steps. DynaVis [48]
synthesizes persistent visualization-editing controls from natural
language requests, letting users adjust results directly rather than
restate each change as a new request.
User involvement also needs to continue when execution en-
counters missing information or decisions that depend on user
preferences [39]. Morae [32], designed for blind and low-vision
users, pauses at decision points where preferences are unclear and
elicits choices through generated interfaces. CowPilot [12] supports
user-initiated intervention: users can pause or override agent ac-
tions, act directly, and subsequently return control to the agent. In
research tasks, ResearStudio [53] exposes a live plan and action his-
tory, allowing users to pause execution, edit the plan or code, and
resume the task. Recent research on computer-use-agent interfaces
likewise highlights that effective user control depends on making
agent behavior sufficiently understandable to support informed
intervention during execution [5].

Together, this work shows how interaction can help form AI
instructions and support inspection and intervention throughout
execution. Yet situated ink requests combine free-form marks, spa-
tial relations, selected materials, and evolving workspace context,
while agents may take multi-step actions that alter the workspace.
How to keep such tasks bounded, inspectable, and steerable re-
mains underexplored; we examine how users can submit ink with
relevant materials, inspect how agents interpret and execute these
requests, and intervene as the task unfolds.
3
Design Foundations
We conducted a formative study to understand how users would
use ink to seek and guide agent assistance in ongoing knowledge
work, and what interaction support they would need.
3.1
Formative Study
3.1.1
Initial Prototype. We based the initial probe on the sense-
making model summarized in Figure 2(a), focusing on the interplay
between information foraging and the development of representa-
tions [33]. Building on the information-foraging loop, the prototype
provided a spatial canvas where users could use ink to steer the
agent to retrieve external resources or generate content, bringing
these materials into the canvas as text, images, webpages, or PDF
objects. To support sensemaking with these materials, users could
then use ink to ask questions about existing objects or indicate
intended changes. After users submitted an ink query, the agent re-
turned results as new canvas objects or updates to existing content
once the run finished, making them available for further exploration
and organization.
3.1.2
Method. We recruited 8 participants (P1–P8; 3 male, 5 fe-
male) with prior experience and an interest in digital handwriting.
Participants were recruited through convenience sampling at our
research institute. Formative sessions lasted about one hour, and
participants each received compensation equivalent to 9 USD.
At the beginning of each session, we introduced the project,
explained how to use the prototype, and invited participants to
explore open-ended tasks based on their own interests. Participants
followed a think-aloud protocol during exploration, while we ob-
served their interaction strategies, breakdowns, and unexpected
system behaviors. After the exploration, we interviewed partici-
pants about their experiences and expectations for ink-initiated
agent assistance. With participants’ consent, we recorded screen
activity and audio in all sessions and automatically transcribed the
audio for analysis. The first author performed open coding of the
recordings and transcripts, iteratively refining the codes through
discussions with a second author to derive the formative findings.
3.1.3
Findings: Activities and Interaction Expectations. Drawing
on participants’ use and feedback together with the sensemaking
model, we identified three activities in which participants wanted
ink-initiated agent assistance (Figure 2(b)). Bringing materials in
uses ink-initiated requests to retrieve or generate resources. Think-
ing with materials combines ink and materials on the canvas to seek
explanations, comparisons, and synthesis. Shaping the space uses
ink to guide the organization of materials and their relationships
into editable visual structures. These activities connect informa-
tion foraging with interpretation and representation development,
including the construction and revision of schemas. They can over-
lap and recur as results become materials for further ink query.
Across these activities, participants expressed four expectations for
ink-initiated agent interaction.
⋄Finding One: Participants wanted to combine brief ink ex-
pressions with spatial references and explicit object selection
to specify their requests. Participants did not expect ink alone to
carry a complete request. P3 noted that the effort of handwriting
would make their expressions shorter and potentially less explicit
than typed requests. Participants instead wanted to use compact
ink to express what they wanted to do while relying on existing
canvas objects to indicate what the request concerned. For exam-
ple, P5 suggested circling a location in a travel guide and writing
“what shops are there?”: the enclosure identified the referent, while
the text specified the desired information. Relying only on local
spatial relationships, however, had limitations. P5 noted that earlier
material in a chat could become visually distant from the current
interaction and difficult to refer back to; on a larger canvas, relevant
objects likewise might not be located near the ink. P8 wanted to
explicitly select which objects were included when submitting an
ink query and envisioned combining a bounded region within an
object with a short instruction. Together, these responses suggest
that participants wanted to combine brief ink expressions with
spatial references and explicit object selection to specify both the
requested action and the materials it concerned.
⋄Finding Two: Participants expected context to follow the
current line of work rather than either an individual query or
the entire canvas. Participants wanted the agent to use the canvas
materials and prior history relevant to the current task, but did not
expect all canvas content to enter a single shared context. P5 valued
that context on the canvas could remain spatially organized rather
than being arranged only chronologically and gradually pushed out
of view, as in a chat history. Preserving context, however, did not
mean expanding it without limit. When P4 asked beside a paper
how to reproduce its 3D MRI pipeline, the system returned a general
learning path; P4 felt that the relationship between the request and
the paper had been lost. Conversely, after a travel request produced
an unexpected response, P7 suspected that the system had used too
much context and argued that the current task required only the
relevant guide material. These interpretations do not establish the
technical causes of the failures, but reveal expectations at both sides
of the context boundary: context needed to preserve the objects,
spatial relationships, and history required by the current line of
work while excluding canvas content unrelated to the task. The
expected unit of context was therefore closer to an evolving line of
work than to either a single ink query or the entire canvas.
⋄Finding Three: Participants wanted visibility into the agent’s
interpretation and progress, with opportunities to intervene
during execution. In the initial probe, results appeared on the
canvas only after an agent run finished, leaving users unable to
inspect or intervene during execution. Participants identified three
related needs. First, they needed basic awareness of whether the
agent was still active. When a precise paper search ran long without

or reorganize cluttered materials into a clear, editable structure
under user control.
3.2
Design Goals
These findings informed four design goals for supporting ink-
initiated agent interaction across the three activities.
⋄DG1: Enable users to combine ink with canvas materials to
specify tasks. Users should be able to combine ink with relevant
canvas materials, without having to restate those materials in the
ink query. Canvas materials, including prior results, should there-
fore remain persistent, selectable, manipulable, and source-bearing
resources available to both the user and the agent. Each task should
remain pen-first: submitted ink initiates the task, while selected or
explicitly referenced materials provide its targets and context. This
allows users to refer to nearby materials through spatial ink and
to distant materials through explicit references without relying on
proximity alone.
⋄DG2: Carry forward relevant context without mixing unre-
lated canvas work. A short ink query may depend on objects,
spatial relationships, and results from earlier tasks. Related requests
should therefore be able to carry forward the materials and history
needed for continued work. At the same time, content should not
enter a task merely because it exists elsewhere on the same canvas.
The system should maintain bounded continuity: users can con-
tinue an existing line of work without restating its context, while
unrelated activities remain separate.
⋄DG3: Keep agent execution inspectable, interruptible, and
incrementally steerable. Submitting an ink query should not turn
the task into an opaque process that users can assess only after

completion. Users should be able to inspect how the agent inter-
preted the request, which materials it is using, and what activity is
underway. They should also be able to stop or redirect an action
without discarding the larger task established on the canvas. When
missing information would affect the task’s direction, the system
should pause and request focused clarification through selectable
options or a short custom response.
⋄DG4: Support continued work by extending or reorganizing
the canvas’s visual structure. Results should return as persistent
and editable canvas objects rather than terminal responses. Their
placement and form should follow the ongoing work: results should
remain near the originating ink and materials or follow spatial con-
straints expressed through ink. When a result contains multiple
elements or relationships, it should use a content-appropriate visual
structure rather than default to a detached block of long-form text.
Users should also be able to use ink to steer the agent in reorganiz-
ing a bounded set of existing materials, including arranging them
along drawn trajectories, and then manually refine the resulting
arrangement. Provenance should remain attached as results are
moved, edited, organized, or reused in subsequent ink queries.

Inkitiate
Inkitiate is a spatial workspace where users guide agent assistance
through free-form ink to advance ongoing knowledge work. Be-
cause this work involves continuous interaction with heteroge-
neous materials, Inkitiate is designed to support an evolving, itera-
tive process. It makes the agent’s task interpretation inspectable,
allows users to intervene during execution, and returns editable,
source-bearing results directly to the persistent canvas.

featuring a hypothetical user, Amy. Together, they demonstrate how
sequences of ink queries help her advance ongoing knowledge work

4.1.1
Exploring The Odyssey. Amy first sketches a boat and writes
“The Odyssey” beside it, then draws an island and a route linking
them to establish a spatial structure for the journey (Figure 1).
Beside the boat, she writes “Find me a short intro” and adds a hand-
written sigil // to explicitly invoke resource retrieval. Inkitiate
brings a source webpage and a concise, source-backed overview
into the canvas. Using the selection tool, Amy selects the curved
route and its accompanying handwritten request, asking Inkitiate
to use the imported webpage # to map key episodes in Odysseus’s
journey from Troy to Ithaca along the route. Inkitiate places the
episodes along the route, turning the initial sketch into a spatial
account that remains available for further annotation and inquiry.
She then writes “Visualize the choice he faced here” beside Scylla and
Charybdis, lassos the ink expression, and submits it as a bounded
query input (Fig. 1 1). An agent activity card appears beside the
query, exposing the agent’s interpretation and current execution
so that Amy can inspect and steer the task (Fig. 1 2). As a result,
Inkitiate writes an organized comparison of Odysseus’s alternatives
back to the canvas as editable, reusable objects (Fig. 1 3).
4.1.2
Reading foundational LLM papers. Amy begins a literature
review in Inkitiate by handwriting an ink query for several founda-
tional papers on large language models (Fig. 3 1). Inkitiate brings the
recommended papers into the canvas as PDF objects and provides
a concise reading summary. She then invokes the organize hand-
writing sigil // and draws two labeled arrows asking the system
to arrange the papers by publication year and research direction
(Fig. 3 2). Inkitiate reorganizes the papers into a tabular literature
overview that exposes both temporal and thematic relationships.
Amy next selects the BERT paper and submits a short ink query
asking for a summary of its pretraining objectives (Fig. 3 3). Finally,
she writes the resource sigil # and selects two papers on scaling
directly on the canvas to bind them to her request (Fig. 3 4). She
then asks how the later work revises the earlier conclusion, and
Inkitiate organizes the original conclusion, its revision, and the key
difference between them into a structured result. Across these steps,
retrieved materials and agent-generated results remain available
for subsequent ink queries and reorganization.
4.2
Interaction Support for Inspecting and
Steering Ink-Initiated Agent Tasks
This section focuses on the lifecycle of a single agent task, de-
tailing how Inkitiate keeps it bounded, inspectable, and steerable
from ink query submission to write-back. Each task begins with a
bounded submission of ink (Section 4.2.1), selected canvas objects,
and optional sigils; Inkitiate then exposes its execution state (Sec-
tion 4.2.2), returns consequential decisions to the user (Section 4.2.3),
and writes reusable results back to the canvas (Section 4.2.4).
4.2.1
Bounding Task Input through Ink and Canvas Objects. As
illustrated in Figure 3, a bounded task input combines ink expres-
sions with user-selected canvas objects. Users lasso the content they
want to include and explicitly submit the selection to the agent.
The submitted ink may include both free-form ink and optional
handwritten sigils.
⋄Ink expressions. Every submission must include ink that ex-
presses the user’s intent within the canvas context. Ink expressions
take two forms: unconstrained free-form ink and handwritten sigils
with predefined semantics for explicitly specifying task resources
or agent capabilities.
(1) Free-form ink. Free-form ink has no prescribed syntax. Users
may write text, draw enclosures or arrows, connect objects,
or combine these forms to express intent through both
marks and spatial relationships.
(2) Handwritten sigils. Handwritten sigils are compact ink ex-
pressions with predefined semantics that make particular
task bindings explicit. A resource sigil # binds an exist-
ing canvas object to the task. After writing the sigil, users
can bind an object by selecting it from the displayed list
or directly on the canvas. A capability sigil // binds the
task to a named agent capability, implemented through a
workflow, skill, or tool. Without an explicit capability bind-
ing, Inkitiate infers an appropriate capability from the ink
query and its context. When a capability is bound, it over-
rides this default inference and routes the task through the
specified capability. The current prototype provides three

⋄Canvas objects. A submission may also include persistent canvas

4.2.2
Exposing and Controlling Task State. After submission, Inki-
tiate creates an agent activity card associated with the originating
location of the agent task. The agent activity card progressively re-
veals information as the task proceeds. Before an intent is available,
it indicates that the request is being analyzed. During execution,
its collapsed state shows the current activity; its expanded state
additionally presents the understood intent, included canvas mate-
rials, completed and current activities, and elapsed time. Users can
stop a task that is no longer needed or is proceeding in the wrong
direction, while an explicit failed state distinguishes execution fail-
ure from an active task. This information represents the public task
state needed for user judgment and intervention rather than the
agent’s private reasoning process. By combining task interpretation,
execution progress, and a stop control in one interface, Inkitiate
keeps an active agent action observable and interruptible.
Figure 4 illustrates this mechanism through a request for an illus-
trated map of Odysseus’s journey home. The query was ambiguous
and was interpreted as a request to generate a new journey map,
whereas the user intended to retrieve an existing, well-designed
map grounded in real geography from the web. The user there-
fore stopped the task, added the capability sigil // to explicitly
invoke resource retrieval, and resubmitted the query. The revised
task returned a set of candidate maps for selection.
4.2.3
Returning Consequential Decisions to the User. When missing
information could materially affect the task direction, Inkitiate does
not continue from an uncertain interpretation. Instead, it pauses
the current action and requests clarification from the user. When
the system cannot determine how the submitted ink and canvas
objects should be used together, the clarification interface presents
a question and several possible interpretations at the relevant can-
vas location. Users may select one of these options or provide a
custom ink response. The response remains staged so that it can be
reviewed, cancelled, or submitted before execution continues.

4.2.4
Writing Results Back to the Canvas. Inkitiate writes struc-
tured results back through task-scoped canvas operations that cre-
ate new persistent objects or modify existing ones. As shown on the
left of Figure 6, write-back determines the objects used to represent
a result, how they enter the visual structure of the canvas, and how
their sources remain inspectable. We organize this process around
three aspects:
⋄Persistent and reusable objects. Through the Create Objects
operation shown in Figure 6, results become persistent canvas ob-
jects rather than remaining in a temporary task history. Depending
on their content, results may be represented as text, images, web-
pages, PDFs, or SVG-describable drawing primitives. Users can
subsequently move, edit, organize, and select these objects, and
submit them with new ink as materials for later agent tasks. They
allow structures, relationships, and processes to be written back as
editable visual results.
⋄Spatial arrangement. Inkitiate uses spatial cues in the submitted
ink to determine where and how results appear. When no such
cues are provided, it places results near the originating ink and
relevant materials. For semantically rich or logically structured
results, Inkitiate composes multiple canvas objects into a legible
visual arrangement and uses Modify Existing Objects to integrate
the result with the canvas’s existing visual structure.

4.3
Context Engineering and Agent Execution
The preceding section described how users submit, supervise, and
continue steering an agent task. This section explains the system
architecture that supports this lifecycle. As shown in Figure 6,
the Host Server mediates between the persistent canvas and a
general-purpose agent runtime. It constructs bounded task context,
coordinates the task lifecycle, and controls how structured results
are written back to the canvas.
4.3.1
Constructing Bounded Task Context. When the user submits
a selection, the canvas sends its ink expressions, explicitly included
canvas objects, and submission location to the host as a bounded
task. In addition to the explicitly submitted content, Inkitiate uses
the location of the ink query to define a bounded neighborhood of
nearby ink and canvas objects. These materials are registered as
read-only context that the agent may inspect when needed. Nearby
content does not become a direct task input or operation target
merely because of its proximity. Explicit selection continues to de-
termine which objects are included in and may be modified by the
task. The ink query compiler organizes the available information
into three components: current ink query, canvas evidence, and task
continuity. The current ink query retains a viewport image cen-
tered on the submission location, the original ink marks, optional
MyScript transcription, resolved sigil bindings, and any clarification
subsequently provided by the user. Canvas evidence includes the ex-
plicitly submitted objects and provides on-demand access to nearby
read-only materials. PDF, webpage, and text objects contribute con-
tent excerpts, while image and drawing objects contribute visual
representations and spatial positions. Task continuity provides the
state of the relevant work branch and textual representations of
prior tasks and results, allowing a new ink query to continue an
established line of work. Together, these components form the task
context sent to the agent runtime. The agent can interpret the query
using its explicit inputs, nearby canvas context, and relevant history
without receiving unrestricted access to the entire canvas.
4.3.2
Coordinating Agent Execution and Write-Back. The agent
runtime interprets and executes the task from the compiled task
context. In addition to built-in tools such as Web Search and Gener-
ate Images, it can use task-scoped canvas tools to read materials,
inspect the canvas, resolve selections, and preview layouts. These
canvas tools allow the agent to gather task-relevant information
and preview proposed arrangements, but they do not allow it to
modify the persistent canvas directly. During execution, the task co-
ordinator sends public state, such as the understood intent and real-
time activity, to the task lifecycle UI. It also returns user controls,
including stop requests and clarification responses, to the agent
runtime. When execution completes, the agent returns a structured
result describing the objects, content, sources, and layout to be
created or modified. The structured result must pass through the
result boundary before canvas operations can create new objects
or modify existing objects within the task scope. By separating
general-purpose agent execution from persistent canvas mutation,
this architecture bounds the context available to the agent, exposes
its execution state, and controls the workspace scope that its results
may change.

4.4
Implementation
Inkitiate was implemented as a standalone web application opti-
mized for iPad Safari. The canvas client uses TypeScript, React [34],
and React Flow [52] to implement the infinite canvas. HTML Can-
vas 2D and Perfect Freehand [37] render pressure-sensitive vector
ink, while the MyScript iink API [29] provides online handwriting
transcription. A Node.js and Fastify [46] host manages canvas state
and agent tasks through HTTP. The task coordinator streams public
task state to the client through Server-Sent Events and relays user
controls, including clarification responses and stop requests, to the
agent runtime. The host connects to an existing general-purpose
Codex runtime through the Agent Client Protocol [57]. The runtime
can use built-in tools such as web search and image generation,
together with task-scoped canvas tools provided through the Model
Context Protocol [28]. These canvas tools allow the runtime to read
materials, inspect the canvas, resolve selections, and preview pro-
posed layouts without directly modifying the persistent workspace.
The study used GPT-5.6-Sol [30] with low reasoning effort.
⋄Scenario-Based System Validation. Before the user study, we
validated the end-to-end workflow of Inkitiate using 34 distinct
ink-query cases. The validation examined the complete workflow
from situated ink-query submission and context construction to
agent execution and canvas write-back, rather than benchmarking
model capability or evaluating user performance. The cases covered
different combinations of free-form ink, canvas object types, sigil
bindings, and agent actions, including continuing tasks that reused
results from earlier ink queries. Stability did not require identical
content across repeated generations. Instead, every run had to
produce a valid and semantically acceptable result while satisfying
the expected canvas behavior. Across the 34 cases, all 102 end-to-end
runs completed successfully and met the stability requirements.
5
User Study: Learning and Appropriating
Ink-Based Agent Interaction
We examined how participants learn and use ink-initiated agent
interaction in Inkitiate through two research questions:
• RQ1: How do participants learn to formulate ink queries
and use them to complete increasingly complex knowledge-
work tasks?
• RQ2: How do participants use ink to pursue their own
goals, what activities and workflows emerge, and what
possibilities and limitations does the system reveal?
To address these questions, we conducted two study phases. In
Reproduction, participants completed prescribed knowledge-work
tasks of increasing complexity. In Freeform Creation, they pursued
their own goals through open-ended exploration.
5.1
Study Design
5.1.1
Participants and Apparatus. Eighteen participants took part
in the user study (12 female and 6 male; age 𝑀𝑎𝑔𝑒= 26.2). Par-
ticipants included nine PhD students, seven master’s or MPhil
students, and two undergraduate students. We recruited partici-
pants through public calls at a university and a research institute.
Eligible participants were required to have prior experience with ei-
ther digital handwriting or visual canvas tools; remote participants
were additionally required to provide their own stylus-enabled
device. Of the 18 participants, 10 participated in person and 8 par-
ticipated remotely. Both modes used the same browser-based ver-
sion of Inkitiate, study tasks, and materials. In-person participants
used researcher-provided device and stylus (iPad and Apple Pencil),
while remote participants used their own stylus-enabled devices.
Sessions were scheduled for 60 minutes; when participants wished
to continue during Freeform Creation, we extended that phase
to allow them to explore the system more fully. Each participant
received compensation equivalent to approximately US$9. All par-
ticipants provided informed consent before the study, including
consent to screen and audio recording.
5.1.2
Procedure. The study session lasted approximately 1 hour
per participant and consisted of an initial tutorial, Phase I (Repro-
duction), Phase II (Freeform Creation), and a concluding interview.
Introduction and Tutorial (∼5 min). Before beginning the study
tasks, participants entered a hands-on familiarization area. They
learned the basic canvas operations by using them, including writ-
ing, erasing, moving objects, and producing separate ink marks.
This stage familiarized participants with the input device and spa-
tial workspace without demonstrating complete solutions to the
subsequent study tasks.
Phase I: Reproduction (∼25min). The Reproduction phase used
a top-down, goal-directed design. Participants worked through a
shared progression of increasingly complex activities. This phase
examined whether they could understand the ink-query interac-
tion and use it for continuing knowledge work. The progression
contained four levels:
(1) L1. Steering with handwriting: initiate an agent response
and formulate a handwritten request for information, such
as a local weather forecast.
(2) L2. Bringing in useful materials
: request different
kinds of canvas content, including a generated image, re-
search papers on a chosen topic, and the website of a con-
ference, laboratory, or project.
(3) L3. Making sense of materials
: ask a question grounded
in an imported article or webpage, and identify a meaning-
ful connection or difference across multiple materials.
(4) L4. Turning materials into a structure
: organize
retrieved papers into a visual account, such as a timeline
including a comparison and summary. In our study, this
final activity was presented as a bonus task.
Rather than asking participants to reproduce a fixed final canvas,
this phase asked them to progressively use a set of system capa-
bilities under shared goals: direct ink submission, retrieval and
generation, artifact-grounded ink queries, cross-material synthesis,
and structured write-back. Our analysis focuses on the completion
of these interaction capabilities.
Phase II: Freeform Creation (∼30min). The Freeform Creation
phase used a bottom-up, open-ended design. Participants started
from a blank canvas and explored or created around a recent re-
search direction or another topic of personal interest. We prescribed
neither a sequence of operations nor a uniform required output.

Post-study measures and interview. After completing both phases,
participants completed adapted 7-point versions of the System Us-
ability Scale (SUS) and NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) once
for their overall experience with Inkitiate, followed by a post-study
interview. Because these measures were administered after the
complete session rather than separately after each phase, we inter-
pret them as measures of overall perceived usability and workload
and do not use them to claim a quantitative difference between
Reproduction and Freeform Creation.
5.1.3
Data Collection and Analysis. Our data included screen and
audio recordings, canvas interaction logs, ink-query and task his-
tories, resulting canvas artifacts, questionnaires, and post-study
interviews. We analyzed these data in two ways:
(1) Quantitative analysis. We used descriptive statistics to
summarize participants’ overall SUS and NASA-TLX rat-
ings and their interaction patterns. For Reproduction, we
coded task completion at four interaction levels (L1-L4)
using interaction records and final canvas artifacts. For
Freeform Creation, we counted ink queries and summa-
rized input-resource types, prior-output reuse, and other
coded characteristics.
(2) Qualitative analysis. We qualitatively analyzed post-study
interviews together with retained canvas artifacts and in-
teraction records to understand participants’ experiences,
strategies, and perceived system boundaries. One author
conducted the initial thematic coding, and a second author
reviewed the codes and emerging themes; disagreements
were resolved through discussion. We also examined se-
quences of related ink queries and their resulting artifacts
to characterize evolving workflows and the compositional
vocabulary through which participants combined linguistic,
depictive, referential, relational, and spatial ink.
5.2
Study Results
We report participants’ performance in Reproduction, the activities
and patterns that emerged during Freeform Creation, and their
feedback on the overall experience.
5.2.1
Task Performance and Interaction Experience.
Reproduction Task Performance. All participants learned to use
ink to initiate and steer agent tasks on the canvas (L1, 18/18) and
incorporated useful generated or retrieved materials into the canvas
(L2, 18/18). Most participants also completed sensemaking activities
such as explaining, summarizing, comparing, or revising existing
materials (L3, 16/18). For the advanced or bonus visual-structuring
task (L4, 7/18), 7 participants organized materials into temporal,
conceptual, or workflow-based structures using spatial layout, con-
nections, or annotations. Three partially completed the task, pro-
ducing summaries or comparisons without forming a complete
visual structure; the remaining eight did not complete it. Some par-
ticipants did not continue in L3 and L4 because of time constraints
or uncertainty about how to begin.
Perceived System Usability. All 18 participants completed both
questionnaires without missing responses (Figure 7). The adapted
7-point SUS yielded an average score of 5.93 out of 7 (𝑆𝐷= 0.25).
Standard SUS grade thresholds were not applied to this adapted
scale. The highest-rated positive items concerned learnability (𝑀=
6.33, 𝑆𝐷= 0.77), confidence (𝑀= 6.28, 𝑆𝐷= 0.75), and willingness
to use the system frequently (𝑀= 6.17, 𝑆𝐷= 0.86); all participants
rated these items between 5 and 7. Negative-item ratings were low
overall (𝑀= 1.83–2.56), although perceived need for assistance (𝑀=
2.56, 𝑆𝐷= 1.20) and unnecessary complexity (𝑀= 2.44, 𝑆𝐷= 1.50)
received comparatively higher ratings among the negative items.

Developing Work through Retained Ink and Materials. The
Freeform Creation corpus comprised 18 cases and 181 ink query
entries, including 14 clarification responses. Information-oriented
activities were most prevalent (𝑛= 17 cases), followed by creative
exploration (𝑛= 7) and speculative or playful exploration (𝑛= 2);
these categories were not mutually exclusive. Across all 181 entries,
more than two-thirds explicitly reused previously generated or re-
trieved materials for further questioning, explanation, comparison,
or revision (𝑛= 125, 69.1%), while the remaining 56 entries (30.9%)
did not. Thirty entries combined multiple selected or linked canvas
objects in a single request. These patterns show that prior outputs
frequently became intermediate resources for ongoing activities
rather than endpoints of individual requests. Figure 8 summarizes
the activity topics and material reuse statistics across all cases.
Qualitative cases further showed how retained materials and ink
supported continued work in three ways. First, in U16’s college-life
scenario, annotations of completed activities and changed circum-
stances informed the next year’s plan; for later years, U16 wanted to
modify and resubmit the existing request rather than rewrite it: “I do
not want to copy it all over again...I can simply ask again.” Second, re-
tained instructions could be repurposed by changing their bindings
or wording. We observed U12 reusing an instruction while rebind-
ing it to a different canvas resource; in researcher-guided exchanges,
U11 explored changing its target, revising individual words, and
adding conditions such as short cards before resubmission. Finally,
participants considered how much instructional content should
remain visible on the canvas. U17 preferred retaining handwritten
keywords but requested speech for longer instructions to “both
keep my canvas clean and let the AI know my intention”; speech was
a requested complement, not an evaluated feature. Together, these
cases show that continued work relied on treating both prior out-
puts and their associated ink as editable resources while allowing
users to manage how much content remained on the canvas.
5.2.3
Referring to Fine-Grained Targets within Canvas Objects. Par-
ticipants used ink to refer to targets within canvas objects at a
finer granularity than the interface’s explicit bindings. While se-
lection and resource-sigil binding operated at the level of whole
objects, participants marked passages or image details within them,
including U01’s reference to one day in an itinerary, U13’s marks on
particular characters in a generated group, and U17’s local map an-
notations (Sections 6.1 and 6.2). In the current implementation, the
agent handled these references primarily by interpreting free-form
ink together with object content, without dedicated interaction
support for specifying or confirming their fine-grained scope. This
support was not always reliable: U07 asked about the song Sugar
within a text object, but the response covered all eight songs, while
U17’s local map edits required repeated revisions. The observed
outcomes do not reveal whether these mismatches arose during
reference interpretation or action execution. Together, these cases
show that within-object reference had already emerged as a mean-
ingful use of ink, while supporting its specification, inspection,
and preservation more reliably remains an important direction for
system improvement.
5.2.4
Seeking Outputs That Support Further Work. Participants
evaluated outputs by how they could read, organize, and revisit
them on the canvas, not only by the information they contained.
Three expectations emerged. First, several participants preferred
visually structured responses to a single or densely written text
object. U14 requested larger headings, smaller descriptions, and
branching levels; U08 valued structured presentation, and U09 as-
sociated visualization with a more concise and convenient canvas
experience. This did not imply a uniform preference for less text,
as U07 considered the explanations received appropriately detailed.
Second, participants distinguished an arrangement of constituent
elements from a single image depicting a similar structure. U14
asked for a flowchart made with “pen strokes and arrows, rather
than an image”, while U15 wanted “a whole set of images, then ar-
ranged into a timeline” of iPhone technology but received one image
instead. Finally, U17 wanted to retain different versions together
with “what I changed at each step, what my goal was, and what
the generated image looked like, all on the canvas”. Together, these
responses indicate that participants wanted outputs whose visual

Case Gallery: Ink-Initiated Agent Interaction
in Practice
This gallery examines how participants composed ink queries and
used them to guide agent actions during Freeform Creation. Com-
plementing the aggregate results in Section 5, we first characterize
this compositional vocabulary, then trace how it supported contin-
uing work across selected episodes of design, scenario exploration,
and storytelling.
6.1
Ink Query Vocabulary
What did users do with ink when they could choose their own
goals? They did not simply replace words with drawings. Often,
they wrote the question or action, then used a circle, a sketch, a
connection, or a path to show the rest: which part they meant, what
something should look like, how ideas belonged together, or where
an answer should go. Percentages indicate observed occurrences
across all 181 ink query entries; a single entry could involve multiple
uses of ink. Figure 9 pairs five submitted-ink excerpts with the small
stories behind them, illustrating five uses of ink:
• Linguistic (97.8%): Handwritten words, micro-prompts [8],
or sentences convey requests or information.
• Depictive (9.9%): Sketches depict visual forms or content
relevant to the request.
• Referential / scope-marking (39.2%): Marks point to or
enclose existing content to identify the target or scope of
a request, referring either to entire canvas objects or to
specific details within them, such as words or sentences in
a text object or regions of an image.
• Relational (6.1%): Connecting marks express relationships
between elements, providing a visual structure that reflects
the logical organization of content.
• Spatial / layout (9.4%): Ink placement indicates the in-
tended spatial organization of content.
These uses of ink often appeared together. In 76 of 181 ink query
entries, ink served at least two confirmed functions; among the
150 records where all five functions could be assessed, writing
combined with marks pointing to a target was the most common
multi-function combination (𝑛= 46). The itinerary example captures
this well: the sentence says what should change, while the circle
says where to look. Drawings, relations, and layout cues appeared
in fewer cases, but brought other possibilities into view, especially
in design and storytelling. The interesting vocabulary is therefore
not a dictionary in which every circle or arrow has one meaning.
An arrow can point to a detail, connect two ideas, or direct an
answer toward a space. For future systems, the practical lesson is
to keep these cues together: read the words, but also follow what
the user has circled, drawn, connected, or laid out.

7
Discussion
Drawing on the study findings, we discuss the value of ink for
directing agents and the challenges of supporting continued work
through it.
7.1
The Distinctive Value of Ink for Steering
Agents
Our study highlights ink’s value as a way to express requests di-
rectly through the materials and visual structures with which users
are working. Participants combined brief handwritten instructions
with depictions, references to materials, and marks expressing rela-
tionships or layout (Section 6.1). Words, marks, and relevant mate-
rials thus jointly expressed the request. This connects handwriting
and sketching as resources for developing ideas [16, 35] with the
joint construction of instructions through language, interaction,
and materials [39].
Within this combination, ink can both refer to what is already
present and express what is not yet there. Selection and drag-and-
drop also ground requests in existing content [25, 49], but referring
to an existing object is only part of what our cases illustrate. A
sketch can depict a proposed form, while connections or a path can
specify relationships or an arrangement to create. These marks let
users show aspects of the intended result alongside the materials
it should build on, without first describing every visual or spatial
detail in words. Selected materials and prior results can in turn
supply what a brief expression leaves implicit. Work on contextual
sketch interpretation similarly shows how marks acquire meaning
in relation to the material being worked on [56]. Ink thus makes
graphical and spatial expression available alongside language as
part of the request.
Ink also lets users develop the content of a request as they work
through an idea. Annotations could develop the content on which
a request depended, while an earlier instruction could be revised or
applied to another material. The retained-ink findings (Section 5.2.2)
and the design and storytelling episodes (Section 6.2) show how
composing the work and composing a request can overlap. Building
on actionable annotations and iterative ink-based ideation [31, 36],
our study illustrates how this overlap can operate across acquiring,
examining, and organizing materials. The effort invested in an
annotation or sketch can therefore contribute both to developing
the user’s ideas and to expressing the assistance they seek, with
retained ink remaining available for further requests.
This value can coexist with complementary input for longer ex-
planations. The preference for retaining useful annotations and key-
words while supplementing instructions with speech (Section 5.2.2)
suggests a way to combine ink’s graphical and spatial expression
with spoken or typed detail, consistent with sketch-and-speech
interaction [11]. Ink is worth supporting because users can seek
assistance through the same handwriting and sketching with which
they formulate, inspect, and revise their ideas.
7.2
Supporting Continued Work in
Ink-Initiated Agent Interfaces
Connecting material acquisition, inquiry, and organization allows
agent assistance to participate in work whose next steps develop
through engagement with earlier results. Participants reused out-
puts to ask further questions, add changed circumstances, and

In Inkitiate, ink provides a way to re-enter this process at the ma-

the ongoing handling of materials, so that users can develop what

users read, organize, and develop their work. Participants’ prefer-

a response matters to both understanding it and working with it
afterward. Designing outputs for the canvas therefore means con-

available for further annotation or revision. A structure assembled

object even when the response did not maintain that local scope.
Fine-grained reference mechanisms [49] offer one route for con-
necting these marks to the relevant passages or regions.
Users also need to retain direction over how agent assistance
enters an evolving activity. When a new annotation draws on ex-
isting materials and prior results, its meaning depends on which
context the agent uses and which changes the user intends. Making
the interpreted task available for inspection and correction could
let users adjust that meaning as their work develops, following
research on interaction-augmented instructions and correctable AI
behavior [1, 39]. In Inkitiate, task inspection and clarification pro-
vide initial support for this involvement. The broader opportunity
is to keep users’ judgments about the current materials connected
to the agent’s next action, including when users revise their goals
or find that a result calls for a different direction.
This involvement can extend from guiding the next action to
reviewing the development of the work. U17’s expectation of re-
taining versions alongside modification intentions (Section 5.2.4)
suggests keeping changes and their purposes available together, so
users can compare outputs in relation to what they were trying to
achieve. For other applications built around reading, annotation, or
visual organization, the transferable direction is to let users seek
and guide assistance through the materials they are already devel-
oping. Ink-based interaction could then accompany the movement
between examining a result, revising an idea, and deciding what
assistance to seek next.
7.3
Limitations and Future Work
Study setting and real-world use. The study was limited to a single
session with 18 undergraduate and graduate students who had prior
experience with digital handwriting or visual canvas tools. How
these practices extend to other user populations and sustained ev-
eryday work remains to be explored. The evaluation was descriptive
rather than comparative, leaving the relative benefits of ink-based
interaction over alternative approaches open for further study. Lon-
gitudinal field deployments with a broader range of users could
explore how ink-based agent interaction fits into real-world work,
how personal ink vocabularies and continuing workflows develop,
and when users choose ink alongside other forms of interaction.
Interpretation and personalization. The expressive flexibility of
free-form ink also introduces uncertainty in how an agent interprets
a request. Rather than relying on predefined mappings between
input and action, Inkitiate depends on the model’s interpretation of
ink in context, which may diverge from the user’s intended meaning.
Advances in contextual ink understanding(e.g., online handwriting
recognition, and vision models) could strengthen the reliability of
this interaction. Because users differ in how they express ideas
and requests through ink, future work could also explore person-
alized learning from users’ corrections and repeated use to adapt
interpretation to their individual conventions and preferences.
8
Conclusion
This work explored how ink can enable users to seek and steer
agent assistance in ongoing knowledge work. A formative study
identified three connected activities and interaction expectations
that informed the design of Inkitiate. The system supports bringing
materials in, thinking with materials, and shaping the space, with
acquired materials and subsequent results retained in the same
workspace for further work. A user study with 18 participants found
favorable perceived usability and low workload, while highlighting
how retained ink and materials supported further inquiries and
revealing challenges with local references and output form. An ink-
query vocabulary characterized intention expression, while cases
illustrated ink’s potential for guiding creation, iterative revision,
and visual organization. These findings highlight ink’s potential
to make agent assistance part of how people think and work with
materials, drawing on the language, sketches, and spatial marks
through which they express and develop ideas.
