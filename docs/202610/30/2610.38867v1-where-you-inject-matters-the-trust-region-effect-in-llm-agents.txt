## ABSTRACT 

LLM agents are increasingly adopted in long-horizon tasks and interact with di-verse external environments. Despite their growing capabilities, these agents re-main vulnerable to real-world attacks such as indirect prompt injections, which manipulate agents through malicious instructions embedded in external environ-ments. While existing risk assessment work has primarily focused on the injection 

content , the effect of where an injection appears has not been systematically char-acterized. Studying injection placement requires agents to operate over longer-horizon tasks and interact with diverse environmental surfaces. Thus, we build three agentic testbeds spanning tool use, coding, and daily workflow domains with diverse injection surfaces. Holding the injection content fixed, we find that agent susceptibility varies across injection placements. We refer to this phenomenon as the trust region effect : agents implicitly assign different levels of trust to different regions of their environments. For example, an agent powered by GPT-5.4 follows an unsafe injection instruction placed in a repository documentation file (i.e., a 

README or TODO file) 2.6× more often on average than the same instruction placed in a code or data file. Building upon this observation, we develop learning-based attackers that generate injection content and select better injection placements. Our approach demonstrates that both where an injection is placed and what con-tent it contains can affect the strength of indirect prompt injection attacks. To the best of our knowledge, this work provides the first systematic characterization for the effectiveness of different injection placements, spanning both coarse-grained environmental surfaces and fine-grained positions. The uneven trust that agents implicitly place across their environments provides important insights for future attack and defense design. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

LLM agents (Yao et al., 2023; Anthropic, 2025; OpenClaw, 2026) have become increasingly capa-ble of performing complex tasks involving tool use, software engineering (Merrill et al., 2026), and everyday workflows such as email and message management. By interacting with external tools and environments, these agents can access and act upon additional information, substantially extending the capabilities of standalone LLMs. Despite this rapid progress in agentic capabilities, significant safety and security vulnerabilities remain. According to the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applica-tions (OWASP Foundation), prompt injection has remained the top-ranked security risk for LLM applications for two consecutive years (2025 and 2026), posing a major obstacle to the large-scale deployment of LLM agents in real-world organizations. Within prompt injection, indirect prompt in-jection is particularly concerning because unsafe instructions originate from external environments, such as documents, emails, or other tool-call results. In an indirect prompt injection attack, benign users neither provide nor even observe the unsafe instructions, yet the agent can still encounter the injection during task execution and be misled into following the attacker’s intended behavior. This threat model is therefore realistic for deployed agents and can pose substantial real-world risks.

This contrast motivates our study of injection place-ment and the trust region effect. To better understand the vulnerability of AI agents to prompt injection, prior work has made sub-stantial efforts to evaluate and improve prompt injection attacks. Existing benchmarks (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024) evaluate how different injection contents, including direct malicious instructions and sophisticated attack prompts, affect attack success rates. Other methods (Wen et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a;b; Syros et al., 2026) develop red-teaming techniques to optimize injec-tion content and construct more effective attacks. Also, several studies investigate indirect prompt injection in specific application domains; for example, Liao et al. (2025); Xu et al. (2024) focus on attacks against web agents. While injection content has been studied more extensively, the effects of different injection placements remain less understood. Prior work has examined placement only in restricted settings, such as positions within a webpage or a file (Liao et al., 2025; Hao et al., 2026), but it remains unclear how placement across heterogeneous environments affects attacks. From an attacker’s perspective, a natural goal is to identify the most vulnerable placement for an injection, since different placements may yield different attack effectiveness due to their distinct semantic roles and contextual characteristics. For example, in a coding task, instructions appearing in a TODO.md 

file may be treated as more trustworthy than those in a data file. Fig. 1 illustrates this effect: the same injected instruction is obeyed when it is placed in a README.md file but ignored when placed in a data file, even though the agent reads the injected content in both cases. To systematically study injection placement across heterogeneous environments, two challenges arise. First, studying placement across diverse environmental surfaces requires longer-horizon be-nign tasks in which agents naturally interact with multiple surfaces; otherwise, the space of possible injection surfaces is inherently limited. Second, a controllable platform is needed to support in-jecting instructions into coarse-grained environment surfaces and fine-grained positions within each surface. To address these challenges, we build 3 agentic testbeds spanning tool use, coding, and daily workflow domains, covering 36 distinct environmental surfaces and 24 risk categories. Our empirical study reveals several key findings. First, most failed indirect prompt injections are ignored rather than explicitly rejected. Second, with injection content fixed, agents exhibit different levels of susceptibility across environmental regions, revealing the trust region effect : agents appear to assign different levels of trust to different regions. Third, these trust patterns vary across agents. Fourth, even advanced models acting as red-teamers cannot reliably choose the most effective injection placements for attack. Building on the testbeds and findings, we further develop a learning-based 2Preprint. attacker that optimizes both injection content and placement. Our results show that incorporating placement optimization further improves attack effectiveness over content-only optimization. Our key contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We identify and systematically investigate a realistic yet underexplored dimension of indirect prompt injection attacks: injection placement. We characterize how different injection placements affect attack effectiveness across diverse agentic en-vironments and show the trust region effect. (2) We build three agentic testbeds spanning 3 different domains and show that the trust region effect consistently emerges across different agents. Our find-ings reveal uneven agent robustness to prompt injections across different environmental regions. (3) We develop a learning-based attacker that optimizes both injection content and placement. Our ap-proach demonstrates that incorporating the placement axis can further improve attack success over content-only optimization. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Indirect prompt injection benchmarks and attackers. In an indirect prompt injection (IPI) attack, an adversary plants adversarial instructions in the content an agent reads while fulfilling a benign user request, misleading the agent into pursuing the adversary’s goal (Greshake et al., 2023). Bench-marks such as AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024) study indirect prompt injection in relatively short-horizon tasks with limited exposure to diverse external environments. AgentLAB (Jiang et al., 2026) extends prompt injection to long-horizon agent interactions, and DecodingTrust-Agent (Chen et al., 2026b) considers multiple injection channels, such as prompts, skills, and tools. However, these benchmarks do not systematically characterize how agent susceptibility varies across environ-mental surfaces and injection positions. Another line trains the prompt injection attacker: AutoIn-ject (Chen et al., 2026a) and RL-Hammer (Wen et al., 2025) both use reinforcement learning to generate stronger injection content. Both optimize what to inject while leaving where to inject fixed, so the injection placement axis stays unexplored. 

The placement of the injection. Although most agent IPI evaluations still place payloads at man-ually specified locations, recent work has begun to recognize that injection location can affect the success of indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks. MUZZLE (Syros et al., 2026) uses a separate LLM to identify and rank high-salience UI elements as candidate injection vessels for web agents. POISE (Hao et al., 2026) embeds a position-aware trigger within a single skill file, and EIA (Liao et al., 2025) examined the different injection positions within a restricted webpage. These studies consider placement only within a specific environment or rely on LLM-based placement selection, leaving systematic variation in agent susceptibility across heterogeneous environmental surfaces and positions underexplored. In contrast, we conduct a systematic, quantitative study of an agent’s sen-sitivity to injections across different environment surfaces and positions. By holding the payload fixed and varying only its location, we isolate the effect of the injection surface from that of the injection content. This allows us to objectively characterize which environmental surfaces and po-sitions agents are more likely to trust and follow, without relying on an LLM to predict promising injection locations. 

## 3 METHODOLOGY 

In this section, we describe our framework for systematically studying the effect of injection place-ment in indirect prompt injection attacks. We first formalize the threat model and injection place-ment problem (§ 3.1). Then we introduce the construction of 3 agentic testbeds containing longer-horizon benign tasks and multiple environmental surfaces. Based on these testbeds, we define the in-jection placement space at both the coarse-grained surface level and the fine-grained within-surface position level, specifying how candidate injection surfaces are identified for each task (§ 3.2). Fi-nally, we introduce a learning-based attacker that optimizes injection content and placement in two stages (§ 3.3). 3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION AND THREAT MODEL 

Agentic task execution. We consider an LLM-based agent A that receives a benign user task t

and interacts with an external environment E through tool calls. During task execution, the agent produces a trajectory 

τ = ( o1, a 1, o 2, a 2, . . . , o K , a K ), (1) 3Preprint. where ok and ak denote the observation and action at step k, respectively. For each benign task t,we denote its associated environmental surfaces as S(t) = {s1, . . . , s m}, where each si represents a distinct surface from which the agent may obtain task-relevant information during execution. 

Indirect prompt injection. We consider an attacker who can modify the content of an environ-mental surface but cannot directly modify the user instruction, system prompt, or underlying model parameters. The attacker inserts an adversarial instruction into an environmental surface that the agent may encounter while completing the benign task. We characterize an injection as 

I = ( c, ℓ ), ℓ = ( s, p ), (2) where c denotes the injection content, ℓ denotes the injection placement or location, s ∈ S (t) denotes the environmental surface, and p denotes a fine-grained position within that surface. In our setting, the fine-grained positions p include the start, middle, and end of each injection surface. 3.2 MULTI -S URFACE AGENTIC TESTBEDS 

To systematically study injection placement, we construct 3 multi-surface agentic testbeds spanning the coding , tool use , and daily workflow domains. Across 3 testbeds, we cover 36 distinct envi-ronmental injection surfaces as shown in Fig. 2; the full per-domain surface lists are given in § B. Overall, the testbeds contain 30 benign tasks and 24 injection tasks; the full set of injection risk categories is listed in § C. 

Design principles. Our construction follows two main principles. First, surface coverage : each benign task should expose multiple heterogeneous environmental surfaces, allowing the same injec-tion content to be evaluated across different surfaces and positions. This naturally calls for longer-horizon tasks that require agents to traverse multiple environments. Second, injection delivery : a meaningful placement comparison requires the agent to perceive the injection; otherwise, attack failure may simply result from the injection never being observed. We take measures to ensure exposure, although some variability remains due to the inherent stochasticity of agent behavior. 

Task construction. We construct longer-horizon benign tasks by composing existing atomic tasks to maximize surface diversity. For the coding domain, we build upon Terminal-Bench-2 (Merrill et al., 2026) and construct 10 longer-horizon benign coding tasks. We first run an agent on all the Terminal-Bench tasks and record the files accessed during execution. We then prioritize task com-binations that maximize coverage over distinct file and surface types while avoiding dependency conflicts. Each composed task contains 2–5 Terminal-Bench subtasks, spanning different execu-tion horizons and progressively increasing the diversity of files and environmental surfaces. The selected subtasks are placed in separate subdirectories, and the composed task is packaged into a unified Docker environment. Each composed coding task instruction follows a three-part instruc-tion format: a shared project-inspection preamble, a directive to complete all included subtasks, and the original Terminal-Bench task instructions (§ A). We preserve the original wording whenever possible, modifying only paths and other environment-specific references required by the merged directory structure. This domain includes 7 injection risks, covering representative attacks such as file deletion and SQL injection. For the tool-use domain, we build on AgentDojo (Debenedetti et al., 2024) and construct 10 longer-horizon benign tasks by composing 6–8 atomic tasks drawn from all four domains in AgentDojo. We prioritize compositions that maximize environmental-surface coverage. Each instruction adds a natural introductory sentence and connects the original AgentDojo prompts into an ordered sequence while preserving their wording. The corresponding environment states are merged into a single initial state, with conflicts among constituent tasks re-solved as needed. This domain includes 12 injection risks, covering representative attacks such as unauthorized fund transfers and sensitive information exfiltration. For the workflow domain, we build on DecodingTrust-Agent (Chen et al., 2026b) and construct 10 benign daily-workflow tasks. Starting from existing tasks, we add task-relevant intermediate requirements that naturally require interaction across multiple application environments while preserving the original benign objective. We populate the corresponding application environments with the necessary seed information and ensure consistency of shared facts across applications. This domain includes 5 injection risks, cov-ering representative attacks such as phishing propagation and misinformation. 

Deterministic task and attack evaluation. We evaluate benign-task utility and attack success us-ing deterministic, script-based verifiers rather than LLM judges. For coding tasks, we combine the original Terminal-Bench test suites into a composite verifier and validate each composed task of-fline with the corresponding oracle solution.sh scripts. This design separates which surface contains the injection from where within that surface it appears. 3.3 PLACEMENT -A WARE ATTACKER 

Given that injection effectiveness can vary across placements, this further motivates us to develop an attacker that jointly exploits injection content and placement to improve attack effectiveness. For each benign task t, we define the candidate placement space as 

L(t) = {(s, p ) | s ∈ S (t), p ∈ P (s)}. (3) Our attacker Gθ takes the benign task t, malicious goal g, and the candidate placement space L(t)

as input, and ultimately produces a structured attack consisting of the injection content, injection surface, and within-surface position: 

(c, s, p ) ∼ Gθ (· | t, g, L(t)) . (4) We adopt a staged training strategy. We first warm up the attacker with supervised examples and optimize injection content under a non-adaptive placement prior. In the second stage, we initialize the attacker from the Stage-I checkpoint and enable placement optimization. Unlike Stage I, where the model-predicted placement is overridden by the task-specific prior πprior (t) before execution, Stage II directly executes the placement (s, p ) generated by the attacker without replacement. We continue optimizing the attacker with GRPO using the same composite reward defined in Eq. (7). Both injection content and placement now affect execution and reward, allowing the attacker to learn more effective surfaces and within-surface positions. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

In this section, we describe our experimental design (§ 4.1) and then investigate how injection place-ment affects attack effectiveness. We find that different injection placements yield different ASRs, and that placing an injection at the end of a surface is generally more effective (§ 4.2). We fur-ther show that different agents exhibit distinct trust patterns across environmental surfaces (§ 4.3), and the LLM-based placement selector does not reliably identify better injection placement than a frequency-based prior (§ 4.4). Finally, we present the results of our learning-based placement-aware attack and evaluate its transferability to a more robust model (§ 4.5). 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate injection placement across the three testbeds introduced in § 3.2. In total, they comprise 30 longer-horizon benign tasks and 24 injection tasks, spanning 36 distinct environmental injection surfaces, each with three within-surface positions: S TART , M IDDLE , and E ND . We evaluate eight 6Preprint. agents: GPT-5.6, GPT-5.4, GPT-5-mini, GPT-4o (Achiam et al., 2023), Gemini-3.1-Pro (Google DeepMind, 2026), Grok-4.6 (SpaceXAI, 2026), Opus5 (Anthropic, 2026a), and Sonnet5 (Anthropic, 2026b). In the coding and workflow testbeds, the Claude models run on the Claude Agent SDK (An-thropic, 2025) and all other models on the OpenAI Agents SDK (OpenAI, 2025); in the tool-use testbed, all agents run on AgentDojo’s native tool-calling pipeline. GPT-4o is evaluated only in the tool-use testbed. To isolate the effect of placement from injection content, all measurement experi-ments use an identical payload, such that only the placement ℓ = ( s, p ) varies across experimental conditions. Attack success and benign task utility are evaluated using deterministic, script-based verifiers rather than an LLM-based judge. Due to the inherent stochasticity of agent behavior, the exact surfaces encountered may still vary across executions. To further separate placement effec-tiveness from injection delivery, unless otherwise stated, we report conditional ASR , measured only among injections that are successfully delivered to and read by the agent: 

ASR( ℓ) = Pr( Attack Success | Injection Read , ℓ ).

4.2 HOW DOES INJECTION PLACEMENT AFFECT AGENT BEHAVIOR ?

Failed injections are predominantly ignored rather than refused. A low ASR could simply mean the agent never read the injection. To rule this out, we check whether the payload appears verbatim in the content the agent retrieved, separating delivery from compliance . Among delivered-but-failed injections, passive non-engagement dominates (Tb. 1): 81 .3% of GPT-4o failures are ignored versus 0.7% explicitly refused, and the more robust GPT-5.4 also ignores more often than it refuses ( 66 .8% vs. 33 .2% ). Unsuccessful injections therefore fail more often because the agent ignores the injected content than because a safety mechanism rejects it. Different agents exhibit distinct trust patterns across surfaces. The influence of injection place-ment is observed across different agents, but the most effective surfaces can vary across agents. As shown in Fig. 3, for the tool use domain: GPT-4o is most susceptible to injections on web, local-file, and review surfaces, whereas Gemini-3.1-Pro is more susceptible on drive-document and calendar surfaces. Code: repository documentation surfaces (README and TODO) are more effective than raw code and data files for GPT-5.4, Grok-4.6, and GPT-5-mini. Gemini-3.1-Pro behaves differ-ently: it opens these two files in under 5% of its runs, against 77 –88% for code and data files. Its documentation ASR is therefore estimated from very few delivered cells and is correspondingly unstable, though it remains high on both surfaces. Workflow: GitHub and Slack surfaces are es-pecially effective for Gemini-3.1-Pro, while Slack is also more effective for Grok-4.6 and GPT-5.4. Together, these results show that the trust region effect holds across agents, while the induced trust structure over environmental surfaces is agent-dependent. As a reference point, § D.7 summarizes the overall ASR of the eight agents we evaluate, which varies substantially across models. 4.4 CAN LLM S IDENTIFY EFFECTIVE INJECTION PLACEMENTS ?

LLM-based placement selection does not reliably outperform a simple frequency prior. Given that placement affects attack success, we next ask whether an LLM-based attacker can identify effective placements in advance. As shown in Fig. 8 and § D.6, we give each selection strategy a budget of B blind attempts over distinct (s, p ) placements while holding the injection content fixed (§ D.6). A prompted GPT-5.4 placement selector does not consistently outperform a simple frequency prior over surfaces: the prior performs better on tool use ( 0.53 vs. 0.42 at B=1 ), GPT-5.4 performs better on workflows at some budgets, and the two are comparable on coding. Overall, the LLM-based placement selector does not reliably rank effective injection placements. 4.5 DOES PLACEMENT -A WARE OPTIMIZATION IMPROVE ATTACKS ?

Training setup. We train each attacker using a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct policy against a GPT-4o vic-tim. We use data from our constructed tool-use domain testbed, which contains 10 longer-horizon benign tasks, 12 injection risks, and 13 environment surfaces. We consider two data splits designed to evaluate different forms of generalization. The leave-task split measures transfer to unseen be-nign tasks, with 84 contexts for training, 12 for validation, and 24 for testing. The leave-risk split measures transfer to unseen attack risks, with 60 contexts for training, 30 for validation, and 30 for testing. Each policy is warm-started with supervised fine-tuning and then optimized with GRPO, as described in § 3.3. During GRPO, we use 24 generations per prompt, 96 rollouts per optimizer step, a learning rate of 10 −5, and 3 training epochs. We additionally sweep the dense-judge weight 

j ∈ { 0, 0.3, 0.5, 1}, where j=0 corresponds to using only the verifier-based success reward. Tb. 4 presents four strategies designed to study content and placement optimization. They differ in their training procedure and how injection placement is selected at test time.

## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we systematically characterize the effectiveness of different injection placements, span-ning both coarse-grained environmental surfaces and fine-grained within-surface positions across three multi-surface agentic testbeds. We find that agent susceptibility varies substantially across environmental surfaces, and that end-of-surface injections are often more effective. These patterns are model- and agent-dependent, while delivered but unsuccessful injections are more often ignored than explicitly refused. We further develop a staged attacker that optimizes injection content and placement, improving attack effectiveness. Our findings establish injection placement as an impor-tant dimension of agent security and motivate defenses that account for injection placement when limiting the influence of untrusted content. We discuss the limitations of this work in § E. 

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work studies realistic security risks posed by indirect prompt injection attacks against LLM agents. All experiments are conducted in controlled, sandboxed container environments, with no real users or third-party systems involved. While research on indirect prompt injection has potential dual-use risks, we mitigate these risks through responsible disclosure of research artifacts. Our goal is to better understand which environmental surfaces agents are more likely to trust, thereby informing the development of more effective defenses.

D.2 BENIGN -T ASK READ ORDER 

For some benign tasks, the agent typically reads the injectable surfaces in a fixed order, while each evaluation injects into only one surface at a time. This raises a potential confound: differences in ASR across surfaces may partly arise from when the injected surface is encountered during task execution rather than from the surface itself. To isolate the effect of read order, we select one workflow task with four injectable surfaces and enumerate all 4! = 24 possible read-order permutations. For each permutation, we generate a naturalized task instruction that preserves the original task semantics while changing only the order in which the four environment surfaces are read. We evaluate each permutation across 4 injected surfaces, 5 injection risks, and 3 within-surface positions, yielding 24 × 4 × 5 × 3 = 1 ,440 runs in total. Under this construction, each surface appears exactly 6 times at each read depth. We use 

d1–d4 to denote the read depth of the injected surface: d1 means the injected surface is read first among the four surfaces, while d4 means it is read last. We evaluate GPT-5.4 with the OpenAI Agents SDK. Conditional ASR varies only modestly across read depths, with overlapping 95% confidence intervals (Tb. 14). At the same time, the relative surface-level pattern remains: Slack and GitHub have higher conditional ASR than Confluence and Jira (Tb. 15). ## E LIMITATIONS 

Our study has several limitations. First, the trust region effect is identified empirically through sys-tematic differences in agents’ susceptibility across environmental regions, rather than through direct measurement of an agent’s internal notion of “trust.” Explaining why agents exhibit different levels of trust across regions would require deeper interpretability analyses of their internal representa-tions and decision processes. We therefore view the trust region effect as an empirical behavioral phenomenon. Second, our evaluation is conducted on 3 human-verified testbeds spanning tool-use, coding, and workflow settings. While these environments are designed to be reliable and functionally validated, they do not cover the full diversity of real-world agent environments. Scaling to substantially more environments would require careful validation of environment functionality, task correctness, injec-tion delivery, and surface semantics, introducing additional construction and verification complexity. Third, this work focuses primarily on indirect prompt injection. The trust region effect may also ex-tend to other agent security risks. Systematically investigating whether similar trust-region patterns emerge under these broader threat models is beyond the scope of this work and remains an important direction for future research. 21
