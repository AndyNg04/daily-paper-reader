Tom VAN ENGERS aa Informatics Institute, University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands

Abstract. To be socially sustainable, Agentic AI systems require normative designs that align responsibilities with the ability to fulfil them. Identifying incongruences such as unsatisfiable expectations or privilege escalation is crucial in avoiding nor-mative pitfalls. In this study, we discuss design dimensions suitable for describing multi-agent systems in light of this norm-ability alignment requirement. We pro-pose a formal framework centred on process-oriented modelling and institutional power for the analysis of a given normatively relevant process, discuss strategies for repairing it, and elaborate on how the symmetrical relation between operational process and institutional state affects the lifecycle of normative positions. 

Keywords. Institutional Power, Ability, Process Models, Normative Positions 

1. Introduction 

Social systems come with social expectations and responsibilities ascribed to the partic-ipating actors. However, if we do not align those with the concrete ability to perform the actions required by those expectations, we enter into an incongruent state of affairs that might render somebody else’s rights vacuous – or better, their exercise frustrated – even without incurring any explicit norm-violation. For instance, if an individual is legally granted freedom of movement in a building while barriers prevent them from ever leav-ing the facility, their right is formally acknowledged yet materially nullified. Previous work, such as [1], started investigating the interaction between deontic and potestative concepts, showing how an instrumental interpretation of the latter towards the former can reveal gaps in institutional design, while distinguishing the two concepts as belonging to two distinct ontological categories. In the context of artificial multi-agent systems, including those currently described as Agentic AI, such incongruence may hinder their controllability. This issue can become apparent even in localised settings, as suggested by the diverging industrial approaches to software development-oriented agentic harnesses such as Claude Code [2], Codex [3], or Pi [4]. As highlighted by recent episodes like the OpenAI incident involving the Hugging

Conversely, assigning tasks to harnessed agents that do not have access to the required operational machinery ( e.g. , Model Context Protocol (MCP) connections) could result in ineffective orchestration at best, and mission-critical failure at worst. In the interest of designing systems that outline a clear liability structure and support socially sustainable governance of agentic artificial intelligence, it is crucial that we avoid such normative pitfalls. Although historically many computational normative frameworks have remained primarily deontic-centric , several explicitly represent institutional power alongside obli-gations and permissions, including [7,8,9,10]. Especially in computational settings, op-erational ability becomes a precondition for sound attribution of institutional power and deontic positions alike; that is, powers and duties become highly problematic in a world where their corresponding actions cannot be performed [11]. In this context, we observe that tracing institutional side-effects remains difficult as long as the operational layer lacks an adequate process model-oriented execution semantics. The main function of this formalism would be to capture action-dependence (see e.g. [12]) in workflows. More-over, we argue for treating power as an independent normative category in its institu-tional, constitutively-ascribed form [13,14] and “instrumental” but ontologically distinct interpretation i.e. , to maintain or update the state of normative systems [1]. Failing to do so hinders the ability to identify some undesirable configurations such as lingering powers and unsatisfiable obligations (see section 4). Such misalignments may give rise to disputes requiring judicial intervention ex-post , motivating their identification and pre-vention at design time. Throughout this paper, we use the following running example: an owner O consigns goods to a broker B, contractually empowered to sell them on O’s behalf; O may termi-nate the consignment, and a creditor C of B may, upon B’s insolvency, seize the goods .The scenario is loosely inspired by a real consignment dispute [15] over a Star Wars Lego collection entrusted to a franchise that later changed hands, with the consignor alleging that neither payment nor the goods were returned. It is a simplification to illustrate our model, as we assume the consigned goods fall into B’s estate on insolvency, and that 

B’s mandate to sell lapses on insolvency. It is not within our scope to model real con-signment law here. The consigned goods represent a contested resource, precondition-ing normative actions and enabling operational continuation of the process in alternative directions. To address the aforementioned issues, in this paper we expand on our previous work on the operational interpretation of power [16] and present the following contributions: 

• We propose a model that formalises the relation between the operational and in-stitutional layers in terms of process execution, relating normative positions to agentic abilities in said process (section 3). 

• Based on the proposed model, we define desirable properties of processes within normative systems that depend on both structural control-flow features and policy specification, namely, operational groundedness , formal institutional legitimacy ,and power-ability alignment (section 4). 

• We demonstrate that the proposed model can capture incongruent institutional states that emerge from the relation between the two layers. 2. Background 

Our contribution lies at the intersection of the research traditions normative multi-agent systems and process-modelling that have partially developed separately. We review re-lated work in both traditions and present bridges between them. 

Institutional Power in Multi-Agent Systems. We distinguish operational power from in-stitutional power and refer to the former as (operational) ability in what follows, reserv-ing power for institutional power. Ability is what an agent can physically bring about in its environment [16]. Institutional power is the capacity, conferred by a social or legal framework, to change institutional facts by acting [14]. Power resides on the potestative 

corner of Hohfeld’s analysis of normative systems (power-liability), distinct from the 

deontic corner (duty-right) [17]. In this sense, power is constitutively connected to the performance of an act producing an institutional effect in a context. A power in itself is conferred constitutively by means of another power [14,13,18]. Institutional power has been formalised in detail, both in logical and legal terms (see e.g. , [19,20,21]). However, actively developed computational normative multi-agent frameworks remain deontic-centric , see e.g. , NPL [22] and its extensions [23], leaving potestative power underserved. Moreover, while domain-specific languages (DSLs) for norm specification such as In-stAL [8] and eFLINT [24,10] explicitly support the synchronisation of the operational and institutional layers ( i.e. , via counts-as events and the sync primitive), the relation between deontic positions and operationalised powers remains implicit instead of being modelled independently. 

Normative Monitoring with Process Models. The lifecycle of Business Process Man-agement (BPM) in organisational settings generally applies model-driven approaches to process design and implementation [25]. Petri nets have traditionally been utilised for the design and structural analysis of control flow in processes, mainly thanks to their intu-itive mapping to distributed processes, and to their soundness properties [26,27]. The line extends to interactions between organisations in inter-organizational workflow nets [28], operating-guideline “contracts” characterising correct partners [29,30], and conformance of observed behaviour to a model [31]. Crucially, extracting Petri nets with process min-ing techniques proved effective for compliance checking by evaluating event logs (i.e. ,full execution traces) against normatively qualified patterns [32]. Finally, methodological contributions relating normative enforcement and monitoring to process models, espe-cially in business settings in the form of Business Process Modelling Notation (BPMN), have outlined a formal framing for process-oriented governance [33,34]. 

3. Theoretical Framework 

In this section we introduce the definitions with which we characterise multi-agent pro-cess execution at the operational and institutional level. 

3.1. Operational layer 

For modelling the occurrence of events at the operational ( i.e. , physical) level of a sys-tem which might cause institutional effects, we will rely on Petri net notation. Through-out this and the following sections we will write agents as capital letters ( e.g. , O, B,C), action-types (net transitions) in sans serif ( e.g. , sell ), and places in italics ( e.g. , goods ). Definition 1 (Petri net) . A Petri net N = ( P, T, F,W ) is a tuple with P a finite set of places p ∈ P; T a finite set of transitions t ∈ T ; F ⊆ (P × T ) ∪ (T × P) a flow relation consisting of input arcs (p,t) ∈ F and output arcs (t, p) ∈ F where T and P are disjoint; and W : F → N a weight function which associates weights to arcs.

t in M produces a new marking M′ such that M′(p) = M(p) −W (p,t) + W (t, p), for all

Definition 2 (Agent mapping) . Given a set of agents A = {a1, . . . , an} participating in a Petri net N = ( P, T, F,W ), an agent mapping mA : T → 2A assigns to each transition t the set m A(t) of agents capable of firing t. Transitions are read as actions by agents, and a firing of t is performed by some a ∈ mA(t).

Based on this, we define ability as the immediate, contextual ability to act, local to t

at the current marking. 

Definition 3 (Ability) . Given a marking M, an agent a and a transition t, a can bring about t at M, written can M (a,t), iff a ∈ mA(t) and t is enabled at M.

We represent normative positions (both deontic and potestative ) by attaching them to specific agents in a declarative fashion. A. Marino et al. / Aligning Institutional Power and Operational Ability 

Definition 5 (Position) . A position is a normative predicate attached to an agent and a transition; we use power positions pow (a,t) and duty positions duty (a,t) for a ∈ A, t ∈ T , and write P for the set of positions. An institutional state is a global finite set I ⊆ P of (currently active) positions. We associate each active position with the set of operational transitions it covers by a function L : I → 2T . For φ (a,t) ∈ I, where φ ∈{pow , duty }, L (φ (a,t)) ⊆ T specifies the transitions covered by that position. We write Lpow and L duty for the restrictions of L to active power and duty positions, respectively. 

Note that the transitions covered by a position act in this framework as alternative ways to exercise a power or fulfil the duty. In the running example, each position covers its named transition: L(φ (a,t)) = {t} for every active position φ (a,t). More importantly, in the proposed notation positions do not aim to express the consequences associated to the performance of actions associated to them; they act only as a first representational interface. We relate the operational and institutional layers through additional predicates. The predicate exec (a,t) represents the action-execution event raised when transition t is fired (executed) by agent a. For normative positions f , the predicates act ( f ) and deact ( f )

represent the activation and deactivation events of f in the institutional state space. We base our potestative operationalisation on reactive rules that synchronise operational ex-ecution with the activation and deactivation of normative positions in a traceable way. 

Definition 6 (Reactive rule) . A reactive rule is a triple r = ( t, ℓ, f ) with t ∈ T ∪ {⊥} a

trigger , ℓ ∈ { act , deact }, and f ∈ P. When t ∈ T occurs, the rule fires reactively : the ac-tion event exec (a,t) automatically triggers act ( f ) (if ℓ = act ) or deact ( f ) (if ℓ = deact ). A rule with no trigger (t = ⊥) is a fact : it has no antecedent and fixes the initial insti-tutional state. Initial rules only activate , as deactivating not-yet-established positions is vacuous. 

A policy is here defined as a finite set of reactive rules meant to update the insti-tutional state on the basis of action events. For simplicity, we assume a global institu-tional state and a conflict-resolved aggregate policy Π. Let Act (t) = { f : (t, act , f ) ∈ Π}

and Deact (t) = { f : (t, deact , f ) ∈ Π}. Firing t updates I by applying every lifecycle event triggered by exec (a,t), i.e. , I ← upd (I,t) where upd (I,t) = ( I \ Deact (t)) ∪ Act (t).The initial institutional state I 0 is Act (⊥). This formalisation of power is constitutive 

(after [13,14]): executing t brings about the institutional lifecycle events its policy pre-scribes, so power is conferred within Π in synchronisation with physical actions, not just defined operationally. Here, the prescribed lifecycle updates occur irrespective of the acting agent’s empowerment; power is used to assess formal institutional legitimacy (see Def. 9). The model does not separately determine whether an unempowered act produces its intended institutional effect. Firing t ∈ T is institutionally legitimate for an agent a iff an active power held by a covers t, that is, ∃u ∈ T : pow (a, u) ∈ I ∧ t ∈ Lpow (pow (a, u)) .The policy Π of the running example is given in Table 1. Most institutionally relevant actions here are one-off : instruct , bankrupt and seize each consume a token that is never replaced, so they cannot recur, and a deact retires the power as it is spent.

4. Power-Ability Alignment 

Based on the previously defined operational and institutional layers, we propose desirable properties of a governed net building incrementally from a single configuration (M, I) ∈

RC (G ).

Definition 8 (Operational Groundedness) . (M, I) is operationally grounded iff every ac-tive position covers at least one transition that is realisable and assigned to its holder. Formally, for every a ∈ A, u ∈ T , and φ ∈ { pow , duty }, φ (a, u) ∈ I ⇒ ∃ t ∈ Lφ (φ (a, u)) :

a ∈ mA(t) ∧ t is realisable at M. 

A position φ (a, u) ∈ I is ungrounded iff no transition in Lφ (φ (a, u)) is both assigned to a and realisable at M. An ungrounded power has no operationally realisable means of exercise available to its holder, while an ungrounded duty has no operationally realisable means of discharge available to its holder. 

Definition 9 (Formal Institutional Legitimacy) . (M, I) is formally institutionally legit-imate iff every agent’s immediate ability is covered by an active power held by that agent. Formally, for every a ∈ A and t ∈ T , can M (a,t) ⇒ ∃ u ∈ T : pow (a, u) ∈ I ∧ t ∈

Lpow (pow (a, u)) .

A pair (a,t) is an illegitimate ability iff can M (a,t) holds but no active power held by 

a covers t. We say that (M, I) is aligned iff it is both legitimate and grounded. Legitimacy requires current abilities to be backed by power, while groundedness requires active po-sitions to remain operationally realisable. A duty or power should not outlive the possi-bility of performing its associated action. We lift these properties to characterise the net as follows: a governed run is legitimate, grounded, or aligned iff its final configuration is, and a governed net G is iff every (M, I) ∈ RC (G ) is. The example’s governed reachability graph can now be classified against these con-ditions as presented in Fig. 1b. Firing sell consumes the goods token so that sell is dead while pow (B, sell ) still stands as an ungrounded power and can be deactivated only by an institutional act ( terminate or bankrupt ). Going bankrupt before selling revokes the institutional pow (B, sell ) but does not touch B’s ability to act. bankrupt leaves the man-date token active in place (as only terminate consumes it) and the goods remain, so once 

B is instructed the marking still enables sell . That residue is an illegitimate ability (a le-gitimacy failure), legible only because ability depends on the marking while authority is read off I. Conflating ability with institutional power would obscure this distinction. Should C seize once B has been instruct ed (the seize branch of Fig. 1b), the goods are gone yet duty (B, sell ) stands as an ungrounded duty .Crucially, the concurrency expressed by the process model ( i.e. , instruct , bankrupt

Our framework makes this dependence explicit through governed reachabil-ity; for bounded operational nets and finitely many normative positions, the resulting state space is finite. Moreover, a deontic framing alone cannot capture the different kinds of misalignment presented here. Keeping the operational and institutional semantics dis-joint over the process, with normative positions the marking is free to contradict, exposes incongruent configurations such as positions rendered vacuous together with the design defects that caused them, present before any agent misbehaves. 4.1. Repairing a Misaligned Normative Design 

The institutional layer never disables an operational transition. In the language of super-visory control [36], the policy is a monitor over uncontrollable events: its only lever is the lifecycle timing of positions ( act /deact ), not the occurrence of actions. This determines what kind of defects each layer can repair. 

Definition 10 (Repair) . A repair is a function of G = ( N, mA, Π, M0) yielding G ′ by producing either an institutional edit Π 7 → Π′ (inserting or removing reactive rules) that leaves (N, mA) fixed, or an operational edit (N, mA) 7 → (N′, m′

In our framework, repair is admissible only if it is a reduction , which removes unsup-ported abilities or retires ungrounded positions. Expanding repairs require an indepen-dent justification for the additional authority or operational behaviour and are outside the present scope. Activating a power covering t for agent a to satisfy legitimacy authorises an ability previously unsupported by power, while making a covered transition realisable for its holder to satisfy groundedness expands the available operational behaviour. With-out independent justification, either approach risks accommodating a potential design defect rather than correcting its underlying cause.

While alignment by itself can help identify degenerate normative designs, it should not be considered as the only repair target ( e.g. , the empty net is vacuously aligned). 5. Discussion and Future Work 

The main goal of our proposed framework is to support the analysis and specification of normative designs for traceable and controllable computational systems. A process-centred modelling approach to the representation of the operational layer in normative scenarios, in the style of [37], has been only partially explored in previous work. Gov-ernatori and Rotolo [33] present a formal approach to deontic policy specification on Business Process Modelling Notation (BPMN), but do not model its relation to potesta-tive power i.e. , power as a stateful object bound to operational transitions. Ramezani et al. [32] also leverage process modelling formalism in combination with event log analy-sis to introduce trace-based compliance checking in a purely deontic sense; as such, their A. Marino et al. / Aligning Institutional Power and Operational Ability 

contribution only tackles ex-post analysis of historical execution. In contrast, our solution specifically targets strategies for evaluating a system before or during its deployment. InstAL [8] explicitly models institutional power and binds it to an event-based transi-tion system. Similarly, contract-oriented frameworks such as Symboleo [38] do repre-sent potestative positions and their institutional effects. However, these frameworks lack a process-centred representation that relates institutional powers to independently mod-elled operational abilities and characterises their alignment throughout execution over consumable resources. Mapping the normative lifecycle to these operational primitives allows us to expand on the traditional separation of [7] by grounding institutional posi-tions onto a process state space. In this context, it is important to remark that our defi-nition of operational groundedness (see Def. 8) only expresses possible future exercise, not guaranteed fulfilment or an agent’s ability to ensure it. Stochastic Petri-net semantics [39] could support quantitative analysis of the likelihood or timing of future exercise. However, relating such measures to agentive reliability as explored in [16] remains future work. Finally, none of the aforementioned contributions addresses the characterisation of alignment between operational and institutional layers. The present work offers a math-ematically grounded framing for the desirable and undesirable normative states reach-able within a given design, and for identifying and tracing misalignments and reasoning about repairs. Developing efficient verification procedures and synthesising repairs that preserve the intended process outcomes remain directions for future work. 

6. Conclusion 

In this study, we present a formal framework apt to represent operational and institutional states to aid normative system design. We reduce operational ability to process mod-elling primitives in order to leverage their concurrent and traceable execution semantics. Based on this stateful representation of the operational layer, we discuss its relation to the lifecycle of normative positions at the institutional level and present desirable prop-erties that characterise design-level alignment between the two. Then, taking inspiration from a reported consignment dispute, we show through a stylised scenario how such a process-oriented representation can support the ex-ante detection of undesirable norma-tive configurations emerging from the system design. The framework distinguishes posi-tions whose exercise has become operationally impossible from enabled actions unsup-ported by institutional power, making both kinds of misalignment explicit over reachable configurations. We position our solution against the related work in computational set-tings, by observing how no previous contribution studies the grounding of institutional state onto resource-constrained process execution. Finally, we discuss future directions leading to the automated detection and eventual repair of misaligned configurations in normative designs.
