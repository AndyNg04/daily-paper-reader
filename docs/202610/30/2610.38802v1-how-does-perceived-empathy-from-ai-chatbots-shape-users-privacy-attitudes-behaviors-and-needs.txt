# How Does Perceived Empathy From AI Chatbots Shape Users’ Privacy Attitudes, Behaviors, and Needs? 

## JIAXUN CAO, Duke University, USA

Prior research suggests that some dimensions of AI empathy may increase users’ disclosure to chatbots, posing growing privacy risks as chatbots become more capable of empathic interaction. Yet this work often conceptualizes AI empathy narrowly, leaving users’ own definitions and broader privacy experiences underexplored. We interviewed 21 AI chatbot users about what AI empathy meant to them and how it shaped their privacy attitudes, behaviors, and needs. Participants characterized empathy through five components—understanding, resonating, caring, expressing, and helping. Most participants viewed resonating and caring as essential components of human empathy but absent from AI empathy. Yet AI’s contextual, in-depth understanding and helpfulness still encouraged highly sensitive disclosure. This highlights that AI empathy can endanger users’ privacy without necessarily making AI seem human. Participants also desired varied forms and levels of empathy, each associated with distinct privacy needs. We offer actionable recommendations for designing privacy-enhancing mechanisms for empathic AI interactions. 1 Introduction 

Generative AI (GenAI) chatbots 1 have rapidly expanded their capabilities, including their ability to produce responses that users perceive as highly empathic. A growing body of research suggests that users may experience AI chatbot responses as empathic—sometimes even more so than human-authored responses [3, 28, 72, 100, 103]. Empathy is widely regarded as beneficial across domains such as healthcare [26, 33, 34, 58, 97], emotional support [14, 38, 59, 97, 109], and customer experience [15, 16, 19, 44, 54, 57]. In Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), empathy has also long been considered a desirable quality and design goal for improving user engagement [42, 55]. However, the expanding empathic capabilities of AI chatbots have introduced safety and ethical risks 2. For example, AI sycophancy—a key empathic cue in

Widespread concern has also emerged about users’ inability to distinguish AI from humans, particularly among vulnerable users who turn to AI for companionship [51]. While the safety and ethical implications of AI empathy have received growing attention, its privacy implications remain underexplored. Two adjacent lines of research offer relevant, though indirect, evidence that AI empathy might pose privacy risks to users with its emotional capacities and humanlike-ness. First, studies have found that users disclose more when interacting with chatbots that exhibit greater emotional expressiveness [66, 67, 73]. Second, more recent research has shown that AI chatbots’ anthropomorphic cues and perceived human-likeness can encourage more sensitive self-disclosure [119, 120]. However, emotional capacity and human-likeness do not fully capture empathy, which is a complex, multidimensional construct. Prior research distinguishes cognitive empathy (i.e., understanding another person’s thoughts or emotions) from affective empathy (i.e., sharing those emotions), and has proposed further dimensions [41, 42]. Lay conceptions of empathy may be even broader and more nuanced than these academic constructs [47]. Yet existing work has neither identified which dimensions users associate with AI empathy nor systematically examined how these dimensions shape their privacy experiences. Distinguishing these dimensions is important because they may shape users’ privacy perceptions and behaviors through different mechanisms, potentially creating distinct privacy risks that require targeted protections. The privacy implications of AI empathy may also extend beyond the level of self-disclosure to users’ privacy attitudes, risk perceptions, protective behaviors, and needs for privacy support. We therefore aim to move beyond examining AI empathy solely in terms of emotional capacity or human-likeness. Specifically, we systematically investigate how users conceptualize AI empathy in their own words, how its distinct dimensions shape their privacy experiences, and what privacy protections they need across different forms and levels of empathic interaction. Toward this end, our work asks three research questions (RQs): 

• RQ1 : How do users conceptualize and perceive empathy in interactions with AI chatbots, and how, if at all, do they distinguish AI empathy from human empathy? 

• RQ2 : What are users’ privacy perceptions and behaviors when perceiving different dimensions of empathy from AI chatbots? 

• RQ3 : What are users’ expectations toward privacy-enhancing empathic AI interactions? To answer these questions, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 21 U.S.-based AI chatbot users to examine their conceptualizations of AI empathy and their privacy perceptions, behaviors, and needs in interactions they perceived as empathic. Participants primarily characterized empathy through five components—understanding, resonating, caring, expressing, and helping. Although participants varied in the components they emphasized or omitted, most distinguished AI empathy from human empathy and did not believe that AI could emotionally resonate with or care about them as humans do ( RQ1 ). Yet participants reported that AI chatbots’ contextual, in-depth understanding and helpfulness still led them to disclose more sensitive information than they otherwise would have shared. These findings highlight that AI empathy can pose privacy risks without necessarily making AI seem human ( RQ2 ). In response to these risks, participants called for greater control over when and how AI chatbots express empathy, alongside safer data practices for empathic AI interactions ( RQ3 ). Grounded in these findings, we discuss design opportunities, recommend data practices, and identify directions for future research to support context-aware, privacy-enhancing empathic AI interactions. Our work makes three contributions to the HCI community: 1) an empirically grounded, user-centered charac-terization of AI empathy across five components; 2) empirical insights into how these components shape users’

2 Related Work 

Cross-disciplinary conceptions of empathy. Studying the impact of empathy on users’ attitudes and behaviors requires an understanding of how empathy is defined. Multiple disciplines, including psychology, neuroscience, sociology, philosophy, and others, debate about the definition of empathy [2, 5, 9–11, 23, 24, 32, 42, 46, 89, 104]. These debates are not fully resolved, but there is nonetheless an emerging consensus that empathy entails some combination of understanding, feeling, and sharing another’s world while maintaining self-other differentiation [32]. Empathy is often discussed in research as a multi-dimensional umbrella covering three main components: cognitive, affective, and motivational empathy [41, 42]. Cognitive empathy refers to the recognition and understanding of another person’s thoughts or emotional states [16, 24, 29, 37, 63, 68]. Affective empathy refers to the sharing or experiencing of another’s emotions, while generally maintaining self-other differentiation [8, 103]. Motivational empathy [40, 107] involves feelings of concern for another, accompanied by a genuine motivation to take actions [11, 90]. At the same time, lay people have been shown to use the term empathy in a much more diverse way than academic constructs suggest [9, 47]. Hall et al. [47], replicated by a 2025 study [9], found that participants varied widely in how they endorsed items drawn from scales based on academic definitions of empathy. This suggests it is important to first understand how a given population defines empathy when interpreting their responses to perceived empathy. Accordingly, we began by asking participants how they define empathy in their day-to-day lives, and then how they define empathy specifically in the context of interacting with AI. 

Societal-wide benefits of empathy. The growing use of empathic capabilities in AI chatbots is grounded in extensive evidence that empathy is beneficial across many critical domains, including physiological and psychological health care, emotional and social support, user and customer experience, and the promotion of prosocial behavior. In mental health care, empathy is associated with stronger therapeutic alliance and better treatment outcomes [26, 33, 34, 36, 50, 58, 84, 97, 111–113]. In contexts where people seek emotional and social support, empathy provides active listening [14, 38, 59, 97, 109], perspective taking [24, 25], and non-judgmental behavior [4, 49, 82]. Consequently, empathy is a desirable quality in customer service and user experience design, as evidenced by higher satisfaction scores when users perceive interactions as empathic [15, 16, 19, 44, 54, 57]. In addition, extensive research identifies empathy as a psychological mechanism that promotes prosocial behavior at scale [16, 22, 30, 31, 53, 68, 86, 91, 102, 105, 108, 118]. Taken together, existing research shows that empathy can produce societal-level benefits, making it a valuable quality for user-facing AI systems to demonstrate. 

Users’ perceptions of empathic capabilities in AI chatbots. Users often perceive empathy from AI chatbots through human-like communication that leverages three types of cues: (1) identity, (2) verbal, and (3) nonverbal cues. 

Identity cues include information such as the chatbot’s avatar, name, and gender [6, 21, 57]. Verbal cues refer to the use of words and phrases [7, 57, 93, 99], e.g., acknowledging users’ feelings. Nonverbal cues refer to non-linguistic behaviors in communication, such as chatbots’ use of emoticons [27]. These three types of cues have been documented in prior work to influence users’ perceptions of chatbots’ empathic capabilities, thereby shaping interaction dynamics [68, 97, 99]. For example, many users have noted ChatGPT’s frequent use of emojis, leading to increased perceptions of the chatbot’s human-like nature [85].

For example, Ayers et al. [3] compared health care professionals’ ratings of ChatGPT-generated responses to patients’ questions with those of human physicians and found that ChatGPT responses were preferred in 78.6% of cases and had responses participants rated as empathetic or very empathetic almost 10 times more than those provided by humans. At the same time, sometimes AI chatbots also fail to provide desirable empathy. For example, Chen et al. [17] used ChatGPT to simulate role-play scenarios between psychiatrists and patients and noted that ChatGPT sometimes forgot initial instructions and often produced repetitive empathic phrases. Similarly, other researchers have also observed repetitive patterns in AI chatbots’ empathic expressions [117, 121]. Schaich Borg and Read [11] suggest that users might prefer chatbots’ empathic responses in some contexts but find those same responses “annoying, eerie, untrustworthy, or unethical,” in other contexts, calling for more research into users’ preferences for empathic qualities in AI chatbots. 

Ethical and safety risks of AI empathy. The surge in empathic AI chatbots, coupled with their expanding empathic capabilities, raises pressing risks, notably the potential for deception and manipulation [1, 23, 51, 101, 106, 110, 116]. In illustration, evidence suggests that one tactic for showing empathy and thus maximizing engagement is to continually agree with users, a phenomenon known as AI sycophancy [18]. This tendency has resulted in negative consequences. Specifically, OpenAI acknowledged in a blog post in April 2025 that an updated version of GPT-4o had begun “validating doubts, fueling anger, urging impulsive actions, or reinforcing negative emotions in ways that were not intended” [88]. AI sycophancy can also contribute to user delusion [51, 62, 92, 115]. McCoy et al. tested 38 major AI models and found that GPT-4o affirmed claims indicative of possible psychosis 68% of the time [51]. Even users without psychosis or underlying mental health conditions can get addicted to such constant validation, as researchers suggest that social validation—a key strategy for signaling empathy—is linked to addiction and emotional dependency [61]. Consequently, some experts worry about users seeking emotional support from AI chatbots, noting that such blind validation without constructive input can narrow users’ worldviews and create a new type of echo chamber [80, 81, 92, 114]. Moreover, another ethical question arises regarding whether and under what conditions users can distinguish AI-simulated empathy from genuine human empathy [23, 101]. As argued by [101], if AI does not experience empathy internally, the deployment of such inauthentic empathy—systems that appear to care but do not, in reality—may be considered deception. Researchers have also called for further work on users’ preferences regarding whether AI chatbots must internally share or experience emotions to deliver desirable empathy [11, 56]. 

Lack of privacy research regarding empathic AI. While ethical and safety concerns dominate media and scholarly discourse on empathic AI interactions, the privacy implications remain underexplored. Prior work has primarily focused on investigating the level of self-disclosure when users interact with chatbots equipped with some human-like features [4, 52, 64–67, 73, 74, 76, 97]. Specifically, research has observed higher levels of self-disclosure when users interact with more emotionally expressive chatbots [4, 64–67, 73, 74, 76]. For instance, Lee et al. [66, 67] found that participants disclosed more when speaking with a chatbot that talked about its own feelings. Still, there is a critical lack of research on how perceived empathy in AI chatbots shapes users’ privacy risk perceptions, decision-making, behaviors, and mitigation practices. These understandings are crucial for informing the design of user-centric privacy solutions. This research agenda is particularly timely and important in an era of rapidly advancing AI capabilities, as users increasingly turn to empathic AI for highly sensitive purposes [69]. Privacy, therefore, is a fundamental issue that warrants closer examination. Our work thus contributes by providing an

3 Methodology 

To elicit empirical insights into how perceived empathy from AI chatbots shapes users’ privacy attitudes, behaviors, and needs, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 21 users of AI chatbots. 

Recruitment. From June 2025 to August 2025, we recruited participants on the Prolific crowdsourcing platform 3. To mitigate social desirability [43] and demand characteristics [77], we did not include any words related to privacy or security in our recruitment and screening materials (see Appendix A). We advertised our study as an investigation of users’ experiences and perspectives toward empathy in AI chatbots. Prolific users interested in our study were redirected to the Qualtrics survey platform 4, where we hosted our pre-screening survey. Our study obtained approval from our institution’s review board (IRB). All participants in the pilot and the main study provided their informed consent before participating. Information about the recruited participants and their AI chatbot usage is detailed in Sec 4. 

Eligibility and pre-screening. To participate in our interview study, participants must (1) be at least 18 years old, (2) live in the United States, (3) have used an AI chatbot at least once, (4) have perceived empathy from an AI chatbot, and (5) have a task approval rate of at least 95% on the Prolific platform. To find eligible participants and to recruit a diverse sample of participants, in the pre-screening survey, we collected information about their AI chatbot usage (all the chatbots used, purposes for usage, and usage frequency) and general demographics (e.g., age, gender, and education). Because one of our goals was to elicit rich empirical insights into how perceptions of AI empathy shaped users’ privacy experiences, we required participants to have used AI chatbots at least once and to have perceived empathy from an AI chatbot at least once, as specified in the screening survey and interview questions. In addition, we sought to capture perceptions of empathy beyond personal and emotional contexts and therefore purposively recruited participants whose AI chatbot use spanned a diverse range of use cases (see Table 1). Requiring a minimum level of AI usage and perceived empathy in AI chatbot interactions helps mitigate potential hypothetical bias [48], wherein participants report attitudes and behaviors that diverge from their actual practices. All the participants, regardless of their eligibility, were compensated at an hourly rate of 21.32 USD through the Prolific platform. The median time for completion was about 2 minutes. The recruitment and pre-screening materials are included in the Appendix A. 

Pilot interviews. Prior to the formal interview study, we conducted pilot interviews with three participants recruited through the first author’s personal connections. The three participants all qualified for our eligibility criteria. The purpose of the pilots was to assess and improve the clarity of the interviews and ensure that the interviews could be completed within 45 minutes to 1 hour. After each pilot, the researchers revised the interview protocol to address any confusing questions. We applied minor wording edits to the interview questions to improve clarity and flow of the script, and removed redundancies to shorten the interview procedure. No improvements or changes were made after the two pilot interviews. 

Interview procedure. We reached out to each participant via Prolific messaging one day before the interview to confirm their attendance. We conducted 21 interviews via the Zoom remote communication platform 5. We reached

We asked the participants about their security and privacy concerns regarding perceived empathy from AI chatbots, if any. We also asked the participants if they shared information of different levels of sensitivity with AI chatbots when they perceived empathy from AI chatbots. We then asked about participants’ mitigation strategies. 

• Section 4: Expectations regarding privacy-enhancing empathic AI interactions (RQ3). We asked participants about their expectations around what features and capabilities they want to better protect their privacy when engaging in empathic AI interactions. 

Qualitative data analysis. All the interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed using the Zoom transcription service for data analysis. The first author proofread all the transcripts before the analysis, ensuring accuracy and readability. Three researchers conducted a thematic analysis using the inductive approach [94]. Our qualitative analysis consists of three iterative stages: 

• Stage 1: Independent coding. Three researchers independently coded three identical transcripts and generated their own codebook. 

• Stage 2: Establishing an initial codebook. Three researchers presented their codebooks to each other. After discussing, merging, and reconciling the codes, three researchers agreed on an initial codebook. 

• Stage 3: Iterating the codebook. Three researchers split the remaining transcripts and coded them using the merged codebook based on consensus. Two researchers coded each transcript. During this stage, new codes were added, removed, and merged. Through several meetings with the entire research team, the researchers discussed their codes and resolved all disagreements. Since all the transcripts were double-coded and disagreements were resolved through the meetings, calculating inter-coder reliability was not necessary [78]. The final codebook is included in Appendix A. 

Limitations. As is common in qualitative studies, our sample size was relatively small and therefore generalizability might be limited in several ways. First, all of our participants were recruited via Prolific, which introduced selection bias [98] toward technically savvy users. To mitigate this bias, we had two pre-screening questions, one about par-ticipants’ education level and the other about their technical background. When selecting participants, we aimed for a balanced distribution among participants’ educational backgrounds and technical expertise. Additionally, we avoided including privacy and/or security-related words in our recruitment and pre-screening materials to mitigate

Second, the study relied on participants’ self-reported information, which may be subject to social desirability bias [43]. Third, we recruited participants who had perceived empathy from AI chatbots in order to elicit richer accounts, but this choice may have excluded the perspectives of users who do not perceive AI chatbots as empathic. We sought to mitigate this bias by purposively recruiting participants with a wide range of use cases, from personal to work contexts where empathy levels may vary. Nevertheless, our findings may not be generalizable to all users of AI chatbots. In addition, because little prior research examines how lay users conceptualize empathy from AI chatbots, we first sought to capture participants’ lived experiences qualitatively, along with their related privacy preferences. Future work should complement this account with quantitative studies, such as lab and field experiments, that examine the relationship between perceived empathy and privacy risks. Our exploratory findings, including the taxonomy of AI empathy perceptions, offer hypotheses for such work to test. 

Ethics statement. Our study received approval from our Institutional Review Board (IRB). All participants in the pilot and main interviews provided informed consent before participation. During interviews, we reminded participants that they could skip any question, pause, or stop at any time without penalty, and that they could keep their camera off. Participants were compensated at a flat rate of 20 USD, aligned with institutional and platform norms. Prior to analysis, the first author proofread and de-identified all raw data by removing names, Prolific IDs, and other identifying information. We used randomly assigned participant codes (P1-P21) in analysis and reporting. Only the research team had access to the raw data. 

4 Results 

We recruited a diverse group of participants with varying levels of experience and familiarity with AI chatbots. Participants’ ages ranged from 18-64, with the age range 35-44 being the most common (9/21), followed by 45-54 (6/21) and 25-34 (3/21). 13/21 participants were male-identifying and 8/21 were female-identifying. Almost all participants viewed AI empathy as a simulation of human empathy. Almost all participants described the empathy they perceived from AI chatbots as “programmed” to imitate empathy in human interactions. P4 distinguished an AI chatbot’s display of empathy from its possession of empathy. They also explained that users could knowingly engage with this simulation and still find it comforting: “AI is trained and programmed to just display (empathy). They perceived this aggregation as lacking the personal connection and nuance of human empathy. As P18 explained: “Humans have their own past experiences that are usually unique and have more nuance than an AI. Instead, they evaluated the internal processes and external enactments of empathy separately. 

For most participants, resonating and caring marked a central boundary between AI and human empathy. 

Most participants located the fundamental difference between AI and human empathy in resonating and caring . They viewed these components as requiring internal emotional experiences that AI chatbots could not possess. For example, P19 explained, “A human is actually able to share the perspective, or maybe connect your experience with their own, whereas a chatbot is just understanding on a theoretical level. It can’t actually [feel] the experience or have that experience itself.” Participants instead associated human resonating with a sense of connection grounded in each person’s emotions and lived experiences.

5.1 Privacy Implications of AI Empathy Through Users’ Conceptions Beyond Human-Likeness 

Grounding AI empathy in users’ own accounts informs a more granular analysis of its privacy implications. 

To understand the privacy implications of AI empathy, we first examined what users themselves consider empathic (Sec 4.1.1). Prior research has distinguished cognitive empathy from affective empathy and proposed additional 

> Manuscript submitted to ACM

How Does Perceived Empathy From AI Chatbots Shape Users’ Privacy Attitudes, Behaviors, and Needs? 19 components to characterize the construct [40–42, 71, 107]. In addition, empirical research suggests that cognitive and affective empathy can shape users’ perceptions of AI chatbots in different ways [55]. For example, cognitive empathy may be perceived as more helpful in emotional support contexts than in interactions focused on efficiency [45, 67, 75], whereas expressions of affective empathy may be perceived as inauthentic and discourage continued use [13, 79]. However, the distinction between cognitive and affective empathy may not fully capture the diversity of users’ conceptions. Research on lay conceptions shows substantial variation in how people define empathy and which aspects they emphasize [47], yet little work has examined how lay users of AI chatbots describe AI empathy. Together, this diversity of perceived empathy dimensions and their distinct influences on users’ perceptions suggest that how these dimensions shape users’ privacy perceptions, behaviors, and needs in AI chatbot interactions may not have been fully examined. We therefore extended this line of work by inviting participants to describe empathy in their own words and used the resulting taxonomy to ground a more granular analysis of users’ privacy experiences when perceiving empathy from AI chatbots. The five components we identified— understanding , resonating , caring , expressing , and helping —were perceived differently in the context of AI interactions (Sec 4.1). The perceived presence or absence of each component shaped participants’ mental models of AI chatbots’ privacy practices, disclosure behaviors, and efforts to mitigate privacy risks in different ways (Sec 4.2). Thus, distinguishing these components provides a basis for designing more targeted privacy protections, which we expand on in Sec 5.2. 

Increased privacy risk despite perceived differences from human empathy. One common assumption in prior work is that human-like features, such as chatbots’ emotional expressions [70] and active-listening cues [20], encourage users’ self-disclosure. At the same time, AI’s human-likeness has drawn widespread public concern [51], and recent legislation now requires AI companions to state, at the start of an interaction and periodically thereafter, that the user is not communicating with a human [83]. However, our findings suggest that even cognitively understanding the differences between AI and human empathy still shaped disclosure and beyond. Specifically, participants identified the absence of resonating and caring as a key reason for viewing AI empathy as artificial, yet described mixed effects of this perceived absence on their willingness to disclose (Sec 4.2.1). In addition, some participants viewed chatbots’ contextual understanding and helpfulness , including the quality of their advice, as sometimes exceeding what an average person could provide (Sec 4.1.2). They described willingly providing further personal contexts to benefit from these instrumental aspects of empathy, not necessarily because they perceived the chatbots as human-like (Sec 4.2.2). Thus, our findings indicate that the privacy risks of AI empathy are unlikely to be adequately addressed by simply reminding users that AI empathy differs from human empathy. Instead, there is a need for more nuanced solutions that account for different levels and types of empathy, aligning them with users’ preferences in different contexts. Toward this end, we detail our recommendations in Sec 5.3. Beyond self-disclosure, our findings also suggest that perceived empathy from AI chatbots may create a false sense of privacy protection. A few participants assumed that their privacy was better protected when they perceived empathy from AI chatbots (Sec 4.2.1). At the same time, almost all participants reported taking no privacy-protective measures despite recognizing privacy risks (Sec 4.2.2). While participants did not explicitly explain this inaction or attribute it to feeling protected, prior work suggesting that privacy settings can create a false sense of protection [12] offers a possible parallel. Perceived empathy may similarly give users a sense of protection that reduces their perceived need to take protective actions, such as reviewing or adjusting privacy settings. This possibility warrants further investigation into how perceived empathy may affect users’ privacy behaviors beyond disclosure and highlights a need for transparency around the privacy practices of empathic AI interaction, which we expand on in Sec 5.3.

5.2 Tailoring Privacy Protections to Users’ Preferences for AI Empathy 

Our participants described preferences for different components and levels of empathy across interaction contexts (Sec 4.3), and we argue that these preferences should inform the amount and type of information chatbots request. Consistent with prior work [23, 45, 67], most participants preferred minimal or no empathy in efficiency-driven contexts, while preferring appropriate levels of empathy in personal and emotional contexts (Sec 4.3.1). Within contexts where they wanted empathy, participants further specified which components they preferred. For example, when participants primarily sought validation, they did not necessarily want more in-depth, reasoned input (Sec 4.3.2). In these cases, validating expressions alone could support their goals without probing for additional sensitive details to formulate advice. In other contexts, participants preferred in-depth, reasoned input over emotional validation (Sec 4.3.2), yet they lacked controls over empathic behavior and had little insight into the underlying data practices at the time of the interviews (Sec 4.3.3). As a result, users have to either disengage from the interaction or continue sharing information without greater transparency or additional privacy protections. These experiences highlight an opportunity to align both chatbots’ information requests and data practices with users’ preferred empathy components and levels. In particular, the depth of understanding a chatbot seeks should reflect the support users want in a given context. Although ChatGPT now offers personalization features that allow users to indicate their preferred personality or tone [87], there remains an opportunity to pair these preferences with data-minimization defaults tailored to different modes. To address this gap, we propose modes that emphasize different empathy components and levels, paired with tailored privacy practices, in Sec 5.3. 

5.3 Recommendations 

We propose three directions for designing safer, privacy-enhancing empathic AI interactions. Notably, these directions follow from exploratory findings, and future work should evaluate their effectiveness in practice. 

Supporting user-controllable empathic AI interactions across contexts. Our participants described preferences for different levels and components of empathy across interaction contexts. Most preferred minimal or no empathy in efficiency-driven contexts, while seeking varied forms of empathic support in personal and emotional contexts (Sec 4.3). Accordingly, these preferences suggest an opportunity to give users control over both chatbots’ empathic behavior and the information they request to provide desired support. We recommend allowing users to first select their desired level of empathy, for example, through a slider or a set of options ranging from no empathy to more extensive empathic engagement. When empathy is desired, users could further specify their preferred components. The five components identified in our findings—understanding, resonating, caring, expressing, and helping—could inform the organization of these options. For example, users might prioritize validating expressions when venting or prefer in-depth understanding and reasoned input when seeking advice. These preferences could apply to individual sessions, be saved for particular contexts, or serve as global defaults, with opportunities to adjust them as users’ goals change. Participants’ concerns about sensitive information resurfacing (Sec 4.3.2) also suggest the value of configurable topic filters. These filters could allow users to specify information, experiences, or topics that a chatbot should avoid

Separately, controls over cross-context information use could allow users to prevent details shared in one empathic context from being reused in another. These boundaries could apply to individual sessions or persist across interactions, allowing users to receive their preferred forms of empathic support while limiting unwanted information requests and reuse. 

Establishing privacy-enhancing defaults for empathic interactions. While user controls can give people greater agency to tailor their experience, privacy-protective defaults for both data practices and system behavior are especially important when users are less aware of privacy risks. Participants, for instance, described how empathic expressions could subtly lower their guard (Sec 4.2.1). We therefore recommend that developers adopt such defaults: constraining, through system prompts, behaviors that invite sensitive disclosure, and minimizing the data collected, retained, and used. For example, when users seek active listening or validation, chatbots should draw on what has already been shared rather than probing for additional personal details in order to offer unsolicited advice. If the desired form of support is unclear, chatbots should ask whether the user wants listening or advice, without requesting further sensitive information. When additional context is necessary for the requested support, chatbots should explain why it is relevant, let users decline, and offer to continue with less specific information when possible. A preference for more empathic engagement should not be treated as permission to solicit increasingly sensitive details. Beyond minimizing collection, as requested by our participants (Sec 4.3.3), developers should also strengthen how disclosed data is retained and secured: deleting sensitive interaction data after a limited retention period, and applying encryption and other technical safeguards against unauthorized access. These recommendations can also inform regulators and auditors overseeing AI safety. Audits could assess, for example, whether chatbots solicit unnecessary personal details when users seek validation, continue probing after users decline to elaborate, or surface sensitive information from other contexts without permission. Such audits should examine system behavior across turns and sessions to determine whether these protections are applied consistently, including when users have not asked for them. 

Making empathic behavior and data practices transparent. Participants described uncertainty about the underlying data practices (Sec 4.3.3), even as their experience of empathic behavior shaped their assumptions about privacy protections (Sec 4.2.1). We therefore recommend that developers make the current empathy configuration, the behavioral rules it implies, and the data practices that apply to it visible within the conversation interface. For example, similar to the idea of privacy labels that detail important data practices [60], an empathy label could display the currently selected empathy level and components, providing a persistent indication of the interaction settings. Users could expand this label to view more detailed explanations of the expected behavior, including whether the chatbot may request additional personal information, draw on previous conversations, or mention topics covered by their exclusions. This expanded view could provide plain-language explanations of the relevant system prompts and allow users to inspect and adjust their preferences. It should also indicate whether the configuration reflects global defaults or settings specific to the current session. By making these behavioral commitments visible, the interface could help users assess whether chatbot responses align with their selected preferences. The label should additionally provide access to information about the data practices that apply to the current interaction, including who can access the conversation, where and for how long information is stored, how it is used, and whether and how encryption or other protections apply. 6 Conclusion 

Our findings show that empathic AI can shape users’ privacy experiences even when users do not perceive AI as genuinely human-like. Participants distinguished AI from human empathy through the perceived absence of resonating and caring, while still describing understanding, expressing, and helping as shaping disclosure, trust, and privacy expectations. By unpacking empathy into distinct components, our work highlights how different forms of perceived empathy create different privacy implications and motivates the design of user-controllable, privacy-protective, and transparent empathic AI interactions.

In this interview, I will be asking you about your usage, understanding, and experiences with AI chatbots. As mentioned in the consent form, we would like to audio-record this interview for analysis purposes. I will keep my video on throughout the interview process; but it is completely up to you if you would like to have your video on or off. May I confirm with you that you allow us to audio-record this interview? [Start the audio recording]. Thank you again. Let’s get started! 

B.2 AI Chatbot Usage 

(1) Do you use any AI chatbots? If so, could you name the products? (2) Could you tell us the purposes for which you use these AI chatbots? (3) How long have you been using these products, and how often do you use them now? (4) Have you ever used any chatbots that you no longer use, and why? 

B.3 User Understandings and Perceived Impacts 

(5) In your own words, how would you define “empathy”? (6) What would it mean to you for an AI chatbot to have empathy? (7) What similarities and differences do you think there are between human and AI empathy? (8) Have you ever had an interaction with an AI chatbot where you perceived it to be empathic? (a) What was the product, purpose of usage, and context? (a) What experiences during the interaction signaled to you that the AI had empathy? (i) With these indicators/characteristics, do you think the way you interact with the chatbot would differ if they didn’t show any such indicators? (a) Could you please tell us more to the extent that you are comfortable with this? (i) What benefits and concerns did you perceive? (9) For what purposes or in which contexts would you want AI chatbots to have empathy, and why? (10) For what purposes or in which contexts would you not want AI chatbots to have empathy, and why? (11) What benefits / risks do you think empathic AI chatbots might have for users and why? 

B.4 Privacy Attitudes 

(12) How, if at all, do you think the information you would share with an empathic AI chatbot would differ from the information you would share with a non-empathic one? (13) In your perspective, what are the risks and benefits of empathic AI chatbots for users’ privacy? (14) How, if at all, would you expect these privacy risks and benefits to differ from non-empathic AI chatbots? (15) What are the ways that you could manage these privacy risks? (16) What challenges do you think users face when managing these privacy risks?
