# Learning Side-by-Side Navigation for Personal Mobility Vehicles from Human Drivers 

Luis Yoichi Morales and Florent Ferreri 

Abstract — This paper presents an approach to achieve side-by-side autonomous motion with a pair of personal mobil-ity vehicles (PMVs). Given a global path, two PMVs plan cooperatively so as to hold a social formation that lets the passengers hold a conversation, while keeping the ride safe and smooth. Each vehicle runs its own sampling-based model-predictive planner; the planners exchange nothing but the odometry each vehicle could observe of the other, and the joint decision is expressed as a pair of utility matrices over pairs of candidate trajectories, resolved by a common rule. The behavioural parameters are fitted by inverse reinforcement learning from 409 minutes of driving by ten pairs of people in human-driven PMVs in an indoor environment, and the lanes the pair can use are derived offline from the corridor itself rather than fixed by hand. A safety layer the cost cannot override executes a move only if the vehicle can still brake clear, measured from its whole body, and a vehicle that is stuck reverses along its own path and continues. In closed-loop simulation over 24 runs in the conditions the drivers were recorded in and 24 in four generated obstacle layouts, no vehicle entered a wall or a cone. The pair holds conversational formation for 60 .5% and 68 .9% of the time among obstacles (anticlockwise and clockwise) and 81 .2% and 91 .5% in a clear aisle, against the drivers’ 34 .0% and 36 .0% , and 52 .9% and 

56 .0% , and it matches their speed. It keeps one lateral formation where the drivers alternated between a close diagonal and a wide abreast one, and it varies far less. Experimental results are based on a differential drive kinematic simulator running on ROS 2 1.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Current robot systems can achieve reliable long-term navi-gation in dynamic environments with pedestrians around [1], [2]. Recent works have placed attention to the pedestrian flow to be able to pass-by walking people in a social manner [3], [4], [5]. These robot navigation approaches consider the humans passing by and the overall safety but do not consider their own motion smoothness. Differently from standard mobile robots, autonomous PMV’s carry human passengers, so the navigation must be safe and comfortable for the passenger. PMV’s can provide transportation services in a smaller scale than car-like vehi-cles in locations such as hospitals, retirement homes, houses, attraction parks. Moreover, in some remote rural areas, there is the need to provide mobility assistance to isolated aged people where PMV’s have the potential to provide personal last mile services such as autonomus ride shares [6] and autonomous personal vehicles on demand [7]. While moving together as a shared activity we shared the common intention of moving towards a destination which is different from the case of moving alongside a stranger by chance where we just happen to collaborate or negotiate traffic for a short period of time [19]. Previous works have studied the human phenomenon of moving together. A landmark work regarding pedestrian be-havior is the social force model where behavior is modeled as several forces actuating within the pedestrians [20]. Several extensions to the model have been proposed, for example [21] models pedestrian group dynamics as a microscopic potential orthogonal to the direction of movement. The work in [12] studied the impact of social groups in crowds where it was found that at low density people tend to move side-by-side. An approach to model pedestrian dynamics in a social group formed of two pedestrians was proposed in [13]. The work in [22] presented a study on proxemic behaviors in groups from dyads to five-person groups. Force model approaches need parameter calibration which is usually environment dependent. Moreover, placement of the goal locations for the agents is not trivial and it could be context dependent. Subgoal computation requires large ammounts of pedestrian data to compute [23]. Work regarding walking together as a social activity was discussed in [24] where the concepts of shared action and plural subject as a group of two or more persons that have a common goal were introduced. In navigation state of the art, it is common to give a global path so that the robot can follow [25] which can be customized to consider human factors in their cost function [26]. In the work of this paper, we model a group composed of two PMV’s moving alongside as a shared activity where a global path is given in advance. A model based on a shared planning approach where each walking partner anticipates the future location of their partner and jointly plans their future location was proposed in [27]. An extension to this model was presented in [28], where the pair could hold the formation even when one agent did not know the goal, by switching between a leader–follower and a collaborative state according to how reliably the follower could estimate the route. Additionally, the work in [29] presented a model for three robots moving as a social group replicating pedestrian behavior. Regarding robot companions, an approach of cooperative robots accompaning people was presented in [15] and a model that allows a robot to approach and engage a group formed of two pedestrians walking side-by-side was presented in [30]. Work on safe and smooth navigation for PMV’s has been done on single robotic wheelchair path planning for smooth autonomous navigation was presented in [31], [32], [26]. Regarding robotic wheelchairs there are previous approaches that dealt with the task of moving with companions [16]. The closest work to our proposal is the work presented in [33], [34] where they introduce a wheelchair system that can keep a formation for users to to be able to avoid obstacles and communicate. These works reactively compute the linear and angular velocities of their own motions. Differently from related works, in our approach, the mo-tion is between two agents that carry passengers maximizing mutual utility rather than a robot and a human. The two plan-ners reach compatible decisions from their own observations to keep a formation so that passengers can converse. III. P ROBLEM DEFINITION 

This section presents the definition of PMV’s, a definition of social mobility and provides the problem statement. 

A. Personal Mobility Vehicles Moving as Social Groups 

A personal mobility vehicle is a self-contained inde-pendent vehicle that transports a single passenger. PMV’s navigate on pedestrian paths interacting with pedestrians and are not regulated by car-like traffic rules. In pedestrian behavior research, a social group consists of two or more individuals who interact socially while moving toward a common goal [35], [24], [36]. Interaction is the oral communication usually accompanied with non-verbal communication such as gestures and gaze exchange [37]. When walking together, people tend to move side-by-side to keep direct line of sight while communicating [38]. 

B. Shared Planning 

Shared planning is a joint intentional activity in which two or more agents cooperate to achieve a joint goal [19]. A group of PMVs engaged in shared planning has the common goal of following a path to a destination. They navigate cooperatively, mutually opening space and keeping a social formation that allows the passengers to converse. Each vehi-cle runs its own sampling-based model-predictive planner at Every cycle it samples its own candidate trajectories, samples a prediction of the partner’s from the partner’s observed state, scores every pair of candidates, selects one pair by a solution concept, and executes only the first command of its own half of that pair. For data collection and experimentation we used two personal mobility vehicles (WHILL Model C) each equipped with a LiDAR (Velodyne VLP-16) and wheel encoders (see Fig. 2). Each robot localized towards a previously built map and shared its pose, linear velocity and angular velocity wirelessly to the partner. Data of both vehicles was time stamped and recorded simultaneously.

C. Parameter Fitting 

The weights wx of features of section IV-A are constants, fitted by maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning using the human driven data of section V-B. Each demonstration is one recorded instant. From the recorded state of one vehicle, the planner’s own rollout generator produces the candidate set the planner would choose from, and every candidate is scored with the features of Table II. The relative features are evaluated against the partner’s recorded future rather than a prediction of it, so that the weights describe the drivers’ preferences instead of absorbing the errors of a partner model. The candidate whose rollout comes closest to what the driver actually did over the same 3 s is taken as the driver’s choice i⋆ 

> t

, and the weights maximise the regularised log-likelihood (11) of those choices under the model (10), with λ = 10 −3 and w ≥ 0.Both vehicles of a pair are demonstrations of the same cost, one on the outer lane and one on the inner, so each sampled instant contributes up to two samples. We draw 900 samples from the ten pairs, 25% of them from instants below 0.6 m/s. The drivers were almost always at cruise, but the planner starts from rest and slows for obstacles, and a fit that never sees low-speed states leaves standing still unpriced. Validation holds out whole driver pairs, since the question is whether the preferences generalise across people: the model is refitted ten times, each time without one pair, and scored on that pair’s choices. Held out this way, the driver’s actual choice ranks at 0 .898 on average among the candidates (normalised, 1 for the best and 0 .5 for chance), between 0 .848 and 0 .934 over the ten pairs, with a mean log-likelihood of −3.360. On the full set the fitted weights reach −3.346, against −3.786 for equal weights and −4.774 for chance. The fit therefore improves on uniform weighting, and the improvement holds on pairs it has not seen. A fit that ranks candidates at a single instant cannot see effects that build up over a run, such as the pair swapping sides or spinning in place, so several of the weights the planner runs differ from the fitted ones. 

D. Safety and Recovery 

The fitted cost decides how the pair moves; whether a vehicle may move at all is decided by hard rules on top of it, which no weight can trade away. 

Body clearance. Every check measures the distance from the vehicle’s footprint rectangle, not from its centre, to the walls of the map and to the detected objects. The candidate obstacle points are taken around the whole outline of the body: taking only those nearest the centre misses a second wall at a corner, and was measured to report clearance for a body already inside a wall. 

Never execute a blocked move. A candidate is executed only if its first 0 .5 s keeps the body clear of walls and objects and if, after executing it, the vehicle can still brake to rest without contact. The braking test integrates the stop through the actuation lag and assumes the command acts for one planning tick plus the measured delay between the state it was computed from and its execution, so a planner that falls behind becomes more cautious rather than late. When no candidate passes, the vehicle stops; it never takes the least-bad candidate. 

Recovery. A vehicle that has been unable to move for half a second reverses along the path it has itself just driven, which is known to be free, under the same braking test, and then rejoins at reduced speed. If it stops again at the same place, it reverses twice as far. Objects are remembered for 30 s after they were last seen, because the laser does not cover the region behind the vehicle. 

Staying together. The vehicle ahead along the route stops when its partner falls more than 3 .0 m behind, and resumes when it is within 2 .2 m, the 99th and 90th percentiles of the drivers’ separation among cones. It starts such a hold only when no object is within 2 .5 m, so that it never waits in a gap its partner still has to pass through. VI. E XPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE AND RESULTS 

The planner was evaluated in closed loop in a simulation of the surveyed seventh-floor environment, with the occupancy map taken from the same survey used for the data collection and the objects entered as data rather than baked into the map. Both travel directions were run, since the route is not symmetric: the two directions differ in where the corridor pinches and in which lane an object blocks. Each run lasted 170 s, long enough for roughly one circuit. The two human conditions were run with 6 repeats per direction each (24 runs) and the generated layouts with three (24 runs), all with one frozen version of the planner and a metrics node sampling both vehicles every tick.

Generated layouts is a third condition with no human counterpart. The lane offsets the planner follows are derived offline, so a test against the cones they were derived from cannot distinguish knowledge that generalises from knowl-edge that was fitted. We therefore generate cone layouts along the route: placements are sampled on the centreline rather than uniformly in the plane, so every cone sits somewhere the pair will actually meet it, each is placed by kind (on the outer lane, the inner lane, the centre, or against an edge) rather than at a uniform random offset, and each is checked to leave beside it a free band at least as wide as the tightest site in the surveyed environment. Cones are written as circles at the measured radius. Nobody ever drove these layouts, so they support claims about generalisation 

and safety — contact, stalls, progress, and the fraction of the route spent in file rather than side by side — but never about resemblance to human behaviour. Two limits apply to this third condition throughout. Four layouts with three repeats is enough to establish mechanisms and to compare arms on stalls and progress, but not to estimate rates: separating a 10% stall rate from a 5% one needs of the order of 74 runs per arm. And contact depth at a given site varies more between sweeps of an identical configuration than between the arms we compare, so we report sites and mechanisms rather than depths. 

B. Conversation and Formation 

Table III divides every moment of the runs by what the pair was doing, beside the drivers in the same condition. The quantity the system exists for is conversational formation: the partner within 25 ◦ of abreast and less than 2 m away. In all four combinations of condition and direction the pair holds it for more of the time than the drivers did: 60 .5% and 68 .9% among the surveyed cones, against the drivers’ 34 .0% and 36 .0%, and 81 .2% and 91 .5% in the clear aisle, against 52 .9% and 56 .0%. The difference comes mostly from the pair not separating. Among the cones the drivers spent 17 .2% and 17 .8% of the time 2 m or more apart, the pair 2 .1% and 1 .3%; filing past the cones took a comparable share for both. The one combination in which a vehicle spent noticeable time stopped is clockwise with the cones, 1 .5% of the time, which is the recovery reported in Section VI-D.

The largest difference in shape is the lateral separation, which is bimodal for the drivers and unimodal for the pair. The two human modes are two formations. In the close one (lateral separation under 1 m) the pair rides staggered on a diagonal — medians 0 .65 m across, 0 .68 m along the route and 1 .00 m apart — and in the wide one it rides abreast, 1.41 m across, 0 .54 m along and 1 .59 m apart. They are not a property of the corridor: the position along the route explains less than a tenth of which mode a pair is in, every one of the ten pairs spends between 38 and 60% of its time in the close mode, and a typical episode of either lasts only 6– 4 s. Each pair alternates between the two at the same places. The cost asks for a single preferred separation, so the pair holds one separation of about 1 .16 m between the two modes, and it cannot reproduce the alternation without a second formation and a rule for switching between them, which the data do not supply. For the purpose of the system the difference is not a loss: only 29% of the drivers’ close-mode time is conversational, against 56% of the wide mode, so a pair that keeps the abreast formation instead of drifting into the diagonal is holding the better of the two for conversation — which is also why it exceeds the drivers in Table III. It is therefore not indistinguishable from a human pair: it is a more conservative and more consistent one, which holds the formation the conversation needs for more of the time. 

D. Safety and Liveness 

Table V reports contact, liveness and recovery in all three conditions. Wall contact is measured independently of the planner, as the deepest penetration of the body into any surveyed map cell, and counted above half a map cell (25 mm). In the two conditions the drivers were recorded in, 24 runs, no part of either vehicle entered a wall or a cone and no vehicle ended a run stuck. Recovery was needed mostly among the surveyed cones, where a vehicle backed up 0 .2times per run anticlockwise and 2 .2 clockwise, and resumed every time; in the clear aisle a vehicle backed up once in twelve runs. The generated layouts are harder than anything the drivers met. Over 24 runs no part of either vehicle entered a wall or a cone, and the vehicles backed up 2 .8 times per run anticlockwise and 0 .1 clockwise. Two anticlockwise runs ended with a vehicle stopped, counted as stuck in Table V. Neither vehicle was stuck: each was holding for its partner (Section V-D), which was still reversing its way out of a blocked corner 3 .5–5 .7 m behind when the run ended. The result that separates cleanly is not among the cooper-ative concepts: it is independent , which ignores the partner and is worse than choosing a feasible rollout at random. Reasoning about the partner at all is what the open-loop comparison establishes. The four concepts that do reason about the partner span 0 .15 m, a sixth of the gap between the best of them and chance. Their end-of-horizon separations are more informative than their errors: the drivers ended 1.439 m apart, and every concept that resolves the partner’s column with a maximum overshoots that (1 .68–1 .82 m), while maximin, which resolves it with a minimum, sits at 1.261 m.

The order of the repairs mattered, and is itself a result. Correcting the floor is what made the prefix rung effective — before it, the only candidate the floor admitted was the fastest, which is also the one most likely to enter the margin — and the rung alone still left one site, which the recovery correction closed. Over twelve runs after all three, no vehicle stalled in either direction. We state the strength of that evidence precisely. Twelve clean runs bound the stall rate below roughly 25% at 95% confidence; against the prior rate of 28% the improvement gives a one-sided Fisher p = 0.06. The case rests on the mechanisms, which are analytic, rather than on the counts. This is a general hazard in evaluating such repairs: at a base rate near 25% and 170 s per run, a six-run comparison cannot distinguish a fix from chance, and two earlier changes were adopted on exactly that evidence and later withdrawn — one of them after it traded a stall for a vehicle grounded 9 mm inside a wall. We therefore verify the mechanism on recorded stall poses replayed deterministically, and use closed-loop runs only to confirm that nothing else regressed. 

E. Discussion 

The pair does what the system is for: it holds conver-sational formation for more of the time than the people it was fitted from, and it almost never comes apart. It does so without resembling them closely. It matches their speed, but it keeps one lateral separation where the drivers alternated between two, and it varies far less than they did. Two causes are clear. The separation target is a single preferred value, so where the drivers switched every few seconds between a close diagonal and a wide abreast formation, a cost that asks for one value settles between the two; since the diagonal is the less conversational of the two, we regard this as acceptable for the purpose of the system, but it is a departure from human behaviour that a model with two formations would be needed to remove. And the safety rules of Section V-D are conservative by construction: they slow the vehicle wherever its room to stop is short, which a human driver, who judges risk rather than proving its absence, does not. The safety rules carry two lessons that are not specific to this planner. Clearance must be measured from the whole body: a check that selects its obstacle points by distance from the vehicle’s centre reported room for a body already inside a wall at a corner, and both the planner and the measurement of the results had relied on it. And a planner must plan from fresh state: the odometry each planning tick used had been a quarter of a second old, and every late stop we traced came back to it. Freshness must hold for every input, and be checked against ground truth: a second thread that freshened the odometry left the obstacle input queued behind the planning timer, so cones reached the planner every few seconds instead of every scan, and the vehicles drove into cones the planner had never been sent. Contact measured against what the planner perceived could not reveal this; only contact measured against the placed obstacles did. Two issues remain open. First, at one generated layout the vehicle whose lane is blocked does not fall in behind its partner in time, and goes round the far side of the cones into a pocket from which it must reverse. Making it stop and wait fixed that site but cost stalls at sites that filed well on their own, so the decision of who passes first needs to be taken earlier, as the drivers took it, rather than corrected late. Second, the evaluation is in simulation, with six repeats per condition and direction, enough to establish mechanisms and large differences but not small rates. These point to the next step. The candidate trajectories are currently arcs in the vehicle’s own frame, which over longer horizons leave the corridor and cannot express “slow down, then move behind the partner”. Generating them relative to the route — a lateral offset that changes smoothly along it, combined with a speed profile, as in planners for road vehicles — would make that manoeuvre an ordinary candidate, keep every candidate inside the corridor, and let the horizon be extended so that the order of passing is settled early. VII. C ONCLUSIONS 

We presented a shared planning approach for a pair of personal mobility vehicles in which each vehicle runs its own model-predictive planner, the joint decision is expressed as utility matrices over candidate trajectory pairs, and nothing crosses between the vehicles but observable odometry. The behavioural parameters are fitted from a corpus of human-driven pairs, and the geometry available to the formation is derived from the corridor rather than fixed by hand — offline from the map, and at runtime from what the vehicles observe, so that an obstacle which has moved since the profile was built still narrows the corridor the pair plans in. In closed loop, in the two conditions the drivers were recorded in, the pair holds conversational formation for 60 .5% and 68 .9% of the time among the cones (anticlock-wise and clockwise) and 81 .2% and 91 .5% in a clear aisle, against the drivers’ 34 .0% and 36 .0%, and 52 .9% and 56 .0%, with no contact with walls or cones and no vehicle left stuck. On the quantity the formation exists to serve it therefore exceeds the humans it was fitted from, though not by imitating them: it matches their speed, but it is more uniform and holds a single lateral separation where they alternated between two, and on lateral separation and stagger it lies outside the spread among the drivers themselves. Safety rests on rules the cost cannot override: moves are executed only if the vehicle can still brake clear, measured from the whole body, and a vehicle that is stuck reverses along its own path and continues.
