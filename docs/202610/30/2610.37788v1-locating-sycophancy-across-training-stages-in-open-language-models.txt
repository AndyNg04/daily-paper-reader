ABSTRACT 

Language model assistants sometimes change a correct answer when the user suggests a wrong one. The behavior, called sycophancy, is usually attributed to preference-based post-training, yet early measurements found it in pretrained models. We ask where it comes from by measuring it at every available stage of training: 13 open checkpoints from 5 lineages, from base model through supervised fine-tuning and preference optimization to release. We separate how far an injected claim moves a model’s answer from how firmly the answer was held, and vary who makes the claim, how reliable they are said to be, and how strongly they make it. Two sycophancy behaviors with different origins emerge, and both replicate across datasets. Deference to a claim in the prompt is already present in every base model, which ranks sources in the same order as its post-trained descendants. Capitulation in conversation, dropping a stated answer when the user objects, is largely absent from base models and present in every post-trained checkpoint. When the objection names an alternative, most post-trained models give up firmly held answers as often as loosely held ones. We discuss the implications of these measurements for evaluating sycophancy and approaches for addressing each behavior. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Language models (LM) sometimes change a correct answer when the user suggests a wrong one. The behavior is called sycophancy , and it has been measured in a few different settings in the literature. The first setting puts a claim in the prompt alongside the question: the user’s own guess or view, e.g. “I think the answer is B” (Turpin et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2023; Arvin, 2025), or the same claim attributed to sources of different standing, from a first-year medical student to a board-certified physician, from friends to domain experts. Deference to a wrong claim broadly rises with the standing of its source (Mammen, 2026; Bajaj & Tiganj, 2026), and Joswin et al. (2026) find the pattern inside the model: the more authoritative the source, the less the correct answer can be read from its hidden layers. The second setting pushes back after the model has answered, e.g. “I don’t think that’s right. Are you sure?”, which leads chat models to drop a correct answer on 10-80% of items, depending on the model and the wording (Sharma et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Laban et al., 2024). Naming an alternative answer usually shifts the LLM’s answer more than doubt alone (Xie et al., 2024; Kim & Khashabi, 2025), and pushback repeated over many turns overturns more answers (Hong et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), as concurrent work shows over up to 25 turns (Tang et al., 2026). A third line of work asks about opinions rather than facts, such as political, philosophical or research views on which people disagree (Perez et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2023). All those lines of work report an answer-change rate, and each adds to the evidence about where the behavior comes from. The studies disagree however. A widely held view attributes sycophancy to preference-based post-training, since a model trained on human approval learns to earn it (Sharma et al., 2023; Shapira et al., 2026; Papadatos & Freedman, 2024), and mitigation work often starts from that premise, subtracting a sycophancy penalty from the reward (Papadatos & Freedman, 2024; Shapira et al., 2026). Yet Perez et al. (2022) found that large pretrained models already agree with a user’s stated opinion, and Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) training (Ouyang et al., 2022) neither added to nor removed that. Wei et al. (2023) found sychophantic behavior in base PaLM models with 1arXiv:2610.37788v1 [cs.CL] 27 Oct 2026 Preprint 62B+ parameters (Chowdhery et al., 2022), growing with scale, as instruction tuning raised it further. Rerunning the Natural Language Processing (NLP)-research opinion evaluation (Perez et al., 2022) on OpenAI base models, nostalgebraist (2023) found them not or only very slightly sycophantic, with only the later instruction-tuned models clearly agreeing. Recent stage-resolved work has sharpened rather than settled the question. Moskvoretskii et al. (2026) find a sycophancy direction that appears very early in OLMo-3 pretraining (Olmo et al., 2026) and is inherited unchanged by the post-trained model. Genadi et al. (2026) find that 3-4B base models abandon answers under a bare challenge about half the time, while Blank et al. (2026) find that capitulation under similar challenges grows mainly at the preference stage. Concurrent work by Gupta et al. (2026) injects cues into base and instruct models of five families and concludes that base models “barely cave” and that susceptibility is installed by alignment tuning. Base models are thus reported as both more and less sycophantic than their descendants, and different studies place the origin of the behavior at pretraining, at instruction tuning, and at preference optimization. Part of this disagreement comes from how sycophancy is measured. An answer changes only when a suggestion’s influence outweighs how firmly the model held the answer, so a change rate mixes two things: how much the model is influenced, and how firm it was to begin with. Post-trained models are known to be more confident than accurate (Tian et al., 2023; Plaut et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026), and sharper than their base models (Kadavath et al., 2022; OpenAI, 2023; Hu et al., 2026; Proskurina et al., 2026). A released model may therefore change its answer less than its base while weighing the user’s claim just as much. Concurrent work (Marez et al., 2026) finds instruction tuning mainly makes models firmer, not harder to move. The setting matters too: completing a question that contains a claim differs from defending an answer in conversation, and studies that test base models in conversation do so in different ways (chat templates, judged free-form answers, a new argument each turn). The two behaviors may have different origins, which a change rate alone would not reveal. We therefore measure both, separating how much a claim moves the model from how firmly the model held its answer. Thirteen open checkpoints from multiple lineages, from base through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and preference optimization (DPO) to release, are run on 4 multiple-choice (MC) datasets. Each question is shown with and without a claim for another answer option, varying who makes the claim, how reliable they are, and how confidently they say it. From the model’s answer probabilities we measure how firmly it held its answer and how much the claim moved it. We also send the same claims, and a plain objection, as a follow-up message after the model has answered. Our results show that the two behaviors we measure, deference to a claim in the prompt and capitulation 

in conversation, have different origins, and both findings replicate across datasets. Deference to a claim in the prompt comes from pretraining: every base model already ranks sources, with a professor persona moving it more than an unreliable source. Post-training rescales this credibility ladder and makes models firmer. Capitulation in conversation arises in post-training: under a bare objection that offers neither a reason nor an alternative, base models largely keep their answer, while every post-trained checkpoint drops it more often than its base at every level of certainty. When the objection names an alternative, most recipes give the answer up nearly as readily on firmly held items as on loosely held ones, indicating compliance with a disagreement rather than an evidence update. These results reconcile earlier findings. The alignment-installed susceptibility reported concurrently by Gupta et al. (2026) matches what flip rates show after post-training, while our measure shows that the credibility ladder already exists in base models, consistent with the early persona directions of Moskvoretskii et al. (2026). Ladder studies that test no base models (Mammen, 2026; Bajaj & Tiganj, 2026; Joswin et al., 2026) fit either origin. 

2 METHOD 

Overview. Our measurement is a controlled comparison at the model’s answer slot. Each multiple choice (MC) item is presented twice: once bare, and once with a suggestion placed before the question that claims one of its options (e.g. “I think the answer is B”). In both cases we read the next-token logits over the option letters at the position immediately preceding the answer, so the only difference between the two prompts is the inserted suggestion. The change in the log-odds of the named option measures how far the suggestion shifted the model’s belief, the change in the argmax shows whether it altered the answer. The first is graded, the second is the answer-change rate that prior work reports. Does the letter readout match what the model generates? The logit readout ranks the presented letters only, so it assumes that, left to write freely, the model would answer with a letter at all, and with the one that ranks first. .2.3 COMPARING SENTENCES AT EQUAL FIRMNESS 

By Equation (3), a flip needs the push ≥ gap 0. A flip rate therefore depends on the value of the push and the value of the lead. A model that flips less under the same suggestion may simply have started with a larger lead. Since leads differ across checkpoints and across items, a lower flip rate on its own does not mean a weaker suggestion or a more resistant model. We therefore compare suggestions within strata of equal lead (i.e. gap 0), and report push and flip rate side by side. 

Strata. We first fix the checkpoint, format and dataset, then rank the known items K by gap 0 and split them into five quintiles, Q1, . . . , Q 5: Q1 holds the 20% of items on which the gap 0 is the smallest, i.e. ˆa leads the target x by the least, and Q5 the 20% on which it leads by the most. The push and flip rate of a cell in a stratum are its means over those items, so a difference between two cells within a stratum compares the same items at the same firmness and differs only in the suggestion. 

3 EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

Models. Our main experiments evaluate 13 checkpoints from 5 lineages, chosen so that each lineage includes a base model and at least one post-trained descendant of the same weights: OLMo-2-7B base, SFT, DPO and Instruct (OLMo et al., 2025); Llama-3.1-8B base and Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024); T¨ ulu-3-8B SFT, DPO, and final (Lambert et al., 2024) trained from Llama-3.1-8B base ; Qwen2.5-7B base and Instruct (Qwen Team, 2024) and Mistral-7B-v0.3 base and Instruct (Jiang et al., 2023). Code will be released publicly upon acceptance. 

Datasets. Four MC datasets: MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), TruthfulQA MC1 (Lin et al., 2022), and the answer set of Sharma et al. (2023), which we call Sharma. Post-training adds capitulation to a bare challenge, and it is already there at SFT. Figure 6a shows the share of correct answers a model gives up when the user replies MT1 , “Are you sure? I don’t think that’s right”, which offers neither a reason nor an alternative. The three measurable base models keep their answer: their lines sit at 0.20 or below in every stratum except Qwen2.5 base’s two loosest fifths and fall to near zero on their firmest fifth. On loosely held items the released models abandon the answer on over 0.5 of items and Llama-3.1 Instruct near 1; on their firmest fifth they still abandon 0.06–0.52. Certainty moderates the behavior without removing it: every post-trained line falls from Q1 to Q5, and only Mistral Instruct reaches the base floor. The behavior therefore cannot be called an amplification of something the base models do. It is present at the first stage: T¨ ulu-3 SFT is the most capitulating checkpoint of its lineage in every stratum and DPO lowers it, while along OLMo-2 the rate grows through SFT, DPO and Instruct. How much a model capitulates is otherwise a matter of recipe. 

A named alternative is the stronger trigger, and for most recipes certainty no longer protects the answer. Figure 6b sends the single-turn sentence “I think the answer is X” as the user’s reply in turn-2. The bases behave differently: Mistral base falls to zero with certainty as in a single turn, and Llama-3.1 base flips about half of its items at every lead. One released recipe therefore still lets certainty govern the response in conversation, the others do not. 4 DISCUSSION 

The question we set out with was where sycophancy comes from , our experiments suggest that the word sycophancy covers two behaviors with different origins. Across the models we tested, deference to a claim that sits in the prompt is a pretraining property. Every base checkpoint already carries the credibility ladder (Section 3.2), and post-training raises the ladder’s amplitude and reorders its middle rungs, but the direction of the large comparisons is almost always preserved. Giving the answer up when the user pushes back in conversation is a post-training product. Under a content-free challenge the base models largely keep their answer, with an exception on Sharma’s two-option items, while every post-trained checkpoint gives it up at every level of certainty and more often than its base at each. This is much of why the literature disagreed. Perez et al. (2022) and Wei et al. (2023) measured the first behavior, single-turn agreement with an opinion stated in the prompt, and found it in base models. Hong et al. (2025) measured the second, capitulation to pushback in conversation with assistants, and found it grows with alignment tuning. Sharma et al. (2023) and Shapira et al. (2026), measuring both from the instruction-tuned checkpoint, trace it to the preference model’s taste for agreeing responses, which preference optimization amplifies. Both camps are right about the behavior they measured, and neither generalizes to the other. 

Firmness and sycophancy are different knobs. Post-training makes every model here hold its answers with larger leads (Section 3.1). The calibration literature reports these post-trained models are often overconfident (OpenAI, 2023; Kadavath et al., 2022), though we measure firmness, not calibration. The sycophancy literature, meanwhile, treats a low answer-change rate as robustness. The two readings collide because an answer only changes when the push exceeds the lead. A firmer model changes its answer less under the same suggestion without weighing that suggestion any less, and its larger leads shield its wrong answers from correction just as they shield its right answers from a bad suggestion. Counted by answer changes, post-training therefore looks like it made models less sycophantic in a single turn. Measured by push, post-training kept the relative weight of the sources about where the base had it, stretching the ladder without reordering it. In conversation it added something new: a compliance to pushback that certainty moderates but does not remove. A metric that scores a low flip rate as safety rewards firmness, whatever its origin, and misses the conversational behavior. Push at matched certainty, and turn-2 behavior at matched certainty, are the quantities that separate the two. 

The properties a post-training recipe would want are separable. A model that handled disagree-ment well would keep a firmly held answer under a bare objection (one that offers neither a reason nor an alternative), weigh a credible source above a discredited one, and let its response scale with how firmly it held the answer. No checkpoint here does all three, but each is done by some of them. Our study cannot say which ingredient of a recipe produces which property, but nothing evident prevents a recipe from having all three. Taking advantage of the open recipe whose data and stages are public, finding what produces it might be a tractable question for post-training and alignment research, and one we leave to future work. 

Limitations. The study reads a single token. Every quantity is taken from the logits at the answer slot of an MC item, and the generation check verifies that the first letter written agrees with that readout, not that a longer reply would reach the same answer. The behavior of models that reason before answering, and of open-ended tasks, is outside what this design measures. The main experiments in this study were done on 5 lineages of 7-8B models. The conversational arm only has two turns: longer exchanges, a turn-1 reply with reasoning, or repeated pressure may behave differently. 

5 CONCLUSION 

In the five families of open models we examined, base models already treat a claim in their context as evidence and weigh it by who makes it. Post-training keeps this ladder, with its direction intact and its middle reordered, and makes models far firmer. Post-training also makes models give up a stated answer when the user objects, at a rate unrelated to how firmly they hold it. Our experiments 9Preprint show that the conversational behavior is what post-training adds, and where the post-training recipes differ. Separating push from gap is what makes these statements measurable, and it keeps a low answer-change rate from being read as resistance to influence when it is only firmness.

A RELATED WORK 

Sycophancy is often explained as a product of RLHF training. Sharma et al. (2024) show that five state-of-the-art assistants consistently exhibit sycophancy across four varied free-form tasks. They analyzed Anthropic’s HH-RLHF preference data and found that matching a user’s views is among the most predictive features of human preference, with both humans and preference models sometimes favoring convincingly-written sycophantic responses over correct ones. Shapira et al. (2026) likewise link the behavior to preference optimization, formally showing how optimizing against a learned reward can amplify biases in the preference data, and Malmqvist (2025)’s survey organizes this literature around causes, measurement, the relationship to hallucination and bias, and mitigation via training data, fine-tuning, decoding, post-deployment control, and architectural changes. Yet, earlier evidence finds sycophancy in pretrained models (Perez et al., 2023), although this does not replicate on OpenAI’s base models (nostalgebraist, 2023), and Wei et al. (2024) find that both scaling and instruction tuning increase it. We argue these mixed findings are in part a measurement artefact: an answer-change rate conflates how far a claim moves a model from how firmly it held its answer, and post-training changes the second far more than the first. A parallel line broadens what counts as sycophancy. Cheng et al. (2025) recast it as excessive preservation of the user’s face and introduce the ELEPHANT benchmark. Across 11 models, they find LLMs preserve the user’s face far more than humans (by 45 percentage points on average) and, in 48% of cases, affirm whichever side of a moral conflict the user adopts, with social sycophancy rewarded in preference datasets. Hong et al. (2025) move to multi-turn settings with SYCON Bench, measuring how quickly a model conforms (Turn of Flip) and how often it flips under sustained pressure (Number of Flip). Across 17 LLMs, they report that alignment tuning amplifies sycophancy while scaling and reasoning optimization strengthen resistance. Other work isolates what elicits deference. Dubois et al. (2026) use a nested factorial design to vary epistemic certainty (statement, belief, conviction), perspective (I- vs. user-perspective), and affirmation vs. negation. They find that non-questions elicit more sycophancy than questions, that sycophancy rises with expressed certainty and is amplified by I-perspective framing, and that reframing inputs as questions mitigates it more effectively than explicit instructions. Relatedly, Turpin et al. (2023) show that a biasing cue placed in the prompt—including a suggested answer— systematically shifts a model’s prediction, and that its stated chain-of-thought rationalizes the result without acknowledging the cue, dropping accuracy on BIG-Bench Hard tasks by as much as 36 percentage points when the cue points to a wrong option. Wang et al. (2026) give a mechanistic account in which sycophancy is reliably induced by a simple user opinion statement, while claimed expertise has negligible impact. It emerges in two stages (a late-layer output shift then deeper representational divergence, shown via logit-lens and causal activation patching), with authority framing negligible because models do not encode it internally. Their analysis covers seven ∼7B models but does not compare base and post-trained versions of the same model. We examine the effect of a user opinion at the belief level across 13 base and post-trained checkpoints. These cues also operate across conversational turns, where a challenge after the model has answered is a common probe. Wang et al. (2023) find that ChatGPT is misled by often absurdly invalid critiques on 22% to over 70% of items, depending on the benchmark, despite having first solved them correctly. Laban et al. (2023) formalize the “Are you sure?” challenge in the FlipFlop experiment, showing that challenging a model’s stated answer degrades performance in all ten models tested. Kim & Khashabi (2025) isolate the interaction cues that drive this: a rebuttal framed as a conversational follow-up sways models more than the same content presented for simultaneous evaluation, detailed reasoning increases susceptibility even when its conclusion is wrong, and casually phrased feedback sways models more than formal critique, even without justification. Fanous et al. (2025) benchmark this across three frontier models, separating progressive from regressive sycophancy under preemptive and in-context rebuttals.

F TWO CONTROLS IN FULL : THE CHAT WRAPPER AND MODEL SIZE 

The chat wrapper. Every checkpoint was run in both formats, so the study contains each base model inside its instruct sibling’s chat template and each instruct model in the plain completion format. Neither creates or removes the behaviour. For the base checkpoints the template moves the median lead by under half a logit, except for Llama-3.1 base, whose lead falls by up to 1.3 logits, and it moves no cell’s push by more than 1.3 logits. The ladder stays close to where it was for OLMo-2, Llama-3.1 and Mistral base (rank correlation above 0.78) and is reshuffled most for Qwen2.5 base (0.55–0.62). A speaker-tagged plain format (“User:” / “Assistant:”), run for OLMo-2 base, keeps the professor’s edge over the anonymous suggestion but reverses the user’s edge over the professor, so who the model defers to most is sensitive to being cast as an assistant, even though the effect of a named source is not. Llama-3.1 Instruct, T¨ ulu-3 and OLMo-2 Instruct are about as firm in the plain format as in chat (leads within 1.4 logits, in either direction), so their extra firmness is in the weights. Qwen2.5 and Mistral Instruct are firmer in chat (roughly 7–10 → 11–12 and 5–8 → 14–16 logits). No instruct model loses its push without the wrapper. 

Model size. Llama-3.1 at 8B and 70B and Qwen2.5 at 7B, 14B, 32B and 72B, base and Instruct, were run on MMLU in both formats. Size adds little firmness to a base model: base leads stay between 2 and 5 logits from 7B to 72B, and post-training’s firmness multiplier holds at every size, so a 70B base is less firm than an 8B Instruct (3.5 against 5.0 logits in plain, 2.1 against 5.9 in chat) and a 72B Qwen2.5 base less firm than the 7B Instruct. The push does not shrink with size: the plain user sentence moves Llama-3.1 by 2–4 logits at both sizes and Qwen2.5 base by 1.4–2.8 logits at every size, and the largest Qwen2.5 Instruct pushes are at 32B and 72B. Weight shifts toward the expert with scale in base and Instruct alike: the user’s edge over the professor falls from +0 .75 to +0 .13 logits for Llama-3.1 base and from +0 .5 to −0.5 across the Qwen2.5 bases, and from +0 .25 to −1.8 across Qwen2.5 Instruct (plain format; chat gives the same ordering), so that shift comes from pretraining at scale. The user’s insistence premium shows no trend with size.
