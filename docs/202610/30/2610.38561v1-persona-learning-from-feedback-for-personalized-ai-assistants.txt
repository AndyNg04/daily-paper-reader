# ABSTRACT 

Personalizing AI assistants requires adapting their behavior to users’ prefer-ences and task contexts. Existing work elicits assistant personas through persona prompting or finetuning on persona-conditioned generations. However, persona descriptions alone do not identify what to change or preserve in a particular re-sponse, or which behaviors suit the current request. We introduce Persona Learn-ing from Feedback (PLF) , a framework that learns reusable, preference-grounded assistant personas as independent LoRA adapters on a shared frozen backbone. During training, a frozen self-teacher conditions on persona specifications and response-specific feedback to provide token-level supervision on each adapter’s own generated prefixes. A feedback gate focuses corrective updates on responses judged to need improvement. At deployment, a supervised router uses the request or dialogue to select a single adapter or a fixed composition, without user-specific finetuning. We evaluate PLF on single-turn personalization with model-generated critiques and multi-turn tutoring with simulated students’ reactions. Across both settings, PLF improves model-judged preference fit over fixed-persona policies and strong baselines including persona prompting and persona distillation, demon-strating how feedback-trained behaviors can be reused for adaptive assistant per-sonalization without degrading general capabilities. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Personal AI agents are designed to support users across ongoing tasks and goals, using context ac-cumulated through interaction. Effective assistance in these settings depends not only on what users want to accomplish, but also on how they prefer to be helped (Kirk et al., 2024; Sorensen et al., 2024). The same user may want a concise, actionable recommendation when making an urgent decision, but a detailed explanation when exploring an unfamiliar topic. We study this behavioral dimension of personalization: adapting how an assistant helps to the preferences and task require-ments expressed in the current request or dialogue. We represent reusable patterns of assistance through assistant personas . Here, a persona specifies preferred response behaviors and behaviors to avoid, such as how much detail to provide or whether to explain a solution directly or guide the user toward it. We ground persona specifications in user preferences, allowing behaviors to be reused across users and tasks and combined when a request calls for multiple preferences (Jang et al., 2023). For example, a user may prefer an answer that is both warm and concise. Existing work elicits assistant personas through role or style prompting (Xu et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2024) and finetuning on persona-conditioned generations (Maiya et al., 2026), with recent ap-proaches also selecting personas according to task utility (Hu et al., 2026). Personalization methods further incorporate retrieved user history (Salemi et al., 2024), learn from heterogeneous user feed-back (Li et al., 2025), or infer response preferences from interaction history (Spohn et al., 2026). These approaches can specify or infer desired behaviors, but a persona description alone does not identify which parts of a particular response should be revised or preserved. For example, when a user requests concise troubleshooting advice, one draft may bury the solution in unnecessary back-ground, while another may omit an essential step. Natural-language feedback can supply these response-specific corrections. Prior work uses critiques to refine outputs (Madaan et al., 2023; Aky¨ urek et al., 2023) or converts textual feedback into training signals through self-distillation (H¨ ubotter et al., 2026). For personalization, however, the desired correction is itself preference-dependent: shortening an answer may improve it for one request while making another less useful. This creates two complementary problems: learning reusable personas from preference-dependent, response-specific feedback, and selecting the appropriate persona or combination from the evidence available before each response. We introduce Persona Learning from Feedback (PLF) , a framework that separates feedback-based persona learning from request-specific persona selection (Figure 1). Each persona is represented by an independent LoRA adapter on a shared frozen backbone (Hu et al., 2022), making its learned pa-rameter updates separately selectable and composable. During training, an adapter generates its own responses and receives feedback evaluated against its intended persona or the corresponding user preference. A feedback gate focuses corrective updates on responses judged to need improvement. A frozen self-teacher conditions on the persona specification and feedback to provide token-level supervision on the student’s own generated prefixes (Agarwal et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026). The adapter thus learns to express its persona without an additional persona prompt at inference. Af-ter persona learning, the adapters are frozen and a supervised router identifies the desired personas from preference evidence expressed in the request or dialogue. A single selected persona activates its adapter and paired selections use a signed weight-space composition with coefficients chosen on validation data. This allows the same adapters to serve different users and requests without per-user finetuning or separate adapters for every persona combination. We evaluate PLF in single-turn personalization and multi-turn tutoring. The single-turn setting uses PRISM requests with assigned preferences grounded in recurring patterns among training users, and model-generated critiques provide response-specific supervision (Kirk et al., 2024). The tutoring setting uses simulated students’ subsequent replies as feedback on preceding tutor responses, start-ing from openings in the Education Dialogue Dataset (Shani et al., 2024). We compare PLF with fixed-persona policies and persona prompting, alongside two training alternatives: persona distil-lation and feedback imitation. We also examine reuse under combined preferences and preference changes during dialogue. Ablations study the effects of feedback gating and the distillation objec-2Preprint. Under Review. tive. We measure model-judged preference fit in both settings. In tutoring, we additionally report simulated student satisfaction and independently judged factual errors. Across both settings, PLF improves average model-judged preference fit over strong baselines. Adapter composition improves preference fit over individual heads for selected preference pairs, and the router can reuse the same heads as preferences change during dialogue. Our contributions are as follows: • We formulate assistant personalization as learning reusable, preference-grounded personas from response-specific feedback and selecting them using observable context, without per-user finetuning. • We introduce PLF, which learns independent persona adapters through feedback-gated on-policy self-distillation and reuses them through supervised routing and signed composition. • We demonstrate improved preference fit over fixed-persona policies, persona prompting, persona distillation, and feedback imitation in both single-turn personalization and multi-turn tutoring, and analyze combined preferences, preference changes during dialogue, and training ablations. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Assistant personas and user personalization. Persona and role prompting can steer LLM be-havior through instructions about role, expertise, style, or interaction strategy, though effects vary across models and tasks (Xu et al., 2023; Salewski et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). Such behaviors can also be internalized through training. Open Character Training learns persistent character traits from preference data and synthetic dialogues (Maiya et al., 2026), while Hu et al. (2026) identify a trade-off between persona expression and task accuracy and introduce PRISM to selectively activate a distilled persona adapter. Complementary work personalizes behavior using user information. LaMP retrieves from user history (Salemi et al., 2024), Personalized-RLHF learns from heterogeneous user feedback (Li et al., 2025), and APM evaluates preference inference from interaction history while reducing reliance on stereotypical associations (Spohn et al., 2026). To-gether, these works separate eliciting a behavioral tendency from deciding when it should be used. 

Learning from feedback and on-policy distillation. Natural-language feedback can improve model behavior through inference-time critique and revision (Madaan et al., 2023; Aky¨ urek et al., 2023), or through training on feedback-informed revisions and optimization signals derived from critiques (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). On-policy distillation provides teacher supervision on student-generated trajectories, reducing mismatch between training data and the policy’s own outputs (Agarwal et al., 2024). Methods condition the teacher on privileged information unavail-able to the deployed student (Zhao et al., 2026), including textual feedback in SDPO and RLTF-SD (H¨ ubotter et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026a). Related work applies feedback learning to person-alization by distilling information from subsequent user messages (Buening et al., 2026) or using natural-language feedback to improve personalized question answering (Salemi & Zamani, 2026). 

Modular personalization and routing. Parameter-efficient and modular adaptation offers an al-ternative to representing all preferences in a single policy. Personalized Soups trains models for dif-ferent preference dimensions and combines them through post-hoc parameter merging (Jang et al., 2023), while mixture-of-experts methods select or combine LoRA modules (Wu et al., 2024). For personalized generation, PROPER uses group-level LoRA adaptations with routing over shared and user-specific preferences (Zhang et al., 2025), and CARD combines cluster-level LoRA adapta-tion with lightweight user-specific personalization (Song et al., 2026b). These approaches motivate modular representations for heterogeneous users and preferences, while leaving open when modu-larization is preferable to a shared conditional policy. 

# 3 METHOD 

Persona Learning from Feedback (PLF) separates learning how to realize reusable personas in in-dividual responses from selecting the appropriate personas for each request or interaction. Persona specifications define the intended behaviors, response-specific feedback guides their learning, and a supervised router selects personas from the observable request or dialogue at deployment. Let q denote the context available before an assistant response, including the current request and any preceding dialogue. PLF assumes a fixed catalog P = {Pk}Kk=1 , where each Pk specifies a reusable assistance behavior associated with a user preference profile. Each persona is represented by an independent LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2022), called a persona head , with parameters θk on a shared frozen base model π0, yielding policy πk. Separate adapters keep the learned parameter updates independently selectable and composable. All adapters start with zero weight updates, so πk = π0

before training, and each learns its assigned behavior across training contexts. We withhold persona descriptions from the student prompt to train the adapters to express their behaviors without persona prompting. The descriptions instead guide feedback and teacher supervision. At deployment, both the router and the response generator receive q, without a separately supplied preference profile. 3.1 RESPONSE -S PECIFIC FEEDBACK 

A persona specification describes the intended behavior but does not identify how a particular re-sponse should change. We obtain this response-specific guidance from feedback evaluated against the persona or its corresponding user preference. For persona k, the current policy samples 

y ∼ πk(· | q) and receives natural-language feedback f and an update gate g ∈ { 0, 1} (Agarwal et al., 2024). 

Single-turn critiques. A critic observes (Pk, q, y ) and identifies changes needed to express the intended persona while preserving useful content and satisfying the task. Its critique supplies f . It may instead report that the response needs no revision. 

Conversational reactions. In multi-turn scenario, the adapter interacts with a user simulator con-ditioned on the learning preference corresponding to Pk. The user’s next reply supplies f directly, without a separate textual critique. The assistant response and user reaction are also used to de-termine the update gate. The reaction is unavailable when generating y, but becomes part of the dialogue context for the next response. 

Feedback gating. We focus corrective supervision on responses that still need improvement, rather than treating satisfactory responses as further revision targets. We set g = 0 when the feedback procedure accepts a response without further correction, and g = 1 otherwise. Critic outputs with no usable feedback also receive g = 0 . Both sources yield examples (q, y, f, g ) for the same gated learning procedure. Feedback prompts and judgment criteria are specified in Section 4.1. 3.2 LEARNING PERSONAS FROM FEEDBACK

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate Persona Learning from Feedback (PLF) in single-turn personalization and multi-turn tutoring. We ask whether natural language feedback produces useful persona heads, whether those heads support combined or changing preferences, and how the training design affects their behavior. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Single-turn setting: preferences in a request. We use opening requests from PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024). Following Kirk et al. (2026), recurring survey preferences among training users motivate two persona axes: warm versus impersonal voice, and concise versus thorough depth. They capture common differences in desired tone and detail, while permitting combinations such as warm and concise. We split requests by user and remove exact and near-duplicate tasks across splits, yielding 6,400 training, 203 validation, and 500 test requests. For routing and evaluation, we minimally rewrite each request to express one of four individual personas or four cross-axis pairs (Spohn et al., 2026). The assigned preference, rather than the user’s original survey response, is the evaluation target. During head training, a Qwen3.5-27B critic reads the original request, the head’s answer, and its persona card, then explains what to change and preserve. 

Multi-turn setting: preferences revealed through interaction. We use teacher openings from the Education Dialogue Dataset (Shani et al., 2024): 4,524 training, 200 validation, and 500 test dialogues, with topics held out across splits. All subsequent turns are generated. Our personas are 

direct instruction , which explains and demonstrates, and interactive guidance , which asks questions and lets the student reason. A Qwen3.5-27B student simulator holds a hidden preference and reacts to each tutor turn. The following student’s turn is the feedback, rather than a separate critique. 

Training. For PLF, we freeze the backbone and train one LoRA head per persona in both settings, using Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al., 2025) for single-turn responses and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) for tutoring. The frozen base, conditioned on the persona card and feedback, teaches the head on its own sampled response prefixes through forward KL distillation. A no-change feedback gate excludes satisfactory responses from the loss. We select single-turn heads on validation and use the final heads from the tutoring run. After freezing the heads, we train MLP routers on the backbone’s last-token representation: the single-turn router selects persona indicators from the request, while the tutoring router selects a head after each student reply. Data, training settings, and prompts details can be found in Appendices B–D.

Learned heads improve on both fixed personas, prompting, and other learning baselines. In single-turn personalization, PLF is preferred over every fixed head and the base model (Figure 2a). No one persona serves the heterogeneous test preferences as well as selecting and combining behav-iors. PLF also achieves a 61.7% win rate against persona prompting with the same router, 59.6% against persona distillation, and 54.4% against feedback imitation. The gain over persona distillation shows the value of response-specific feedback beyond persona-conditioned supervision, while the comparison with feedback imitation favors PLF’s on-policy objective over learning from complete teacher-generated responses. The prompting comparison holds preference selection fixed, show-ing that learned heads add value beyond identifying a suitable persona description. The smaller advantage over persona prompting than over fixed heads is consistent with preference matching contributing substantially to the full system’s gain.

PLF also improves over persona distillation with a 56.2% win rate, while feedback imitation remains competitive. Single-turn experiment: combine voice and depth. We test composition on requests expressing both a voice and a depth preference. Heads are trained only on individual personas; the router sees both single and paired labels during training. At inference, we combine adapter weights using a validation-selected rule that adjusts voice and depth strength separately (Appendix E.4). Table 1 isolates composition on correctly routed requests. 

Composition enables learned personas to serve combined preferences. Warm-concise gives the clearest improvement over both constituent heads, combining brevity with encouragement. Impersonal-thorough improves over both heads, while warm-thorough improves over warm. The limited gains for impersonal-concise suggest that composition is most effective when behaviors are complementary. The warm-concise advantage persists when routing errors are included. On valida-tion, calibrating voice and depth weights outperforms simple summation or averaging, with weaker voice weights selected for thorough pairs. The results show that independently learned personas can be reused to serve combined preferences without training separate adapters for each combination. 

Multi-turn experiment: respond to a preference switch. For each test opening, the student’s preference switches once, in both directions, at a uniformly sampled turn j ∈ { 2, . . . , 7}. The switch is unannounced: its first evidence is the student’s reply to turn j, so the router can first respond at 

j + 1 . The router is frozen and was trained only on fixed-preference dialogues. We compare it with fixed heads, the base, and an oracle that knows the new preference in advance and switches at turn 

j. The judge sees both preferences and the marked switch point. 

Routing generalizes to changing preferences. Although trained only on fixed-preference dia-logues, PLF successfully transfers to settings where students change their preferred teaching style during a conversation. PLF is preferred over both fixed persona heads and the base model in both switch directions (Table 2). It also achieves the highest whole-lesson satisfaction among non-oracle policies (76.7%), approaching the oracle (79.2%) that knows the preference changes in advance (Figure 3a). Notably, satisfaction rebounds at j + 1 , the first turn at which the router can use student feedback reflecting the new preference (Figure 3b). Feedback gating improves preference fit through selective updates. Feedback gating consistently benefits single-turn personalization, achieving a 61% win rate over ungated training at matched checkpoints, with improvements across both single-persona and paired-preference requests. In multi-turn tutoring, gating also improves the interactive persona, achieving a 55% win rate while en-couraging more question-asking and reducing complaints about repetitive responses. The preference advantage persists on held-out evaluations even as earlier training gains diminish, while the direct persona remains near parity. Interestingly, gated and ungated models achieve similar critic approval rates despite their differences in preference fit. This suggests that updating on every response is not necessarily beneficial: selectively focusing learning on responses that require correction can better preserve and refine the desired persona behavior. Additional analyses are provided in Appendix F. 

KL direction has persona-dependent effects. At the same step checkpoints, forward KL achieves a 55% preference win rate against reverse KL over 499 routed single-turn requests. The advantage is strongest for impersonal-only and impersonal–thorough requests (68% and 69%). Across all re-quests involving each preference, forward KL also benefits the thorough preference, while warm and concise remain inconclusive. Under reconstructed teacher contexts, reverse-KL heads have lower entropy, closer to the teacher’s, while forward KL retains more probability mass on hedge terms. Forward-KL impersonal responses use more hedging while reverse-KL thorough responses are longer, but this additional length does not correspond to a preference advantage under the up-dated judge. These associations suggest that KL direction can affect persona fit beyond top-token agreement. Additional analyses are in Appendix G. 4.5 GENERAL CAPABILITY RETENTION 

To assess whether improvements in personalization compromise general capabilities, we evaluate the routed systems on IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), and MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023). Table 4 shows that PLF performs compara-bly to the base model across all four benchmarks in both settings, with no statistically significant degradation. Unlike feedback imitation, PLF does not exhibit a significant MMLU-Pro drop in single-turn personalization; it also achieves the highest observed MT-Bench score in multi-turn tu-toring. Together with its preference gains, these results show that PLF improves personalization while retaining strong general-purpose performance.

We introduced Persona Learning from Feedback (PLF), which learns reusable persona adapters from response-specific feedback through on-policy self-distillation and selects or combines them using contextual preference evidence. The learned personas also support combined and changing preferences. This section describes the data, PLF training configurations, routing procedures, and evaluation pro-tocol used in Section 4. Each persona head is an independent LoRA adapter on a frozen backbone. Both settings use rank 32, LoRA scale αL = 64 , no adapter dropout, and adapters on all attention and MLP projections. Heads sample at temperature 0.7 and are trained with AdamW at learning rate 10 −5 and 3% warmup. Data source and preference screening. We use the opening requests and survey prefer-ences from PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024), joining conversations.opening prompt and 

survey.system string by user id . Only the first user message is retained from each con-versation. It contains 8,011 conversations from 1,396 users who entered the conversation phase. Preferences are manually screened for whether a single response from a model without tools can sat-isfy them. We exclude 198 users whose preferences request sourcing or verification and 31 whose preferences primarily concern unavailable capabilities, such as cross-conversation memory, brows-ing, or real-world actions, and therefore retain 1,167 users. 

Persona catalog. Following the use of group-level preferences in Kirk et al. (2026), we code the re-tained preferences for response style, excluding topics, capability requests, and shared requirements such as accuracy, honesty, safety, and respect. Co-occurring style codes define warm and impersonal voice groups and concise and thorough depth groups. A user is assigned by the style they emphasize most, or by the first secondary style associated with a group when the primary style has no group. Users with no grouped style enter a base group, while a separate clarifying-question group is too small to train as a persona. Group summaries use only training users, with 254 warm, 326 impersonal, 127 concise, and 75 thorough users. Each summary retains response-style requirements expressed by at least 20% of the group’s training users and omits clauses that cannot be judged from the response without knowing the correct content. The resulting persona cards each describe one axis and are fixed before the reported training and evaluation. Their exact wording is given in Appendix C.1. The original sur-vey preferences and group summaries are not supplied as separate conditioning text to the student, teacher, critic, or router. The teacher and critic receive the target persona card instead.

The 239 users inspected during design are excluded from validation and test. We remove contentless openings and exact duplicates. Near duplicates have token-set Jaccard similarity at least 0.90, or at least 0.75 with containment at least 0.95 and at least five tokens in the shorter request. Candidate pairs are blocked using tokens with document frequency at most 100 and grouped into connected components; cross-split matches are excluded without moving requests between splits. Data and simulated students. We use the Education Dialogue Dataset, which contains 8,000 Gemini-generated teacher–student dialogues across 77 topics (Shani et al., 2024). Filtering for dialogues that begin with a teacher turn, contain at least four turns, and carry one of four student-reaction annotations yields 5,350 candidates. After removing one topic with a corrupted name, we split by topic into 4,524 training dialogues across 65 topics, 200 validation dialogues across three topics, and 500 test dialogues across eight topics. The test topics span literature, biology, physics, politics, and history. Only the opening teacher turn is retained, with string-escaping artifacts re-moved. All subsequent student and tutor turns are generated, and the dataset’s original preference and reaction annotations are not used. Each simulated student is assigned a hidden preference for direct instruction or interactive guidance and a reaction style chosen from frustrated, rude, anxious, and disengaged. The reaction style re-mains fixed within a dialogue and shapes responses to unsatisfactory tutoring. The preference also remains fixed except in the switching evaluation. During head training, the reply to the dataset open-ing is preference-neutral. During router training and evaluation, the student expresses its assigned preference from that first reply onward. The student simulator uses Qwen3.5-27B with reasoning disabled. Its prompt templates are provided in Appendix D.3. 

Head training. The backbone is Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, with 83.9M trainable parameters per adapter. Each head trains against students whose hidden preference matches its intended behav-ior. Training consists of 100 rollout cycles. In each cycle, each head tutors 32 training openings for eight generated turns, yielding 256 tutor turns per head. Tutor turns are capped at 256 tokens. Batches contain 64 tutor turns, giving eight optimizer batches across the two heads per cycle. The learning rate is constant after the 3% warmup, and evaluation uses the final heads. A tutoring cycle therefore differs from a single-turn training batch in both the number of examples and the number of optimization steps. The student’s next reply supplies the feedback for the preceding tutor turn. The teacher treats this reply as a reaction to an earlier attempt and answers the original student message again, without receiving the sampled tutor response as a draft to copy. The exact framing is given in Appendix D.2. 

Router. Router features are the final-layer hidden state at the last token of the chat-templated tutor context, computed with adapters disabled and standardized using training statistics. A three-layer MLP of width 512 predicts the student preference using the categorical cross-entropy objective in Eq. 3. The router always selects one of the two heads. Its data collection crosses both generating heads with both student types on 100 training openings, yielding 400 dialogues. Each dialogue contributes nine prefixes ending at student replies, for 3,600 labeled examples. Crossing heads and preferences prevents the generating head from determining the target label. The number of training epochs is selected using 20 held-out training openings, without using validation or test topics for fitting or epoch selection. The router is frozen before both fixed-preference and preference-switching evaluation. 18 Preprint. Under Review. 

Human calibration of the student simulator. Before fixing the simulator prompt, we use 50 held-out tutor–student contexts to check whether simulated reactions are consistent with the assigned direct-instruction or interactive-guidance preference. Three human annotators independently judge preference consistency, and we select the prompt configuration with the highest majority agreement. These examples are disjoint from the reported test set, and the simulator prompt is frozen before model training and evaluation. B.3 EVALUATION AND STATISTICAL REPORTING 

Policies and generation. We compare PLF with the frozen base, each fixed persona head, and the three baselines defined in Section 4.1. Persona prompting uses the same router as PLF but supplies the selected persona descriptions to the frozen base instead of activating adapters. For paired single-turn requests, all trained systems use the composition rule in Appendix E.4. The tutoring oracle selects the head matching the hidden preference and, in switching evaluations, knows each change before generating the switch-turn response. Single-turn responses use greedy decoding. For tutoring, all policies receive the same test opening and the same initial student reply, while subsequent turns are sampled independently for each policy. Each dialogue contains eight generated tutor turns at temperature 0.7, with at most 256 tokens per tutor turn and 128 tokens per student reply. Both student types use the same 500 test openings. 

Pairwise preference fit. The preference judge, gpt-6-luna , supplies no training feedback. It receives the assigned preference and compares either two single-turn responses or two complete tutoring dialogues in both presentation orders. A win or loss requires the two orders to agree on the winner. Order disagreements, explicit ties, and identical single-turn responses count as ties. Switching evaluations also treat conflicting before- and after-switch verdicts as ties. For counts of wins W , ties T , and losses L, the reported preference win rate is (W + T / 2) /(W + T + L).All reported confidence intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals. Single-turn preference in-tervals use 4,000 resamples of requests. Fixed-preference tutoring intervals use 4,000 resamples of the 500 openings, retaining both student types together and weighting the types equally. Preference-switching win-rate intervals use 2,000 resamples of openings, retaining both switch directions to-gether. Thus, the two types or directions for one opening are not treated as independent units in the aggregate preference intervals. 

Tutoring satisfaction and factual errors. We complement pairwise preference fit with satisfaction and factual-error rates in simulated tutoring. Satisfaction is the fraction of replies to generated tutor turns that are not flagged as objections; the shared reply to the dataset opening is excluded. Factual-error rate is the fraction of generated tutor turns flagged by the fact-checking judge. These auxiliary judges use Qwen3.5-27B with reasoning disabled and greedy decoding. The satisfaction metric uses an objection criterion related to the training gate, while factual errors are assessed directly from tutor turns. Paired differences in satisfaction and factual-error rates use 2,000 bootstrap resamples of openings under fixed preferences and of opening–direction pairs under switching preferences. Switching satisfaction intervals use the latter units as well; policies remain paired within each unit. 

Human calibration of the preference judge. We calibrate the pairwise preference judge on 50 held-out response pairs labeled independently by three human annotators. We select the judge prompt with the highest agreement with the human majority judgment and freeze it before the re-ported test evaluation.
