# MPBRS: Multi-Potential-Based Reward Shaping for Unified Reward Configuration in Humanoid Locomotion 

Van Chung Nguyen 1, and Hung Manh La 1

Abstract — Designing and tuning reward functions remains one of the main challenges in building effective reinforce-ment learning (RL) pipelines for humanoid locomotion. While shaping rewards can accelerate learning, naively formulated terms often conflict with the desired behavior, overfit to a specific robot, or become unstable under small weight changes. Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) offers an alternative solution: shaping the reward via differences of a potential function to guide exploration without changing the optimal policy. In this paper, we study Multi-PBRS for humanoid locomotion and propose a unified reward configuration. We then evaluate the same reward structure and weights across nine humanoid robots, requiring only robot-specific metadata (joint/link mappings and default poses) rather than per-robot reward redesign. Our experiments show that, compared to standard shaping, MPBRS provides more consistent scaling behavior and reduced tuning sensitivity across robots, making it easier to tune without altering the optimal policy. 

I. INTRODUCTION Recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has become one of the most widely used approaches for humanoid locomotion. The use of RL for humanoid locomotion is due to its ability to learn coordinated, whole-body behaviors that are difficult to design by hand [1], [2]. However, despite the success of RL in humanoid locomotion, its application depends heavily on the reward function design [1], [2]. The design of the reward function greatly affects the behavior of the humanoid robot. For example, when the reward is specified in a naive way-such as a quadratic tracking error or a sparse task-success signal-the agent may learn strategies that maximize the reward while producing unintended behaviors that do not match the designer’s intent [3], [4]. Conversely, well-shaped rewards can substantially speed up learning, whereas poorly scaled or conflicting terms can cause brittle behaviors, reward hacking, unstable training, or policies that only work for a single morphology and fail under small changes in dynamics or sensing. As a result, developers often spend significant time tuning reward terms and weights to reach an acceptable balance between training stability and behavioral quality. This tuning process is further complicated by the sensitivity of humanoid RL to hyperparameters and reward scaling, which makes it difficult to isolate the effect of individual reward terms and tune them systematically. Consequently, many locomotion pipelines rely on extensive reward design and per-robot tuning: weights are repeatedly adjusted until training stabilizes and the resulting gait appears plausible. Unfortunately, this workflow is time-consuming, hard to reproduce, and often does not transfer even between similar humanoids. A reward that works well on one robot may require significant modification on another due to differences in morphology, actuation, or physical design. In [1], Ng, Harada, and Russell proposed a class of shaping rewards defined by differences of a potential function that is policy-invariant in discounted MDPs. This method has appeared in many researchers because it can provide richer learning signals while leaving the baseline task objective unchanged, effectively separating task specification from convergence acceleration. Later, several follow-up works [5]–[7] examined PBRS in RL and concluded that it leads to faster learning. However, this evidence is largely drawn from grid-worlds or other low-dimensional domains. Meanwhile, high-performing RL results in robotics-especially for legged locomotion-still de-pend on straightforward, manually tuned shaping rewards that provide no policy-invariance guarantees [8]. In this paper, we examine deep RL locomotion in 3D under a unified reward design and test how multi-PBRS compares to standard shaping when deployed unchanged on nine humanoid robots. Rather than delivering large im-provements in convergence rate, MPBRS primarily improves training stability and portability, showing lower sensitivity to weight selection and reward magnitude. A. Reward shaping for locomotion 

Recently, high-performing RL locomotion systems often rely on direct reward shaping (DRS). The DRS is a dense terms that encode task-relevant objectives such as velocity tracking, uprightness, base-height regulation, and regulariza-tion penalties on energy or action changes [9]–[12]. The DRS is attractive because they are simple to compute and do not require external demonstrations, but their effectiveness depends strongly on careful scaling and balancing of terms. In humanoid locomotion, small changes in reward weights can produce different behaviors, ranging from overly con-servative gaits to unstable oscillations. The small changes in reward weight can lead to solutions that exploit a proxy objective rather than the intended task [13]–[16]. A second common approach is imitation learning, which provides dense rewards by tracking reference signals . Im-itation learning strongly encourages the robot to replicate the desired behavior. In physics-based character control, imitation and reference tracking have been particularly suc-cessful for producing coordinated whole-body motion and locomotion skills [17]–[20]. In robotics-oriented biped learn-ing, iterative design pipelines often incorporate structured objectives and reference-like heuristics (e.g., posture tar-gets, height/orientation regulation, cadence-related signals) and emphasize that reward shaping and tuning are major determinants of training stability and final gait quality [21], [22]. While imitation learning can substantially accelerate learning, it introduces an additional design burden: the choice of reference quantities and their weights can bias solutions toward reproducing the reference rather than solving the underlying task, and these choices frequently do not transfer cleanly across morphologies [23]–[25]. This lack of portabil-ity motivates shaping methods that provide dense guidance while reducing sensitivity to hand-tuned scaling. 

B. Potential-based reward shaping (PBRS) 

Ng, Harada, and Russell [1] proposed potential-based reward shaping (PBRS), showing that shaping rewards con-structed in this way preserve the set of optimal policies in discounted MDPs. This property has appealed to many researchers, as it suggests a principled separation between (i) a base reward that expresses the task objective and (ii) an auxiliary shaping signal that improves the robot’s behaviour without altering an optimal solution. Wiewiora [5] further showed that PBRS is closely related to Q-value initialization, providing intuition for why PBRS can speed up early learn-ing: an informative potential effectively redistributes long-horizon value into denser step-wise feedback. Subsequent work has explored PBRS in broader settings (e.g., episodic tasks and learned or dynamic potentials) and generally re-ports faster convergence in low-dimensional environments such as gridworlds or classic control [26], [27]. For humanoid locomotion, however, the primary practical benefit of PBRS appears to differ from the common expec-tation of large convergence gains. Jeon et al. [4] benchmark PBRS-style shaping in humanoid locomotion and find that improvements in convergence speed can be modest, while PBRS terms are notably more robust to reward scaling than typical direct shaping. This motivates treating PBRS not only as a tool for faster learning, but as a mechanism for 

reducing tuning sensitivity and improving reward portability. Building on this perspective, our work evaluates a single unified multi-PBRS reward configuration reused across nine humanoid robots, with the goal of measuring how MPBRS affects robustness to scaling and the degree of per-robot reward retuning required. Compared to (4), PBRS shifts the learning signal from ‘being in a good state’ to ‘moving toward a better state.’ In particular, the shaping contribution is near zero when the potential is stationary and becomes positive only when the next state increases the potential. This transition-based structure tends to produce shaping signals that are less dominated by absolute offsets and more directly tied to action consequences, which can make learning less brittle when potentials differ in baseline magnitude across morphologies. From this perspective, PBRS is appealing to researchers who seek to preserve optimal policies while improving learning through the reshaping of intermediate rewards [1]. In practice, however, PBRS does not automatically guarantee faster convergence in high-dimensional locomotion: when the potentials are imperfect or only weakly predictive of long-horizon success, PBRS may provide only modest ac-celeration. Empirical benchmarking in humanoid locomotion suggests that a more consistent practical advantage of PBRS is improved robustness to scaling and weight selection, which reduces the tuning burden even when convergence speed gains are marginal [4]. 

c) Multi-PBRS: Humanoid locomotion typically re-quires shaping multiple aspects of behavior simultaneously (tracking, posture, upright, and symmetry). For this reason, we adopt multi-PBRS , in which several complementary po-tentials are shaped in parallel using (5). Concretely, our unified configuration uses three potentials shared across all robots: (i) a base-height potential computed from the ray-based ground distance, (ii) an uprightness potential computed from the gravity vector. IV. SYSTEM OVERVIEW To examine whether MPBRS remains advantageous in continuous-control, contact-rich, and high-dimensional lo-comotion, we evaluate it within a single unified training pipeline spanning nine simulated humanoid robots with sub-stantially different morphologies. Rather than redesigning re-wards per robot, we hold the learning setup fixed and isolate the effect of shaping by directly comparing the DRS against the MPBRS terms under the same task objectives. This experimental design prioritizes cross-morphology generality: any observed performance differences can be attributed to the shaping formulation rather than to robot-specific reward engineering.

For learning running locomotion, we fix the arm joints at nominal angles and reduce control of the system to only the legs and the waist yaw. If the waist has roll, pitch, and yaw joints, we set the waist roll and pitch to their default values and control only the yaw.

C. Training setup 

For the training setup, we define the observation space as shown in Table II. Its dimension is 18 + 3 Na, where 

Na depends on the humanoid robot type. We design the observations to minimize reliance on external sensors, which simplifies future real-world deployment. To model sensor imperfections during training, we corrupt the observations with uniformly sampled noise (Table II). This helps the policy become more robust to measurement errors. For the humanoid reward design, the baseline locomotion reward is defined in Table III. In this baseline, only the first two terms-linear velocity tracking and yaw-rate tracking-are directly task-related. The remaining terms act as regularizers to promote smoother motion and encourage energy-efficient locomotion. The tracking parameters are set to σv = σω =0.5. Termination thresholds are chosen based on the robot type and the nominal height of its base link . For direct reward shaping (DRS), we define three terms analogous to those used in MPBRS, and the corresponding DRS functions are also listed in Table III. The reward tuning was performed to obtain moderate robot behavior. For the potential-based reward shaping (PBRS), each shaping objective we define a scalar potential function ϕ(s) and convert it into a shaped reward as in (5). In our experiments, we set γϕ = 1 .00 for all PBRS terms to improve training stability and speed, as dis-cussed in [4], [6], [28], [29]. Our findings are consistent with prior work and further support the use of an undiscounted potential in PBRS. As discussed before, we choose three shaping rewards commonly used in humanoid locomotion: 1) Height potential ϕh(s): We set σh = 0 .08 .2) Uprightness potential ϕup (s): We use the gravity vec-tor component gzb to encourage locomotion when the robot base remains upright. 3) Joint-regularization potential ϕj (s): We set σq = 0 .5.

D. Implementation Details 

The locomotion task is implemented in NVIDIA Isaa-cLab using the PPO-Clip algorithm with RSL-RL [30]. All hyperparameters are listed in Table IV. The task is trained on a computer equipped with an Intel i7-9700F CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2060 GPU (6 GB). The simulation runs at 200 Hz, and the control frequency is 100 Hz. Each training run uses 2048 agents and lasts for 1000 interaction steps. The training takes approximately 50 minutes to complete. V. RESULTS In this section, we present results for the proposed method across nine humanoid robots using the same reward con-figuration, without any changes to the environment setup. We also benchmark our approach against two baselines: (i) the baseline locomotion reward and (ii) the baseline reward augmented with direct reward shaping (DRS) terms. For a fair comparison, we tune the baseline reward weights to achieve stable and reasonable locomotion performance, and we similarly tune the weights when adding the DRS terms. In contrast, for our method we keep the MPBRS weight fixed at 1.0 for all robots, demonstrating that it does not require robot-specific reward tuning. Finally, we compare both the cumulative reward and the reward command-tracking perfor-mance across all three methods to highlight the robustness of the proposed approach across different humanoid platforms. Robot trajectories are collected over 64 timesteps. All agents are trained with randomized initial joint positions, where each joint is perturbed uniformly with an amplitude of 0.1.A detailed sensitivity analysis of the tuning weights was conducted on the Fourier N1 platform and is reported in the paper. For the remaining robots, we provide open-source code so that users can reproduce the experiments and bench-mark performance across all humanoid platforms. A. Training Benchmark 

We benchmark three training settings-Baseline, direct re-ward shaping (DRS), and MPBRS-across nine humanoid robots, as shown in Fig. 3. All three settings converge on all nine platforms. In the total-reward curves, DRS often appears to converge faster. This trend is expected because DRS adds auxiliary reward terms directly to the optimization objective, which increases the total reward signal. However, this also places DRS on a different reward scale than the Baseline and MPBRS settings. Therefore, total reward values are not directly comparable across all three settings, and DRS requires more careful reward-weight tuning. Across training runs, MPBRS remains behaviorally con-sistent with the Baseline and does not produce notice-able changes in the learned policy. This is consistent with the PBRS formulation, where the shaping contribution ap-proaches zero near the optimal state. In contrast, DRS introduces persistent auxiliary rewards into the objective. As a result, if the shaping weights are not carefully tuned, DRS can influence both the learned policy and the resulting robot behavior. These effects are also reflected in the benchmark curves. MPBRS generally follows the Baseline trends, particularly in fall rate, whereas DRS shows larger deviations in total reward and, for some robots, in fall rate due to the added shaping terms. At the same time, all three settings achieve similar tracking performance overall, as indicated by com-parable rewards for planar velocity tracking ( x-y) and yaw-rate tracking ( z-axis angular velocity). Thus, although DRS alters the reward landscape more strongly, the final tracking capability remains broadly comparable to the Baseline and MPBRS settings. Another important observation is that convergence speed is strongly robot-dependent. Some humanoids improve rapidly and stabilize early, whereas others learn more gradually un-der all three settings. This indicates that morphology, actuator limits, and nominal robot dynamics remain dominant factors in training difficulty, even when the reward shaping strategy is changed. These results confirm that our method can run on multiple platforms without retuning the reward function and is also less sensitive to reward function tuning. Overall, the benchmark highlights a practical trade-off. DRS can improve apparent learning speed, but it modifies the optimization objective and may introduce behavioral bias if the shaping weights are not tuned carefully. MPBRS provides a more conservative alternative: it improves training guidance while preserving baseline-like behavior and compa-rable tracking performance across a diverse set of humanoid robots. B. Sensitivity analysis 

In this section, we present a sensitivity analysis of the proposed method compared to the DRS. To analyze the sensitivity of the reward function tuning, we train the robot with different weight vectors for the reward. As shown in Figure 5 and Figure 6, we vary the weights from 0.1 to 500 and benchmark the results to evaluate whether the robot successfully converges and tracks the command velocity. The total reward and tracking reward are presented in Figure 5. The results show that even when the weight is set to 500, PBRS still helps the robot converge to the optimal policy and track the velocity command. In contrast, DRS is sensitive to the weight: when the weight is too high, the robot simply stands in one position, as the rewards for height, upright orientation, and joint regulation dominate other terms. The advantage of MPBRS is further illustrated in Figure 6. When all rewards converge to zero, it indicates that the robot has reached the optimal state—meaning the rewards for height, upright posture, and joint regulation have achieved their optimal values. We further investigate the tracking ability of the robot in Figure 7 with a weight of 500. Our method successfully helps the robot find the optimal policy, whereas DRS only causes the robot to stand in one position. VI. CONCLUSION This paper examined multi-potential-based reward shaping (MPBRS) for 3D humanoid locomotion using a single, unified reward configuration reused across nine humanoid robots. We combined standard direct shaping terms for com-mand tracking and posture stabilization with three PBRS po-tentials: ray-based base-height regulation, gravity-alignment uprightness, and morphology-aware joint regularization (in-cluding ankle roll/pitch symmetry and hip-yaw penalties). Across diverse morphologies, we found that MPBRS pro-vided only modest improvements in convergence speed. Its main benefit was practical: MPBRS was substantially less sensitive to reward scaling and weight choices, which simplified tuning and enabled more reliable transfer of the same reward design across different humanoids. Future work will investigate learned or adaptive potentials, improved sensor and noise modeling for sim-to-real robustness, and tighter integration with morphology-conditioned policies and domain randomization to further improve cross-robot gener-alization.
