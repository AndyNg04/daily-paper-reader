## When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement 

Hao Chen 1, Yukun Yan 2*, Ziyu Han 1, Ye He 1, Chuanzhe Guo 1, Shangzhan Li 1,Qi Shi 2, Qingfu Zhu 1, Maosong Sun 2, Wanxiang Che 1*1Research Center for Social Computing and Interactive Robotics, Harbin Institute of Technology 

2Department of Computer Science and Technology, Institute for AI, Tsinghua University 

Abstract 

Autonomous agents increasingly depend on harnesses that govern interaction, memory, tools, and execution around large language models (LLMs). While recent work has begun to evolve these harnesses from execution expe-rience, the supervision used to guide evolution often remains relatively static, making it diffi-cult to track changing behavioral weaknesses across rounds and potentially limiting general-ization to unseen tasks. To address this issue, we propose RuHarness, a rubric–harness co-evolution framework for continual agent self-improvement. RuHarness continually discov-ers and reassesses behavioral criteria from ex-ecution trajectories, prioritizes the most rele-vant unresolved weaknesses, and uses them to guide targeted harness updates while keeping the task objective fixed. Extensive experiments across diverse agent tasks and backbone mod-els show that RuHarness outperforms strong harness-evolution baselines, particularly on un-seen held-out tasks. Further analyses show that adaptive rubric prioritization tracks shift-ing weaknesses and supports more sustained improvement and stable generalization across evolution rounds. 

1 Introduction

Recent work has begun to evolve the harness itself from execution experience, enabling agents to improve without updating model parameters (Zhang et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2026b; Lee et al., 2026a). While such approaches can yield substantial gains, recent con-trolled evaluations suggest that these improvements may weaken on unseen tasks (Wang et al., 2026c),

One challenge behind this limited generalization is that repeated harness optimization may gradu-ally overfit to task-specific failure patterns exposed during evolution (Srikanth et al., 2026). Existing methods optimize harness against task-level feed-back, which indicates whether the agent succeeds but provides limited information about the behav-ioral deficiencies underlying failure (Zhang et al., 2026a). This limitation becomes increasingly im-portant during continual evolution: as the harness improves, previously dominant failures may be mit-igated, while subtler or previously unseen failure patterns emerge (Nie et al., 2026). Con-sequently, the system lacks an explicit mechanism for tracking how the importance of different weak-nesses changes throughout evolution. Continual harness evolution requires adaptive diagnosis that continually reassesses and reprioritizes behavioral weaknesses while keeping the underlying task ob-jective fixed (Dou et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c). To this end, we propose RuHarness ( Ru bric– 

Harness Co-Evolution), a framework for continual agent self-improvement through joint rubric and harness evolution. RuHarness continually derives behavioral criteria from execution trajectories, re-assesses their relevance under the current harness, and prioritizes those that best capture unresolved weaknesses. The resulting rubric frontier guides targeted and coordinated updates to the correspond-ing harness components, while candidate harnesses are selected using task performance on an inde-pendent validation set. By adapting the diagnostic guidance as agent behavior changes while keeping the task objective fixed, RuHarness continually fo-cuses harness optimization on the weaknesses that matter most at each stage of evolution. Extensive experiments across diverse agent tasks and backbone models demonstrate that RuHarness consistently outperforms strong harness-evolution baselines, with particularly clear gains on unseen held-out tasks. The analyses also show that the rubric frontier adapts to shifting behavioral weak-nesses, concentrates on diagnostically relevant cri-teria, and supports more sustained improvement across evolution rounds. These results indicate that co-evolving rubrics with the harness provides adap-tive supervision for continual agent improvement, supporting both continued evolution across evolu-tion rounds and stronger generalization. 

2 Related Work 

Self-Evolving Agents. Agent self-evolution has gradually shifted from storing interaction ex-perience to optimizing persistent external state. Reflection- and memory-based methods refine ex-perience to improve future decisions (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2023), while skill evolution treats reusable skills as editable knowledge that can be refined from execution feedback (Zhu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). These paradigms extend naturally to the broader agent harness, where the optimization target expands from a single external component to the execution in-frastructure, including prompts, tools, memory, and control logic (Zhang et al., 2026d). Recent harness-evolution methods seek to diagnose fail-ures from trajectories and iteratively revise this in-frastructure (Jiang et al., 2026b), with subsequent work broadening the editable space and making the evolution process more observable and verifi-able (Lee et al., 2026b; Lin et al., 2026a). More broadly, recent studies have explored recursive self-improvement and harness–model co-evolution across repeated adaptation rounds (Xia et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b; Fan and Huang, 2026). Yet these approaches still largely treat the diagnostic signals guiding evolution as fixed, leav-ing how such guidance should adapt to shifting behavioral weaknesses underexplored. 

Rubrics for Self-Improvement. Rubrics have evolved from structured evaluation criteria into a flexible interface between evaluation and opti-mization (Chen et al., 2026a). By decomposing complex objectives into explicit behavioral criteria, they provide fine-grained supervision and have in-creasingly been incorporated into reward modeling and reinforcement learning (Gunjal et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a). More recent work makes the rubric itself adaptive: EvoLM (Li et al., 2026b), EvoRubrics (Ding et al., 2026), and Dynami-cRubric (Wang et al., 2026a) co-evolve evalua-tive criteria with the optimized policy (Guan et al., 2026), while Wang et al. (2026b) extends this idea to a self-evolving loop over responses, rubrics, and policy. These studies suggest that effective supervision should evolve with the behavior it guides (Jiang et al., 2026a). However, existing work mainly co-evolves rubrics with policies or re-sponses (Li et al., 2026d), leaving rubric adaptation for continual harness evolution largely unexplored.

3.2 Rubric Evolution: Adapting to Shifting Agent Weaknesses 

Given trajectories from Ht, RuHarness summa-rizes each failed run into a structured failure report and aggregates these reports to identify recurring cross-task behavioral patterns. These patterns are converted into new criteria Rnew 

t+1 , which capture recurring weaknesses across tasks while remaining verifiable from trajectory evidence. 

Outcome-blind behavioral assessment. Newly discovered criteria are evaluated together with exist-ing criteria on trajectories generated by the current harness. For each criterion c and trajectory τ , an outcome-blind judge assigns: 

J(c, τ ) ∈ { Satisfied , Violated , N/A }, (3) where the judge has access only to behavioral ev-idence in the trajectory and is blinded to the fi-nal task outcome. This prevents criterion assess-3ment from directly leaking success or failure labels. Here, N/A indicates that the behavior specified by the criterion does not occur, whereas Satisfied indi-cates that it occurs and is handled correctly. 

Criterion admission and lifecycle. Based on these assessments, newly discovered criteria first undergo a birth check stage before entering the maintained rubric pool. Criteria with insufficient diagnostic value are filtered at this stage, includ-ing those that are rarely applicable, fail to identify violations on unsuccessful trajectories, or trigger in-discriminately across outcomes. Criteria that pass this admission stage enter a four-state lifecycle: 

probation , active , dormant , and retired . Newly ad-mitted criteria enter probation and are promoted to active once they exhibit sufficient diagnostic value. Active criteria become dormant when their asso-ciated failures are no longer observed, and may be reactivated upon recurrence, while persistently uninformative criteria are eventually retired. Only 

active criteria are subsequently prioritized and used to guide harness evolution.

3.3 Harness Evolution: Optimizing against the Moving Frontier 

To structure the harness search space, we represent the editable harness using six functional coordi-nates: Equip , Instruct , Act , Observe , Remember ,and Terminate . These coordinates respectively capture resource provisioning, behavioral instruc-tion, action policy, observation processing, context management, and completion control. Each co-ordinate defines a corresponding set of editable components in the agent system. 

Frontier-guided targeting. Given the current rubric frontier bRt+1 , RuHarness identifies the har-ness coordinates that can directly address each prioritized behavioral weakness. The criterion-to-coordinate mapping is not constrained to be one-to-one, since a single failure pattern may involve multiple parts of the harness. Instead, each crite-rion may target multiple coordinates, allowing the corresponding interventions to be planned jointly. 

Coordinated candidate generation. Based on these criterion-to-coordinate assignments, RuHar-ness jointly plans a small set of candidate har-ness updates. Each candidate specifies a concrete hypothesis for addressing the diagnosed weak-nesses and may modify multiple related coordi-nates jointly. 4 Experimental Methodology 

This section presents our experimental setup, cover-ing models and environment, benchmarks, metrics, baselines, and implementation details. 

Models and Environment. We conduct experi-ments with Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Team, 2026) and GLM-4.7-Flash (Team et al., 2025) as the back-bone models. The agent is instantiated with Deep-Agent (Li et al., 2026c), which provides the basic execution framework for tool use, filesystem in-teraction, and multi-step task solving. Based on this framework, we construct the initial harness and expose its configurable components for sub-sequent evolution, while keeping the underlying model fixed throughout the experiments. All stages of task execution and evolution are performed us-ing the corresponding backbone model itself. 

Benchmarks and Metrics. Evaluation is con-ducted on EvoAgentBench (Gao et al., 2026), a multi-domain benchmark for long-horizon agent self-evolution. It covers five agent domains: infor-mation retrieval based on BrowseComp-Plus (Chen et al., 2025), mathematical reasoning based on Om-niMath (Gao et al., 2024), software engineering based on SWE-Bench (Jimenez et al., 2024), code implementation based on LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024), and knowledge work based on GDP-Val (Patwardhan et al., 2025). We use Pass@1 as the primary metric and evaluate each domain with its benchmark evaluator (Tang et al., 2026). 

Baselines. For comprehensive comparison, we compare RuHarness against representative self-evolving agent methods that optimize different external states. ACE evolves persistent contex-tual knowledge from interaction experience (Zhang et al., 2026b), while EvoSkill discovers and refines reusable skills through failure analysis (Alzubi et al., 2026). For harness evolution, we include Self-Harness, which mines model-specific weak-nesses and proposes targeted updates (Zhang et al., 2026a); Meta-Harness, which searches executable harness code using accumulated candidates and tra-jectories (Lee et al., 2026b); and Agentic Harness Engineering (AHE), which improves harnesses through observable and verifiable component up-dates (Lin et al., 2026a). Together, these baselines cover context, skill, and full-harness evolution. 

Implementation Details. We use disjoint evo-lution ( held-in ) and validation splits, with the test set ( held-out ) reserved for final reporting only. At each round, RuHarness generates up to three co-ordinated harness candidates and accepts at most one update when its validation improvement ex-ceeds ϵ = 0 .05 . For diagnostic utility, we set 

α = γ = 1 .0 and β = δ = λ = 0 .5. Rubric guid-ance is obtained by ranking criteria with the q-th posterior quantile ( q = 0 .2) and selecting the Top-

K criteria with K = 10 . Each candidate modifies at most three harness components. More experi-mental details are provided in the Appendix A.1.

5.1 Overall Performance 

We evaluate RuHarness across five agent domains on EvoAgentBench using Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and GLM-4.7-Flash, as shown in Table 1. Overall, RuHarness outperforms all the baselines, achieving the best average performance on both held-in and held-out sets across the two backbone models. 

Overall Effectiveness. On Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, RuHarness improves the strongest baseline by 1.7 points on held-in and 4.5 points on held-out. The gains are even larger on GLM-4.7-Flash, reaching 2.7 and 5.3 points, respectively. These results in-dicate that rubric–harness co-evolution provides stable improvements beyond a specific backbone. 

Generalization to Unseen Tasks. The advan-tage of RuHarness is more pronounced on the held-out sets. Although several baselines achieve com-petitive or even better results on individual held-in tasks, RuHarness attains the highest average held-out performance for both models and exhibits a smaller held-in–held-out gap. This suggests that its improvements are less tied to the evolution tasks and transfer more effectively to unseen tasks. 

Robustness across Tasks and Models. RuHar-ness performs consistently well across heteroge-neous tasks and both backbone models, achieving the strongest average held-out performance and competitive results across individual domains. This suggests that its gains are not tied to a specific task type or backbone. 

5.2 Ablation Study 

We further examine the contribution of key compo-nents of RuHarness, as summarized in Table 2. 

Necessity of the Rubric Module. Removing the rubric module lowers the average held-in score from 62.4 to 57.1 and the held-out score from 53.7 to 45.8. This indicates that directly evolving the harness from task feedback and trajectories pro-vides insufficient behavioral diagnosis, highlight-ing the role of the rubric in translating task out-comes into actionable weaknesses. 

Effect of Rubric Evolution. To isolate the effect of rubric evolution, we retain the initial rubric as a fixed diagnostic rubric throughout the evolution process. Surprisingly, this variant performs even worse than removing the rubric module entirely, suggesting that a static rubric can become coun-terproductive as the harness evolves. When pre-viously dominant failures are mitigated, outdated criteria may continue to steer optimization toward less relevant behaviors. Further analysis of this experimental process is provided in Appendix A.6. 

Role of the Rubric Frontier. Using the entire ranked rubric pool instead of the selected frontier also leads to performance degradation. This sug-gests that more rubrics do not necessarily provide better guidance, as focusing on relevant criteria reduces interference from low-value signals and yields more effective harness updates. 5.3 Analysis of Adaptive Rubric Tracking 

To examine how the diagnostic focus changes throughout harness evolution, we measure the Jaccard similarity between rubric frontiers across rounds. Specifically, each frontier is represented by its selected criterion IDs, and we compare the frontier at round t with both the initial frontier F0

and the frontier from the preceding round Ft−1. We conduct this analysis on two representative settings, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on Code Implementation and GLM-4.7-Flash on Software Engineering. As shown in Figure 3, the similarity to the initial frontier decreases sharply in the early rounds and remains low thereafter. By contrast, the similarity between consecutive frontiers first declines, then gradually increases and stabilizes at a relatively high level. These two trends reveal a characteristic evolution pattern: the diagnostic frontier progres-sively departs from its initial configuration, while changes between adjacent rounds become increas-ingly gradual. This behavior suggests that early harness updates substantially reshape the set of weaknesses that require attention, whereas later evolution proceeds through more localized adjust-ments. Overall, the results confirm that the be-havioral weaknesses most relevant to harness im-provement are not static, supporting the need for an evolving rubric that continuously tracks changes in the agent’s failure landscape. 5.4 Effectiveness of Adaptive Rubric Prioritization on Harness Evolution 

To examine whether adaptive rubric prioritization translates into subsequent behavioral improvement, we analyze criterion-level dynamics around fron-tier transitions and after frontier entry. Specifically, we first compare how the diagnostic components of criteria change when they enter, remain in, exit, or stay outside the frontier. We then align newly prioritized criteria at their frontier-entry round and track how their violation rates evolve over subse-quent rounds. To further relate this trend to harness evolution, we additionally distinguish cases with and without committed harness updates. 

Frontier transitions track diagnostic rele-vance. To characterize how diagnostic relevance changes with frontier membership, we group crite-ria by their transitions between two adjacent evo-lution rounds: entering the frontier, remaining in the frontier, exiting the frontier, or staying outside 7the frontier. For each group, we measure the round-to-round changes in the diagnostic utility compo-nents defined in Section 3.2, as shown in Figure 4a. As expected, criteria entering the frontier show in-creases in discrimination, coverage, and predictive validity together with reduced redundancy, while exiting criteria exhibit the opposite pattern. Criteria that remain inside or outside the frontier change substantially less. These results confirm that fron-tier turnover reflects changes in criterion-level diag-nostic relevance rather than arbitrary replacement. 

Prioritized weaknesses are progressively mit-igated. To assess whether frontier prioritization translates into subsequent behavioral improvement, Figure 4b examines what happens after a crite-rion enters the frontier. The violation rate remains nearly unchanged in the first round after entry, but decreases noticeably over the following rounds. This delayed reduction suggests that newly pri-oritized weaknesses are not necessarily resolved immediately, but are progressively mitigated as subsequent harness evolution proceeds. The trend therefore links frontier prioritization with later be-havioral improvement, suggesting that the evolving rubric does more than reorganize diagnostic sig-nals and helps focus subsequent optimization on weaknesses that can be progressively reduced. 

Committed harness updates yield stronger longer-horizon mitigation. To more directly relate behavioral improvement to harness evolution, Fig-ure 4c compares frontier-entry criteria under com-mitted and non-committed harness updates, where 

committed denotes a candidate update that passes the validation gate and is adopted for subsequent evolution. The committed group shows a clear and sustained increase in violation reduction over sub-sequent rounds, whereas the non-committed group fluctuates without a consistent improvement trend. This contrast indicates that frontier-guided priori-tization is more likely to translate into persistent behavioral gains when the proposed harness update is successfully validated and adopted. Taken together, these results show that adaptive rubric prioritization tracks changing diagnostic rele-vance and helps focus subsequent harness evolution on weaknesses that are progressively mitigated.

6 Conclusion 

This paper presents RuHarness, a rubric–harness co-evolution framework for continual agent self-improvement. RuHarness dynamically adapts di-agnostic criteria to the agent’s evolving behavioral weaknesses and uses the resulting rubric frontier to guide targeted harness optimization. Extensive ex-periments across diverse tasks and backbone mod-els show that RuHarness achieves strong gains in both held-in performance and generalization to un-seen held-out tasks. Further analyses show that the rubric frontier tracks shifting diagnostic relevance and supports sustained harness improvement over repeated evolution rounds. These results highlight the importance of jointly adapting diagnostic su-pervision and the agent harness for more sustained and generalizable agent evolution. 8Limitations 

Although RuHarness is evaluated across multiple tasks and backbone models, all experiments are conducted within a single underlying agent frame-work. Evaluating the six-coordinate harness ab-straction on a broader range of agent architectures would further establish its generality. In addition, due to computational resource constraints, our ex-periments are conducted on benchmark tasks under a fixed evolution horizon. Examining longer-term and more open-ended adaptation settings would provide a broader view of the resulting co-evolution dynamics.

A.4 Details of Posterior Estimation and Conservative Ranking 

In this subsection, we provide the details of the con-servative ranking strategy introduced in Section 3.2. Directly ranking criteria by their point-estimated utility can be unreliable when only a small number of trajectories support a criterion, since a few ob-servations may produce an artificially high score. We therefore rank criteria using a lower posterior estimate of their diagnostic utility. For each criterion, we construct a 2 × 3 contin-gency table over task outcome ( pass or fail ) and criterion assessment (V IOLATED , S ATISFIED , or N/A). Since discrimination, coverage, and predic-tive validity are all computed from this same table, we estimate them jointly. We draw B = 2000 samples from this poste-rior. This requirement is subse-quently strengthened from a verbal confirmation into programmatic verification , making the check directly executable. In the final stage, verification is moved earlier in the workflow, from post-hoc confirmation to pre-access checking before down-stream file use. The trajectory therefore progresses from execution, to diagnosis, to artifact verification, to executable verification, and finally to proactive verification. 

Information Retrieval. The second trajectory shows a parallel progression from coarse evidence checking to structured auditing. It starts by requir-ing full-document retrieval instead of relying on search snippets, establishing a stronger evidence basis for candidate matching. The focus then advances from merely verifying a candidate to mak-ing the verification explicit and auditable , requir-ing the agent to state which constraints are satisfied and provide supporting evidence. Finally, the pro-cess becomes systematic : individual evidence state-ments are organized into a constraint-satisfaction matrix so that all constraints are checked collec-tively rather than independently. Thus, the diagnos-tic trajectory evolves from source verification, to immediate verification, to explicit evidence ground-ing, and finally to structured constraint coverage.
