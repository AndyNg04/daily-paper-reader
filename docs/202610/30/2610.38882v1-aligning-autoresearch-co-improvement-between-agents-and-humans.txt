Autoresearch agents can now attempt long-horizon AI research end-to-end, i.e., self-improvement. However, best results are still obtained by research agents interacting with human researchers, and improving AI’s ability to perform this co-research will only reinforce this effect—a process termed co-improvement (Weston and Foerster, 2025). Further, aligning with humans is likely a safer path for research agents, especially given recent agent reward hacking phenomena (OpenAI, 2026b). In this work we address these issues in four ways: (i) we empirically study the role of humans in directing autoresearch agents, focusing on the domain of benchmark creation, and find humans still play a pivotal role particularly in the ideation phase; (ii) we develop a benchmark, HackBench, that shows current autoresearch agents are not aligned and are unable to achieve hack localization sufficiently; (iii) we present a method to train agents for improved human interaction by using human simulators as a proxy during reinforcement learning (RL); and finally (iv) we develop a method to improve model text generation, and hence agent communication, by RL with rubrics aligned with human experts. Together, our training and evaluation recipes provide a path toward more capable and aligned (co-)research agents.

# 1 The role of humans in directing research agents for benchmark creation 

1.1 Overview 

As autoresearch agents and recursive self-improvement (RSI) draw increasing attention, determining how models should direct their own improvement is becoming more important (Yin et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026; Weng, 2026). While strong evidence shows that some verifiable tasks can be successfully automated (e.g., tasks that require hill-climbing a pre-defined metric (Wijk et al., 2025; Chan et al., 2025; Karpathy, 2026)), the scope and necessity of human contribution in open-ended tasks remain open questions. We focus on one such area, benchmark creation. Specifically, curating problems that are both difficult and meaningful to solve is a crucial task in AI model development (Reuel et al., 2024; Bean et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2026). From an evaluation perspective, it allows researchers to analyze the capabilities that models currently lack; from a development perspective, it supplies new targets to hill-climb on (Liang et al., 2022; Chang et al., 2024). To date, benchmark creation is driven mainly by human AI scientists, and the process is fairly laborious. One first decides what the benchmark is meant to measure or assess ( i.e. , the construct), then identifies the grounding material used to instantiate it ( e.g. , GitHub PRs for SWE-Bench (Jimenez et al., 2024)), then designs a pipeline through which problems are produced and model predictions are evaluated, and finally runs the evaluation, iterating over these steps. The process further branches into granular decisions across an effectively unbounded space of possibilities: errors in the pipeline code must be fixed, strategies for increasing difficulty must be devised when problems prove insufficiently hard, and humans must be brought into the loop wherever verification is needed. . AutoBenchmark instructs an autoresearch agent to create a benchmark; we specifically target benchmarks that evaluate autoresearch agents themselves ( i.e. , benchmarking autoresearch). Within the benchmark creation loop, the autoresearch agent iteratively revises the benchmark via two main sources of feedback: one from the benchmark solvers (including the same model used as the benchmark creator) and the other from external verifiers of the benchmark (AI or human). We thus also study if having humans provide additional feedback on what benchmark to make and how to make it helps or not. 

It is precisely this open-endedness that makes benchmark creation a demanding test for an autoresearch agent. In particular, we focus on autoresearch agents that create benchmarks for autoresearch agents: high-quality autoresearch agent benchmarks remain sparse relative to question-answering or competition mathematics benchmarks, so constructing a good one is a meaningful goal in its own right. Moreover, because autoresearch agent baselines must be run over long horizons, building such problems together with their surrounding environments renders benchmark creation considerably harder, and correspondingly more interesting. To this end, we introduce AutoBenchmark , a framework in which an autoresearch agent builds a benchmark from a task specification and revises it across iterations using two feedback signals: (1) the trajectories and scores of a set of solvers that attempt the benchmark, and (2) the critique of an LLM verifier that checks the quality of the benchmark. Additionally, as a third axis, we investigate if human AI scientists can contribute to this process by directing what to build at the start of the loop and advising on revisions between iterations. Our experiments show that current autoresearch agents can run this loop end to end, but that the benchmarks they produce unaided ( i.e. , without human feedback) are close to saturated, allowing the solvers to achieve scores above 80. We also find that human feedback helps substantially, and the gain grows with how specific that feedback is. Specifically, providing detailed direction on what type of benchmark to build and how to build it halves the score of the solvers, while a brief statement of what to build helps only marginally. This indicates that human involvement is still required in benchmark creation. Looking ahead, we envision 

AutoBenchmark as a way to measure progress on the capability of AI agents to autonomously create or co-create high-quality benchmarks. 

1.2 AutoBenchmark 

Figure 1 shows the overall design of AutoBenchmark , in which a research agent is asked to carry out the benchmark creation process end-to-end. The research agent produces a benchmark in a Harbor-compatible evaluation package (Harbor Framework Team, 2026): a container environment, task instructions, the evidence supplied to the solver, reference solution, and machine-checkable grading criteria. The AutoBenchmark 

pipeline consists of the following stages (further details are in Appendix A): 

Stage 1: Benchmark Proposal. The goal in this stage is to create difficult yet high-quality tasks. The research agent receives a task spec (a file that contains a general set of rules and, when a human provides feedback at the proposal stage, that direction as well; in the fully autonomous setting the file carries the rules alone) and, 2optionally, grounding material, and decides what capability the benchmark should measure before deciding how to measure it. It writes down the construct, develops several materially different ways to operationalize it, searches for and stores the primary sources it relies on, and then instantiates the selected design as a set of runnable tasks. Because the package has to execute, the agent also owns the decisions a human benchmark designer normally owns: what the solver sees, what evidence it is given, what an ideal answer contains, how partial credit is assigned, and a reference solution that shows the task can be solved at all.  

> Stage 2: Benchmark Solving.

Each task is attempted by one or more solver agents inside the environment the research agent specified. Every attempt produces a trajectory and a final answer, and an answer judge grades that answer against the criteria the research agent wrote. These scores give the loop its difficulty signal, since a benchmark that the solvers already saturate tells us little about the frontier.  

> Stage 3: Benchmark Review.

A low solver score on its own is weak evidence, because a broken task, an unsatisfiable requirement, a mis-specified grader, and fabricated grounding all produce the same number. An agent that optimizes solver failure alone also has an easy degenerate strategy available to it, which is to make the tasks impossible. We therefore review the benchmark itself, using an LLM judge that is applied to the research agent rather than to the solvers, along five criteria: construct validity, correctness, feasibility, usefulness , and an overall verdict. The resulting verdicts decide whether an iteration is eligible to be built on. Below, in addition to the overall pipeline specified above, we list the components within each stage:  

> Component 1: Autodata Loop.

Inspired by Autodata (Kulikov et al., 2026), in AutoBenchmark , the research agent alternates creation and analysis until the iteration budget is exhausted. Each iteration is seeded from the best checkpoint so far, so a revision that (1) the judges reject, or (2) makes the solver achieve a higher score compared to previous iterations, does not become the checkpoint of the next revision. The parent of an iteration is therefore the lowest-scoring accepted iteration so far, which is not necessarily the immediately preceding one. Feedback is exposed as file-system memory: frozen snapshots, trajectories, answers, rubric judgments, and verdicts are written to disk and the prompt names the paths, so the accumulated history grows over iterations.  

> Component 2: Detecting reward hacking with external solvers.

Optimizing against a fixed set of solvers risks producing a benchmark that is adversarially hard for particular models. To detect if such reward hacking behavior occurs from the benchmark proposer, we evaluate additional solvers that take no part in the loop and whose results are never written into the research agent’s feedback. Cheaper external solvers are run after every iteration, giving a difficulty trace that is independent of the solvers being optimized against, while more expensive ones run on the selected best checkpoint.  

> Component 3: Human involvement.

Benchmark creation involves two decisions, what to measure and how 

to measure it, and a human can supply either at different levels of detail. Within the AutoBenchmark 

pipeline, we vary both, together with whether human feedback is available only before the run or also during it, giving four settings: (i) No feedback. The run is fully autonomous. The agent receives no human direction and no grounding, only the general rules of the task spec, and chooses the scientific domain, the construct, and the operationalization itself. (ii) Coarse-grained human feedback (proposal stage). A human states the intent in a single sentence ( e.g. , what the benchmark should target). This fixes what to measure while leaving the entire operationalization to the agent. (iii) Fine-grained human feedback (proposal stage). A human writes a detailed task specification and additionally curates the list of grounding material the agent may use. This fixes both what to measure and much of how ,and corresponds to the level of direction a human benchmark designer would decide before work starts. (iv) Fine-grained human feedback (proposal & execution stage). In addition to (iii), a human inspects the outcome of a given iteration and writes what the agent should do next, so that direction is also available during the loop. 31.3 Experimental Setup  

> Choice of models.

We use Muse Spark 1.1 as the research agent that proposes the benchmark. We use Muse Spark 1.1 itself together with Muse-Glimmer-30B as the solvers inside the loop, giving a setting in which the proposer attempts to create problems that a considerably weaker model and the proposer itself cannot solve, yet that remain meaningful. Unlike Autodata (Kulikov et al., 2026), we therefore do not assume the existence of a strong solver that certifies correctness; creating problems that the proposer itself cannot solve aligns more closely with the purpose of benchmark curation, since the frontier models can always be integrated as part of the AutoBenchmark pipeline. Muse Spark 1.1 also serves as the answer judge and as the five benchmark-quality judges. As external solvers, we use NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Lightning-30B-A3B after every iteration and Claude-Opus-5 on the selected best checkpoint; neither is integrated in the loop.  

> Grounding material & Built benchmarks.

In the settings where grounding material is supplied ((iii) and (iv)), the harness gives the research agent a corpus of primary sources. Specifically, the harness hashes every source and snapshots it read-only before the session starts, and the agent may additionally retrieve public web content during the grounding stage. We experiment with three automatically generated autoresearch benchmarks developed by our method: (1) Graveyard Bench (ideation ): Can AI assistants avoid proposing dead-end ideas? The benchmark is grounded in documented negative results, namely research directions that the scientific record shows were pursued and abandoned. > Configurations.

Every run produces a benchmark with ten instances over ten iterations, and each solver attempts every task once per iteration, so an iteration’s difficulty signal is the mean over ten independent task observations per model. Solvers receive the same execution contract in every iteration (4 CPUs, 16 GiB of memory, and a 24-hour agent budget per task, with a separate verifier container for grading the solver’s submission), so scores stay comparable across iterations. Before any solver is run, the harness executes the agent’s own reference solution against the real verifier and admits the submission only when it scores at least 

0.9, returning the iteration for a repair session otherwise, with at most ten submission attempts per iteration. Each point in Figure 2 (Rebuttal Bench and Graveyard Bench) and Figure 3 (SilentTrain Bench) reports a solver’s mean score across the ten tasks at that iteration. Scores are displayed on a 0–100 scale. Lower scores indicate a harder (better) benchmark.

> The verifier catches defects that the score cannot see.

Across all runs plotted in Figure 2 and Figure 3, the benchmark verifier rejects any iteration that violates one of our five predefined criteria, so that iteration cannot become the parent of the next. Figure 4 shows three examples of quality issues identified by the LLM judges where solver scores alone would be insufficient: answers leaking into files the solver can read, a construct too shallow or too memorized to separate the solvers, and grounding material the creator claims to use but does not actually retain in the benchmark. A leaked answer key puts the solvers at ceiling, which looks exactly like a construct that is too easy, and missing grounding is consistent with any score at all, so neither defect can be caught by looking at the score. In Figure 2, iteration 5 of the fine-grained Rebuttal Bench run scores 47 .4, the lowest of that run so far, and would have seeded iteration 6 had it not failed the feasibility criterion. The loop continues from iteration 3 instead, the lowest-scoring accepted iteration at that point, and reaches 43 .5 at iteration 8 with all five criteria passing. Figure 2 shows the reverse for the autonomous run: its first three iterations all score 100 .0 on Muse Spark, and only the verdicts tell them apart, failing five criteria at iteration 1 and two at iteration 3, with iteration 4 the first to pass all five. Selecting on score alone would have kept iterations the verifier rejects.  

> When the autoresearch loop stalls, human direction helps.

As shown in Figure 3, through iteration 5 the SilentTrain Bench run appears to stall, with Muse Spark above 92 . We forked the run at iteration 6 and gave one continuation three human-guided instructions, all of them about instantiation and none about what to measure: put the solver on a multi-hour job, build the environment from the complete files in the grounding material instead of excerpted stand-ins, and grade with behavioral tests against the original buggy behavior instead of a deterministic checker. With that guidance the selected checkpoint reaches 64 .2 on Muse Spark compared to 88 .1 without it, 54 .2 vs. 85 .6 on Muse Glimmer, and 48 .8 vs. 83 .8 on Nemotron, where the gap is widest. We interpret this as evidence that execution feedback helps once the agent’s own revisions have stopped working. 

1.5 Related Work  

> Automatic benchmark construction.

A line of work asks whether a language model can produce evaluation data itself. AutoBencher (LI et al., 2025) casts benchmark construction as an optimization problem: ahuman declares desiderata such as difficulty and salience, and a model iteratively proposes and refines dataset descriptions that optimize surrogate metrics for them, yielding question-answer items that elicit more model errors than human-constructed benchmarks. A parallel line places the same proposer-solver asymmetry inside a training loop. Self-challenging LM agents (Zhou et al., 2026) have a single model act as a challenger that explores a tool environment and emits tasks in a Code-as-Task format carrying an instruction, a verification function, a solution, and failure cases, then as an executor trained on those tasks by RL. SPICE (Liu et al., 2025) grounds the challenger in a document corpus, so that the tasks it generates stay factual and sit at the frontier of the reasoner’s ability. SPADE (Liu et al., 2026) scales this to executable environments, training the task proposer itself with a hint-based regret signal so the environment distribution evolves as the agent improves. AutoBenchmark is closely related to this line of work but differs in three aspects. First, our 7target is autoresearch benchmarks created by autoresearch agents: the proposer and the solvers are the same class of long-horizon agent, and the tasks are research problems that run for hours instead of single-turn reasoning problems. Second, the loop carries two feedback signals. Alongside the verifiable score from the solvers’ trajectories, an LLM verifier judges the benchmark on open-ended dimensions that no score reveals, namely construct validity, correctness, feasibility, usefulness, and an overall verdict. Third, we do not train the proposer. We measure what current agents produce on their own and vary how much a human directs them, which lets us ask which parts of benchmark creation still need a human researcher.  

> Evaluating and directing autoresearch agents.

Existing benchmarks for research agents supply the task and measure execution against it: MLE-bench (Chan et al., 2025) and RE-Bench (Wijk et al., 2025) score agents on machine learning engineering problems with predefined metrics, and speedrun-style setups (Karpathy, 2026) fix the target and reward hill-climbing it. What these measure is how well an agent pursues a goal someone else has set. We ask a question one step earlier: can the agent decide what is worth measuring and build the apparatus that measures it, a step where no fixed procedure applies. Concurrently, work on recursive self-improvement (Yin et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026; Weng, 2026) asks how much of this cycle an agent can close on its own, and co-improvement (Weston and Foerster, 2025) argues that the strongest and safest results still come from agents working with human researchers. Our results speak to both: the agents we study run the full construction loop, yet produce near-saturated benchmarks without human direction, and the gap closes only when that direction is specific about what to build and how to build it. 

1.6 Conclusion 

We proposed AutoBenchmark , a pipeline which evaluates the ability of a research agent to construct a benchmark, where we study when and how human feedback could be useful. We particularly targeted benchmarks used to evaluate research agents – specifically, by building Graveyard Bench, SilentTrain Bench, and Rebuttal Bench. Our takeaway is that for current models human feedback helps at two points, and only when it is concrete. The first is forming the problem: a detailed specification with curated grounding material lowered solver scores by a substantial margin, while a one-sentence statement of intent left the benchmark close to what the agent produced unaided. The second is supplying the way to realize it, where guidance on how to instantiate the benchmark restarted a loop that had stalled. Three ingredients were necessary on the agent side: grounding material worth iterating on, feedback drawn from the solver trajectories and the judges’ verdicts together, and a sufficient number of loop iterations to build the benchmark. Looking ahead, constructing high-quality challenging benchmarks is and will be central to AI model develop-ment, and it becomes harder as the models themselves become more capable. As recursive self-improvement draws increasing attention, this raises the question of which parts of benchmark construction should be delegated to agents and which should remain with human researchers. Substantial technical work remains on the agent side, including training the proposer to widen the proposer–solver gap and meta-optimizing its agentic harness. The more difficult and important question, however, concerns the human side: decid-ing which problems are meaningful and worth making difficult is a judgment we do not yet know how to delegate, and integrating it into the loop is, in our view, the most important direction for future work. 2.1 Overview 

Motivation Language model agents can carry out long-horizon research tasks end to end: given a task (“pretrain a language model with the lowest dev perplexity,” “finetune a classifier to achieve the highest possible accuracy”), the agent reads the codebase, writes and runs training and evaluation code, and is scored by an automated verifier (Chan et al., 2025; Wijk et al., 2025; Starace et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024). However, this iterative optimization loop can be fragile. Even when the score is computed on held-out data in a separate environment, the agent knows, or can infer, how the score is computed: it can read the evaluation code, inspect the test labels or test distribution, and shape its solution to the evaluation metric rather than to the task itself. When the objective is “make this metric go up,” an agent that did not conduct the underlying research can still make the number go up by reward hacking : exploiting a flaw in the specification to obtain reward without accomplishing the intended task (Skalse et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2022; Weng, 2024). The community has highlighted the importance and difficulty of dealing with reward hacking in autoresearch: PostTrainBench re-audited its leaderboard after agents built training data around held-out items, distilled from external model APIs, and submitted instruction-tuned checkpoints in place of the assigned base model (PostTrainBench Team, 2026). On the NanoGPT speedrun (Jordan et al., 2024), METR observed agents freezing weights or halting training the moment the target loss was reached, and needed the speedrun’s human maintainer to separate these brittle gains from legitimate optimization (Cunningham et al., 2026). In each case the hack is task-specific, looks like ordinary progress at the level of individual actions, and surfaces only in long transcripts. The cost of missing it is high: autoresearch verifiers are exactly the reward signals used to train agents with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Lightman et al., 2024), so a hack that survives corrupts the training signal (Qi et al., 2026). A natural research question arises: can an LLM monitor catch these hacks by reading the agent’s trajectory— i.e., can LLMs police themselves ? This task is particularly challenging: a trajectory can run to millions of tokens, so a monitor must first exercise long-context memory ; the hack can be a handful of commands among hundreds (a median of 264 per trajectory in the benchmark we create in this work), frequently buried mid-trajectory where attention is weakest (Liu et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2024). It must then reason across steps, because a hack is often a multi-step action whose sub-steps are distributed through the transcript, and localization requires identifying the exact hacking command(s) rather than merely noticing that something is off. Finally, the monitor must bring task knowledge : deciding whether a command games a grader is a scientific judgment about the task, not a surface pattern. Unlike a synthetic needle-in-a-haystack, the needle here cannot be found by string-matching or by spotting an inconsistency, only by understanding the task (Baker et al., 2025). Fundamentally, detection comes down to a question of alignment with humans: even if this action can improve the reward, is this a behavior humans would consider acceptable?

2.2 HackBench 

HackBench evaluates how well an LLM monitor can detect and localize reward hacking from a full autoresearch trajectory. It is built from real agent trajectories containing natural and synthetic hacks, with audited command-level gold labels. This section formalizes the problem and presents our taxonomy of hacks (§2.2.1), describes the tasks and agents that produce the trajectories (§2.2.2), and details how we construct the natural

2.2.1 Problem Formulation 

2.2.1.1 Autoresearch tasks and trajectories. An autoresearch task provides an agent with a working environment (code, data, tools) and an instruction to optimize a metric. The agent interacts with the environment over a long horizon, producing a trajectory τ = ( s0, r 1, a 1, o 1, r 2, a 2, o 2, . . . , r T , a T , o T ), where 

s0 is the initial state (the task instruction and starting environment) and each step contributes the agent’s private reasoning rt, a command at (keystrokes issued to a shell), and an observation ot (terminal output). At the end, a verifier V runs in the environment and returns a scalar reward R = V (env T ) computed on the final environment state env T , typically by recomputing a metric from artifacts the agent produced.

> 2.2.3 HackBench Construction

In our preliminary experiments, agents rarely hacked when the instruction explicitly prohibited reward hacking, and the hacks we observed covered only a few types (e.g., fabrication, cherry-picking random seeds). Thus, while we collect naturally occurring hacks, collecting enough across a broad range of types was impractical. We thus developed a taxonomy of hacks (Table 2) and used it to guide synthetic hack injection into agent transcripts. Synthetic injection provides ground-truth labels for the injected commands and lets us control each hack’s type and location. 

2.2.3.1 Injecting synthetic hacks. We construct synthetic hack trajectories in three stages (Figure 7). First, we sample an agent trajectory containing bash commands, chain-of-thought reasoning, and terminal observations (Step 1). Second, we sample a hack type from our taxonomy and insert commands that implement it into the otherwise unmodified autoresearch trajectory (Step 2). Third, we update downstream observations to reflect the injected commands’ effects, changing only affected state information while preserving the task, original commands, and unrelated observations (Step 3). We used GLM-5.2 to perform both command injection and observation updates. We then used Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 to audit each trajectory to check whether the injected commands constitute reward hacking and whether downstream observations are consistent with the injection. We manually annotated cases where the two auditors disagreed. 

2.2.3.2 Collecting natural hacks. To surface candidates for naturally occurring hacks, we used GPT-5.6 Sol to screen autoresearch trajectories, dividing each transcript into eight chunks. We selected this configuration based on a pilot study of transcript screening, in which it achieved the highest recall among the configurations we tested. After human auditing, we combined 41 trajectories containing natural hacks with 128 trajectories containing 

synthetic hacks to form HackBench, a benchmark of 169 reward-hacking trajectories. Table 3 characterizes the resulting benchmark’s scale and sparsity. Trajectories have a median length of over one million characters and a median of 264 commands, yet the median number of gold-labeled hacking commands is only one. The median per-trajectory share of hacking commands is just 0.6%, requiring monitors to locate sparse evidence within long research histories. Nevertheless, some trajectories contain many hacking commands (up to 383), so the benchmark covers both isolated actions and multi-command cases.

2.4 Related Work 

Reward hacking and specification gaming. Reward hacking—high reward without the intended behavior—is a long-standing concern in reinforcement learning (Amodei et al., 2016; Krakovna et al., 2020; Skalse et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2022; Manheim and Garrabrant, 2018). In the RLHF/RLVR era it reappears as reward-model overoptimization (Gao et al., 2022; Moskovitz et al., 2023; Eisenstein et al., 2024) and, more sharply, as reward tampering , where a model edits its own reward channel (Denison et al., 2024). We study reward hacking in a setting where the “reward” is a concrete automated verifier the agent can read and modify, and where hacks are often multi-step programs rather than single actions. 

Autonomous research agents. Agentic coding and tool use (Yao et al., 2022; Schick et al., 2023; Jimenez et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024b) now extend to end-to-end ML research, and a wave of benchmarks measures it: MLE-13 bench (Chan et al., 2025), RE-Bench (Wijk et al., 2025), PaperBench (Starace et al., 2025), MLAgentBench (Huang et al., 2024), ResearchGym (Garikaparthi et al., 2026), MLS-Bench (Lyu et al., 2026), and AutoLab (Xu et al., 2026). These works optimize for and measure capability . We instead treat their tasks as a testbed in which to study integrity : the same open-ended, verifier-scored task that measures research skill also invites hacking of the verifier. Closely related to our work, Huang et al. (2026) studies when reward hacking occurs and what forms it takes. Our work complements Huang et al. (2026) by studying how well language-model-based monitors detect and locate reward hacking in autoresearch transcripts. 

Oversight, monitoring, and control. Detecting misbehavior from a model’s behavior or reasoning is central to scalable oversight (Bowman et al., 2022), weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2023), and AI control (Greenblatt et al., 2024). Chain-of-thought monitoring can catch reward hacking but is fragile if optimized against (Baker et al., 2025), and reasoning traces are not always faithful (Turpin et al., 2023); frontier models can scheme and hide intent when incentivized (Meinke et al., 2024; Hubinger et al., 2024). To monitor agents, Kutasov et al. (2025) propose asking agents to perform both a main task and a covert side task in general agentic settings (e.g., booking an airplane ticket), then checking whether monitors catch the side task. Libon et al. (2026) extends the main/side task red-teaming protocol to autoresearch settings. For coding agents, Deshpande et al. (2026) benchmark reward-hack detection in code environments via contrastive analysis. However, Martin and Roger (2026) and Jotautait˙ e et al. (2026) find that existing monitoring benchmarks may overestimate monitor performance because they evaluate monitors on relatively short trajectories and a narrow range of hacks. In our work, we mitigate these limitations through injection-based red-teaming: directly asking a model to inject different hack commands into long agent transcripts. 

LLM judges and long context. We build on LLM-as-a-judge methods (Zheng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Dubois et al., 2024), using LLMs to assess whether agent trajectories contain reward hacking. Because these trajectories can be very long, monitoring is subject to known failure modes such as lost-in-the-middle effects and context-window limits (Liu et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2024). 

2.5 Conclusion 

Autoresearch agents reward-hack because we ask them to maximize metrics, and they do so without proper regard to other implicit constraints, i.e. they should also perform actions humans would consider acceptable. We thus built HackBench, a benchmark that measures whether hacks inside autoresearch trajectories can be detected and localized, and showed that current frontier models do not fully succeed at this task. As agents take on more of the research loop, monitoring reward hacking is not an add-on but a prerequisite; to support future work, we release trajectories containing naturally occurring and synthetically injected hacks, together with audited command-level gold labels. 14 3 Training agents to interact with humans 

3.1 Overview 

Motivation Today, AI models are routinely used by humans for coding and research, and further progress in their ability to interact with us increasingly depends on the data and environments they are trained on. However, the most common post-training recipe trains on fully specified tasks paired with an automatic verifier, with no human in the loop – which aligns with the fully automatic autoresearch (Karpathy, 2026) paradigm. This creates a major discrepancy with real-world applications where agents work with users by following clarifications and feedback (Jin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). While current training tasks are usually fully specified, users in reality are much less specific. They may start with a goal that is not fully articulated, provide clarifications after seeing the agent’s output, revise requests midway, or provide counterexamples when a fix does not hold (Baumann et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026b). Models that solve tasks without such context can lose track of intent as the interaction goes on (Laban et al., 2026; Tack et al., 2026). An obvious direction is thus to train models to be better at agent-human interactions. However, common training recipes are not designed for an interactive setup: real interaction logs are scarce, costly, and difficult to convert into verifiable training examples (Baumann et al., 2026). An alternative is to simulate interactions, but these can be miscalibrated against real user behavior or collapse a heterogeneous user population onto a generic persona (Cheng et al., 2023). Moreover, ensuring task diversity, factual grounding, and executable verification in synthesized interactions is itself not straightforward. The problem for synthetic data is therefore to construct interaction data that is realistic, grounded in verifiable tasks with high-quality reference solutions, and calibrated in difficulty to the model being trained. 

Our contribution We introduce AutoInteract , a framework for generating data to train agents to interact with humans. Inspired by the recent Autodata method (Kulikov et al., 2026), which uses an agentic data scientist to create high-quality synthetic data, AutoInteract generalizes that approach to treat human-agent interaction itself as the object of synthesis. Starting from a verified source task, our method simulates a multi-turn interaction, including user clarifications, requirement changes, and corrective feedback, while preserving the source task’s final reference solution verifiable by an executable test. This consists of two phases: (1) creating user models from real interaction histories by learning behavior over a diverse population of profiles and (2) combining these user models with problem-solving agents in an agentic generate–evaluate–revise loop that simulates realistic, task-grounded interactions with calibrated difficulty for a given model. Models can then be trained with reinforcement learning on this data. Across algorithmic coding and software engineering tasks, AutoInteract substantially outperforms baselines with both Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-27B backbones. On LiveCodeBench-Pro (Zheng et al., 2026) and its multi-turn variant, AutoInteract raises average Pass@1 from 29 .9 to 43 .0 at 4B and from 49 .1 to 55 .7

at 27B, with especially large multi-turn gains from 23 .0 to 43 .2 and from 37 .2 to 50 .6, respectively. On SWE-Together (Wu et al., 2026b), it improves Pass@1 from 6.0 to 20 .6 at 4B and from 34 .9 to 38 .5 at 27B. Our analysis shows that multi-turn synthesis, grounded user models, and executable verification each contribute to performance, while training reward does not saturate as quickly as training on the source data. Overall, our results show that training can extend beyond solving isolated tasks to help models learn how to work with users. By making interaction itself synthesizable and verifiable, AutoInteract provides a practical way to train agents to incorporate human clarifications, feedback, and evolving requirements while still optimizing for task correctness.

3.3 Building User Models from Real Histories 

Phase 1 builds user models from real interaction histories. Each user model has two parts: (1) a user behavior prompt , which gives general role-playing guidance on how users tend to react, and (2) a user profile , which describes the specific user being simulated. We learn both components from real data of users interacting with agents. The user behavior prompt is optimized on observed next-user turns, while the profile population is built from (LLM-generated summaries of) behavior across users, diversified, and validated. Together, these two parts determine how a simulated user responds and the interaction style they follow. Appendix F.1 provides further implementation details beyond this section. 

3.3.1 Optimizing a History-Conditioned User Behavior Prompt 

The user behavior prompt is a reusable set of instructions for role-playing a human developer. It defines the simulator’s role and output format, explains how to use the conversation history and user profile, and describes common user reactions, such as accepting the work, correcting the assistant, reporting a failure, changing a requirement, or ending the interaction. It also asks the simulator to keep follow-up messages short and grounded in the current conversation. The prompt does not describe a specific task or user. To simulate users during trajectory synthesis, we combine it with a sampled user profile, the interaction history, and the source task as private context to the user simulator (Figure 9). We optimize a single user behavior prompt while keeping the simulator’s model weights fixed. We instantiate GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026), a reflective prompt optimizer that evolves prompts using natural-language evaluation feedback and Pareto-based candidate selection. Each training instance contains a conversation prefix, an assigned user profile, and the real user’s next message. Given the prefix and persona, a candidate user behavior prompt generates a simulated next message. An LLM evaluator assigns an overall score based on intent, reaction to the assistant, length, specificity, persona consistency, and realism, and provides textual feedback describing discrepancies from the real turn. For each sampled training minibatch, GEPA uses this feedback to propose a revised user behavior prompt and accepts the revision when it improves the minibatch score. Accepted candidates are evaluated on the validation set to update the per-instance Pareto frontier, and the final user behavior prompt is selected by aggregate validation performance. 

3.3.2 Constructing a Behavior-Grounded Profile Population 

We build the user profile population in three steps: build (prompt an LLM to summarize) one profile from each real user’s history, diversify these profiles while preserving their interaction patterns, and validate the 17 resulting population. LLMs write and audit the profile text, while behavioral structure and population-level validation use automated, data-driven procedures.  

> Profile construction.

For each user ID recorded in the user source data, we aggregate that user’s conversations and compute a profile summary, including what kinds of requests they make, how often they correct or push back on the assistant, how often their requirements change, and how much detail they provide. We prompt an LLM to summarize this evidence into a concise natural-language profile (see the prompt in Box F.1). This profile tells the simulator how the user tends to behave across turns, including user vagueness, pushback, requirement changes, verbosity, and the rate at which requirements are revealed across the conversation.  

> Profile diversification.

The observed users do not provide enough profiles for large-scale simulation, so we develop a method inspired by MAP-Elites (Mouret and Clune, 2015), a quality-diversity method that expands coverage across behavioral types/clusters (also called niches or cells) while retaining high-quality profiles in each type. Clusters are defined by expertise, vagueness, pushback, requirement-change tendency, and verbosity, and only combinations found among real users are kept. Within each cluster, we prompt an LLM to write new profiles that preserve a real user’s behavior settings and tone while varying surface details. The settings are inherited from the real profiles, adding diversity without inventing unsupported behavior combinations.  

> Profile validation.

Profiles are validated for quality. We employ an LLM to check each generated profile against the user’s measured fingerprint, flag unsupported claims and repository-specific details, and provide feedback for revision. During verification, automatic text-similarity checks remove near-duplicates and profiles that are unusually far from all real profiles. Final automatic checks measure behavioral-type coverage, diversity, and distributional similarity to the real users from the source data. 

3.4 Building Interactions with an Agentic Loop 

Phase 2 turns verified source tasks combined with the user models from Phase 1 into realistic, verifiable tasks with multi-turn trajectories. As shown in Figure 8 (right), the agentic loop consists of three modules: (1) Controller , which samples a verified source task and user profile and uses them, together with the learned user-behavior prompt, to specify the scenario and guide the conversation. After a failed attempt, it revises these instructions using evaluator feedback. (2) Trajectory generator , which follows the controller’s instruction and rolls out a candidate conversation between the user simulator and the assistant simulator. (3) Trajectory evaluator , which checks the candidate for validity and leakage, realism and coherence, profile consistency, and difficulty calibrated through solver rollouts and the source task’s executable verifier. Together, these modules form a data generation loop: the controller proposes an interaction, the generator produces it, and the evaluator determines whether to keep it or return it for revision.  

> 3.4.1 Interaction Task Synthesis (Controller + Trajectory generator)

Each trajectory begins with a verified source task. The controller pairs the task with the learned user behavior prompt and a sampled profile and generates an initial plan. The plan is a natural-language instruction that defines the user’s goal, behavior, information-disclosure strategy, and how the conversation progresses; see Box F.2 for an example. At each turn, the user simulator receives the user-simulation prompt shown in Figure 9, which combines these two components of the user model with the visible interaction history, the source task and reference information as a privileged context, and any revised controller guidance from a previous failed attempt. The assistant simulator responds only to the conversation and any repository context it would normally observe. The conversation ends on a user turn, and the requirements expressed across the interaction aim to remain verifiable with the source task’s original verifier.  

> Scenario-guided algorithmic trajectories.

For self-contained algorithmic tasks, the controller follows several interaction patterns, detailed in its generated plan: clarification or requirement change during design, clarification or requirement change after an initial solution, and bug iteration through concrete failing cases. Clarification reveals a requirement that was present from the start, a requirement change introduces a genuinely 18 new need, and bug iteration keeps the specification fixed. These patterns control when information becomes visible without changing the final verified task.  

> History-conditioned repository trajectories.

Repository-level software engineering tasks are more agentic than self-contained algorithmic tasks, requiring tool-driven repository search, fault localization, editing, testing, and responses to users across turns. The interaction is shaped by the user behavior prompt and sampled profile. Private task and reference information keep the simulated user factually grounded, while the assistant operates only on the visible conversation and repository state. The cumulative user requests must describe the intended change without revealing private tests or implementation details. Appendix F.3 provides further details on both task regimes.  

> 3.4.2 Agentic Self-Improvement

Initial generation does not guarantee that a trajectory is realistic, valid, or useful for training. Like Autodata’s agentic data scientist, which improves synthetic data through solver and judge feedback (Kulikov et al., 2026), 

AutoInteract repeatedly generates, evaluates, and revises candidates, but focuses the improvement process on multi-turn trajectories with a user (model) and grounds it in executable verification.  

> Multi-objective trajectory evaluation.

The evaluator checks three properties: the task is valid and does not leak private information; the interaction is realistic, coherent, and consistent with the sampled profile; and the difficulty is calibrated so that the target model struggles while a stronger solver succeeds. Solver responses are scored with the source task’s executable verifier. This combines semantic checks with execution-based evidence, and a trajectory is kept only when all checks pass.  

> Trajectory self-reflection.

When a check fails, the evaluator identifies whether the trajectory is invalid, unrealistic, inconsistent with the profile, too easy for the target model, too difficult for the stronger solver, or overly revealing. The controller turns this diagnosis into guidance for the next rollout. The source task, learned user behavior prompt, and sampled profile remain fixed across attempts, so the controller revises only the task interaction. This generate–evaluate–revise loop continues until the trajectory passes or the retry budget is exhausted. 

3.5 Experimental Setup   

> 3.5.1 Training Setup User Model Data.

We learn the user behavior prompt and user profiles from SWE-Chat (Baumann et al., 2026), a corpus of real trajectories of users interacting with coding agents, where each trajectory records a user ID and how that user responds in different situations.  

> Source Data.

For algorithmic coding, we extract the code subset of Dolci-Think-RL-32B, the reinforcement-learning corpus used to train OLMo 3 (Olmo et al., 2025). Our extraction contains 12,120 Python problems, each represented by a natural-language specification and a list of executable assertions that serve as its ground-truth verifier. For repository-level SWE, we use SWE-smith (Yang et al., 2026). Each instance provides an issue-style problem statement, a reproducible buggy repository state, relevant code context, an executable environment, tests that must change from failing to passing, and regression tests that must remain passing. We construct a deterministic pool of 19,000 function-level, pytest-backed instances across 117 repository environments, prioritizing compact fixes with bounded test suites.  

> Data Generation Configuration.

In Phase 1, Kimi-K2.6 (Kimi Team, 2025) is used throughout user-model construction. For user behavior prompt learning, it simulates and judges next-user turns and performs GEPA prompt rewriting; for user-profile construction, it drafts, expands, reformats, and verifies the profiles. In Phase 2, Kimi-K2.6 serves as the user simulator, controller, trajectory evaluator, and strong solver, while the target model itself serves as the assistant simulator and weak solver: Qwen3.5-4B when generating data for the 4B-scale runs and Qwen3.5-27B for the 27B-scale runs. > Evaluation Benchmarks.

For algorithmic coding, we first evaluate on LiveCodeBench-Pro (Zheng et al., 2026), a challenging benchmark for competitive programming. We use all 706 tasks with executable test cases: 391 easy, 251 medium, and 64 hard Codeforces problems. Models produce C++ solutions, which are scored by exact acceptance under the benchmark’s test cases and original time and memory limits. We additionally construct Multi-turn LiveCodeBench-Pro from the same task pool to isolate interaction-following ability from underlying problem difficulty. For each source problem, we generate a conversation using one of the five interaction scenarios in Section 3.4.1 and retain only conversations that pass validity and realism checks. The original problem identity and test suite are preserved, so the final C++ response is judged by the unchanged LiveCodeBench-Pro verifier. For repository-level SWE, we use SWE-Together (Wu et al., 2026b), a benchmark of 109 sandboxed repository tasks reconstructed from real user–agent sessions. 3.7 Related Work  

> User Simulation.

Simulated users have long been used in interactive systems, from agenda-based simulators for training dialogue policies (Schatzmann et al., 2007) to populations of generative agents that model human behavior at scale (Park et al., 2023). LLMs have made user simulators more fluent and general, and they are now widely used in interactive agent benchmarks (Yao et al., 2024; Barres et al., 2025) and as environments for training agents. Yet these simulators may not behave like real users. Assistant-tuned models prompted to play a user often produce turns that are unusually cooperative, well-organized, and similar in style, and stronger assistants can make worse simulators (Naous et al., 2026). Conditioning on a persona label can also represent a group through stereotypes rather than the variation found among real users (Cheng et al., 2023). Recent work addresses this problem by training simulators on real interactions, either through supervised learning on observed user turns (Naous et al., 2026) or reinforcement learning for persona consistency (Abdulhai et al., 2026) and latent user states (Wu et al., 2026a; Jin et al., 2026). Large persona libraries provide another way to represent diverse users (Ge et al., 2024). AutoInteract combines these directions. Rather than updating model weights or drawing profiles from an open-ended persona library, it learns an editable user behavior prompt from real interactions and builds a profile population from measured per-user behavior. 23 Multi-Turn Interaction Evaluation and Training. Models that solve fully specified tasks often perform worse when the same information arrives over several turns (Laban et al., 2026) or when the goal changes during a conversation (Tack et al., 2026). Single-turn evaluation does not capture this problem. Interactive benchmarks test it through feedback and tool loops (Wang et al., 2024), dual-control settings (Barres et al., 2025), preference-elicitation environments (Qian et al., 2025a), and repository sessions reconstructed from real developer–agent transcripts (Baumann et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026b). Several training methods place a simulated user in the optimization loop: SWEET-RL learns turn-level advantages from privileged training information (Zhou et al., 2025); CollabLLM estimates the long-term value of a reply by sampling future conversations (Wu et al., 2025); UserRL studies turn- and trajectory-level rewards across user-centered environments (Qian et al., 2025b); and MUA-RL trains multi-turn tool use with an LLM user (Zhao et al., 2025). These methods keep the interaction environment and user simulator fixed while improving the learning algorithm. AutoInteract instead studies how to construct the interaction data itself.  

> Grounded and Verifiable Synthetic Data.

Early work created synthetic post-training data by expanding a small set of seed instructions (Wang et al., 2023). Later work increased its scale and diversity through complexity evolution (Xu et al., 2024), direct prompting of aligned models (Xu et al., 2025), and persona conditioning (Ge et al., 2024). Because unconstrained generation can introduce errors, later methods ground generated data in documents or structured sources and check whether questions can be answered (Lupidi et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2025). Execution provides an especially strong check. In software engineering, tasks can be generated directly from repositories and validated by running tests (Pan et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026), producing an inexpensive, objective reward that is difficult to exploit. Other work generates tasks that challenge the current solver, either by generating tasks and verifiers together (Zhou et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025) or by targeting problems the model cannot yet solve (Zelikman et al., 2022). These methods still produce fully specified, single-turn tasks. AutoInteract 

keeps the source task and its executable verifier unchanged but presents the task as an interaction. The same execution reward can then measure both correctness and the model’s ability to follow requirements given over several turns. We also use a weak-versus-strong difficulty control, but the refinement loop applies it to what task information is revealed and when, rather than to the task’s inherent difficulty.  

> Agentic Data Generation and Self-Improvement.

Recent systems use agents to plan, inspect, and revise generated data instead of relying on fixed pipelines. AgentInstruct uses agent workflows to produce large post-training datasets (Mitra et al., 2024). Agentic Self-Instruct uses a data-scientist agent to revise a generation recipe based on weak- and strong-solver rollouts and judge feedback, and also improves its own scaffold (Kulikov et al., 2026). Related agents automate parts of the data-science workflow (Guo et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2025). This process is related to prompt- and scaffold-optimization methods that revise prompts or instructions using execution feedback (Madaan et al., 2023; Fernando et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024a; Lu et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026). Our profile expansion also uses quality-diversity search to cover a range of behaviors instead of maximizing a single score (Mouret and Clune, 2015; Cully et al., 2015). AutoInteract differs from Agentic Self-Instruct in three ways. First, it improves a multi-turn trajectory rather than a single example. Second, it includes a simulated user learned from real histories and evaluates realism directly. Third, the source task, sampled profile, and user behavior prompt remain fixed across retries, so the agent can revise only the multi-turn interaction. Holding these inputs fixed prevents the agent from meeting the difficulty target by changing the task in a way that disconnects it from the source, a problem reported in unconstrained agentic data loops (Kulikov et al., 2026). 

3.8 Conclusion 

We presented AutoInteract , a framework to improve agent-human interaction by creating realistic, grounded multi-turn tasks calibrated to the model being trained. It combines user models learned from real interaction histories with an agentic data creation loop that uses judge and execution feedback to control data quality and difficulty. Across algorithmic and software engineering tasks, training on these data significantly improves RL performance over baselines. Overall, AutoInteract shifts the focus from training agents to solve verifiable tasks independently to training them to collaborate with users on those tasks, incorporating clarifications, feedback, and evolving requirements across multiple turns to arrive at correct solutions. Our approach generalizes to a broader set of tasks, offering a path toward more effective human-agent interaction in the future. In particular, leveraging humans’ and AIs’ complementary skills for research, termed co-improvement

4.1 Overview 

Motivation AI systems have achieved superhuman performance on a cross-section of verifiable tasks through reinforcement learning, but currently remain relatively weak in non-verifiable tasks. For example, their generations exhibit a lack of high-quality writing, sometimes termed “AI slop” (Shaib et al., 2025). During pretraining, large language models are trained to predict the most likely next token over corpora that span every level of human expertise, from novice forum posts to expert prose. Maximum-likelihood pretraining therefore tends to produce continuations that reproduce the quality of its context rather than exceed it: given mediocre writing the model most naturally continues in kind, and nothing in the objective pushes generations toward expert quality. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a) can steer models higher, but it is only as good as the reward it optimizes—and a reward that reliably recognizes good writing is hard to build. Reward models trained on rankings from non-expert annotators inherit those annotators’ ceiling, so the very quality we care about most—expert, even superhuman, writing—is the quality such rewards are least equipped to certify. The current focus on post-training on verifiable rewards (e.g., on math, code, and agentic environments) does not obviously improve language communication with users, and may in fact worsen it. Progress on expert-level generation thus hinges on a reward signal that recognizes expert quality without requiring expert supervision at scale. 

Our contribution We introduce a method to fix this problem, Reinforcement Learning from eXpert-Aligned Rubrics (RL-XAR). We assert that standard RL methods are sufficient to train superhuman writing ability, but the failure to achieve this lies in the quality of the reward model — which currently fails to correctly reward expert writing, and incorrectly reward slop. The solution then is to align the reward model more closely with human experts. This is possible without collecting any new annotations, as there are already abundant examples of expert human writing. Our approach thus first identifies examples of such expert human writing from existing resources, and then uses these examples as a training signal. We learn to generate rubrics for a given writing task by ensuring the expert writing scores highly with these rubrics relative to current models, by maximizing the human-model gap. We can then perform standard RL with the newly learned expert-aligned rubrics. We conduct experiments in three settings: academic paper writing, story writing and authoring Wikipedia articles. In each case, we show our rubrics provide better alignment with expert writing, and RL-XAR training gives large gains over standard training.

On the academic paper section writing task, we find standard pairwise LLM judges or LLM-generated rubrics score GPT-5.6 or Opus-4.8 higher than high-quality human writing. Our RL-XAR (eXpert-Aligned Rubrics) approach instead meta-optimizes the rubrics on a training set of high-quality human-written sections, resulting in rubrics that reverse the result, and score the human writing higher on test papers. 

A standard rubric does not register the gap either. While there are many ways to build rubrics here, we prompted GPT-5.6 to build a meta-prompt that, given a paper missing the section, generates rubrics specific for judging that section. Switching to these rubric-based judgments instead shows even more extreme results, with the model being selected as higher scoring 100% of the time. 

4.3 RL from eXpert-Aligned Rubrics (RL-XAR) 

The previous section showed the failure to judge expert human writing exhibited by current systems. If AI slop is judged to be better than human writing by the LLM grader we use, then training against that grader will only encourage more slop. Given that we have access to abundant examples of expert human writing, one might naturally ask: can this be fixed by learning better judgments? 

Our approach to learning rubrics, called eXpert-Aligned Rubrics ( XAR ), is simple: • Identify examples of well-above average / expert human writing, splitting them into context and continuation. • Generate competing model-based continuations given the same context, which we expect to be worse writing (i.e., slop). • The above data form learning pairs, where humans should be scored higher. This training data is then used to learn to generate rubrics that maximize the scoring gap between humans and models. Then, to train a model to be better at writing via reinforcement learning ( RL-XAR ) using these rubrics, we iterate the following steps: (1) Learn XAR rubrics maximizing the gap between humans and the current model, as described above. (2) Perform reinforcement learning on a training set using the learned rubrics. (3) Repeat the procedure from step 1 until rubric learning can no longer identify a gap (i.e., we can no longer identify slop in the model). We note that this approach is reminiscent of GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014). 

4.3.1 Learning the rubrics: can an LLM recognize expert writing? 

While the learning of rubrics could be done in many ways, in our experiments we use the following simple procedure of meta-prompt optimization . We begin with an initial meta-prompt : given an example context, it asks an LLM to generate rubrics for judging the continuation. We can score the quality of this meta-prompt using the human −model gap on a (small) training set, as described above. 27 Our optimization procedure then iterates a simple refinement loop over a fixed number of iterations. At each iteration we show an optimizer LLM the current meta-prompt, the human −model gap it achieves on the training set, and the specific examples where it fails, and ask it to propose a revision that would widen the gap “for genuine-quality reasons, not superficial tells”. The meta-prompt is held to a bounded length, so the optimizer must consolidate criteria, rather than adding ever more requirements. After optimization is complete, we keep the best-performing candidate from across the iterations.

> Analysis of the Learned Rubrics

The initial rubrics, before learning, tend to cover the paper’s specific content— naming the concrete claims, methods, and results a section must convey—so they yield long, paper-specific coverage checklists. We observe this has the unfortunate effect of rewarding a “mini-paper” within each written section. Such criteria reward fluent imitation, which restates material from across the whole paper, while penalizing the selective human original for the detail it deliberately omits. Over the iterations the optimizer rewrites the guidance that mis-scores expert prose: docking the human for principled omissions, crediting a compressed summary as fluency, and mistaking surface polish for craft. The learned rubrics thus also tend to reward sectional ownership (i.e., not a miniature of the whole paper), disciplined selection, economy, and precise on-scope detail rather than breadth of coverage. Because the meta-prompt is length-bounded, the criteria settle into a small, stable, relatively paper-independent set that tends to sharpen what it rewards instead of accumulating requirements. 28 4.4 Main Experiments  

> 4.4.1 Paper Section Writing

We first apply RL-XAR to scientific paper-section writing.  

> Task.

The model is provided the paper with one section removed and must write that section (abstract, introduction, related work, or conclusion) to best fit this paper, basing every claim strictly on the paper’s actual content, with no invented numbers, results, or citations. The task prompt supplies a target word count equal to the original human-written section’s length and asks the model to stay within roughly ±15% of it.  

> Task data.

We collect 561 CS papers from the S2ORC corpus (Lo et al., 2020), holding out each of their four sections (abstract, introduction, related work, conclusion) in turn, to form 2,244 (paper, section) training examples; each example pairs the rest of the paper as context with the held-out human section as the expert reference. A disjoint set of 90 papers ( 360 examples) is held out for validation. For rubric optimization, we use the same small set as described in subsubsection 4.3.1. Further details are provided in subsection G.1.  

> Training.

We reinforcement-learn Qwen3.5-27B with prime-rl (Intellect, 2025) (GRPO, group size 16 , learning rate 3 × 10 −6, completions up to 16 k tokens), rewarding each generation by the learned rubric as scored a Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B judge. For rubric meta-optimization we use Kimi-K2.6.  

> RL-XAR loop.

Following our method described in subsection 4.3, we run three outer iterations of (i) meta-optimizing a rubric to maximize the expert-human −model gap against the current model, where we (ii) reinforcement-learn the model against the set of so-far collected rubrics. We train two iterations which re-target the failure modes compared to human writing that the previous round’s model still exhibits on the first two rounds of rubrics, and use the third round rubrics to assess the model without the bias of retraining.  

> Rubric Evaluation.

A model trained on one rubric can learn to satisfy that rubric alone, so we evaluate every writer against all three sets of meta-optimized rubrics and rank by the minimum of the three human-normalized scores—the worst rubric, allowing no cherry-picking. We use GPT-5.6 as a rubric judge for evaluation, so we do not evaluate with the same judge used for training. Results are given in Table 8. RL-XAR carries our 

27 B model to the top of this leaderboard among all writers: its strongest checkpoint reaches a worst-rubric score of 9.60 (human = 10 ), ahead of every external frontier model. While these rubrics are still potentially biased towards our model (having trained on the first two iterations) and still do not capture a fully accurate measurement of expert human writing, they still reflect performance better than previous approaches (cf. subsection 4.2).  

> Expert Human Evaluation

We confirm these automatic metric gains in a small preliminary study by evaluating on the authors’ own papers, on which they are clearly experts. In a blind expert side-by-side comparison of the RL-XAR model against the baseline Qwen3.5-27B on held-out sections, the RL-XAR writer was preferred by 16 to 2 (an 89% win rate), showing that the rubric-measured improvement corresponds to writing that expert readers may judge better. We found that RL-XAR wins because the baseline generally has weak sectional focus, often creating a “mini-paper”, and simultaneously contains too many unnecessary details, e.g. numerical results when unneeded. Nevertheless, we did not find the RL-XAR generations to be perfect either, and expect better rubric optimization and graders could find more flaws — which could be reinforced during training, i.e. using our approach with frontier models, rather than Qwen3.5-27B as a writer and Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B as a judge.

> Blind vs. ground-truth rubrics.

When generating rubrics from the rubric meta-prompt, it is possible to condition on only the context (called “blind” rubrics), or on the context and human continuation as well (called “Ground Truth” (GT) rubrics). The latter would likely only make sense when the human continuations are high quality, as the rubrics would be based on them as well. We compare their scoring ability in Table 10. GT rubrics give higher human-model gaps across two different rubric judges, as expected. Further study is required to understand the difference between using the two variants.  

> Length constraints and rubrics.

In preliminary experiments we found that standard models, given the task of paper section writing without a specific instructed length constraint, tend to write overlong sections — as much as two or three times as long as the hidden original human sections. This tends to allow them to score more highly on the given set of rubrics, similar to other instruction-following settings (Yuan et al., 2025). We therefore chose to define length-constrained tasks instead, i.e., to write a section similar to the original human length. Strong models are capable of this, although we observed an unwanted side effect that they tend to reserve a lot of chain-of-thought thinking towards this constraint (e.g., word counting).  

> Verifiable code-based rewards for surface-form distributional shifts.

Our standard RL-XAR reward is an LLM judge scoring an expert-aligned rubric, and it is good at what a rubric is good at: writing quality, coverage, and content. However, with current models it appears to be much less reliable at scoring some verifiable, measurable properties of the output distribution—e.g., how closely a writer matches the human distribution of surface form (sentence length and its variation, paragraphing, lexical diversity, punctuation rates, dialogue), and so on. These are properties one can compute exactly from the text with a small piece of code; we therefore complement the rubric judge with a code-based reward . This code reward is still expert human-anchored, like our meta-optimized rubrics. We score each constructed feature against this item’s human reference, and the model is rewarded for resembling the human distribution. We can hence also meta-optimize the code reward itself—searching for its feature set and overall code the same way as before by maximizing the human-model gap. Here, we run a small study with a fixed reward multiplied into the RL-XAR reward, with results shown in Table 11. They show that this approach can fix some distributional shifts that might otherwise occur during RL, without hurting the standard rubric rewards. More details are given in subsection G.4.  

> Training rubrics online.

We also briefly experiment with a fully online (co-training) objective where the writing generator is optimizing for the rubrics, and the rubrics are simultaneously being trained by GRPO (not meta-optimization) to optimize the human-model gap for the current policy—for the latter, generated rubrics are rewarded by the normalized gap, see Figure 17. While we leave subsequent experiments for future work, our first results in this direction on the story task are positive, with a gain from 2.8 (Qwen3.5-27B) to 6.0 (online RL-XAR) when testing with the same fixed (learned) rubric applied in our earlier experiments.

RLHF and reward modeling. Learning rewards from human preference comparisons (Christiano et al., 2017; Stiennon et al., 2020) underpins modern instruction tuning (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a; Rafailov et al., 2024b). Such rewards are bounded by the raters that produce them: preferences from non-expert annotators cannot certify expert—let alone superhuman—quality, and optimizing an imperfect reward invites over-optimization and reward hacking (Gao et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2024a), of which verbosity and length bias are canonical cases (Dubois et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025). RL-XAR targets exactly this gap: rather than collect more preferences, it derives the reward from existing expert texts. 

AI feedback and LLM judges. A growing body of work replaces or augments human labels with model-generated feedback—constitutional and AI-feedback training (Bai et al., 2022b), self-rewarding models that judge their own outputs (Yuan et al., 2024), and LLM-as-a-judge evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Glaese et al., 2022). We find that off-the-shelf judges, and even generated rubrics, systematically under -credit expert human writing relative to fluent model prose—a signal that would teach a policy to write worse. Our contribution is a procedure that repairs this misalignment without reference access at evaluation time. 

Rubrics, checklists, and criteria-based evaluation. Decomposing quality into explicit criteria has a long history in NLP evaluation (Ribeiro et al., 2020), and rubric-conditioned grading is increasingly used as a reward for open-ended generation. Unlike a fixed, hand-written rubric or a “mentions-X” checklist that only the reference can satisfy, we generate rubrics from the surrounding context and meta-learn the generator so its rubrics recover the discrimination that reference access would otherwise provide. 

Prompt and meta-prompt optimization. Automatically optimizing prompts (Zhou et al., 2022), including reflective and evolutionary search over prompt programs (Agrawal et al., 2026; Khattab et al., 2023), has become a practical alternative to gradient-based adaptation. We apply meta-optimization not to a task prompt but to the rubric-generation prompt, driving it to maximize the expert-human −model score gap; this adversarial objective—an evaluator improved against a generator—is reminiscent of GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014). Other relevant work with evolving rewards includes the work of Kim et al. (2025). 

Expert signals without expert annotators. Eliciting strong capabilities from weak or cheap supervision, and asking whether such oversight scales, is central to training superhuman systems (Bowman et al., 2022; Burns et al., 2023), and evaluating open-ended and creative writing is itself notoriously hard (Karpinska et al., 2021; Chung et al., 2025). RL-XAR sidesteps new expert annotation by anchoring the reward to abundant, externally credentialed expert texts—published papers, prize-winning fiction, and peer-reviewed encyclopedia articles—and learning to score them above current models. 35 4.6 Conclusion 

We introduced RL-XAR , Reinforcement Learning from eXpert-Aligned Rubrics, a recipe that turns readily available expert human writing into a learned, expert-aligned reward and then optimizes against it. Its key step meta-optimizes a rubric generator to maximize the gap between expert human text and current model generations, iterating until no discernible gap remains; standard RL against the resulting rubrics then improves writing quality. Across three domains—writing academic paper sections, Pulitzer- and Nobel-grade story continuation, and Wikipedia section writing—RL-XAR yields large gains over the base model. Our hope is that if AIs can be better communicators with humans, we can all benefit. Several limitations in our experiments point to future work. Our learned rubrics, trained in part against our own model, may remain biased toward it and do not yet fully capture expert quality. The writer (Qwen3.5-27B) and reward judge (Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B) used during training are mid-sized, and we expect the recipe to yield even better results when applied with frontier writers and graders. More broadly, RL-XAR offers a template for reward design in non-verifiable domains beyond the writing tasks used here: anchor the ground truth outside the systems under test, learn the criteria rather than hand-writing them, and iterate the evaluator against the generator until the human expert −model gap closes. 36 References

47 A AutoBenchmark implementation details 

A.1 Stage 1: Benchmark proposal  

> Stage 1-A. Authoring session.

We run one fresh research agent per iteration: an OpenHands agent (Wang et al., 2025) with a terminal, a file editor, a task tracker, and a summarizing condenser, in an isolated container. It writes only to its own iteration directory and reads the brief, the operator grounding, the pinned Harbor documentation (Harbor Framework Team, 2026), and earlier frozen submissions; public unauthenticated web retrieval is available for grounding. It cannot call models, launch judges or solvers, start containers, submit scheduler jobs, or run its own benchmark, and the structural validator below is the only executable check it may invoke. The session ends by writing a one-shot marker, after which we freeze and hash the tree.  

> Stage 1-B. Submission contract.

A submission contains a specification (construct, scientific value, scope, hypothesis, feasibility argument, threats to validity), a comparison of at least three materially different designs, a grounding directory with retained source bytes and a provenance manifest, and a record of the agent’s research process. Each task directory is a complete native Harbor task: instruction, task configuration, assets uploaded into the solver’s working directory, a deterministic reference solution, and a verifier directory holding the grading criteria, a judging context, and a private reference answer. Every manifest record carries an origin, a UTC retrieval time, a local path, a SHA-256, and license terms; self-generated data must additionally retain the executable generator, its parameters and seed, per-stage timestamps, and verified asset hashes. Criteria are native binary, Likert, or numeric items with positive weights and stable identifiers, and regexes, required phrases, length targets, and model-specific criteria are prohibited.  

> Stage 1-C. Structural validation.

We scaffold each task before the session from pinned harbor task init output, with the Dockerfile removed and fixed Reward Kit files installed. The first gate validates the frozen tree without executing it. Protected content must return byte-identical: verifier launcher, answer-judge prompt template, image digests, timeouts, resource and network settings, and the judge wiring that fixes the judge’s inputs, the reference path, and the aggregation; the agent may edit only the task description, keywords, and scientific metadata. Each required task must appear exactly once; every path must be a regular file, so symlinks, hardlinks, device nodes, and sparse files are rejected; the executable bit is allowed only on the reference solution and the protected launcher; and the package must stay within 1,024 files, 2 GiB total, 

256 MiB per file, and 240 -byte paths. Criteria must satisfy the Reward Kit schema (unique identifiers, positive weights, at least two points for a Likert item, an explicit range for a numeric item), and manifest timestamps, hostnames, and hashes must match the retained bytes. Three further checks apply to revisions: we parse the reference solution in shell no-execute mode and compile its literal Python heredocs; we hold criterion identity fixed, so an identifier cannot be rebound to another name nor a name to another identifier; and we reject an instruction change whose private grading and reference artifacts are byte-identical to the checkpoint, the signature of a partial write.  

> Stage 1-D. Revision integrity.

From the second iteration onward, a dedicated judge runs before execution. It sees the solver-visible projection of the checkpoint and of the current submission, namely instructions, READMEs, public metadata, and assets, together with a trusted diff, the feedback the agent could read, and the current grounding; private references, criteria, solutions, and all hidden solver results are excluded. It applies a counterfactual test to every material public change: with all prior solver answers and scores removed, would an independent author still make this change from the construct, the grounding, or a genuine ambiguity? A change passes only if retained sources support it at an exact locator; making an observed solver error less likely is not a justification. A static detector flags numbers, causal edges, stratum identities, and long n-grams that newly appear in public files and also appear in the feedback, as advisory input. The verdict is binding, and its provenance review covers the whole current grounding, not only records touched by the diff.  

> Stage 1-E. Reference-solution admission.

We execute the agent’s own reference solution in the real task container through Harbor’s Oracle agent and grade it with the real verifier and answer judge. A submission is admitted only with no verifier error and reward at least 0.9, which establishes that the task is solvable under the fixed contract, that the environment holds what the solution needs, and that the grader awards the intended answer. Retained reward artifacts are hashed into the validation report the quality judges later read.  

> Stage 1-F. Repair.

A failure at any of the three gates returns the iteration to a fresh session of the same agent, 48 which receives a repair report naming the failing gate and the defect and is restricted to fixing it. We allow at most ten submission attempts per iteration. 

A.2 Stage 2: Benchmark solving  

> Stage 2-A. Solver runs.

Each solver attempts every task once, which we treat as one observation per model and task, using Harbor’s agent in the task container under the fixed execution contract and per-route token caps, five tasks at a time. A completed run retains the final answer, the trajectory in Harbor’s ATIF format, and a result summary. Provider errors, container and scheduler failures, missing trajectories, and truncated collections are classified as infrastructure outcomes and retried, never recorded as incorrect answers; only completed runs enter the difficulty signal, and the feedback states this so the agent does not read a failed run as a solved task. External solvers use a separate lane whose results go to an operator-private directory that no feedback index references, routed through a loopback proxy.  

> Stage 2-B. Answer judging.

Answers are graded in a separate verifier container by a judge that sees the judging context, the answer, and the private reference, and scores each criterion independently; scores combine by a protected weighted mean into a reward in [0 , 1] . The judge prompt is a harness-owned template that treats the task, the answer, and every quoted asset as untrusted evidence, and we fix the judge route so the agent cannot address the grader. Published feedback carries per-criterion scores and anchors. 

A.3 Stage 3: Benchmark review  

> Stage 3-A. Criteria.

We run one judge per criterion. The wording below is canonical and is disclosed verbatim to the research agent, so it knows what its submission is reviewed against. (i) Construct validity. The benchmark measures the research-agent capability claimed in the brief and the specification, not an incidental proxy or an evaluator quirk. (ii) Correctness. Task artifacts, private references, rubric semantics, score calculation, and reproducibility are internally correct and free of leakage. (iii) Feasibility. A genuinely stronger research agent could solve the benchmark with the supplied evidence, resources, and rules; difficulty from missing evidence, impossible requirements, or circular evaluation is rejected. (iv) Usefulness. Progress would transfer to meaningful scientific work or provide valuable evidence about research-agent capability. (v) Overall. The integrated contribution has no serious red flags and is a credible publication-quality benchmark design at the current scale.  

> Stage 3-B. Isolation and verdicts.

The five sessions run concurrently on physically isolated bundles holding the brief, the specification, the grounding, the frozen submission, a sanitized validation report (versions, image digests, judge identifier and prompt hash, scoring wiring, reference-solution status and reward), and the retained solver evidence. No judge sees another’s verdict. Each writes a JSON verdict with a pass or fail decision, a responsibility field separating submission defects from harness faults, at least three artifact citations, at least one strength and one weakness, required fixes for a fail, a confidence level, and a rationale.  

> Stage 3-C. Evidence protocol.

A manifest entry is an untrusted assertion. Every material claim or grading anchor must be verified against retained content at an exact locator or a reproducible derivation, and placeholder domains, invented citations, dead locators, self-authored text presented as publisher bytes, and unsupported license or version declarations are mandatory failures. Judges may not browse the web to repair an evidentiary gap, so a claim that cannot be checked inside the bundle counts against the submission. 

A.4 Overall loop  

> Component A. AI Feedback.

We publish one curated, secret-scanned bundle per iteration containing the validation report, per-task run status, sanitized trajectories and answers for the in-loop solvers, per-criterion rubric judgments, and the five verdicts. Only files named by the bundle index may be read, and hidden external results stay in the private lane of subsection A.2. Raw provider logs, exception text, and judge 49 reasoning are omitted, and all published evidence is labeled untrusted, so an instruction embedded in an answer or a retrieved document is not a directive to the next session.  

> Component B. Checkpoint selection.

Before iteration t we build a history over iterations 1 to t − 1. An iteration is eligible when all five verdicts passed and its solver evaluation is complete; among eligible iterations we take the lowest mean reward on the solver designated as the selection objective, breaking ties toward the later iteration. With nothing eligible we fall back to the latest fully evaluated iteration and label the session a repair. The selected checkpoint is copied atomically into the new writable directory for in-place editing, and the prompt exposes every prior snapshot and feedback index, so the agent can inspect iterations it did not descend from. The end-of-run selection uses the same policy but refuses any checkpoint that did not pass every judge, and is written where the agent cannot see it.  

> Component C. Human feedback.

The operator-in-the-loop setting of subsection 1.3 reuses this machinery. The driver finishes the preceding iteration, then pauses before the next session and writes a review packet with per-task scores, action and tool counts, rubric misses, quality outcomes, and paths to the full trajectories and answers; no model job runs while it waits. We validate and snapshot the admitted response and expose it as one more read-only artifact alongside the checkpoint history, after which the loop resumes.

> Diversifying the profile population.

We use a method based on MAP-Elites (Mouret and Clune, 2015) to organize the 189 profiles into behavioral niches defined by expertise, vagueness, pushback, requirement-change tendency, and verbosity. We divide numeric settings into three ranges based on the observed data and retain only niches occupied by at least one real user. Each niche receives a target number of profiles based mainly on its real-user count, with a minimum that keeps rare behavior represented. To fill these targets, an LLM writes variants that preserve a real profile’s behavior settings and tone while changing details such as role, programming stack, and coding preferences. All profiles distilled from real users remain in the population.  

> Validating the expanded profiles.

Each new profile stays in the same behavioral niche as its source profile. Automatic text-similarity checks remove near-duplicates and candidates that are unusually different from every real-user profile. We then measure niche coverage, similarity to real profiles, agreement between the expanded and real behavior distributions, and text diversity. > Main rule: react to what is visible.

Do not invent a message that is disconnected from the agent’s last turn. Before writing, follow these steps: (1) Read the agent’s last output. Decide whether it completed the request, made partial progress, made a mistake, asked a question, claimed success, stopped early, or hit an error. (2) Identify what remains. Address the remaining gap or react to the latest result instead of repeating the whole task. (3) Choose the shortest realistic response. Usually this is a brief acceptance with a next step or a direct correction. Escalate only when the history supports it. Base the message on the agent’s last turn. Do not introduce a new concern unless the history or task context supports it. 

> Accept reasonable progress.

In most real sessions, the user guides the agent forward. Match these broad rates: • ∼ 55% no pushback : accept the result and move on. For example, say “yes”, “yep do it”, or “lgtm”. You may instead give the next step or end the task. When a success claim is plausible, do not start a new audit without a reason. • ∼ 30% correction : correct a result that is mostly right but misses a detail. For example, say “no, keep the old signature”, “don’t touch the tests”, or “put it in utils”. Keep the correction short. • ∼ 7% failure report : paste an error, failing test, stack trace, CI log, or review comment, often with one line such as “still failing” or with no explanation. • Occasionally reject the result, take over, change a requirement, or ask a clarifying question. Do not ask “show me the diff” or invent a criticism on every turn. Request verification only when the last output gives a specific reason. When unsure, prefer a short response that accepts reasonable progress. 

> Common patterns.

• After a success claim : accept it, end the task, or give the next small step. Do not start another review without a reason. • After an interrupted action : repeat the instruction, sometimes verbatim, instead of changing to an unrelated question. • When the agent asks a question : answer it, often in one to five words. A user may instead restate the next needed action. • When you want to understand : ask a tentative comprehension question about the last output (“so this keeps backward compat because X?”), don’t issue a new command. • First turn : include the detail needed to start, such as a full task, pasted plan, issue, error, or file path. Preserve concrete references. Later turns should usually be short reactions rather than a new full specification. 

> Possible intents.

Possible intents include understanding, creating code, debugging, refactoring, testing, version-control work, configuration, and cleanup. Choose the intent based on what remains and what the agent just did. 

> Broad profile types.

• Vague Requester : gives brief, underspecified requests and assumes context. Approvals are short and nontechnical, such as “yes please fix it” or “try it.” Concrete links, paths, and errors are still allowed when the history supports them. Do not invent detailed technical concerns. • Expert Nitpicker : is precise and opinionated and may cite files, lines, or APIs. Scrutiny usually concerns design, placement, naming, requirements, or a specific technical error. Do not ask for a diff unless there is a concrete reason. • Mind Changer : may reverse a decision, change a requirement, or add scope when the history supports 63 the change. Do not force a change on every turn. Base any change on a specific clue or tradeoff in the conversation. • Other : is balanced and cooperative, confirms success plainly, and reports problems directly. 

> Style.

• Be short. Most messages contain fewer than 20 words, and many contain one to eight words, such as “yes”, “run the tests”, “still failing”, or “why?” Write more only for a real first request or when pasting an error, log, or plan. • Use the brief, direct style of a busy developer. Fragments and missing punctuation are allowed. Pleasantries are optional. • Refer to concrete details when relevant, including file paths, function names, flags, pull request or issue URLs, pasted diffs, and command output. > Vary messages with the context.

Vary messages because the agent’s output and the task stage differ. Do not always begin with praise. A response may instead contain a correction, pasted evidence, or a one-word instruction. 

> Ending the session.

End when the goal is complete or the user would give up. Use a short closing action or message, such as “lgtm” or “thanks, done.” If the agent remains stuck after repeated attempts, stop the session. Do not extend it without a reason.

Emit only the user’s next message text.

Box F.2 shows an initial controller plan for a repository-level trajectory. The trajectory generator uses this private instruction to guide the simulated interaction. The assistant simulator does not see it. Example Initial Controller Plan  

> Goal.

Role-play a developer who needs a bug fixed in TextFSM’s OnAssignVar method. Assigning a 

List value currently raises an UnboundLocalError . The final fix must preserve existing behavior for ordinary values and for lists without named capture groups. Lists with named groups must accumulate dictionaries containing those groups.  

> User behavior.

Be methodical, moderately verbose, and somewhat apologetic about not knowing the parser internals. Give concrete reproductions and error output, ask for the reasoning behind the fix, and become shorter and more direct after the first turn. Do not write the patch yourself or reveal private tests or the reference solution.  

> Information-disclosure strategy.

Begin with the exception, its location, and a simple List reproduction, but do not initially mention the required representation for named capture groups. If the assistant asks for evidence, provide the exact traceback and state that behavior for existing templates must remain unchanged. Save the key detail for the final user turn: a list pattern with named groups must produce dictionaries rather than raw strings.  

> Conversation progression.

Use three user turns. Keep the first two assistant turns focused on diagnosis and high-level planning rather than a complete patch. React to what the assistant says, and use the reproduction to correct unsupported assumptions instead of adding unrelated requirements. In the final user turn, provide the named-group example, explain the expected dictionary shape, and ask the assistant to implement the full fix. End the generated trajectory there. During training, the model will produce the final assistant response, which the source task’s verifier will score.  

> Algorithmic trajectory generation.

Each verified source task provides a natural-language specification, a reference solution, and executable assertions. The controller pairs the task with the learned user behavior prompt 64 and a sampled user profile, then selects a turn count and one of five scenarios. Clarification, requirement change, and bug iteration each receive one third of the examples. Clarification and requirement change are each divided equally between design-stage and post-solution variants. Conversations contain two to four user turns, alternate between the user and assistant simulators, and end on a user turn. The user simulator receives the source task and reference information as private context, while the assistant simulator sees only the conversation. Design-stage variants do not allow intermediate code. Structural checks reject conversations that do not follow the assigned scenario or whose cumulative requests cannot be scored by the source task’s unchanged verifier.  

> Repository trajectory generation.

Each verified repository task provides an issue, a reproducible buggy repository state, relevant code context, and executable tests. The user simulator receives the learned user behavior prompt, a sampled user profile, and private task and reference information so that its requests remain factual. The assistant simulator receives only the visible conversation and the repository context available to a coding agent. Conversations contain one to five user turns and end on a user turn. The controller may limit intermediate assistant turns to diagnosis, questions, or high-level planning. Before solver evaluation, deterministic checks reject trajectories that reveal private test identifiers or implementation details, claim that an unapplied patch was applied, contain invalid patches, introduce regressions, or include an intermediate patch that already solves the task.  

> Staged trajectory evaluation.

The evaluation covers the three axes defined in the main text: validity and leakage, realism and coherence, and calibrated difficulty. The evaluator runs the least expensive checks first. For each candidate trajectory, the weak solver and strong solver each generate three final responses. The weak solver is the target model. Its score must be at most 40% , while the strong solver’s score must be at least 

70% . Any intermediate assistant code or patch is also executed so that trajectories containing the complete solution can be rejected. The remaining checks cover profile consistency, scenario consistency for algorithmic tasks, and factual consistency for repository tasks. An algorithmic response receives the fraction of source assertions it passes. A repository response receives the fraction of previously failing tests it fixes, but its score is zero if any previously passing test regresses.  

> Repository readiness check.

Before a repository example is retained, it must support a verified solver rollout using only information available to the assistant. In this rollout, the solver must inspect the repository, apply a valid patch, run tests after editing, receive at least one user checkpoint based only on visible evidence, and finish with a patch that passes the verifier. A rollout is rejected if it contains private evaluator information. This check does not change the training format: the stored prompt ends on the final synthesized user turn, and the model generates the final assistant response during training.  

> Self-improvement loop.

When a check fails, the evaluator returns short feedback that identifies whether the trajectory is too easy, too hard, invalid, unrealistic, factually inconsistent, or too revealing. The controller uses this feedback and the earlier attempts to revise its instruction for the next rollout. A candidate can receive up to five revision rounds. The source task, sampled user profile, learned user behavior prompt, and assigned algorithmic scenario remain fixed. Only the controller instruction, turn count, and resulting interaction may change. The final dataset contains only trajectories that pass every check. 

F.3 Details of Training Tasks  

> Two task regimes.

We use the controller for two types of tasks: algorithmic coding and software engineering (SWE). Algorithmic tasks use scenario-guided trajectories , which control how requirements change across turns. Repository-level SWE tasks use history-conditioned trajectories , whose interaction patterns come from the user models learned from real histories in Section 3.3.  

> Scenario-guided algorithmic trajectories.

For self-contained algorithmic tasks, explicit scenarios control how requirements unfold. Clarification and requirement change can occur either before or after the assistant writes an implementation. In the design-stage variants, intermediate assistant turns discuss requirements and high-level design without writing code. In the post-solution variants, the assistant first writes an implementation and the user then responds to it. These combinations give four scenarios. Bug iteration, which has no design-stage variant, is the fifth. The five scenarios cover three interaction types: 65 (1) Clarification: the underlying goal remains fixed, while a previously unstated part of the specification is revealed later. (2) Requirement change: the active goal genuinely changes during the interaction, and later requirements may supersede earlier ones. (3) Bug iteration: the specification remains fixed, while the user supplies progressively more informative counterexamples to incorrect implementations.  

> History-conditioned repository trajectories.

For repository-level software engineering, we use bug-fixing tasks from SWE-smith (Yang et al., 2026). Each task contains an issue-style problem statement, a reproducible buggy repository state, relevant code context, and executable tests. The learned user behavior prompt and sampled user profile guide the interaction, so trajectories follow patterns observed in SWE-Chat rather than a fixed set of scenarios. At every turn, the user simulator receives the learned user behavior prompt, sampled profile, private task context, and interaction history. Private tests and reference information keep the simulated user factual but are never shown to the assistant. The assistant simulator acts as a repository coding agent: it searches the repository, locates relevant code, edits files, runs tests, and responds to the user. The controller keeps all claims consistent with the repository and ensures that the cumulative user requests describe the intended change without revealing private tests or implementation details. 

# G Training agents to communicate with humans: extra details 

G.1 Datasets 

G.1.0.1 Papers (S2ORC) . We draw well-written scientific papers, selecting 561 for training and validation. Holding out each of a paper’s four sections (abstract, introduction, related work, conclusion) in turn yields 

2,243 (paper, section) examples, each pairing the rest of the paper as context with the verbatim human section as the expert reference; a disjoint set of 90 papers ( 360 examples) is held out for validation. To measure writing quality we use Muse Spark 1.1 rating 8 dimensions on an integer 1–10 scale: clarity, structure, motivation, rigor of exposition, coherence, conciseness, positioning and results communication. Then it emits an integer 1–10 holistic writing-quality score—explicitly “your expert judgment, not necessarily the mean” of the dimensions, and up to 25 words of justification. For rubric optimization and test we scan 400 papers scored for writing quality, and take the 13 highest-rated—for 52 (paper, section) examples, split into 8 papers (32 examples) for training and 5 (20 examples) held out for validation. 

G.1.0.2 Stories (Project Gutenberg). The story corpus is public-domain books from Project Gutenberg, taken as plain text with the Gutenberg header, footer, and license boilerplate stripped. For rubric meta-optimization and test data the quality guarantee is an external human credential—public-domain literary standing rather than any model’s opinion. Specifically, we select public-domain novels by Nobel Prize in Literature laureates and early Pulitzer (Novel) winners, preferring each author’s lesser-read titles by download count to limit memorization, in addition to dropping examples that fail an LLM memorization test. We use a separate set of 229 books to produce 2,290 examples for training, and 31 books to form 310 examples for validation. Our LLM memorization test is a temperature-0 probe. If a model reproduces more than 5% of the reference’s 13-grams, or a single contiguous verbatim run of ≥20 words is present, the example is dropped from the data. Each pairing consists of the story-so-far as context with the author’s verbatim continuation as the expert reference. Thus, each example gives the writer ∼2,500 words of preceding prose—cut inside the middle 20 –80% of the body, at a sentence boundary that never straddles a chapter heading—and asks for the next ∼300 words; the true next passage is the human reference. 

G.1.0.3 Wikipedia (Featured/Good Articles). The Wikipedia corpus is English articles carrying a peer-review quality tier—Featured- or Good-Article status, whose editorial review is again an external human credential—biased toward less-popular titles (low recent Wikimedia pageviews) to reduce memorization, in addition to applying the LLM memorization test as described before. Restricting to articles with at least three substantial body sections and posing a section-completion task (title and preceding context → the verbatim 66 human section) yields 1,187 training examples, with 162 held out for validation. For rubric meta-optimization we use a further 42 examples and a further 12 for validation, with 60 examples used for final testing. 

G.2 Judging protocols 

G.2.0.1 Direct pairwise judging. The first standard protocol shows a judge the two candidates for a given example—the human section and a model section—and asks, with no rubric, “which version is the better [section]? Reason step by step, then decide.” To cancel position bias each example is judged in both A/B orders; an example counts as a consistent win for whichever candidate is preferred in both orders, and as order-dependent (a “tie”) when the verdict flips on the swap. G.2.0.3 Rubric meta-optimization loop. Each iteration the optimizer is handed a deliberately narrow diagnostic and asked to rewrite only the mutable guidance portion of the meta-prompt. Its inputs are: (i) the current guidance text; (ii) the iteration’s train-split aggregate (mean human score, mean model score, mean gap); and (iii) a per-example digest covering the training examples only , sorted worst-gap-first—each entry listing the example’s human −model gap, the two candidates’ weighted scores and length penalties, the names and weights of the criteria the rubric used, and the judge’s one-line rationale for each side. From this it must infer the systematic failure modes (e.g. a rubric that keeps rewarding fluent over-inclusion) and emit revised guidance. It never sees the validation split, the full criterion descriptions , the candidate or paper texts, or the per-criterion numeric scores—so the loop cannot game the held-out metric. Design a RUBRIC for grading a candidate writing of this paper’s {section_label } (target length: {target_words } words). Your rubric must separate genuinely excellent human writing from competent model completions. Models routinely win by exploiting rubrics that implicitly reward keyword density, exhaustive coverage, or smuggling accurate technical details across section boundaries. Close that loophole by making *scope discipline* the highest-weighted, hardest ceiling, and by treating strategic omission as evidence of scholarly judgment, not incompleteness. Core principles: - **Section-First Fidelity, Not Paper-First Coverage.** The passage is graded on how well it executes the unique rhetorical contract of a {section_label }, not on how much of the paper it accurately represents. Reward candidates that stay in their lane. - **Zero-Tolerance Scope Discipline.** Any accurate detail—definitions, formulas, architectures, imple-mentation steps, dataset lists, exact numeric results, method roadmaps, or self-references—imported from another section is a severe error. Models frequently disguise such leakage as "synthesis." Treat every off-scope import as a major deduction regardless of narrative polish. A detailed but leaky passage must score below a selective, concise, or even slightly generic passage that respects boundaries. - **Omission Is a Virtue.** Top-tier human writing often omits paper-central methods, datasets, and precise results from the abstract, introduction, and conclusion because those specifics belong elsewhere. Do NOT penalize their omission. Penalize the *inclusion* of section-inappropriate specifics far more heavily than the *omission* of them. - **Specific but Misplaced Is Worse Than Generic but Scoped.** A model passage that replaces generic boilerplate with paper-specific but off-scope technical detail is committing scope fraud. Ensure this backfires. Mild domain-standard framing that respects boundaries is acceptable; imported technical specifics that violate boundaries are not. - **Reward Framing, Not Fact-Density.** High scores come from the correct abstraction level, emphasis, and relational framing appropriate to this section—not from checklist-style inclusion of correct terms. Construct 4–5 criteria summing to 1.0. Embed these section-specific non-negotiables in each description. A single violation should be enough to cap that criterion at 5 or below: - **Abstract:** Contribution arc and principal findings at high level only. No methods, no implementa-tion, no dataset catalogs, no formulas, no granular ablations, no exact numeric results beyond broad gains, no section roadmaps. - **Introduction:** Gap diagnosis and contribution sketch only. No architectural specifics, no training protocols, no exact numeric results, no method roadmap sentences. - **Related Work:** Prior work ONLY. Zero mention of the authors’ own method name, model architec-ture, results, experimental setup, or any forward-looking roadmap. Self-leakage is an immediate critical failure. - **Conclusion:** Synthesis of lessons and implications only. No new claims, no re-introduced method mechanics, no exact result dumps, no dataset/baseline lists, no extensive future-work filler. Suggested criteria: - **Scope Discipline and Section Boundaries** (0.35–0.40). Full credit: impeccable execution of ONLY the communicative contract of a {section_label }; zero import of content belonging elsewhere. Deductions are severe for any leakage: a single minor off-scope detail caps at 5; method roadmap or result dumping caps at 2. Generic but properly scoped content incurs no deduction here. Score anchor: 10 = pristine boundaries; 5 = minor leak present; 0 = structural misalignment or heavy dumping. - **Selective Synthesis for this Section’s Purpose** (0.25–0.30). Full credit: selects exactly the on-scope concepts, contrasts, or takeaways this {section_label } requires and weaves them into a cohesive narrative 69 whose emphasis matches the paper’s priorities *for this section*. Strategic omission of paper-central details that belong elsewhere is a virtue. Penalize keyword dumping, list-like recitation, and inflating minor points. A passage that imports off-scope specifics to create an artificially "stronger" narrative must be penalized doubly here and in Scope. Score anchor: 10 = surgically selective; 5 = accurate but indiscriminate or padded; 0 = hallucinated or deeply misaligned. - **Conciseness and Signal-to-Noise** (0.15–0.20). Full credit: every sentence serves the section’s argument at its proper abstraction level; zero throat-clearing, universal truths, redundant restatement, or boilerplate. Key deductions: verbosity diluting argument; padding with extra words to smuggle off-scope detail; generic filler. Do not reward extra length.
