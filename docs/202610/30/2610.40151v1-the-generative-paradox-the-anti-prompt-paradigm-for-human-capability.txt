Abstract 

Generative AI in education is usually judged by the output of human-AI teams. That focus 

overlooks a second outcome: what people can still understand, challenge, repair, and 

adapt when assistance changes or disappears. We call this tension the generative paradox 

and distinguish four manifestations: performance, assistance, ownership, and capability 

stock. We propose developmental function allocation, which complements operational 

function allocation and meaningful human control by treating retained human capability as 

a design objective alongside current joint performance. The Anti-Prompt paradigm 

reverses the default interaction order: when immediate generation would preempt a

consequential human act, AI should first create the conditions for human construction. We 

distinguish the validity of a product, responsibility for its construction, and evidence of 

capability after support changes. Representative Anti-Prompts, a canonical interaction 

sequence, and stress tests spanning mathematical reasoning, experimental science, 

software diagnosis, and lesson design make these distinctions concrete. We conclude with 

falsifiable predictions about retained capability and a system-level hypothesis about the 

reproduction and distribution of expertise. 

Keywords: Generative AI; artificial intelligence in education; human-AI interaction; scaffolding; 

metacognition; agency 

## 1. The generative paradox 

Generative AI is often evaluated by the quality and efficiency of the products that people and 

systems create together. Such gains matter for productivity and access (Noy & Zhang, 2023; Yan et 

al., 2024). Yet immediate joint performance is only one outcome of assistance. A second is what 

people can understand, challenge, repair, and reproduce after assistance changes or disappears. 

As educational AI shifts from supporting discrete tasks to generating complete explanations, proofs, 

code, and lesson materials, the distinction between a polished product and the capability retained 

by the learner becomes a design problem for artificial intelligence in education. 

Learning research has long distinguished performance during instruction from durable learning 

(Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). Khosravi et al. (2026) bring this distinction to AI learning-companion 

design. Our focus is the allocation decision within an interaction: which consequential act should 

remain open to the learner, when should assistance disclose it, and what later evidence shows that 

the target capability has developed? 

These outcomes can diverge. Across four experiments involving 3,562 participants, GenAI 

collaboration improved immediate professional output, but those gains did not consistently persist in 

subsequent solo work and were accompanied by lower intrinsic motivation and greater boredom (S. Page 2 

Wu et al., 2025). In two logical reasoning studies involving 698 participants, AI assistance improved 

accuracy, whereas participants substantially overestimated their own performance (Fernandes et 

al., 2026). Product quality, independent capability, motivation, and calibrated self-knowledge can 

therefore move in different directions. 

Evidence from STEM learning makes the same dissociation particularly visible. Unrestricted GenAI 

access improved assisted mathematics performance but reduced short-term unaided performance 

in one field experiment, while a teacher-informed guarded tutor largely mitigated this loss (Bastani 

et al., 2025). By contrast, a carefully designed AI tutor produced substantial learning gains in 

undergraduate physics (Kestin et al., 2025). In a recent preprint, developers assigned AI assistance 

while learning an unfamiliar programming library showed weaker subsequent conceptual 

understanding, code reading, and debugging, on average; exploratory analyses associated more 

cognitively engaged interaction patterns with better learning (Shen & Tamkin, 2026). The relevant 

question is not whether AI assistance is beneficial or harmful in general, but which functions it 

performs, which human operations remain available for practice, and which outcomes are 

measured and when (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). 

We call the problematic case generative substitution: AI production displaces the human 

construction, evaluation, or revision through which a target capability is intended to develop or be 

maintained. Human responsibility alone does not make delegation substitutive. Delegating 

transcription, translation, or routine calculation may reduce incidental burden without displacing the 

capability at stake. Delegating proof strategy, causal diagnosis, model selection, or the choice of a 

lesson's central representation may instead remove the very operation through which future 

capability develops. The same AI contribution can therefore be peripheral for an expert, an 

essential accommodation for one learner, and substitutive for another. The paradox is relational, not 

a fixed property of a tool.

1.1 Four manifestations of the generative paradox 

The generative paradox appears in at least four forms. A performance paradox arises when better 

joint output coexists with weaker or unchanged independent capability. Unchanged independent 

performance is not, by itself, evidence of deterioration. Assisted improvement without learning, 

foregone learning relative to an appropriate alternative, and loss of a previously available capability 

require different comparisons. An assistance paradox arises when additional help improves 

progress while removing opportunities for generation, retrieval, selection, justification, or monitoring 

through which later human capability develops. An ownership paradox arises when visible 

authorship and consequential control diverge: a person may produce or edit the final artifact while 

AI has framed the problem, selected the strategy, or supplied the justification; conversely, AI may 

execute an operation while the person retains the capacity to challenge or override it and recover 

from its failure. A capability-stock paradox arises when locally efficient delegations accumulate in 

ways that narrow the future distribution of expertise needed to supervise AI-supported practices, 

recover from failures, teach successors, or improve those practices. 

These are not logical contradictions but observable dissociations among outcomes measured at 

different times and levels of analysis.  Conventional indicators such as output accuracy, time saved, Page 3 

user satisfaction, or the proportion of AI-generated content can conceal both who controlled the 

consequential act and what capability remains after support changes. This concern is also reflected 

in Agentivism, which similarly cautions against treating final product quality as sufficient evidence of 

learning (Yan & Gašević, 2026). Box 1 translates each manifestation into an observable 

dissociation and an empirical test, while Fig. 1 summarizes the underlying distinction between 

immediate joint performance and retained human capability .

## 2. Developmental function allocation 

Allocating functions between people and machines is a longstanding problem in human factors 

research. Existing frameworks distinguish stages and levels of automation, treat allocation as 

context-dependent, and analyze the coordination and monitoring demands that arise when control 

is divided between human and automated agents  (Dearden et al., 2000; Feigh & Pritchett, 2014; Li 

et al., 2013; Parasuraman et al., 2000; Pritchett et al., 2014a, 2014b) . Bainbridge's classic "ironies 

of automation" identified a durable problem: people may remain responsible for exceptional failures 

after automation has reduced the opportunities through which they develop and maintain the 

expertise needed to detect and recover from those failures (Bainbridge, 1983). 

Three strands of work converge on this problem. Human-factors research on function allocation 

addresses how tasks and control are distributed to support current performance, safety, workload, 

and situation awareness (Bainbridge, 1983; Dearden et al., 2000; Feigh & Pritchett, 2014; Li et al., 

2013; Parasuraman et al., 2000; Pritchett et al., 2014a, 2014b). Research on learning, agency, and 

deskilling increasingly foregrounds how assistance can shape the opportunities through which 

human capability is developed, exercised, or lost (Chang et al., 2023; Ferdman, 2026; Ganuthula & 

Singh, 2026; Pelechano et al., 2026; Tsim & Gutoreva, 2026). Research on meaningful human 

control asks whether people retain the knowledge, authority, and capacity required for responsible Page 5 

oversight (Cavalcante Siebert et al., 2023; Davidovic, 2023). Developmental function allocation 

connects these strands by treating the capability produced or retained through today's allocation as 

an outcome of the allocation itself. It asks not only how work should be distributed now, but how 

that distribution changes what people will later be able to understand, challenge, recover, adapt, 

and teach. 

Retained human capability is not a single score. It is a provisional profile across understanding, 

justification, monitoring and error detection, recovery skills, and adaptation or transfer. These 

dimensions need not move together. A person may understand an AI-supported result yet fail to 

reconstruct it. Capability should be assessed alongside the authority and practical opportunity to 

exercise it: detecting an error does not entail permission to override the system, and formal 

authority does not establish competence. Recent work on epistemic agency, epistemic co-agency, 

and the principle "scaffold, do not substitute" converges on the need to preserve active human 

reasoning (Favero et al., 2026; Samuel, 2026; J. Y. Wu et al., 2025). 

Khosravi et al. (2026) organize AI learning companions around pedagogical, adaptive, and 

responsible-design foundations and explicitly call for evaluation of what learners can understand, 

transfer, and do independently after support is withdrawn. They also address which cognitive work 

should remain with the learner. Anti-Prompt can operate within such a companion: it connects a 

prospectively specified act, the timing and content of assistance, and later evidence of capability. 

The contribution is this integrated design and evaluation focus, not the invention of withholding, 

learner agency, or post-support testing. A learning companion can sustain the orientation across 

sessions; a single well-written prompt cannot guarantee that the interaction follows it. 

The Agentivism preprint explains learning with AI through selective delegation, evaluation and 

reconstruction of AI contributions, and transfer under reduced support (Yan & Gašević, 2026). It 

also argues for preserving learner responsibility for framing and justification. Anti-Prompt is 

compatible with this account. We develop a specific design commitment: identify a consequential 

act prospectively, preserve a tractable opportunity to construct or justify it before disclosure, and 

test the resulting capability after support changes. The proposed comparison isolates the timing and 

content of assistance rather than treating learner agency or post-support assessment as newly 

introduced principles. 

Operationalizing this framework requires consequential acts to be identified prospectively from the 

curricular or professional objective, expert task analysis, known novice failure modes, and the 

transfer or recovery demands of the practice. Different studies may legitimately protect different 

acts within the same task. The target and its prerequisites must be specified before learner 

responses or outcomes are observed. Responsibility is then represented as a time-varying profile 

across framing, initiation, selection, execution, justification, monitoring, and authority to override and 

recover. A protected opportunity is not evidence of successful participation: the record should 

distinguish an invitation to reason, an attempt, and a justified contribution, while allowing alternative 

valid solution paths. The framework asks three questions: Which act is being developed? Who or 

what supplies its consequential reasoning? What can the person do when equivalent assistance is 

unavailable? Capability dimensions should be tested as a possible common factor or task-specific 

profile; authority and opportunity should be recorded separately. 

## 3. The Anti-Prompt paradigm 

The Anti-Prompt paradigm translates developmental function allocation into an interactional design 

orientation. The anti-prompt is itself a prompt: it asks AI to preserve the conditions for human 

construction before producing the target. We use the term "paradigm" deliberately. The claim is not Page 6 

that questioning, scaffolding, or backward reasoning are new. It is that the default order of human-

AI interaction should be reversed whenever product generation would preempt the practice 

through which the target capability develops. The design question shifts from "What should AI 

produce?" to "What human capability must remain?" Earlier AIED paradigm accounts distinguish 

AI-directed, AI-supported, and AI-empowered learning (Ouyang & Jiao, 2021). Anti-Prompt cuts 

across these roles by asking when AI should withhold a consequential step so that the learner can 

construct or justify it. Box 2 gives two representative requests. 

> Box 2. Two representative Anti-Prompts

Mathematical proof.  Help me prove that  √2 is irrational, but do not give me the proof or the decisive 

contradiction. Begin by asking me how to represent the assumption that  √2 is rational. Introduce the 

lowest-terms and parity ideas only if my responses show that I need them. After I finish, test what I can 

still do independently by asking why the same argument does not prove that  √4 is irrational and how it 

changes for  √3.

Experimental science.  I want to test whether a pendulum's period depends on the attached mass. Do 

not tell me the answer or design the experiment. Ask me to state a prediction, choose the variable to 

change and the conditions to hold constant, and propose a measurement plan. Then raise one confound 

at a time. After I propose a design, ask which assumptions belong to the ideal model, which features of 

the real apparatus could make mass appear to matter, what evidence would count against my 

prediction, and how I would estimate uncertainty. Finally, ask me to adapt the design to test a different 

variable without further hints.

Its psychological rationale concerns cognitive opportunities. Premature production can remove 

generation, retrieval, comparison among alternatives, commitment to a provisional model, and 

error-driven revision. Such delegation can function as cognitive offloading, shifting parts of the task 

from internal processing to an external aid (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Fluent AI output can also be 

mistaken for evidence of one's own understanding, weakening confidence-accuracy calibration and 

reducing the perceived need for further checking (Tankelevitch et al., 2024). Different forms of 

assistance can displace different operations: complete answers can remove generation and 

retrieval; highly directive hints can remove selection; authoritative rationales can remove 

justification; and automatic evaluation can remove monitoring. 

Learning research provides both mechanisms and limits. Self-explanation and constructive 

engagement can elicit inferences that passive inspection does not (Chi et al., 1989; Chi & Wylie, 

2014). Productive failure can support later consolidation when tasks are tractable and feedback is 

well timed (Kapur, 2014; Loibl & Rummel, 2014). Scaffolding and the assistance dilemma, however, 

show that withholding is not intrinsically beneficial (Koedinger & Aleven, 2007; van de Pol et al., 

2010; Wood et al., 1976). Novices may need explicit modeling before independent work, while 

support that benefits novices can become redundant for experts (Kalyuga et al., 2003; Renkl & 

Atkinson, 2003). Anti-Prompt therefore seeks the least substitutive assistance consistent with 

progress, access, and responsible performance, not the least assistance possible. Page 8 

The same concern extends beyond formal learning. Repeated exposure to biased AI can alter later 

independent judgments (Glickman & Sharot, 2025), AI-supported science can produce illusions of 

understanding (Messeri & Crockett, 2024), and human-AI combinations do not consistently 

outperform their strongest member (Vaccaro et al., 2024). Cognitive-forcing interventions can 

reduce overreliance, sometimes at the cost of convenience (Buçinca et al., 2021), while generative 

tools can increase individual creativity yet narrow collective diversity (Doshi & Hauser, 2024). These 

findings  reinforce the central dissociation : gains in one visible outcome can coexist with losses in 

another. 

Questioning alone is therefore insufficient. A model can ask leading questions that narrow the 

option space so tightly that the learner merely executes an AI-framed solution. Conversely, a 

delayed worked example, proof checker, simulator, or test generator may preserve capability when 

introduced after a meaningful human attempt. Cognitive apprenticeship, educational reverse 

prompting, and Socratic LLMs provide important precedents (Chang et al., 2023; Collins et al., 

1989; Ding et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). The distinctive Anti-Prompt claim is that such mechanisms 

should be organized around a protected consequential act and evaluated by what the person can 

still reconstruct, challenge, transfer, or recover after equivalent support is no longer available. 

Consider two learners who finish the same correct proof. One proposes and justifies a divisibility 

inference; the other receives the inference inside a leading question and writes it down. The 

products alone cannot distinguish these interactions. Nor does the record establish that the first 

learner retained more: who first states a step is not a complete measure of who understands it. The 

comparison must record what each learner was invited to do, what assistance supplied, and what 

each can later justify or adapt. Changing an answer into a question does not necessarily return the 

reasoning to the learner. 

## 4. When paradox makes revision necessary 

Paradox can serve as one form of productive challenge within the Anti-Prompt paradigm. The term 

operates at two levels. The generative paradox describes a dissociation between immediate joint 

performance and retained human capability. A pedagogical paradox is a counterintuitive result or 

boundary case that exposes the limits of a learner's current model. 

The Koch snowflake provides a transparent illustration. As the construction is iterated, its perimeter 

grows without bound while its enclosed area converges to a finite value. A learner who expects 

perimeter and area to move together must identify the failed assumption and construct a more 

precise account of why perimeter and area have different convergence behavior. The pedagogical 

value lies not in surprise itself, but in the conceptual revision required to explain it. Research on 

proof comprehension and GenAI-assisted proving similarly emphasizes structure, dependency, and 

critical validation rather than local correctness alone (Mejía-Ramos et al., 2012; Selden & Selden, 

2003; Stylianides et al., 2024; Yoon et al., 2024; Yoon et al., 2026; Zhuang, 2026). 

Generative AI can preempt this opportunity by presenting both the anomaly and its resolution 

before the learner has committed to an interpretable expectation. An Anti-Prompt sequence instead 

elicits a provisional model, introduces the smallest anomaly or boundary case that the model cannot 

explain, and asks the learner to diagnose the failure before supplying the decisive distinction. 

Contradictory information alone is insufficient: learners may discount, reinterpret, or 

compartmentalize it unless a coherent alternative becomes available (Potvin, 2023). 

The resulting checkpoint has four functions: commitment to an explicit expectation, exposure to a 

minimally sufficient anomaly, diagnosis of the failed assumption, and reconstruction tested against Page 9 

a neighboring case. The protected act is not the experience of surprise but the work of conceptual 

revision. An anomaly is productive only if it exposes a misconception or model boundary that 

matters to the practice being developed. Generic surprise is not enough. 

## 5. Stress tests across STEM practice 

The proof case is deliberately compact. In a proof of the irrationality of  √2, the protected act may be 

identifying the contradiction or justifying the parity step rather than reproducing the completed proof. 

The Box 2 prompt is novice-facing: it signposts part of the architecture while preserving execution 

and justification; an advanced version can withhold the lowest-terms and parity cues (Mejía-Ramos 

et al., 2012; Selden & Selden, 2003; Stylianides et al., 2024; Yoon et al., 2024; Yoon et al., 2026; 

Zhuang, 2026). In experimental science, the pendulum prompt protects prediction, variable control, 

and the distinction between an ideal model and a real apparatus. 

Software incident response provides a professional test. The visible product may be a successful 

patch, while the consequential act is causal diagnosis: identifying the failure mechanism, tracing 

dependencies, and selecting a safe recovery strategy. An AI-generated fix can restore service 

without preserving that capability. Stronger evidence of retained human capability is the ability to 

explain the causal chain, reject a superficially effective but unsafe intervention, and recover from a 

related failure under altered conditions. 

Lesson design provides a teacher-side test. GenAI can generate pedagogical architecture, not 

merely format materials. Studies show variation in whether teachers adopt, contextualize, reject, or 

revise model suggestions during AI-supported planning (Choi et al., 2026; Krushinskaia et al., 2026; 

Velander, 2026). Research on pedagogical reasoning, design capacity, and technology 

orchestration treats teaching as the adaptation and transformation of resources rather than their 

passive use (Beyer & Davis, 2012; Brown, 2009; Drijvers et al., 2010; Shulman, 1987). An Anti-

Prompt interaction protects consequential design decisions, such as the target idea, anticipated 

student thinking, representation sequence, and evidence of learning, before materials are 

generated. Here, too, polished output is insufficient evidence of ownership; stronger evidence lies in 

justified rejection, counterfactual adaptation, and enactment under changed conditions. 

## 6. The capability-stock hypothesis 

The capability-stock manifestation motivates a system-level hypothesis: repeated allocation 

decisions change not only present products but also the future distribution of expertise. We call this 

the capability-stock hypothesis . It is not yet a validated stock measure. It proposes that 

opportunities to practice consequential acts are inflows to future capability, while nonuse, attrition, 

and organizational exit are sources of depreciation. Local efficiency can therefore diverge from 

long-term resilience. 

Possible indicators include the number and distribution of independently capable practitioners, 

recovery performance after automation failure, training throughput, the breadth of unaided 

alternatives generated, and succession or apprenticeship rates. An observational analysis of AI 

adoption in science found higher individual output alongside smaller teams, fewer junior 

researchers, and a narrower collective research focus (Hao et al., 2026). These associations do not 

establish deskilling, but they are consistent with a possible bottleneck in the reproduction and 

distribution of expertise. The capability-stock hypothesis should therefore be tested longitudinally 

rather than inferred from output alone. Page 10 

This is also an equity problem. If consequential decisions remain largely the domain of established 

experts while novices are confined to inspecting finished AI products, automation may concentrate 

rather than democratize opportunities to develop expertise. A capability-preserving institution must 

consider access to formative practice, not merely access to AI. The objective is not to preserve 

manual performance for its own sake, but to maintain a sufficiently broad distribution of human 

capability for independent challenge, recovery, teaching, and innovation wherever those capacities 

remain consequential. 

## 7. What would count as a decisive test? 

A strong first comparison would contrast direct AI generation, a complete AI-generated solution 

followed by structured self-explanation, high-quality Socratic assistance, and Anti-Prompt allocation. 

Consistent with Khosravi et al. (2026), outcomes should distinguish immediate joint performance 

from delayed reconstruction, near and far transfer, detection of unfamiliar errors, confidence 

calibration, and recovery. The act-level question is which parts of construction, evaluation, or 

revision were supplied by the learner, AI, or other tools. Comparisons should distinguish those 

actual allocations rather than rely on labels such as "Socratic" or "Anti-Prompt." 

The Austin-Law Proof-Synthesis (ALPS) preprint offers a methodological contrast, not evidence 

about human learning. Xie et al. (2026) check both whether submitted equations entail a target law 

and whether they admit a structure with at least two distinct elements. In the separate proof 

channel, supporting software may supply short missing derivations before the completed proof is 

checked (Appendix B.2 and Figure A1). This distinction matters educationally: verifying a submitted 

step is different from supplying one. We therefore propose recording tool-generated reasoning 

alongside learner and AI contributions, even when that reasoning is absent from the conversational 

transcript. This is an educational inference from the benchmark design, not an ALPS learning result. 

A focused study could randomly assign learners with the specified prerequisites to attempt a target 

inference before disclosure or to receive it before explaining it. The task family, model, final 

explanatory material, correctness checker, and scheduled support budget would be matched; actual 

assistance and time used would be recorded. After a prespecified delay, independent assessors 

would score learners' responses to a prevalidated task variant completed without assistance that 

supplies the target reasoning. The primary analysis should follow the assigned conditions and 

include learners with incomplete products, rather than only successful submissions. Attrition should 

be reported explicitly. Product success, later capability, and instructional costs should be reported 

together. The test concerns what the allocation changes, not which condition produces more 

attractive dialogue. 

No single omnibus comparison can identify every active ingredient. Component studies can 

separately vary construction protection, visibility of responsibility, and the withdrawal or 

transformation of support; a further study can compare forward and backward hurdle sequencing. 

Primary outcomes and scoring rules should be set in advance, with scorers unaware of condition 

where feasible. Analyses should distinguish total intervention effects from pathways through 

engagement and preserved opportunities for generation, selection, justification, and monitoring. A 

statistical mediation result alone would not establish a causal mechanism. Responsibility profiles 

should be coded independently of the outcome assessments. 

The central hypothesis is conditional: for learners with the required prerequisites, preserving a 

tractable opportunity to construct or justify the target act will improve later performance on that act 

relative to matched early disclosure. The expected benefit should be smaller or absent when the act 

is already mastered or when indispensable prerequisites are missing (Kalyuga et al., 2003; Renkl & Page 11 

Atkinson, 2003). These are predictions to test, not observed Anti-Prompt effects. Predictive 

analyses of responsibility profiles should remain separate from estimates of intervention effects. A 

study should prespecify the smallest educationally meaningful advantage and report uncertainty; a 

nonsignificant difference alone does not establish equivalence. An estimate precise enough to 

exclude the predicted meaningful benefit would count against that prediction. Implementation-level 

superiority requires comparison with strong Socratic assistance (Ding et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), 

independent-first use (Yoon et al., 2026), or other capability-preserving approaches (Chan, 2026; 

Chen, 2025; Ramos-Benitez et al., 2026; Shintani, 2026; Vendrell & Johnston, 2026). Equivalent 

outcomes between interventions that preserve the same acts establish no superiority of either 

implementation. The paradigm must survive comparison, not merely attract attention. 

## 8. Assessment and governance 

A verified product is not a verified learner.  A proof certificate supports a formal claim under its 

checking assumptions; it does not by itself establish a person's understanding, the originality of the 

reasoning, or who supplied a decisive step. The ALPS example makes the limits of this inference 

concrete (Xie et al., 2026). Three questions therefore require different evidence: Is the product 

valid? Who or what supplied the consequential reasoning? What capability remains after support 

changes? Fresh task variants can reduce exact-answer rehearsal, but a new instance does not 

guarantee new reasoning. Educational variants need verified solutions, appropriate difficulty, and 

alignment with the protected act. Assessment should sample delayed reconstruction, unfamiliar 

errors, recovery from misleading suggestions, and adaptation to changed constraints. Removing 

equivalent reasoning assistance need not remove accessibility accommodations or ordinary tools 

that do not perform the target act. No single indicator is decisive: reconstruction can be rehearsed, 

confidence can be miscalibrated, and participants can shape interaction traces strategically. 

Governance should likewise distinguish product-oriented from development-oriented AI use. 

Policies should specify where direct generation is appropriate, where capability-preserving 

allocation is warranted, which accessibility or safety considerations override default constraints, and 

who retains responsibility when AI guidance fails. Interfaces could display current allocations and 

allow users to reframe or override them. Research records should capture the assistance needed to 

interpret the specified target act, not an unrestricted record of learner activity. Privacy, 

transparency, and human agency remain central constraints (Kasneci et al., 2023; OECD, 2026; 

UNESCO, 2024). 

The aim is not to preserve effort, manual performance, or existing occupational boundaries for their 

own sake. It is to preserve a sufficiently broad distribution of human capability for informed 

delegation, independent challenge, recovery, teaching, and innovation. This objective is compatible 

with hybrid intelligence and shared epistemic agency: consequential work can be distributed across 

people and tools without requiring every operation to remain human, provided that responsibility 

remains visible and recoverable (Borba et al., 2026; Damşa et al., 2010; Molenaar, 2022). Page 12 

## 9. Conclusion 

Generative AI creates a paradox that better output alone cannot resolve: the same assistance that 

improves present performance can displace the practice through which future human capability 

develops. Developmental function allocation makes retained human capability a design outcome 

alongside current joint performance. The Anti-Prompt paradigm translates that principle into a 

reversal of the default order: when immediate generation would substitute for a consequential 

human act, AI should first create the conditions for human construction. 

The most capable AI need not always act first. In some contexts, its most valuable contribution is a 

question, an obstacle, a counterexample, or a paradox that leaves the consequential act open to 

human construction. The joint product is one outcome of human-AI interaction. Retained human 

capability after assistance changes is a second outcome. In AI-enabled learning environments, and 

in the professional practices that such environments prepare learners to enter, both should be 

designed for, measured, and valued .
