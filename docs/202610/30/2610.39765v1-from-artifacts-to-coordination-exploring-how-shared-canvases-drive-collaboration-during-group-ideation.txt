# From Artifacts to Coordination: Exploring How Shared Canvases Drive Collaboration during Group Ideation 

SARAMSH GAUTAM, Louisiana State University, USA 

ANDREW M. WEBB, Louisiana State University, USA 

MAHMOOD JASIM, Louisiana State University, USA Recent years have seen a surge in tools that support collaborative ideation through shared canvases. While these canvases offer flexible workspaces, how collaborators leverage them to iteratively develop ideas by externalizing thoughts, organizing artifacts, and coordinating tasks remains underexplored. To address this gap, we conducted an exploratory study with seven triads planning a travel itinerary using an infinite-canvas technology probe. Drawing on participant interviews and video analysis of their canvas interactions, we observed that collaborators contributed in parallel and arranged artifacts freely without a predefined structure. This spatial organization allowed collaborators to coordinate implicitly, made less vocal participants’ contributions visible, helped distinguish private exploratory regions from shared working areas, and supported the emergence of distinct roles through repeated patterns of contribution. We discuss design implications for canvas-based ideation tools, including surfacing unnoticed contributions, supporting emergent roles without formalizing assignments, and keeping spatial cues legible for AI integration in collaborative canvases. 1 Introduction 

Collaborative ideation involves groups of people generating, sharing, and discussing ideas together. The effectiveness of the ideation process often depends on how ideas are represented [19, 22, 57]. Representations that make ideas visible allow groups to build on each other’s work, while persistent representations allow them to revisit contributions and compare ideas over time [3, 57]. To support these processes, groups rely on various digital tools, including video conferencing tools such as Zoom [75] and Microsoft Teams [46], messaging tools such as Slack [62] and Discord [13], and word processing tools such as Google Docs [23]. However, these tools are disconnected from one another, which scatters ideas across separate spaces and makes it difficult for groups to maintain a shared, persistent view of contributions [15]. This creates challenges in maintaining awareness and coordination—key components of collaborative ideation [15, 26]. For instance, Google Docs

In recent years, infinite canvases, such as Miro 1, have received attention for providing an integrated, free-form space where collaborators can express ideas, arrange and revise contributions, and work together on multiple ideas within the same shared space [21, 28, 44, 60]. Such canvases have the potential to address the disconnection of ideas by bringing contributions into one persistent space that all collaborators can access [24]. They also support the curation of heterogeneous content, letting collaborators collect, place, and annotate different media types — text, images, and video — within the same space [35, 72]. Prior work has explored how participants collaborate in co-located tabletop surfaces [58], large shared displays [37], and domain-specific professional tools [20]. However, collaboration in remote canvases differs from co-located tools in one key aspect relevant to ideation: participants are not co-present, so they cannot rely on gesture, gaze, and proximity to understand intent and coordinate actions [10, 39]. Hence, how remote collaborators use the open layout and persistent artifacts of infinite canvases to coordinate ideation activities, maintain awareness of others’ work, and develop shared work over time remains underexplored. In this work, we explore the following research questions: 

• RQ1: How do remote collaborators express and build on each other’s ideas in infinite-canvas workspaces? 

• RQ2: How does spatial flexibility influence coordination and interpretation during collaborative ideation? To investigate how remote collaborators engage in collaborative ideation using an infinite canvas, we conducted an exploratory study with seven triads. Each triad engaged in a remote travel-planning session using an infinite-canvas technology probe. The canvas allowed participants to collect, organize, and annotate multimedia artifacts, including text, images, sketches, audio, video, and shapes. We asked each triad to collaboratively plan a travel itinerary for a randomly assigned continent, requiring participants to gather and organize information into a shared plan by the end of the session. We investigated how collaborators expressed ideas, arranged artifacts, revisited prior contributions, and developed shared plans through sustained interaction with the canvas. We also conducted semi-structured group interviews to capture participants’ reflections on their collaboration process and their experience working on a shared canvas. We conducted video-based interaction analysis [33] of each collaborative planning session, com-plemented by thematic analysis of post-task semi-structured interviews. We found that spatial actions, such as placement, movement, and grouping of artifacts, served as an implicit commu-nication resource that allowed participants to signal intent and priority without explicit verbal exchange, and that gave less vocal collaborators a visible way to contribute. Over the course of collaboration, this spatial organization gave rise to distinct regions on the canvas, with central areas reserved for shared, finalized ideas and peripheral areas supporting more private, exploratory work. Our analysis also shows how roles emerged through participants’ organizational work on a remote infinite canvas, with responsibilities derived from their work instead of explicit negotiation, extending what is known about implicit coordination and emergent roles in other collaborative set-tings [27, 73]. Based on our findings, we discuss design implications for canvas-based collaborative ideation tools that better support coordination, awareness, and role emergence in remote settings. • An empirical account of how small groups of remote collaborators express ideas, organize artifacts, and develop shared plans through sustained interaction on a multimodal infinite canvas;

2 Related Work 

In this section, we situate our work within prior research on collaborative ideation, digital collabo-ration workspaces, and canvas environments. We review how ideation unfolds through iterative interaction with externalized artifacts, how existing collaboration tools support that process, and how spatial organization has been studied in co-located and domain-specific settings. 

2.1 Collaborative Ideation as Iterative and Externalized Work 

During collaborative ideation, participants iteratively develop, reinterpret, and extend each other’s contributions through interaction over time [19, 57]. Prior work has shown that ideas in collaborative settings often emerge gradually, with early contributions functioning as prompts, fragments, or starting points that are revisited and transformed as collaboration unfolds [3, 19, 57, 70]. From this perspective, collaborative ideation goes beyond generating novel suggestions towards navigating and sustaining ideation through revisiting and building on evolving contributions. Research on creative practice, visual thinking, and external cognition has emphasized the im-portance of external representations in supporting the creative ideation process [7, 31, 36, 63]. Sketches, notes, diagrams, and other intermediate artifacts allow people to express incomplete thoughts, compare alternatives, and offload emerging ideas into forms that can be revisited and revised [4, 7, 22, 36]. These representations enable idea development by making tentative thoughts available for reflection, reinterpretation, and further elaboration [31, 44, 53]. In collaborative set-tings, such externalizations also become shared referents that participants can point to, modify, and discuss together, helping groups construct shared understanding through ongoing interaction with evolving materials [3, 17, 68]. Collaborative ideation often involves incomplete ideas and ongoing changes which can benefit from environments that allow collaborators to put ideas down, revise them, and reorganize them without having to commit to a fixed structure [7, 28, 60]. Therefore, the design of ideation environ-ments matters for how ideas are recorded and how participants engage with each other through shared idea representations. This underscores the importance of examining how collaborative systems support the externalization, visibility, and continued development of ideas in practice. 

2.2 Workspaces in Collaboration and Ideation Support Tools 

Collaborative work is often distributed across systems that support communication, coordination, and shared production across time and space [25, 27, 52]. Messaging platforms such as Slack [62], Discord [13], and Microsoft Teams [46] help organize discussion chronologically supporting idea exchange and coordination, while shared documents such as Google Docs [23] and Microsoft Word [47] support co-authoring within a linear textual form. Such tools privilege sequential, text-centric exchange, in contrast to the flexible externalization, spatial comparison, and simultaneous engagement that ideation requires [21, 60]. Dedicated collaborative workspaces address this gap by giving teams a shared, persistent space in which to place, arrange, and revise material as a group [27, 28]. Sustaining parallel and evolving work on such a workspace requires mechanisms that let col-laborators track each other’s activity and the changing state of shared material [26, 27, 56, 66]. Prior work in computer-supported cooperative work and collaborative ideation has explored how

Also some works examine how contributions accumulate and persist on physical whiteboards [8] and large shared displays [37]. Recent work extends this to digital whiteboards examining collaboration dynamics in hybrid teams around a shared canvas [48, 49]. However, this body of work studies coordination where collaborators share physical proximity or hybrid setup [24, 48]. Fully remote collaborations lack physical proximity and cues such as gesture and gaze that supports coordination in co-located settings [10, 39]. This motivates closer attention to how workspace itself shapes expression, coordination, and the interpretation of evolving collaborative work. 

2.3 Canvas-based Workspaces and Spatial Organization 

Canvas workspaces, such as Miro 2 and Figjam [18], offer a more flexible environment by removing the boundaries of fixed surface that constrains collaborators to work within the edges of tabletops, displays, or pages [28, 44]. These environments allow collaborators to pan and zoom freely, and place contribution wherever they choose across an unbounded space. This flexibility suits collaborative ideation process, since it allows collaborators to externalize partial thoughts, explore alternatives, and revisit evolving material without committing to a structure early on [21, 44, 60]. Research on distributed cognition shows that thinking and coordination are distributed across people, tools, and representations [2, 31]. From this perspective, collaborative ideation unfolds as members of a group externalize and act on shared artifacts [36]. On a canvas, this externalization is inherently spatial because every artifact a collaborator places also carries a position relative to previously added contributions. While prior work has established the importance of shared artifacts, spatial organization, and workspace awareness, less is known about how these dynamics unfold in remote canvas-based environments during collaborative ideation, where multiple contributions can emerge, shift, and be reorganized across a shared space over time. 

3 Methodology 

We studied seven triads using an infinite-canvas technology probe to examine how participants expressed and discussed their ideas, organized curated artifacts, and coordinated ideation activities while collaboratively planning a travel itinerary. Each collaborative session concluded with a group interview to collect participants’ reflections on their experience. 

3.1 Infinite Canvas Technology Probe 

We built a web-based technology probe [32] to support multimodal, infinite-canvas collaboration. We chose to build a custom probe rather than using a commercial tool such as Miro to enable detailed interaction logging at a level that commercial platforms do not support, and to provide a simplified environment that would not overwhelm participants with features irrelevant to our research questions. The probe was built using TLDraw 3, an open-source infinite-canvas library. It supported free-form web curation, allowing multiple users to simultaneously collect and spatially organize artifacts across various media types, including text, images, sketches, audio, video, and shapes (Fig. 1, B1). Participants could freely place artifacts anywhere on the canvas, move and reposition them at any point, resize, rotate, and layer contents, and group related artifacts together. Participants could also annotate existing content, draw freehand, and rearrange artifacts without any fixed layout constraints. A usage log panel allowed participants to review a chronological

3.2 Participants 

We advertised our study through academic mailing lists, flyers, and posts on LinkedIn and Bluesky. We selected participants at random from a pool of interested individuals who met our inclusion criteria—an adult aged 18 to 65 and fluent in English. 3.3 Procedure and Task 

We conducted all study sessions remotely via Zoom, and each session followed a sequence of phases, illustrated in Fig. 2. The session began with a brief onboarding (M = 5 min, range = 2–9 min; Fig. 2A), during which the researcher introduced the canvas probe through a demo video and answered clarifying questions. We assigned a randomly selected continent to each group and instructed them to use the shared canvas to collaboratively plan a vacation itinerary, gathering and organizing ideas around destinations, activities, and logistics. Participants were first asked to work individually on the shared canvas to become familiar with it and add initial ideas (M = 20 min, range = 9–31 min; Fig. 2B). The facilitator then prompted each group to begin collaborative planning. This individual-first structure follows established best practice in collaborative ideation research, which suggests that individual generation before group work reduces fixation effects, minimizes production blocking, and supports more diverse contributions [12, 38, 50]. The collaborative planning phase concluded when the group considered their plan complete (M = 35 min, range = 26–44 min; Fig. 2C). During this phase, participants discussed, refined, and spatially organized their ideas into a shared plan.

3.4 Data Collection and Analysis 

We collected data from three sources: a pre-questionnaire that captured demographic information; 600 minutes of screen recordings along with mediators’ observation notes of group behavior and interaction flow; and post-task interviews that collected participants’ feedback and reflections. We employed a multi-method analysis strategy, combining video-based interaction analysis [33] and thematic analysis [5]. For the video analysis, two coders independently reviewed the screen recordings and developed time-stamped logs of participants’ canvas interactions using spreadsheets. The recordings of the post-task interviews were transcribed, and the same coders independently analyzed the transcripts using a separate spreadsheet application. As the unit of analysis [65], we used each individual action on the canvas for the video-based analysis and participants’ utterances for the interviews. The codes were consolidated and refined across eight sessions over two months. The researchers used six of these sessions to deliberate, resolve conflicts around the interpretation of data, and revise the codes, and two sessions with the full research team informed the development of the three themes. The result of these deliberative sessions was an iteratively developed codebook, which the first author used to conduct a final round of coding across the dataset. The researchers’ prior experience with collaborative ideation tools might have drawn attention to spatial organization. We considered this perspective when interpreting the canvas actions alongside participants’ discussions and interview accounts. We therefore present the themes as situated interpretations of these seven groups’ interactions (Table 1), recognizing that other researchers may interpret the same actions differently [6]. We did not calculate statistical inter-coder reliability because the codes were developed iteratively throughout the analysis. Instead, we used negotiated

4 Findings 

Our analysis of canvas interactions and post-task interview responses revealed three themes (Table 1) around how participants used artifact placement and arrangement as a coordination resource to signal intent and participate without verbal exchange, how these contribution patterns gave rise to a central and peripheral spatial logic on the canvas without explicit negotiation, and how these repeated patterns of contribution and artifact work made divisions of labor visible over time, from which collaborative roles emerged without explicit assignment. These themes reveal how spatial organization in an infinite canvas can shape participation, coordination, and role differentiation during remote collaborative ideation.

4.1 Artifact Organization Supports Implicit Coordination and Quiet Participation 

Participants spatially arranged multimedia artifacts to communicate relationships among ideas, signal priorities, and guide the next steps in the planning process without explicitly stating their intent. Across groups, participants added notes beside or overlapped with images to annotate them. They clustered and connected artifacts using arrows and links to indicate shared themes or order. Moreover, when they contributed new ideas, those artifacts were often added in line with the existing arrangements, continuing the groups’ organizational patterns, without much verbal discussion. For example, in Group C, participant C3 first added a written list of stops while C1 and C2 worked on different ideas. Then, C3 added a map of the continent and annotated it with labeled stops and day markers, which became a shared planning region on the canvas. Later, C1 and C2 began contributing to the map in the same manner, without deliberate discussion (Fig. 3). Reflecting on this during the interview, C2 explained, “Seeing how others had arranged content made it easier to know where to add mine,” suggesting that participants read the existing layout as a guide for where and how to contribute.

4.3 Roles Emerge Through Persistent Patterns of Contribution on the Canvas 

We observed that participants gradually took on different collaborative roles through repeated pat-terns of contribution rather than through explicit assignment. Some participants mostly organized artifacts, while some added content. In 5 of the 7 groups, one participant emerged as the primary canvas organizer, responsible for arranging, grouping, and maintaining the shared layout, while other members expanded on ideas by adding specific artifacts, such as images, web content, or list. Also, some participants contributed primarily through verbal discussions while making fewer direct edits on the canvas. These contribution behaviors remained visible over time on the canvas, and

For instance, in Group A, A1 arranged notes and images in aligned rows, A3 contributed only a few images, and A2 produced a composition with notes and images, connected artifacts with arrows, and incorporated videos. E3’s sustained organizing work led E3 to take charge of the plan, while E1 helped coordinate discussion and E2 remained mostly verbal. After a while, we found each other’s strength and assumed responsibility, then I think it went smoothly.” The same pattern appeared in Group F, where F1 arranged the group’s contributions into image-and-note pairs and added arrows to show chronological order, while F2 and F3 expanded on ideas by adding images and text (Fig. 9).

Across all seven groups, the participant who did the most organizing work also volunteered to present the final plan, including A2, B3, C3, D1, E3, F1, and G1. F3, who contributed by bringing in images of places, reflected, “F1 suggested to me what we should add next, and how to add them and I could just help F1.”

5 Discussion 

In this study, we examined how groups in remote settings used an infinite canvas to collaboratively ideate. Drawing on our findings, we discuss what these interaction patterns and experiences suggest about coordination, participation, and role emergence in canvas-based collaboration, and implications for the design of future tools. 

5.1 The Canvas as a Communication Surface: Spatial Action as an Alternative Participation Medium 

Our findings suggest that the infinite canvas provided less vocal participants with a pathway into the collaboration that did not depend on securing the conversational floor. Rather than waiting for a pause or explicitly asking to be heard, these participants placed artifacts near the group’s active planning region. As the session progressed, collaborators noticed, discussed, and incorporated many of these contributions into the shared plan, even when their authors remained relatively quiet. Although persistent shared materials can support contribution in other settings, the canvas’s open layout allowed participants to communicate tentative ideas by placing artifacts near ongoing work without fitting them into a fixed sequence. This allowed ideas to remain peripheral or provisional until others were ready to engage with them, creating a form of delayed participation that did not require interrupting the ongoing conversation. These observations extend prior research on workspace awareness and collaborative work showing that participation in shared environments does not depend on explicit verbal negotiation, but can instead emerge through actions made visible to other collaborators [15, 27, 40]. Kirshenbaum et al. [37] report a related pattern on shared large displays: placed content persists over time rather than disappearing as speech does, and proximity between related ideas signals their connection to other collaborators. The canvas in our study did not impose a fixed structure, so participants could express intent and priority simply through where and how they placed artifacts, without stating it aloud. This appears to be the mechanism behind quiet participation in our data: an artifact, once placed, remained visible to the rest of the group, and its position relative to the group’s active work conveyed relevance on its own, even absent the gaze, gesture, and proximity cues a co-located group would ordinarily rely on. Shared text documents leave far less room for this kind of participation, since contributions there are bound to a linear order and fixed formatting [29]. However, relying on visibility and proximity alone can also work against quiet participation. If more contributions accumulate on the canvas, or if a group includes several quiet participants at once, noticing and interpreting each placed contribution becomes harder for everyone else, and some contributions may go unseen. Future work should explore ways to support quiet participation without placing this burden entirely on other collaborators. For example, canvas tools might surface unnoticed contributions for group attention, or use subtle awareness cues to flag new placements near an active region [9, 41]. 

5.2 Designing for Implicit Coordination and Emergent Role Awareness 

Our findings show that the infinite canvas supported diverse contributions from group members, which gradually coalesced into roles, even though no roles were assigned at the outset. Participants made different contributions, where some arranged content or kept the layout coherent, while others extended existing structures, collected new material, or evaluated and annotated ideas. As these actions repeated over time, they made unspoken roles visible. Notably, the participants who spoke the most did not always lead the collaboration. Instead, influence came from sustained  

> Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact., Vol. 11, No. 1, Article GROUP19. Publication date: January 2027. GROUP19:16 Saramsh Gautam, Andrew M. Webb, and Mahmood Jasim

organizational work on the canvas. Those who structured and maintained the shared layout often shaped how the plan developed and how others contributed to it. These observations resonate with prior work in Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW) showing that coordination in shared environments can occur through visible actions, which go beyond explicit verbal negotiations [14, 27, 40]. Others suggest that UX professionals increasingly use infinite-canvas tools such as Miro 6 and Figjam [18] to develop and maintain shared awareness of each other’s contributions through visible artifacts and annotations [17]. Because the canvas itself does not impose a fixed structure, participants could express their artifact organization through actions. We argue that, during collaboration, as more visible actions accumulate over time, the organizational contributions and competence become visible, enabling the emergence of roles through repeated patterns of contribution. Similar role dynamics can occur in shared text documents, where organizational work follows a more linear structure [29, 41]. In our study, the open canvas made emerging responsibilities visible through participants’ repeated arrangement and maintenance of shared artifacts. However, the lack of organizational structure in infinite canvases invites contradiction to the emergence of such roles. For example, if participants’ contributions are spread across large areas of the canvas, this may lead to disorientation [11], as tracking contributions becomes more challenging and their visibility is affected. Further investigation is needed to develop alternative approaches for supporting emergent role patterns without turning them into rigid assignments. For instance, infinite-canvas tools might rotate structural stewardship, surface neglected or orphaned content for collective attention, or make organizational edits more legible through subtle awareness cues [9, 41]. 

5.3 Spatial Organization as a Design Resource for Future Support Tools 

Across our findings, participants used spatial organization as an active resource to coordinate, participate, and signal intent (Sec. 4.1). This observation points to a design direction where future tools could treat spatial layout as a signal. Prior systems, for instance, IdeaHound, demonstrate this feasibility by inferring relationships between ideas from how participants arrange them [61]. Extending this idea, such design implications become more significant as AI facilitating agents enter the collaborative ideation tools. This raises questions around ways in which AI agents could operate on canvases where meaning is carried by placement as well as the content. The recent surge of AI has already permeated the collaborative ideation tool design space, with recent works demonstrating how AI agents can support human effort in spatial and relational structuring to high-level idea curation [30, 54, 74], and researchers have begun exploring how AI might be integrated into collaborative ideation [42, 55, 59]. However, like many other areas that value human ingenuity, scholarship on the collaborative ideation space also promotes caution in integrating AI agents into collaborative workflows without careful consideration of human agency and control [43, 64, 67, 69]. For instance, Endsley [16] shows that adding AI teammates to human teams often reduces coordination and trust, particularly when AI actions are opaque to other collaborators. In our study, coordination also depended on participants being able to read each other’s spatial actions. AI agents that add artifacts or reorganize layout without making their reasoning visible risk disrupting this coordination mechanism, replacing legible human action with opaque automated change. This concern aligns with established guidelines for human-AI interaction [1], which emphasize that AI actions should be visible, understandable, and dismissible. Leveraging the spatial affordances of infinite canvases, one potential approach to designing AI-assisted collaborative ideation tools could be to explore the separation of spaces for humans and AI agents. This aligns with prior work by Webb et al. [71], who propose central workspaces, “hot spaces” , where human users

actively construct and refine ideas, while peripheral regions, “cool space” , are used by automated agents to gather, suggest, and organize information in the background. Our findings suggest that future AI-supported canvases should preserve the spatial cues through which collaborators interpreted one another’s contributions. For example, automated additions or layout changes could be clearly attributed to the system and left for participants to accept or revise, consistent with prior work on transparency and human control in human-AI interaction [1, 16]. 

6 Limitations and Future Work 

Our findings should be interpreted in light of following limitations. First, our study involved seven triads (N = 7 𝑥 3 = 21). Our findings are grounded at the group level, and having only seven groups might constrict the scope of interpretation and generalization. Second, all groups consisted of three participants, and it remains an open question whether the coordination mechanisms, spatial structures, and role patterns we observed would hold in larger groups. Third, our study focused on collaborative planning task in a remote setting and should not be interpreted as representative of workplace collaboration more broadly. Behaviors may differ in professional settings, domain-specific tasks, or longer-term collaborations. Fourth, participants had different levels of experience with collaborative tools, and the study did not require prior experience with canvas-based tools. Participants’ familiarity with canvases may have influenced how they organized the canvas and used its features. Our study does not establish how prior canvas experience shaped these behaviors. Finally, our study used a custom technology probe rather than a commercial tool such as Miro, which allowed for detailed interaction logging but means some behaviors may reflect the specific affordances of the probe. Future work could study open-canvas collaboration in larger groups, longer-term collaborations, and a wider range of task types such as brainstorming, sensemaking, or design work, where spatial organization may function differently. Another direction is to investigate how systems might better support the transition of ideas across central and peripheral regions, make quiet forms of participation more visible, or help collaborators recognize emergent roles as they develop. More broadly, future research could explore how the spatial and multimodal properties of open canvases can be designed to better support awareness, coordination, and inclusive participation in both remote and hybrid collaboration. 

7 Conclusion 

In this work, we examined how remote groups collaborate in a multimodal infinite-canvas, and how ideas were contributed, organized, and incorporated into shared work over time. In a study with seven triads, we observed that collaboration on the canvas unfolded through discussion and ongoing interaction with shared artifacts. Over time, spatial organization emerged without predefined constraints, with central shared regions and peripheral spaces for tentative ideas, taking on different functions as ideas were revisited, clustered, and negotiated. We also found that the placement and organization of artifacts supported implicit coordination, helping collaborators interpret each other’s actions and determine where and how to contribute. Repeated forms of artifact work further made division of labor visible, as participants gradually took on different roles through arranging, extending, and maintaining shared content. These findings show how collaboration on an infinite-canvas develops through spatial, material, and social interaction, and they point to opportunities for future systems that better support shared awareness, coordination, participation, and emergent roles in collaborative ideation.
