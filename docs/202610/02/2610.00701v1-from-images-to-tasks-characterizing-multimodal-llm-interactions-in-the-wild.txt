From Images to Tasks: Characterizing Multimodal LLM
Interactions in the Wild
Jinyi Ye1,2∗, Scott Counts2, Gaurav Verma2, Kate Lytvynets3, Weiwei Yang3
1University of Southern California, 2Microsoft, 3Microsoft Research
{jinyiy@usc.edu, counts@microsoft.com, gauravverma@microsoft.com}
Abstract
Multimodal large language models (LLMs) increasingly integrate vision
and text, yet how people use them in natural settings remains underex-
plored. We seek to answer the question: when users upload images, what
tasks are they trying to accomplish? Analyzing over 40,000 de-identified
image-upload conversations from Microsoft Copilot, we characterize real-
world multimodal use through a hierarchical framework of ten capabilities,
spanning perception, cognition, and generation. First, we characterize the
distribution and composition of these capabilities, finding that the major-
ity of image-upload tasks involve multiple capabilities. Second, we find
that multimodal use spans a broader and more diverse task space than
text-only interactions, with asymmetric coverage and task classes that rely
on cross-modal grounding. Third, mapping observed capability demand
onto 253 existing benchmarks reveals uneven alignment between bench-
mark coverage and real-world use: benchmarks concentrate on perception
and reasoning toward fixed answers, while common workflows involving
text, code, and data generation remain comparatively undertested. We
validate our taxonomy and findings on an independent ChatGPT dataset.
Our results provide a large-scale empirical characterization of what users
seek to accomplish with multimodal LLMs and highlight opportunities for
benchmark design grounded in observed user demand.

Introduction
Multimodal interactions, which integrate text inputs with images, documents, audio and
videos, offer a more natural and efficient way to engage with conversational AI systems
(Zhang et al., 2024; Sundar & Heck, 2022; Koh et al., 2023). In typical workflows, people
fluidly work across different media: data may be presented in visual plots or tables, websites
captured as screenshots, or information conveyed through photos and diagrams. Although
most generative AI chatbots now support multimodal inputs (OpenAI, 2023; Team et al.,
2023; Ray, 2025), large-scale analyses of user interactions remain focused on the text modality
only (e.g., public ChatGPT conversations and analyses (Zhao et al., 2024; Chatterji et al.,
2025), telemetry analyses of Copilot usage (Hoak et al., 2025), occupational task mapping
(Handa et al., 2025), and use-case taxonomies drawn from public discussions (Cheng et al.,
2025)). We still lack empirical insight into how often users invoke multimodal features in
their inputs and which tasks they perform with them in the wild.
In order to characterize multimodal interactions with large language models (LLMs) at
the task and workflow levels, we should first ask: What multimodal capabilities do users’
tasks require when they attach non-text inputs in conversations? Real tasks typically chain
capabilities across three layers. Perception extracts structured signals from non-text media
and aligns them with language Baechler et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2024e); Radford et al. (2021).
Cognition composes these signals with the model’s knowledge to reason over interleaved
inputs Fu et al. (2026); Yu et al. (2024a); Yue et al. (2024). Generation produces the outputs
users ultimately request, like text, image, structured documents, or code (AI et al., 2025).

A gap remains in mapping real-world, multi-turn use cases to the specific multimodal
capabilities and their combinations that these tasks exercise in practice.
Beyond establishing when and how people invoke multimodal features, we also need to
know why they choose them and what marginal value these inputs add over text alone. It
remains unclear how multimodal usage relates to text-only usage. Some tasks, such as
debugging code or translating short passages, can be represented equally well in text or as
screenshots; others are inherently multimodal because success depends on combining visual
and textual cues, as when a clinician interprets a medical image alongside notes (Corrado &
Barral, 2024) or a developer pastes a UI screenshot to generate its HTML/CSS code (Native,
2023). Whether and to what extent multimodal and text-only task spaces overlap or diverge
is therefore a question to be explored.
Finally, given the taxonomy and distribution of real-world multimodal use, a natural
follow-up question is: do existing multimodal benchmarks evaluate the capabilities (and
combinations) that users actually invoke? Recent benchmarks (e.g., Fu et al. (2026)) have
evaluated multimodal capabilities spanning perception, reasoning, instruction following,
interleaved interactions, and long-context understanding. Their coverage, however, has not
been examined against actual usage in the wild.
In this study, we bridge these gaps by investigating multimodal interactions with LLMs
through a large-scale analysis of image-upload conversations from two widely used AI
assistants (Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT). Our contributions are threefold. First, we derive
a hierarchical taxonomy (Figure 1) of multimodal capabilities from real-world interactions
and characterize their distributions and combinations. Second, we compare the semantic task
spaces of multimodal and text-only interactions to examine the extent to which multimodal
usage overlaps with or extends the text-only task space. Third, we use the taxonomy
to evaluate the coverage of existing multimodal benchmarks and identify gaps between
benchmark design and real-world usage. Our findings provide empirical basis for designing
multimodal benchmarks and conversational interfaces that better reflect how users interact
with multimodal LLMs.

Related Works
Understanding LLM use in the wild.
Recent work has begun to characterize how people
use large language models in natural settings through large-scale analyses of real-world
usage. Prior studies span opt-in public conversation corpora (Zhao et al., 2024), privacy-
preserving analyses of millions of on-platform conversations (Tamkin et al., 2024; Chatterji
et al., 2025), occupational and economic tasks performed with LLMs (Handa et al., 2025),
and use cases mined from public discussions and news reports (Cheng et al., 2025). Recent
work has extended such analyses to multimodal interactions through crowdsourced VLM
evaluation platforms, including WildVision and VisionArena (Lu et al., 2024b; Chou et al.,
2025). We complement these studies by analyzing naturalistic image-upload interactions

Evaluating multimodal capabilities.
Multimodal LLMs are commonly evaluated with
benchmarks targeting capabilities such as visual perception, OCR, spatial reasoning, knowl-
edge reasoning, and code generation (Fu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024d; Li et al., 2024a; Si et al.,
2025). Broad benchmark suites aggregate many such tasks into unified evaluations (Yue
et al., 2024; Ying et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025), while recent work extends evaluation to
integrated capabilities (Yu et al., 2024b), interleaved multimodal interactions (Xia et al.,
2025a), and long-context understanding (Wang et al., 2024a). Surveys document this rapidly
expanding benchmark landscape (Li et al., 2024b). However, benchmark coverage remains
largely top-down, reflecting the evolution of vision–language research rather than observed
user behavior. Even benchmarks and evaluation platforms motivated by real-world use,
including WildVision and VisionArena, are designed around curated task categories, model
comparison, or human preference (Lu et al., 2024b; Chou et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Bitton
et al., 2023). We complement this line of work by using large-scale interactions to charac-
terize which multimodal capabilities and capability combinations users actually invoke,
providing an empirical basis for future benchmark design.
3
Datasets
Primary dataset.
Our primary dataset consists of a two-month anonymous random sample
of Microsoft Copilot conversations collected between August and September 2024. The
sample excludes enterprise, educational, and commercial accounts.
Validation dataset.
To assess the reliability and generalizability of our taxonomy and
empirical findings, we repeat the same analyses on an independent ChatGPT dataset
spanning November 2024 and January 2025. Additional statistics on user intents, application
domains and validation results are provided in Appendix A.
Multimodal sessions.
A session is a complete user conversation with the assistant, consist-
ing of one or more turns. A multimodal session is any session in which the user uploads at
least one image. We focus exclusively on multimodal inputs, excluding multimodal outputs
such as text-to-image generation. This yields 42,617 multimodal sessions from Microsoft
Copilot and 23,413 from ChatGPT, forming the basis of our analysis.
Privacy considerations.
All data used in this study are de-identified prior to analysis. Raw
conversation transcripts are first processed by automated systems to remove personally
identifiable information, including names, contact details, and other sensitive attributes.
These sessions are further summarized into concise, privacy-preserving representations,
without reproducing original user text or requiring access to raw images. All subsequent
stages, including taxonomy generation, labeling, and hierarchical refinement, operate
exclusively on these summaries. All processing takes place within secure, access-controlled
environments with strict data retention policies. Internal privacy reviews are conducted
before and throughout the study. All findings are reported in aggregate form.

Developing a Taxonomy of Multimodal Capabilities
Our first research objective is to understand what multimodal capabilities users invoke when
they upload images to LLMs. Specifically, we ask: (i) What multimodal capabilities do real-
world image-upload tasks require? and (ii) How frequently are these capabilities and their
combinations invoked?
We conduct our analysis at the session level, treating each conversation as a user task
supported by one or more uploaded images. We first derive a hierarchical taxonomy
of multimodal capabilities from these interactions and then annotate each session with

We adopt the TnT-LLM framework (Wan et al., 2024) to derive a taxonomy of multimodal
capabilities and annotate our dataset at scale. TnT-LLM iteratively generates and refines a
taxonomy using LLM reasoning over unlabeled data. Building on this framework, we imple-
ment the following pipeline using gpt-4o-mini (full prompts are provided in Appendix B).
Summarization.
To standardize inputs while preserving privacy, we generate two sum-
maries for each session (each limited to 50 words): (i) a task summary describing the user’s
overall objective, and (ii) an image summary describing the uploaded image and the re-
quested operation. Because raw images are inaccessible, we adopt an eyes-off setting and
rely exclusively on these summaries throughout the subsequent analyses. Figure 2 shows
an example.
Taxonomy induction.
We randomly sample 10,000 sessions for taxonomy induction. Start-
ing from seed categories adapted from prior multimodal benchmarks (Yu et al., 2024a),
we iteratively refine capability names and descriptions over 50 batches of 200 sessions
until convergence. The resulting taxonomy contains ten multimodal capability categories
(Table 1), with concise descriptions and examples in the main paper and detailed definitions
in Appendix D.
Dataset annotation.
Using the induced taxonomy, gpt-4o-mini assigns each session a
primary capability and, when applicable, a secondary capability. These annotations enable
analyses of both individual capability frequencies and capability combinations across real-
world multimodal tasks.
Hierarchical refinement.
For each of the ten capability categories, we reapply the taxon-
omy induction procedure to derive finer-grained subcategories, yielding the hierarchical
taxonomy shown in Figure 1. The resulting capabilities naturally organize into three func-
tional layers—perception, cognition, and generation—following prior work (Fu et al., 2026; AI
et al., 2025). We further distinguish between supporting capabilities, which enable downstream
tasks, and terminal capabilities, which correspond to users’ intended outputs (Figure 1).
Validation.
We evaluate the pipeline along three dimensions: taxonomy robustness across
prompt configurations and datasets, agreement between LLM and human annotations
(N = 200), and an eyes-on audit verifying that privacy-preserving summaries preserve the
capability signal present in the original conversations (N = 200). Across all evaluations, the
taxonomy remains stable, LLM annotations closely match human judgments, and labeling
from summaries achieves comparable performance to labeling from raw conversations. Full
protocols and quantitative results are reported in Appendix C.

5.2
Task Space Analysis Between Multimodal and Text-Only Interactions
Our second research objective is to compare the semantic task spaces of multimodal and
text-only interactions. To ensure comparability, we sample text-only sessions of equal size
from both Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT. All sessions are summarized using the same
procedure described in Section 4. Because text-only sessions contain no uploaded images,
only task summaries are generated. We compute embeddings for all task summaries using
text-embedding-3-large, which form the basis for subsequent analyses. We evaluate the
relationship between the two task spaces using three complementary analyses: (i) cross-
modality perplexity, (ii) out-of-distribution (OOD) detection in embedding space, and (iii)
t-SNE visualization.

ity between models fine-tuned

Multimodal task spaces are broader.
From an
information-theoretic perspective, perplexity mea-
sures how well a model trained on one dataset pre-
dicts another’s distribution (Rosenfeld, 2002). We
fine-tune a Llama-3.1 8B model separately on the
task summaries from each modality (multimodal vs.
text-only), and then evaluate per-sample perplexity
on the other modality’s task summaries. Because all
summaries are short (<50 words) with low length
variance, sequence-length effects are controlled (Xia
et al., 2023). Lower perplexity implies that the train-
ing dataset better “covers” the task space of the
other (Zhao et al., 2024). Cross-dataset asymmetries
in perplexity thus indicate diversity differences: if
dataset A models dataset B well but not vice versa,
then B contains a broader or more heterogeneous
task space. As shown in Figure 4, models trained on
multimodal data achieve lower average perplexity
when evaluated on text-only summaries than vice
versa (65.55 vs. 76.61; Mann–Whitney U, p < 0.001).
We corroborate this geometrically using out-of-
distribution (OOD) detection in embedding space with KNN distance (Sun et al., 2022;
Zhou et al., 2021) and Isolation Forest (Liu et al., 2008; Bejan et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2025),
each trained on one modality and evaluated on the other. As Table 3 shows, ooth methods
exhibit a clear directional asymmetry: detectors trained on text-only embeddings classify
a substantially larger fraction of multimodal tasks as OOD (76% under KNN and up to
∼30% under Isolation Forest) than the reverse (43% under KNN and <1% under Isolation
Forest). The asymmetry persists after removing modality-specific vocabulary (e.g., “image”
and “chart”), indicating that the broader task space is not merely a lexical artifact. Details
of the OOD methods, hyperparameter settings, and robustness analyses are provided in
Appendix A.

The t-SNE projection (Figure 5)
corroborates both the perplexity and OOD analyses: although multimodal and text-only task
spaces overlap substantially, multimodal embeddings extend beyond the text-only region
into distinct clusters. A per-capability analysis (Appendix A) shows that Code Generation,
Document Processing, and OCR & Text Extraction exhibit the highest OOD ratios, indicating
that these capabilities contribute most to the expansion. The most frequent OOD capability
combinations (e.g., Recognition + Reasoning and Recognition + Code Generation; Appendix E,
Table 14) further suggest that the expansion arises less from visual input alone than from
how users compose visual grounding with downstream reasoning and generation. These
findings motivate examining whether existing multimodal benchmarks adequately cover
the capability combinations observed in real-world use.
A substantial share of tasks genuinely require visual input.
Perplexity and OOD char-
acterize differences between task distributions but do not establish whether individual tasks
actually depend on visual input. We therefore conduct a human audit of 200 multimodal
sessions, asking two annotators whether each task could reasonably be completed from
text alone. On average, 30% of sessions (60/200) are judged uniquely multimodal, providing
direct evidence that a substantial share of real-world tasks require visual grounding rather
than merely containing images. The annotation protocol is described in Appendix C.
5.3
Multimodal Evaluation Benchmark Analysis
Our third research objective asks whether existing multimodal benchmarks evaluate the
capabilities and capability combinations that users actually invoke in the wild. We map
the taxonomy from Section 4 onto a large corpus of published multimodal large language
model (MLLM) benchmarks and compare benchmark coverage with the real-world demand
characterized in Sections 4 and 5.1.
Benchmark corpus and capability labeling.
We draw candidate benchmarks from the
survey of Li et al. (2024b), whose companion repository1 provides a comprehensive catalog
of MLLM evaluation benchmarks. We retrieve 253 unique benchmark papers and use
GPT-5.4 to extract 1,173 evaluation tasks, as a single benchmark may contain multiple tasks.
Two LLM annotators (GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3.1-Pro) independently determine whether
each task involves static-image input and, if so, which of the ten capabilities it requires.

Implications for benchmark design.
Benchmarks are strongest when the target output is a
label, value, caption, or short explanation, but real multimodal use routinely continues past
these endpoints, to a document rewritten for an audience, a mockup turned into working
software, or a chart turned into a decision. Closing the gap calls for benchmarks built around
complete visual-to-artifact workflows, scored on the fidelity, executability, and decision
relevance of the produced artifact rather than on a single correct answer. Such task families
would complement, not replace, current perception and reasoning benchmarks.
6
Discussion and Conclusion
This study provides one of the first large-scale empirical characterizations of how people use
multimodal LLMs in the wild, focusing on sessions where users upload images. Drawing on
conversations from Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT, we introduce a hierarchical taxonomy
of ten multimodal capabilities spanning perception, cognition, and generation, and show
that image-upload tasks are predominantly compositional: most sessions couple perceptual
entry points with downstream reasoning and generation. We further find that multimodal
interactions occupy a broader task space than text-only ones, driven by cross-modal ground-
ing rather than the presence of images alone, and that current benchmarks concentrate on
perception and reasoning while the compositional generation workflows that dominate real
use remain undertested.
A gap between how multimodal LLMs are evaluated and used.
Existing benchmarks are
largely built top-down from expert taxonomies and evaluated under controlled settings,
concentrating on tasks where an image maps to a well-defined answer (Yu et al., 2024a; Hao
et al., 2025; Ramachandran et al., 2025). Real usage rarely stops there: Recognition, OCR &
10


Preprint.
Text Extraction, and Knowledge typically serve as entry points into multi-step workflows that
terminate in generation. This gap is uneven across capabilities. Combinations that read
an image and reason toward a fixed answer are well covered, whereas the writing-, code-,
and data-generation workflows common in real-world use, led by the single most frequent
combination Knowledge + Language & Text Generation, are evaluated only incidentally. Closing
this gap calls for benchmarks organized around complete visual-to-artifact workflows,
built from observed task chains (e.g., extract →interpret →generate) and grounded in
usage frequency rather than research convention, and scored on the fidelity, executability,
and decision relevance of the produced output rather than on a single correct answer. A
complementary direction is to study how upstream perception errors (e.g., OCR) propagate
to downstream reasoning and generation.
The cognitive character of multimodal use and its implications for interface design.
Two findings suggest that image uploads reshape the cognitive profile of a task rather than
simply re-expressing it in another modality. First, users employ images to reduce effort:
frequent uploads of structured textual content such as “code snippet,” “HTML/CSS,” and
“Excel/table” (Table 2) indicate that users share screenshots rather than retype formatted text,
offloading the burden of reproducing layout and context that is cumbersome to transcribe.
This is consistent with cognitive offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) and with prior work
identifying screenshots as lightweight tools for memory and communication (Cramer et al.,
2023). Second, the tasks themselves are more demanding: classifying sessions under
Anderson and Krathwohl’s revised taxonomy of cognitive processes (Wilson, 2016), we
find multimodal sessions skew toward higher-order activities (e.g., Apply, Analyze, Create)
relative to text-only sessions (χ2 test, p < 0.001; Appendix A). Image uploads are thus
associated with more complex, generative objectives, expanding the functional scope of
what users attempt. These patterns point to a shared interface implication: systems should
treat such uploads as first-class structured input, recovering the underlying code, table,
or layout rather than a flat image, and accept mixed image-and-text prompts, so that they
lower the friction of moving formatted content into a conversation and support the more
generative workflows that images tend to accompany.
Conclusion.
By grounding capability taxonomies and benchmark design in observed
user behavior, this work offers an empirical basis for evaluations and interfaces that reflect
real-world needs. Our findings suggest that the next generation of multimodal benchmarks
should be organized less around tasks that map an image to a fixed answer and more around
the compositional, generation-terminating workflows that characterize usage in the wild,
and that interfaces should be designed to support the offloading and generative objectives
these workflows entail.
Limitations.
Our study has several limitations. First, due to privacy constraints, the
analysis is conducted in an eyes-off setting: we rely on privacy-preserving task and image
summaries, which may omit fine-grained visual details. Our eyes-on validation indicates
that these summaries preserve the capability signal with limited loss. Second, our findings
may not fully generalize to future multimodal systems, as advances in model capabilities and
interfaces could change how people interact with LLMs or give rise to new task categories.
To mitigate this concern, we validate our taxonomy and findings on two independent
datasets spanning different AI assistants and collection periods, providing evidence that
the taxonomy is not specific to the Copilot sample; longitudinal studies will nonetheless be
important for tracking how usage evolves. Finally, privacy constraints prevent releasing
the original conversations, making the development of methods for sharing realistic yet
privacy-preserving datasets an important direction for future work.
Ethical Considerations
All data analyzed in this study were collected and processed in accordance with established
privacy, security, and ethical standards. The primary dataset consists of de-identified Copilot
conversations analyzed within secure, access-controlled research environments, and the
11


Preprint.
validation dataset was obtained from an independent provider under appropriate data
protection safeguards and informed user consent.
All conversations were de-identified prior to analysis, with automated processing removing
personally identifiable information such as names, contact details, and other sensitive at-
tributes. To further protect privacy, the analysis was conducted under an eyes-off setting:
conversations were transformed into concise task and image summaries without direct
access to raw images or reproduction of original user text, and taxonomy induction, annota-
tion, and all subsequent analyses operated exclusively on these summaries. We made no
attempt to re-identify users or infer sensitive personal attributes, the study did not involve
interaction with human participants, and all results are reported only in aggregate.

Out-of-distribution Detection.
While perplexity measures how well one task distribu-
tion predicts another, it does not capture the geometric relationship between task spaces.
We therefore perform out-of-distribution (OOD) detection on task-summary embeddings
generated by text-embedding-3-large. Because these embeddings are high-dimensional
and non-Gaussian, we adopt two widely used nonparametric detectors: k-nearest neigh-
bor (KNN) distance (Sun et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2021) and Isolation Forest (Liu et al.,
2008; Bejan et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2025). Each detector is trained on one modality (treated
as the in-distribution) and evaluated on the other. For Isolation Forest, we grid-search
nestimators ∈{50, 100, 200} and contamination rates {0.001, 0.005, 0.01}.
The complete OOD results are reported in Table 5. Consistent with the findings reported
in the main paper, both detectors exhibit strong directional asymmetry, indicating that
multimodal task representations cover a broader semantic space than text-only interactions.
Per-capability OOD Analysis.
To identify which capabilities contribute most to the expan-
sion of multimodal task space, we compute OOD ratios separately for each capability using
Isolation Forest (nestimators = 100, contamination= 0.005). OOD counts are aggregated over
both primary and secondary capability labels.

Robustness to Modality Vocabulary.
A potential concern is that the observed asymmetry
arises from modality-specific vocabulary rather than differences in task semantics. To
evaluate this possibility, we remove explicit modality tokens (e.g., “image,” “picture,”
“photo,” “screenshot,” “visual,” “chart,” “table,” and “diagram”) from multimodal task
summaries, recompute embeddings, and repeat the analyses.
The directional asymmetry persists across both detectors. Averaged over Isolation Forest
settings, Text→Multimodal OOD rates remain substantially higher than the reverse direction
(15.24% vs. 0.16%). KNN exhibits the same pattern (77.09% vs. 41.46%). These results
indicate that the broader multimodal task space is not merely a lexical artifact.
OOD Capability Combinations.
The expansion of multimodal task space is not explained
solely by structured visual inputs. We therefore examine the most frequent capability
combinations among OOD sessions. Representative combinations are reported in Table 14.
The dominant OOD workflows combine perceptual capabilities with downstream reasoning
or generation (e.g., Recognition + Reasoning, Recognition + Code Generation, and OCR & Text
Extraction + Language & Text Generation), suggesting that semantic expansion arises from
cross-modal grounding and capability composition rather than from image modality alone.

Task Complexity Analysis.
We measure task complexity using Anderson and Krathwohl’s
revised taxonomy of cognitive processes (Wilson, 2016), following the LLM-based classifier
of Suri et al. (2024). Each conversation is assigned one of six cognitive levels (Remember,
Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create) according to the effort required for a human
to complete the task without AI assistance. Following Suri et al. (2024), we further group
tasks into low complexity (Remember) and high complexity (all remaining levels). A chi-square
test reveals a significant difference between multimodal and text-only sessions (χ2(8) =
6087.13, p < .001). As shown in Figure 7, multimodal sessions contain substantially more
higher-order cognitive activities, particularly Apply (51.79% vs. 36.13%), Analyze (12.05% vs.
5.01%), and Create (13.38% vs. 10.01%), whereas text-only sessions more frequently involve
lower-order tasks such as Understand (29.17%) and Remember (14.70%). These results suggest
that multimodal interactions are associated with more complex user objectives.
A.2
ChatGPT Dataset Validation Results
To validate the reliability and generalizability of our taxonomy and findings, we conduct
parallel analyses on a second dataset collected from a different AI chatbot: ChatGPT. This
dataset spans two months, November 2024 and January 2025, and includes ∼732,000
conversation sessions from 7,676 unique users. Among these sessions, 23,413 contain at least
one image; the remaining sessions are text-only. Each session is annotated with a variety of
metadata fields, including intent labels, domain categories, and an image flag indicating
whether the session involved any visual content.
We note several caveats that limit the dataset’s comparability with the Copilot dataset: (i)
all interactions were conducted on desktop devices, (ii) users are located exclusively in
the United States, and (iii) the dataset does not distinguish between image uploads and
image generations, relying only on a binary flag indicating the presence of any image. Due
to these constraints, we treat this dataset as a validation dataset rather than a primary source
of analysis. We repeat our analyses on this dataset and report the results as follows.
Applying the Copilot-derived taxonomy to the ChatGPT validation dataset yields a capabil-
ity distribution with Image Editing & Generation, Language & Text Generation, and Recognition
as the three most frequent primary capabilities (Figure 9). Image Editing & Generation domi-
nates the distribution, partly because the ChatGPT dataset does not distinguish between
user-uploaded images and model-generated images. As a result, image creation activities
are aggregated into the same multimodal category, increasing its relative frequency. Only
62 sessions (0.26%) are labeled as “Undefined”. Overall, the distribution supports the
cross-platform robustness of the taxonomy.

Eyes-on Validation of the Summarization Pipeline
The main analysis relies on privacy-preserving eyes-off summaries rather than direct access
to images. We therefore evaluate whether the summarization step preserves the capability
information required for taxonomy induction.
We randomly sample 200 sessions and pair each conversation with its corresponding task
and image summaries. Since raw images are inaccessible for privacy reasons, the conversa-
tion serves as the closest available proxy to the original interaction.
Two human annotators and gpt-4o-mini independently assign capability labels using (1)
the original conversation and (2) the generated summaries. Table 10 reports the resulting
agreement.
Human agreement remains high between the two conditions (F1 = 0.73; hit rate = 0.91).

conversations (F1/hit rate = 0.80/0.96 versus 0.85/0.95).
These results indicate that the summarization step preserves the capability information
required for taxonomy induction and dataset annotation while enabling privacy-preserving
analysis.

Detailed Taxonomy Description
Table 12: Taxonomy of multimodal capabilities with detailed descriptions (up to 200 words
per category). This version complements the concise 50-word descriptions shown in Table 1
in the main text.
Id
Label
Description
1
Recognition
Visual recognition captures the model’s ability to identify, classify, and describe
objects, scenes, actions, and attributes within images. This includes tasks such
as object detection and counting, scene understanding, color and shape analysis,
and domain-specific recognition (e.g. species, brands, artistic styles).
2
Knowledge
Knowledge covers the model’s ability to retrieve, synthesize, and apply factual,
commonsense, and contextual information—including social, historical, scien-
tific, and time-sensitive knowledge—from both text and visual inputs to answer
questions, provide explanations, and solve complex, multi-step problems across
diverse domains.
3
OCR & Text Extrac-
tion
OCR & Text extraction captures the model’s proficiency in detecting, reading,
and interpreting text embedded within images and documents via Optical
Character Recognition (OCR). This includes extracting printed or handwritten
text and converting it into editable, machine-readable formats.
4
Spatial Awareness
Spatial awareness captures the model’s ability to interpret and reason about the
precise positioning, relationships, and interactions of objects and text regions
within images or diagrams. This includes understanding layouts, distances,
dimensions, and configurations (e.g. maps, flowcharts, geometric figures),
enabling tasks such as scene composition analysis, layout design, and spatial
context interpretation.
5
Language & Text
Generation
Language generation captures the model’s ability to produce coherent, en-
gaging, and contextually appropriate natural-language outputs according to
multimodal inputs. This includes crafting summaries, paraphrases, translations,
and creative content (stories, captions, marketing copy); generating structured
documents (emails, reports, code comments); and offering detailed explana-
tions—adapting tone, style, and depth to user needs and multimodal inputs.
6
Reasoning
Reasoning encompasses the model’s ability to apply structured, rule-based
logic and general world knowledge to draw inferences, analyze relationships,
perform cause-and-effect and mathematical reasoning, and solve complex,
multi-step problems by synthesizing insights from combined visual and textual
inputs.
7
Code Generation
Code generation captures the model’s capacity to create, modify, and optimize
programming code across languages based on both textual and visual prompts.
This includes generating scripts, functions, classes, and algorithms to meet user
specifications; debugging, refactoring, and correcting existing code; interpreting
code snippets or UI designs from images; and producing explanatory comments,
documentation, or sample implementations for web development, data analysis,
automation, and other software tasks.
8
Data Analysis
Data analysis captures the model’s ability to interpret and analyze numeri-
cal and textual data from diverse formats—including images, charts, graphs,
spreadsheets, and documents—by extracting and summarizing key metrics,
performing statistical and arithmetic calculations, identifying trends and re-
lationships, and producing structured outputs (tables, summaries, reports) to
generate actionable insights.
9
Image Editing &
Generation
Image editing & generation captures the model’s ability to both transform ex-
isting images and synthesize new visuals based on user inputs. This includes
editing operations—cropping, background removal, color, brightness and con-
trast adjustments, sharpening, filter and effect application, and object insertion
or removal—as well as creative generation tasks like style transfer, upscaling,
inpainting, and producing entirely new scenes, illustrations, or designs tailored
to specified themes or prompts.
10
Document Process-
ing
Document processing captures the model’s ability to interpret, extract, and
manage structured and unstructured information from documents—including
forms, reports, contracts, and PDFs—by leveraging layout understanding,
visual-text alignment, and natural language capabilities. Tasks include form
parsing, table extraction, document classification, and multi-field entity extrac-
tion across varied domains.
