# Abstract 

This research describes the adaptation of Large Language Models (LLMs) for economic monitoring in the public sector to automatically determine whether an article discusses Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU) and to identify its specific type. Previous studies either rely on keywords, which often result in a high count of false positives, or use Machine Learning (ML) approaches that require a large number of quality human labeled data that is costly and time consuming to acquire. In this study, we propose approaches based on weak supervision techniques, using generative LLMs to create synthetic labels through prompting, making the approach both cost-effective and scalable. Additionally, we propose methods for for multi-label and hierarchical classification of articles related to EPU. 

# 1 Introduction 

The growing availability of digital text, from news media to online platforms and institutional records, has created new opportunities for studying economic and political dynamics at scale. Computational analysis of such unstructured text has become an important tool in disciplines including economics, sociology, and political science Gentzkow et al. [2019], Grimmer and Stewart [2013]. In economics, textual indicators have been derived from central bank communications Rybinski et al. [2019], corporate earnings calls Keith and Stent [2019], news articles Baker et al. [2016] and social media data Indaco [2020]. A well known application is the EPU index by Baker et al. (2016), which measures the frequency of policy- and uncertainty-related terms in news articles Baker et al. [2016]. While influential, this keyword-based method is prone to false positives and fails to capture policy context with precision. To overcome these limitations, a range of ML techniques have been explored. Unsupervised ap-proaches such as topic modeling and embedding-based expansions provide improved coverage of relevant content Azqueta-Gavaldón [2017], Azqueta-Gavaldon et al. [2020], Azqueta-Gavaldón et al. [2023], Kaveh-Yazdy and Zarifzadeh [2021], Nyman and Ormerod [2020], Wang et al. [2024], Miranda-Belmonte et al. [2023], Jiang et al. [2023], Xu et al. [2023], but they remain less accurate than supervised methods. Traditional supervised ML and more recent deep learning techniques Loli´ c et al. [2022], Tobback et al. [2018], Sadorsky [2022], Wang et al. [2022], Rao et al. [2024], Chen et al. [2024], Qureshi et al. [2021], Keith et al. [2020], CHANG et al. [2024], Audrino et al. [2024], Yeh et al. [2024], Ito et al. [2025] have improved predictive performance but rely on costly human-labeled datasets. This dependence on manual annotation constrains scalability and limits adaptation to new time periods, countries, or policy domains. The most closely related work is by Paul et al. (2023), which uses labeling functions to create synthetic labels for training LLMs. However, these generated labels are often unstable, and designing high-quality labeling functions requires substantial domain expertise Trust et al. [2023]. Furthermore, most prior research has focused only on binary classification, identifying whether an article discusses EPU without distinguishing between specific types of policy uncertainty. 

Preprint. Under review. arXiv:2609.35856v1 [cs.LG] 25 Sep 2026 This research proposes a weakly supervised framework for EPU classification that addresses these challenges. LLMs are prompted to generate synthetic labels, which are then refined using aggrega-tion methods such as Data Programming (DP), Dawid–Skene, Worker Agreement with Aggregate (WAWA), and Majority Vote. The resulting labels are used to fine-tune LLMs: BERT, RoBERTa, and Long Short Term Memory (LSTM), thereby reducing dependence on human annotation. Beyond binary classification, we extend the task to multi-label and hierarchical settings, enabling the assign-ment of articles to specific policy subcategories once identified as EPU-related. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 presents the methodology, Section 4 reports the experimental findings, and Section 5 presents the conclusion of this work.

The reliance of traditional ML on manual feature engineering and limited capacity to capture complex language patterns has motivated a shift towards deep learning models and LLMs for more advanced text understanding.

Audrino et al. (2024) adapted various LLMs, including GPT-4, LLAMA, and Zephyr-7B for EPU classification Audrino et al. [2024]. Yeh et al. (2024) applied LLMs such as GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4, and Claude3 to generate keywords for EPU classification Yeh et al. [2024]. Ito et al. Although these methods also used LLMs like the approaches proposed in this research, their reliance on costly human-curated labels limits their applicability. 

Research Gap : Numerous previous studies discussed focused on unsupervised ML approaches, primarily using topic models and keyword matching. While these methods are simple and do not require training, they are less effective than supervised ML techniques. In contrast, supervised ML methods, including traditional ML and deep learning, rely on human-curated datasets, making them less scalable. Constructing a new index for a different year, country, or region requires training new models and generating new labeled datasets, which is resource-intensive. Furthermore, prior research has been limited to binary classification, identifying only whether an article contains EPU or not, with no existing studies attempting to classify specific types of EPU within an article. In this research, we present our proposal to use LLMs for classifying news articles containing EPU. Instead of relying on human-curated datasets, which are costly to obtain, it proposes a weakly supervised learning approach. LLMs are leveraged to generate training labels or directly classify news articles through prompting. The labels obtained via prompting can either serve as the final classification labels or be refined using ensemble aggregation techniques with multiple LLMs to reduce label noise. Furthermore, this research proposes extending beyond binary classification by adapting LLMs for multi-label and hierarchical classification of EPU-related news articles, an aspect not previously explored in existing research. 

# 3 Methodology 

This section describes the methodology used for analyzing and classifying news articles related to EPU. The process begins with EPU classification, where weakly supervised labels generated by LLMs are compared with human annotated labels. Next, multi-label text classification is applied to identify different types of EPU present in the articles, with labels assigned only to articles classified as EPU-related. Since this classification follows a hierarchical structure, additional experiments are conducted to assess whether incorporating this hierarchy enhances classification performance. 33.1 Weakly supervised EPU classification 

For the weakly supervised approach used for EPU classification, we consider a dataset DU of n

unlabeled data points: DU = {xi}ni=1 . The goal of text classification is to assign each data point xi

to a label yi where each label yi belongs to one of m possible classes: C = {c1, . . . , c m}, resulting in a labeled dataset: D = {(xi, y i)}ni=1 . Unlike traditional supervised learning, which relies on large volumes of human labeled data, weak supervision relies on synthetic labels generated by LLMs to train the classifiers. However, a human labeled dataset is used during evaluation to accurately assess the model’s performance. We formulate the EPU classification as a binary classification task with two target classes: C =

{“No EPU" , “EPU" }. The proposed approach comprises three main stages. In the initial stage, LLMs are used to generate labels via prompting, guided by instructions embedded in the prompt illustrated in Figure 1. This prompt design is inspired by the annotation guidelines developed by Baker et al. (2016) for human annotators Baker et al. [2016]. While the work by Paul et al. (2023) Trust et al. [2023], which this research builds on employed multiple labeling functions such as keyword lookups and semantic similarity, the results presented here are based solely on LLM-based prompting. A single prompt (Figure 1) is used to encode the labeling logic, serving as a soft rule-based alternative that simplifies the process and leverages recent advances in LLM capabilities. As a result, a label matrix is automatically constructed, where each news article receives multiple weak labels from k LLMs represented as: λ = {λ1, . . . , λ k}. For a given LLM λj , the output is 

λj (xi) ∈ { 0, 1}, where 1 corresponds to an “EPU" label and 0 corresponds to a “No EPU" label. Since different LLMs may assign different labels to the same input xi, the second stage focuses on aggregating the k generated synthetic labels to produce a less noisy single label. In this stage, various aggregation methods including: Data Programming (DP), Majority Vote, Worker Agreement with Aggregate (WAWA), and the Dawid-Skene model, are used to consolidate multiple noisy labels generated by different LLMs into a single label. • Majority Vote : Majority Vote Penrose [1946] is a simple yet powerful model often used in ensemble learning techniques. [2016] is an aggregation method that assigns a noisy label to each news article in an unsupervised learning manner. The outputs generated by LLMs through 5prompting can be viewed as multiple annotators labeling the same article, similar to a crowd-sourcing setup Mishra et al. [2021]. These labels may contain conflicts and correlations, even when assigned by domain experts. Instead of a simple Majority Vote, DP employs a probabilistic framework to capture the structure and relationships within the label matrix. The probabilistic model Pθ (Λ , Y )

is defined as the joint probability of the LLMs’ outputs (label matrix) Λ and the latent (unobserved) true class labels Y . The label matrix is encoded using a factor model with three factor types representing conflicts, correlations, and propensity (instances where LLMs generate valid labels). It begins by determining the Majority Vote label for each task. Then, it assesses each worker’s skill by measuring the proportion of their responses that align with the Majority Vote. Finally, it computes a weighted Majority Vote, where the weights are derived from these skill estimates. The algorithm is based on the premise that responses agreeing with the Majority Vote are more likely to be accurate, and that a worker’s reliability can be inferred from the consistency of their answers with the majority. • Dawid-Skene : The Dawid-Skene aggregation model Dawid and Skene [1979] is a prob-abilistic method used to estimate the expertise level of workers (in this case LLMs) in a crowd-sourcing environment. This model evaluates the reliability of each worker by con-structing a confusion matrix that captures the probability of correctly or incorrectly labeling. The confusion matrix provides insight into how consistently a worker assigns labels in a classification problem. In addition to individual worker reliability, Dawid-Skene incorporates a prior class prob-ability vector, which represents the likelihood of each class occurring before considering the responses provided by workers. Since the true label of a task is unknown, it is treated as a latent variable that the model seeks to estimate. The probability of a task belonging to a specific class is influenced by the prior probabilities and the observed responses from workers. The optimization of these parameters, including the prior class probabilities, worker confusion matrices, and latent variables representing true labels, is achieved using the expectation maximization algorithm. 

3.1.2 Discriminative Model 

The aggregated labels were used to fine-tune pre-trained LLMs: BERT and RoBERTa, and to train an LSTM model. For pre-trained LLMs, this was achieved by adding a feedforward neural network and a classification head to the last layers while keeping the other pre-trained layers frozen to facilitate adaptation to our downstream EPU classification task. For an LSTM, the news article text was first tokenized and converted into sequences of integers. These sequences were then mapped into dense vector representations using an embedding layer, which captures semantic relationships between words. Initially, these embeddings are randomly initialized with a uniform distribution and are progressively updated during training. The transformed embeddings are then fed into the LSTM layers, which learns to identify sequential dependencies and contextual relationships within the text. For both LLMs and an LSTM, the final layer consists of a dense network with a softmax function, which assigns the text to the appropriate EPU category. 3.1.4 Hierarchical Classification for EPU Articles

3.2 Data 

We used a dataset of 12 , 000 news articles provided by Baker et al. (2016), selected from USA news articles that included the terms “economy” and “uncertainty”. Domain experts assigned binary labels to these articles based on whether they discussed EPU. Among these, 4939 were identified as related to EPU. These articles were then classified into one or more multi-label subcategories, which explains why the total count in the class distribution shown in Table 2 is 6037 instead of 4939 , as some articles fall into multiple subcategories. The subcategories include: government spending (entitlement programs, social safety nets, welfare programs), trade policy (tariffs, quotas, trade rules), financial regulation (banking and equity markets), energy and environmental regulation (natural resources, commodities), sovereign debt (exchange rate policy, foreign reserves), political conflict and leadership changes (elections, governance crises), and legal policy (judicial decisions, regulatory changes). Further details about the dataset can be obtained from the authors’ website 1 Baker et al. [2016].

For synthetic label generation, we primarily utilized generative LLMs, specifically open-source models from the LLAMA, Mistral, and Qwen families, using the prompt shown in Figure 1. The selected models represented the SoTA at the time of the experiments, that we could perfom inference on with the hardware that was available to us. The specific versions used in the experiments include: Mistral-24B, Qwen2-7B, LLAMA3-8B, Mistral3-7B, and Qwen2-14B. Binary classification performance was evaluated using accuracy, F1 , Geometric Mean (GM), Area Under the Curve (AUC), and Area Under the Precision Recall Curve (AUPRC). Accuracy represents the overall proportion of correctly classified instances. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. GM accounts for both sensitivity and specificity, suitable under class imbalance setting. AUC measures the model’s ability to distinguish between classes across different threshold settings, while AUPRC assesses precision-recall trade-offs, making it particularly relevant for imbalanced data. Multi-label classification performance was further assessed using Exact Match Ratio (EMR) and Label Ranking Precision (LRP) Zhang and Zhou [2013]. EMR calculates the percentage of instances where the predicted set of labels exactly matches the true set. LRP evaluates how well the model ranks correct labels higher than incorrect ones, focusing on the order of label relevance rather than requiring an exact match. In addition to classification metrics, we used Cohen’s Kappa (CK) and Matthews Correlation Coeffi-cient (MCC) to measure the correlation and agreement of different label generators with humans. CK Hsu and Field [2003] evaluates the level of agreement between the predicted and actual labels while accounting for the possibility of random agreement. MCC Chicco and Jurman [2023], on the other hand, provides a balanced measure of correlation between predictions and ground truth, even when class distributions are uneven by considering all elements of the confusion matrix. The code, datasets, and hyperparameters referenced in this research are available in the GitHub repository: https://github.com/TrustPaul/ Weak-supervision-in-Economic-Policy-Uncertanity .

Beyond correlation and agreement metrics, classification performance is of greater interest for this task. DP achieved the highest performance results, with a GM of 70 .77% and an F1 score of 70 .78% , followed by Mistral-24B, which obtained a GM of 66 .61% and an F1 score of 66 .80% . The lowest performance was observed with Mistral3-7B and the Majority Vote method. Based on these results, labels generated by DP were selected as synthetic labels for training other models. The findings indicate that ensembling improves performance, but the choice of aggregation method significantly impacts the outcome. The zero-shot performance of LLMs like Mistral-24B suggests that sufficiently large and high-performing models can achieve reasonable classification results through prompting alone. However, even in cases where zero-shot prompting performs well, achieving such results requires models with at least 7 billion parameters, which come with significant inference costs. 10 Therefore, it remains beneficial to use labels generated by these LLMs or aggregation methods in a distillation process to train smaller models, such as BERT, which offer lower training and inference costs.

Hierarchical EPU Classification : Since the task was hierarchical, where articles classified as EPU could be further assigned to 16 multi-label categories, we investigated multiple strategies for hierarchical text classification. Details on these methods are provided in Section 3.1.4 of the methodology. The strategies include a basic approach, where the model predicts both binary and multi-label classifications simultaneously, a masking approach, where multi-label predictions are made only for articles classified as EPU, a weighted approach, which applies an additional loss penalty for misclassifying articles as not containing EPU, and a two-stage approach, where two models are trained separately, one for binary classification and another for multi-label classification.
