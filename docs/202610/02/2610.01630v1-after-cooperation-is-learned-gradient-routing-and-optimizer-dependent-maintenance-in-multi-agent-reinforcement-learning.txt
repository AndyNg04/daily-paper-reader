# After Cooperation Is Learned: Gradient Routing and Optimizer-Dependent Maintenance in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 

Chaoyuan Hao, Wentao Yue, Tianyou Lai, Hongji Li, Jiayi Zhou, Qingyu Mao, Qilei Li 

Abstract 

Cooperative MARL is commonly evaluated through coop-eration discovery from random initialization, leaving open whether continued optimization can destabilize learned co-operation. Actor–critic comparisons can also conflate critic presence with value gradients entering shared actor represen-tations. We study cooperation maintenance , defined as the survival of a behaviorally verified cooperative policy under continued training. We formulate maintenance as a right-censored event-time problem and compare matched warm starts: X0 allows value loss gradients to update shared actor features, X1 retains the critic while blocking those gradients, and X5 removes the learned critic as a critic-free reference. This isolates direct value-gradient access while controlling initialization, critic computation, and evaluation. Positive re-ward scaling preserves strategic preferences and equilibria while perturbing learning dynamics. Gradient audits confirm the intended routing pathways, and frozen-policy torso per-turbations probe whether route-induced updates align with local cooperation boundaries. In confirmatory MinEx and CleanUp-lite experiments, higher scales selectively increase maintenance sensitivity in X0; X1 remains near the censoring ceiling, and X5 has no confirmed events in the tested set-tings. In CleanUp-lite, route-by-scale displacement is asso-ciated with reduced local cooperation margins; MinEx shows a weaker, optimizer-dependent effect. These results identify a conditional, scale-sensitive maintenance risk associated with direct value-gradient routing rather than a universal failure of critics. 

Introduction 

Most cooperation research in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) asks whether agents can discover a co-operative policy. Sequential social dilemmas and broad eval-uation suites have made this question increasingly precise (Leibo et al. 2017, 2021; Guo et al. 2026). Deployment, however, raises a different question: after cooperation has been learned, can continued optimization erase it? End-point performance from random initialization cannot isolate this maintenance problem because failure may instead reflect insufficient exploration, failed coordination, or equilibrium selection. We focus on one mechanism that can produce such post-discovery failure: interference through shared actor–critic representations . Although such sharing allows policy and value learning to exploit common features, it also gives value optimization a direct route into the representation that deter-mines behavior. The critic may improve credit assignment, yet its gradients can simultaneously move the actor away from cooperation. Critic presence and value-gradient access are therefore distinct factors. Because representation sharing and objective separation are active design choices in mod-ern policy-gradient methods (Cobbe et al. 2021; Raileanu and Fergus 2021; Andrychowicz et al. 2021), comparisons between unrelated actor–critic algorithms cannot isolate this route from differences in architecture, initialization, or opti-mization. To address both the evaluation and identification gaps, we define cooperation maintenance as the survival of a behav-iorally verified cooperative policy under continued learning and evaluate it from a common cooperative checkpoint. All runs begin from byte-identical actors and follow one of three matched training arms: X0 allows the value loss to update features shared with the actor; X1 retains the same critic and its forward computation as X0 but blocks the value gradient at the shared features; and X5 removes the learned critic. With the arms defined, the X0–X1 contrast isolates direct value-gradient access while controlling the initial actor, critic computation, and evaluation, whereas X5 serves as a critic-free reference for failures that persist without value learning. We then apply positive reward scaling as a game-preserving intervention : it leaves strategic preferences, best responses, equilibria, and linear incentive constraints unchanged while perturbing value targets and optimizer dynamics. Frozen evaluations along each learning trajectory record first fail-ure, sustained failure, and recovery. Treating these outcomes as right-censored event times distinguishes transient devia-tions from persistent collapse and includes runs that remain cooperative through the evaluation horizon. Across two structurally distinct environments, the experi-ments reveal a conditional, scale-sensitive maintenance risk concentrated in X0 rather than a universal failure of critics. In the confirmatory cells, X0 becomes more fragile as reward scale increases, while X1 remains substantially more robust and the critic-free X5 reference has no confirmed events. Direct gradient audits verify that the intended route is ac-tive only in X0, and local behavioral-margin tests show clear adverse alignment in CleanUp-lite but weaker, contrast-dependent evidence in MinEx. Complementary optimizer settings yield either all-arm survival or failure even with-arXiv:2610.01630v1 [cs.MA] 1 Oct 2026 out a critic, showing that the observed routing-specific risk depends on both optimizer and environment. Our contributions are: 

• We formulate cooperation maintenance as a right-censored temporal problem, separating post-discovery behavioral survival from exploration, coordination, and equilibrium-selection failures. 

• We introduce a matched X0/X1/X5 warm-start design that isolates direct value-gradient access to actor repre-sentations, and combine it with game-preserving reward scaling, exact fixed-batch routing results, and production-path gradient audits. 

• We provide fresh-seed confirmatory evidence across MinEx and CleanUp-lite, together with local behavioral-margin and optimizer-boundary diagnostics that separate outcome association from behavioral mech-anism and delimit the claim. 

Related Work 

Cooperation and social dilemmas in MARL. Sequential social dilemmas formalize cooperation as a temporally ex-tended policy rather than a one-shot action (Leibo et al. 2017). Recent suites broaden evaluation across substrates, unseen partners, and efficiently implemented social dilem-mas (Leibo et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2026). Cooperative MARL has also expanded the mechanisms used to obtain strong joint behavior, including reward-assisted learning and se-quence models for coordinated action (Wang et al. 2022; Wen et al. 2022). These lines of work sharpen the ques-tion of whether cooperation can be discovered, transferred, or generalized. Our estimand starts after acquisition: it mea-sures how long a specific, verified checkpoint remains within an operational cooperation set while optimization continues. Conditioning on prior success removes initial exploration and equilibrium selection from the primary comparison, al-lowing post-discovery behavioral loss to be studied directly. This focus is related to, but different from, recent work on plasticity loss. Deep-RL training can produce primacy bias, dormant representations, reduced capacity, and a declining ability to incorporate experience (Lyle, Rowland, and Dab-ney 2022; Nikishin et al. 2022; Sokar et al. 2023; Abbas et al. 2023; Dohare et al. 2024). Resets and plasticity injection can restore learning (D’Oro et al. 2022; Nikishin et al. 2023). We fix the task and strategic ordering, then test whether updates erase successful collective behavior. Plasticity loss impairs acquisition; maintenance loss can occur while the actor con-tinues changing. Both show that current performance does not certify later behavior. 

Actor-Critic interference and representation sharing. 

Actor–critic methods are sensitive to implementation and optimization choices (Schulman et al. 2017; Andrychow-icz et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2022). PPG separates policy and value phases to limit objective interference (Cobbe et al. 2021); IDAAC uses separate policy and value representa-tions to improve generalization (Raileanu and Fergus 2021). In cooperative MARL, centralized and structured critics can coordinate policy improvement, but their factorization, pa-rameter sharing, and dependency structure affect what is learned (Peng et al. 2021; Fu et al. 2022; Chen and Zhang 2023). Thus, critics are not interchangeable components. We isolate one direct value-gradient route and test its effect on learned-cooperation survival. Parameter sharing improves sample efficiency but couples representations and updates (Christianos et al. 2021). Co-operative PPO is a strong baseline when its implementation is carefully controlled (Yu et al. 2022). MARL benchmarks emphasize matched implementations, multiple seeds, uncer-tainty, and transparent aggregation (Papoudakis et al. 2021; Gorsane et al. 2022; Ellis et al. 2023; Bettini, Prorok, and Moens 2024). Accordingly, our arms share the architecture and initial actor, use paired seeds where appropriate, fix the endpoints in advance, and report complete separation. 

Reward scale and reliable deep-RL evaluation. Positive reward scaling preserves fixed-policy strategic ordering, but deep-learning updates are not generally scale invariant. PPO practice includes reward, value, or advantage normalization, clipping, and adaptive optimization, which can introduce scale dependence (Andrychowicz et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2022). We exploit rather than eliminate this gap: scaling preserves the strategic problem while probing the optimizer and direct value route. Reliable RL evaluation requires more than final means from a small number of runs (Agarwal et al. 2021; Gor-sane et al. 2022). Our primary outcomes are event-time and RMST, supported by continuous behavior, raw event counts, and paired-seed analyses. These summaries remain inter-pretable when an arm has no observed events.

Matched Learning Arms 

X0: direct value-to-torso route. The policy and value heads share the actor torso. The combined loss updates the torso, so value-to-torso gradients are generally nonzero. Re-ward scaling can therefore alter actor features through the shared value route even when the matched policy-gradient contribution is unchanged. 

X1: stop-gradient route control. The critic remains present and its head continues to learn, but the value branch receives detached actor features. Thus the value-head gra-dient is nonzero while the direct value-to-torso gradient is exactly zero. X0 versus X1 isolates direct access under un-clipped SGD; global clipping can retain an indirect scale dependence through the critic’s contribution to the full gra-dient norm. 

X5: no learned critic. X5 is an actor-only Monte-Carlo reference. It removes both the direct route and the learned value head. Because its return estimator differs from X0/X1, X5 is a reference for critic-free optimization rather than a second exact stop-gradient control. The contrasts therefore answer different questions. X0 ver-sus X1 isolates direct value-gradient access while retaining the critic and forward architecture. X1 versus X5 measures what remains when a critic is trained without torso access, including differences in return estimation and, when present, global clipping. Experiments 

Experimental Settings 

Environments and Metrics We evaluate cooperation maintenance in two structurally distinct multi-agent environ-ments, each with an environment-specific cooperation crite-rion and a common temporal evaluation protocol: 

• MinEx. A two-agent repeated production-and-exchange game with complementary resource advantages. We use three profitable, incentive-compatible contexts spanning wide to narrow strategic margins (Table 2); frozen evalu-ations assess specialization, exchange, resource flow, and return gain. 

• CleanUp-lite. A compact two-agent reconstruction of the CleanUp public-resource dilemma (Hughes et al. 2018), where cleaning sacrifices immediate harvest to preserve future resources. A behavior-cloned scripted teacher provides the cooperative warm start; frozen evalu-ations require sustained return, apple collection, and pol-lution control. Complete dynamics and operational success thresholds appear in Supplementary Material Sections 2–3. All experimental cells use the frozen temporal endpoint defined above. Evaluations use raw, unscaled rewards; sur-vival and RMST summarize sustained maintenance, while return and behavioral trajectories retain transient failures and recoveries. 

Learning Configurations and Baselines We use the rout-ing arms in Table 1. X0 versus X1 is the primary matched contrast because it changes direct value-to-torso access while retaining the critic and its forward computation. X5 removes the learned critic and uses a Monte-Carlo policy estimator, so it tests critic-free optimization without serving as a second exact routing control. The confirmatory MinEx and boundary CleanUp-lite cells use globally clipped Adam; the opposite cells use unclipped SGD. Adam retains clipping-mediated coupling, whereas SGD provides the cleanest direct-route X0/X1 com-parison. Supplementary Material Sections 6 and 8.1 give the clipping analysis and implementation details. Positive reward scales are applied only during training. Because all scales are strictly positive, they preserve fixed-policy strategic orderings while changing value targets and optimizer dynamics. The X0/X1 comparison is the primary routing contrast; the X5 arm tests whether observed failure also arises without a learned critic. 

Training and Evaluation Protocol Every arm–scale pair within a seed loads byte-identical actor parameters and uses matched initial evaluation episodes. X0 and X1 use the same critic initialization and critic warm-up, during which actor parameters remain frozen. Training targets use scaled re-wards, whereas all reported evaluation returns and coop-eration thresholds use raw rewards. Reward scale therefore cannot mechanically alter the evaluation criterion. Training alternates on-policy collection, learning updates, and frozen evaluation. Runs continue after confirmed events, and later training data diverge with the policies despite matched initialization. Gradient audits use the production update path. Supplementary Material Section 8.2 specifies the step-0, randomness, checkpoint, and recovery controls. 

Local Behavioral-Margin Audit The local audit asks whether the measured value-to-torso route points toward a behavioral failure boundary, rather than treating param-eter movement itself as harm. At frozen cooperative actors, we compare matched X0 and X1 production updates at low and high reward scales. We then perturb only the shared torso along the resulting route-by-scale and high-scale X0– X1 directions, keeping the policy head fixed, and measure how cooperation margins change in both directions. Repeat-ing the audit across checkpoint or context variants, training batches, and evaluation seeds separates a consistent local direction from sampling noise. Because exact greedy behav-ior in MinEx is locally piecewise constant, its differentiable audit uses a cooperative soft-greedy surrogate. Table 3 summarizes the experimental cells. The confir-matory cells test routing-specific scale effects in distinct en-vironments and optimizers, while the opposite optimizer– environment cells provide boundary conditions. Exposure units are analyzed within cells rather than pooled. The confirmatory studies cross all three learning configu-rations with the prespecified reward scales and fresh seeds. MinEx uses Adam with global clipping across all three strate-gic contexts, whereas CleanUp-lite uses unclipped SGD with paired seeds. Both studies evaluate the common initial checkpoint and the full maintenance horizon. 

Statistical Analysis The statistical tests follow the inter-vention: reward scale should increase maintenance risk more strongly when value gradients directly enter actor features. For MinEx, a discrete-time hazard model tests the X0 scale trend and its contrasts with X1 and X5 while accounting for training time and strategic context. For CleanUp-lite, paired tests compare how each arm’s RMST changes with scale. We correct each hypothesis family for multiple testing. When an arm has no observed events, we report survival and finite RMST contrasts instead of forcing an unstable hazard estimate. The Supplementary Material gives endpoint and environment details in Sections 2–3, audit construction in Section 7, and implementation and statistical specifications in Sections 8.1–8.3.

Mechanism and Robustness Diagnostics 

The outcome contrast identifies a routing-specific risk, but the mechanism claim additionally requires evidence that the intended gradient route exists and points in a behaviorally adverse direction. 

Direct gradient audit and optimizer coupling. The production-path audit in Figure 4 verifies the treatment di-rectly: X0 has nonzero value-to-torso gradient, while X1 has exactly zero gradient along that route despite an active value head. Their actor parameters diverge after one update. The clipping audit also shows why stop-gradient is not complete optimization independence: value-head gradients still enter the global norm and can rescale X1’s policy update. The unclipped CleanUp-lite study removes this indirect route, yielding the cleanest X0/X1 comparison. 

Behavioral-margin alignment. The local audit supports adverse alignment in CleanUp-lite but yields only partial evidence in MinEx under Adam. In CleanUp-lite, both the route-by-scale direction and the high-scale X0–X1 direction lower every active greedy cooperation margin. Perturbing the full actor produces nearly the same response as perturb-ing only the torso, which localizes the adverse effect to the shared representation. Discussion and Conclusion 

Interpretation, limitations, and conclusion. The matched design verifies direct value-to-torso routing, and confirma-tory contrasts show that scale-sensitive maintenance loss concentrates in X0. The local audit supplies the behavioral condition: adverse alignment is clear in CleanUp-lite–SGD but weaker and contrast-dependent in MinEx–Adam. Route existence, adverse alignment, sufficient movement, and a confirmed event are therefore distinct evidentiary steps. Pos-itive scaling preserves strategic comparisons but changes maintenance time, so game stability does not certify learn-ing stability. Our evidence is limited to two compact en-vironments, including a reconstructed CleanUp-lite, and to verified warm starts rather than cooperation discovery. Checkpoint perturbations are local diagnostics, not inde-pendently trained solutions: the audits estimate derivatives rather than threshold crossings; the MinEx surrogate has curvature that limits extrapolation; and gradient and clipping audits do not fully capture later on-policy trajectories. Opera-tional event definitions, finite-horizon survival, and complete separation provide only finite descriptive evidence. Within these limits, continued optimization can erase verified co-operation without changing strategic ordering when value-gradient displacement is adversely aligned and reaches a failure boundary. Direct value-gradient access is thus a con-ditional, scale-sensitive risk shaped by the optimizer and en-vironment. MARL evaluations should report acquisition and survival trajectories and treat routing, reward scale, clipping, and optimizer as algorithmic choices.

From Parameter Displacement to Maintenance Risk 

The previous results identify a parameter-update route, not a theorem that cooperation must fail.
