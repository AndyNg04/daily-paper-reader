Abstract
Generative AI systems increasingly mediate how people seek in-
formation, yet communicating AI factuality to laypeople remains
challenging. Through a mixed-methods investigation involving in-
terviews (N=8), a survey (N=1,000), and an experiment (N=5,151),
we examine how people evaluate AI-generated information for
gardening, mathematical, and medical queries, and how factuality
indicators (confidence and sources) shape perceptions. Our inter-
views and survey show people selectively verify AI responses using
external and AI-provided evidence, moderated by contextual fac-
tors. Although confidence-indicators affected trust and accuracy
perceptions, people hold diverse, ambiguous interpretations of AI
confidence. Sources were viewed as crucial, yet users raised con-
cerns about source reliability and verification effort. Our experiment
showed that confidence and source-based indicators influenced ini-
tial perceptions, but effects disappeared once participants learned
the response’s correctness. Medical queries elicited skepticism re-
gardless of indicator. Findings motivate helping users interpret
model confidence and integrating source information into the in-
terface to support calibrated trust in AI-supported information-
seeking.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as tools for
information-seeking [8, 29] due to their growing prominence in on-
line search and social media platforms [15, 21], and conversational
interfaces. Yet the fluency of LLM-generated responses does not
guarantee their factuality: models can produce plausible-sounding
but inaccurate, hallucinated text that is not reliably grounded in

any verifiable source [2, 18, 26], which can lead people to follow
misleading advice.
Prior work has explored two main approaches to helping users
assess the factuality of AI-generated responses: communicating the
model’s own confidence [5, 19, 27, 34], and providing sources or
citations that allow a claim to be independently checked [20, 38].
While these approaches are promising, findings suggest that their
impacts depend heavily on how the information is presented, and
it is unclear how such indicators should be designed.
As AI chatbots take on a larger role in how people access and act
on information, understanding how everyday users evaluate and
make decisions about when to trust an AI-generated response is es-
sential to designing systems. However, large-scale studies assessing
people’s verification behaviours and attitudes are scarce, making
it difficult to design effective factuality indicators for everyday
chatbot usage.
Furthermore, most empirical studies have been focused on spe-
cific tasks, such as fact-checking [38], however, people increasingly
use AI chatbots for everyday personal questions related to hob-
bies, work, and health. Comparatively little is known about how
everyday LLM chatbot users perceive and interpret confidence- and
source-based indicators.
To address these gaps, we posed the following research questions:
RQ1: How should factuality indicators be designed to help peo-
ple assess AI factuality?
RQ2: How do laypeople assess the factuality of information from
AI chatbots?
RQ3: How do factuality indicators affect perceptions of AI chat-
bots?
We address these questions through a mixed-methods approach
comprising three complementary studies. First, we conducted in-
depth interviews with eight AI users to surface how they per-
ceive AI confidence, sources, and verification behaviours (RQ1),
as well as their reactions to speculative design concepts for fac-
tuality indicators. Second, we conducted a nationally representa-
tive survey of 1,000 U.S. AI users to characterise how, when, and
why people currently verify information from AI chatbots, and
what additional information and support might be useful (RQ2).
Third, we conducted a large-scale between-participants experiment

(N=5,151) that systematically varied the type of factuality indica-
tor shown (confidence-labels, confidence-percentages, confidence-

learned whether the response was actually correct (RQ3).
Our interviews revealed that while users value confidence indi-
cators, they hold diverse and often conflicting interpretations of
what confidence means. Sources were perceived as highly impor-
tant, given that they provide evidence that can be independently
evaluated, although users also described the effort and other lim-
itations associated with source verification. Our survey showed
that most people verify AI responses at least some of the time, but
they primarily do so through external information seeking and
source inspection rather than through AI-provided confidence in-
formation. Finally, our experiment found that both confidence and
source indicators have a small effect on people’s perceptions of an
AI chatbot before a response’s correctness is known, yet this effect
dissipates once the correct response is revealed. We further find
that contextual factors, particularly the stakes associated with a
task, influence the effectiveness of factuality indicators.

Related Work
2.1
Assessing AI factuality
Although LLM chatbots can produce fluent, convincing outputs,
they often also produce false information that can mislead their
users and others [2, 18]. This can lead people to erroneously trust or
follow AI advice, often termed overreliance [17], which can lead to
inaccurate decisions, skill erosion, and other potential harms [7, 24].
The superficially plausible and persuasive nature of LLM outputs
makes it important to be able to distinguish factual information
from false information, and so recent work in human-AI interaction
has explored methods of supporting AI users to assess the factuality
(that is, a response that is truthful, or based on fact [2, 14, 18]), of
an AI response, so that they can decide whether or not the response
is trustworthy [22].
Recent work has shown that factuality indicators, that is, design
features that provide the user with additional information to assist
with evaluating the factuality of a response, can be useful supports
and help people to appropriately calibrate their reliance on AI ad-
vice. For example, Do et al. [14] show that incorporating in-line
highlighting in LLM responses based on factuality levels made it
easier for users to validate the accuracy of the LLM’s response and
increased their trust, compared to a baseline where no style was ap-
plied. Another study found that highlighting uncertain parts of the
model output did reduce overreliance, but also made people more
likely to reject correct AI responses [6], while other approaches
have shown that removing factually incorrect information from
responses can enhance user trust [13]. Although various methods
of signalling the factuality of AI responses have been studied, it
remains unclear how these various methods are understood and
used by people in everyday interactions with AI chatbots across
various contexts. In this paper, we study two prominent categories
of factuality indicators for LLM-based chatbots – confidence and
source indicators – in order to understand how they impact peo-
ple’s perceptions of accuracy and trust in AI, and how they can
be designed to more effectively support people in AI-supported
information-seeking.
2.2
Effects of communicating confidence and
uncertainty
Various methods of communicating AI uncertainty to human users
have been proposed with the aim of allowing people to better cali-
brate their trust and reliance in human-AI decision-making [5, 9].
For example, pre-LLM work has found that presenting numerical
confidence (e.g., percentages) to users can improve task perfor-
mance [3] and trust calibration [40]. Confidence estimates and
explanations have been found to have complementary effects on
people’s accuracy in predicting a model’s output [25], while other
work has found that automatically highlighting snippets of code
that are likely to be edited helped programmers to complete their
tasks faster and were subjectively preferred [34]. Visualising AI
uncertainty has also been proposed as a method of getting people
to slow down and think more deliberatively [27]. Although these
methods facilitate precise, granular uncertainty quantification, they
can be challenging for people to interpret [16] and put into action
[37]. Uncertainty communicated through natural language may be
more intuitively understood, though people also differ in how they
interpret them (e.g., individual differences for what people consider
“very confident” tend to be large) [4, 31, 36, 41]. Recent work in
LLM-specific settings has found that how an LLM verbalises uncer-
tainty in its own generated text (e.g., by prepending statements like
“I’m not sure, but...”) shapes both reliance and trust [19]. However,
the same study also found that people’s perceptions and behaviours
are highly sensitive to how such verbalised uncertainty is phrased.
It remains unclear how different styles of confidence indicators
shape people’s perceptions of AI chatbots, and how they should be
designed to support factuality assessment more effectively. In this
study, we address this by examining how people interpret various
sorts of confidence indicators (numerical percentages, categorical
labels, and natural language) and systematically comparing how
they impact perceptions and trust in AI responses.
2.3
Effects of providing sources, evidence and
citations
An intersecting line of research has examined how providing sources
in various forms alongside a generated response can support peo-
ple to assess AI factuality. Presenting sources in the form of cita-
tions or links has become a common design pattern in commercial
LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), however these approaches
are not always reliable: they can misrepresent the original source
(e.g., [23]) as well as fabricate non-existent sources (e.g., [1]). Kim
et al. [20] found that providing sources alongside AI responses
made people more confident in their responses in an AI-supported
information-seeking task and appeared to increase the credibility
of the LLM response. They also found that verifying the sources
increased people’s performance in determining when they could
or could not rely on the AI system’s answer, though it did not
eliminate overreliance entirely. However, some studies have shown
that people often do not verify sources when provided as clickable

links to external information [19], while other studies indicated
that presenting evidence from sources in a more integrated way
(e.g., dropdown buttons) can improve people’s engagement with
sources when evaluating whether a fact-checking claim is true or
false [38]. This evidence suggests that the way in which sources
are presented to users plays a significant role in their verification
behaviour. However, the best approaches to designing source indi-
cators to support factuality assessment are unclear. Furthermore,
much of the existing literature focuses on tasks in which partici-
pants must evaluate AI responses focused on general knowledge or
fact-checking question-answering, rather than on everyday LLM
chatbot users who are concerned with specific personal queries.
In the current paper, we examine the role of sources in people’s
verification behaviour, how they determine source credibility, and
how providing sources alongside AI responses impacts people’s
perceived accuracy, trust, information sufficiency and impressions
of an AI chatbot, across a variety of topics, ranging from benign
everyday queries to high stakes medical ones.
3
RQ1: How should factuality indicators be
designed to help people assess AI factuality?
To investigate and develop an initial understanding of how laypeo-
ple perceive and reason about AI factuality cues, including confi-
dence indicators and source-based verification features, we con-
ducted an exploratory qualitative study with AI chatbot users.
3.1
Method
We conducted semi-structured interviews to enable participants to
articulate existing mental models of accuracy, trust, and verification
while providing detailed feedback on a set of speculative design
concepts. Interviews were conducted in March 2025.
3.1.1
Participants. Eight participants from the United States were
recruited to participate in one-on-one virtual interviews (see Ta-
ble 1 for an overview of demographics). All participants reported
experience using generative AI tools either weekly or daily.

3.1.2
Interview Protocol. Each participant completed a 60-minute
remote interview. Sessions were audio-recorded. Interviews began
with a warm-up discussion about how participants used AI, and
how much they relied on them. The main interview focused on five
topics:
(1) Current behaviours and expectations around information ac-
curacy: Participants were asked about how they assessed
the accuracy of information from (i) people they knew,
(ii) news and social media, and (iii) AI tools. Questions fo-
cused on what evidence they expected to be provided, how
they determined source credibility, and how sources (could)
present indicators of credibility or accuracy.
(2) Communicating AI accuracy: Participants were prompted
to imagine how AI confidence could be communicated in
a useful way, and react to design stimuli illustrating AI
confidence indicators such as labels and percentages. Par-
ticipants were also asked what they considered acceptable
levels of confidence, and what they interpreted such confi-
dence labels to be based on.
(3) Using confidence indicators: Questions focused on how AI
factuality indicators might be useful to them, and what
additional information and explanations they might require.
Participants were then asked to react to further design
stimuli showcasing additional information about sources
or confidence and discuss their utility and preferences.
(4) Citations and sources: Participants were asked about their
experiences with AI-provided sources and their verification
behaviours. They were shown design stimuli showing ex-
amples of how source information could be provided (e.g.,
pop-ups or inline with responses) and asked to evaluate
them and imagine how they could be used and improved.
(5) Contextual factors: Participants were asked to imagine how
they would react in certain situations, such as if an AI chat-
bot’s response was labelled with ’low accuracy’, or if a con-
fidence indicator was misaligned with the actual accuracy,
as well as about how specific topics or situations might
impact how important or useful AI factuality indicators
would be.
Finally, participants were asked for any further thoughts or com-
ments they had. Throughout the interview, participants were asked
follow-up questions, and encouraged to explain their reasoning,
describe their expectations about what an AI chatbot should do,
and compare alternatives.
3.1.3
Design Stimuli. We created a set of design concept images
which were presented during the interviews to elicit participant
reactions, expectations, and explore other possible approaches for
communicating AI factuality. We designed the images to show a
conversation with an AI chatbot on a mobile device, to resemble a
scenario in which people use AI to seek information for a medical
query. We chose this medical context to prompt people to reflect
on how to assess AI factuality for a high-stakes topic, similar to
previous work (e.g., [19]). The content consisted of a two-turn in-
teraction with the chatbot, in which the user first makes a query
about side-effects of a medication, and the chatbot response probes
for more information. The response is accompanied by a high confi-
dence indicator. Then, the user responds to the follow-up question,
and the chatbot provides a response, this time with a low confidence
indicator. All participants were shown the same design concepts,
but the order of different options for the same concept was ran-
domised between participants (e.g., one participant saw the options
in Fig 1 in the order: Option 3, Option 1, Option 2, Option 4).

Design Stimulus A (see Fig. 1) presented four different versions of
AI chatbot confidence indicators: (i) Confidence-labels (high/low), (ii)
Confidence-percentage (85%/30%), (iii) Confidence-labels with inline
dropdown (check mark / warning symbol and dropdown arrow), and
(iv) Confidence-aligned language (“reliable sources agree” vs “I can’t
confirm this information”). We chose these four indicator types to
exemplify different approaches: confidence labels for categorical
assessment, confidence percentages for quantitative, more granular
information, dropdown for providing additional detail on demand,
and confidence-aligned language for a more natural conversational
indicator as opposed to specific interface details.
Design Stimulus B (see Fig. 13) showed percentages underneath
the AI responses with three different candidate labels: confidence,
reliability, and certainty, to understand how different linguistic
framings shaped people’s interpretations of AI-generated informa-
tion and gauge preferences. Each design was otherwise identical.
Design Stimulus C (see Fig. 14) showed two ways users could
learn why a response received a particular confidence rating. The
first example, an explanatory bottom-sheet, showed a pop-up that
appeared after interacting with the confidence indicator and ex-
plained that the confidence rating was based on the quality of the
sources. The second example showed source information in the
form of an expandable inline dropdown, which users could read
without navigating away from the chat interface.
Design Stimulus D (see Fig. 15) showed three examples of how
AI chatbots could provide sources and citations. In the first example,
clicking on an in-text citation triggered a pop-up panel containing
excerpts from the cited webpage, and the URL to the specific page
used to generate the answer. In the second example, specific text was
highlighted within the response, and clicking on this text brought
up a pop-up panel also containing a URL and excerpts from a
relevant webpage. In the third example, a bubble underneath the
main response which read ‘Researched’ could be clicked which
would dropdown to reveal the number of sources consulted and an
overview of the source.
3.1.4
Data analysis. We followed an inductive thematic analysis
approach. The first author read the interview transcripts to famil-
iarise themselves with the data. They then conducted the primary
analysis using a bottom-up coding process. After coding four in-
terviews, the first author iteratively compared codes across partici-
pants and grouped related codes into broader conceptual categories
(e.g., ‘verification cues’ or ‘high-stakes contexts’), before coding the
next 1-2 interviews, repeating the process until all transcripts had
been analysed. The broader research team then discussed the emerg-
ing concepts, reviewing and refining the category structure and
iterating over larger themes until consensus was reached regarding
the key final themes.
3.2
Findings
Our analysis surfaced insights into how participants used and in-
terpreted confidence and source indicators, how these features
impacted their verification behaviour, as well as how their use and
perceptions of these indicators was shaped by contextual factors
such as topic, stakes, and complexity.
3.2.1
Confidence indicators. Participants agreed that knowing how
confident a chatbot was in its response would be beneficial. Specifi-
cally, participants mentioned using confidence indicators as cues to
determine when they could rely on an answer and when additional
verification might be required. For example, several participants
described confidence indicators as a cue for deciding whether to per-
form further fact-checking or search for information independently.
One participant explained that confidence could help determine
“how much research I should put in myself” (P7). Confidence was
therefore viewed by some people less as a direct measure of truth
and more as a guide for allocating verification effort.
Participants also frequently described confidence indicators as
useful for aligning their expectations with model accuracy: “it’s
good to know that you can recognize... that the system can recognize
whether it’s fully accurate or not and if it’s very confident or not.”
(P3) Participants often felt that low-confidence indicators would
prepare them for possible inaccuracies and reduce disappointment
if an answer proved incorrect. As P5 explained: “at least I under-
stood that it was low confidence and it was incorrect.” Similarly,
P1 interpreted low-confidence signalling as an act of transparency:
“They’re like, you can’t trust what we say, but we want to let you at
least know we’re trying.” In contrast, participants reacted strongly
to imagined scenarios where confidence indicators were incorrect.
While low confidence paired with a correct answer was generally
viewed as confusing but acceptable, high confidence paired with
an incorrect answer was perceived as a serious issue that eroded
participants’ trust and would discourage future use of the system.
As P7 stated: “I’ll be very mad. And I would probably not use AI
again.”
Communicating confidence. Although participants unanimously
agreed on the importance of knowing how confident a chatbot
was in its responses, many differing definitions and interpretations
of confidence emerged during the interviews. Some participants
described percentage confidence as the probability that the AI’s
response was correct, while others conceptualised it as the propor-
tion of the response that was accurate, or the proportion of the
response that was based on the source that it cited. As P7 explained:
“So it’s kind of giving off the idea, well, to me that it’s 100% accurate
to what the source said.” One participant expressed the belief that
high confidence meant that the AI system had already performed
verification on their behalf: “Because if you tell me like, you’re very
confident your answers, I know, okay, you double-checked, you
verified. So I wouldn’t need to do that” (P3). Interestingly, several
participants shifted between different interpretations of confidence
during the same interview, i.e., their descriptions of how they inter-
preted AI confidence suggested multiple, conflicting mental models
of what this confidence represented. These findings suggest that
confidence indicators may be susceptible to multiple competing
mental models, creating ambiguity around their intended meaning.
Participants also frequently highlighted that they expected expla-
nations describing how confidence scores were generated, and what
information they were based on. P7 suggested making uncertainty
more concrete by highlighting certain parts of the AI response that
were less confident: “a disclaimer at the bottom saying like a per-
cent or sentence saying I feel this is [...] 100 confident, 50 confident,

Presentation and Context. Most participants preferred numerical
confidence percentages over categorical labels such as ‘high’ or
‘low’ confidence. Numerical values were perceived as more ‘exact’
and precise. However, this preference was not universal. Some
participants argued that percentages were inherently vague because
they did not explain how the values had been calculated or what
exactly they represented.
Participants generally agreed that AI confidence was particularly
important for high stakes information, such as “health or career re-
lated finances” (P1). Several participants also distinguished between
simple and complex queries. For straightforward questions with
clear answers, categorical indicators such as ‘high confidence’ were
often considered sufficient. However, for nuanced or consequential
topics, participants preferred numerical confidence estimates that
provided additional detail. For example, P7 stated: “since it’s a drug
and relates to health, I would prefer like I would feel more safe and
trusting if it said the percent. Because you know [...] if it were my
health, I wouldn’t just want to be high confidence, I would want it
to be 100%” (P7)
Similarly, the same participant suggested that percentages be-
come more useful as questions become more complex: “I think it
really depends what I’m asking for, how much research goes into
the question. If it’s just a one sided question, then yeah, I would
like high confidence. But since this is an in-depth question, there
are a lot of aspects, I do like the percent level.”
Although participants valued confidence indicators, many ar-
gued that confidence information alone was insufficient. Instead,
participants frequently requested accompanying sources that would
allow them to understand why a response received a particular con-
fidence score and independently assess its credibility. Confidence
indicators were therefore often interpreted as a signal to inspect
supporting evidence rather than as evidence in themselves.
3.2.2
Source indicators. Participants generally viewed sources as
useful, and in some cases, crucial elements to accompany AI re-
sponses. Sources served several functions: they acted as proof for
claims, provided tangible evidence that could be inspected directly,
and helped users determine whether information was trustworthy.
As P5 explained: “I just like having sources and like just showing
proof of like what it’s mentioning.” Similarly, P1 described the im-
portance of having information that users could explore further for
themselves: “Having an article, something to click on, something to
learn more information about, something to pinpoint more.” Several
participants also interpreted sources as evidence that the AI system
had actively gathered supporting information. As P7 noted: “But
with the link it makes it seem like they actually went in and did
research to formulate this answer.”
Source credibility. Participants referred to a variety of source
types when describing credibility, including mainstream news or-
ganisations, government websites, and academic research papers.
Credibility was also linked to reputation and prior experience. P1
described relying on collective judgments, and reputation built over
time to assess source quality: “From [...] people that used the sources
before, people that know what it’s about [...] Knowing people used
it before, knowing that it’s provided accuracy for years.” However,
participants varied in how many sources they considered sufficient
to accompany a response. For example, some participants took the
view that “the more sources the better” or that “at least 10 good

sources” might be appropriate. Others believed that “one source is
enough”, provided it was credible. P5 emphasised the importance
of source diversity, suggesting that AI responses should include:
“three different places, three different sources.”
Limitations of sources. Despite placing high importance on the
role of sources in assessing AI factuality, participants also identified
several concerns about providing sources alongside AI responses.
First, checking sources can require considerable effort. While
sources provide opportunities for verification, many participants
acknowledged that they may not always take the time required to
inspect them: “Yeah, I’ll click on it, but then let’s say I’m in a rush. I
probably would not want to because then I would have to do extra
work to like, probably [check] the information myself.” (P3)
Second, participants expressed concerns about retrieval accuracy.
Some participants had experienced doubts regarding whether an AI
system had correctly interpreted their queries and selected relevant
information. P1 expressed uncertainty about whether the system
would retrieve the appropriate content: “If they interpreted my
answer right, if they look up for the right information.” P7 observed
that AI inaccuracy, despite there being correct answers available
online, was particularly frustrating: “There are accurate answers all
over the Internet ... and it’s giving me inaccurate responses, then
I’d prefer just to go to the source itself.”
Third, participants were sceptical regarding whether AI systems
could reliably provide genuine sources. Several participants de-
scribed past experiences where AI systems generated fabricated or
inaccessible source links. As P7 explained: “Because in my experi-
ence I got sources and links that led to fake websites or websites
that didn’t exist.”
Context and presentation. Participants described how task con-
text influenced how likely they were to check sources. Academic
work and healthcare questions were frequently identified as situa-
tions where sources would be particularly beneficial: “People that’s
like maybe doing a project or something for college or, you know,
they want definitely accurate information.” (P1)
Participants also had clear preferences regarding source pre-
sentation. Participants tended to prefer dropdown designs over
pop-up ones, mentioning that sources should be easy to locate,
directly accessible, and connected to specific claims within the AI
response. Participants also preferred designs that presented ex-
cerpts or pull quotes from source material because these reduced
the effort required to verify information and helped identify poten-
tial hallucinations: “it’s saying exactly which part was taken from
the source” (P7). Rather than simply providing links, participants
wanted transparency about exactly which information was being
used to support a claim.
The analysis of the semi-structured interviews provided rich
insights into how laypeople use AI chatbots for information-seeking
in their everyday lives. Building on these qualitative findings, in the
next section, we developed a survey and collected data from a large
U.S. representative sample, in order to gain broader understanding
of how people evaluate AI factuality.
4
RQ2: How do laypeople assess the factuality
of information from LLM chatbots?
We conducted an observational survey with the aim of understand-
ing people’s AI verification behaviours.
4.1
Method
4.1.1
Participants. The sample consisted of 1000 adults recruited
through YouGov’s online research panel in the United States (48.1%
female, 51.9% male), aged 18-86 (𝑀𝑎𝑔𝑒= 46.26, 𝑆𝐷𝑎𝑔𝑒= 16.24).
We recruited the sample through YouGov’s opt-in panel [39].
The sample was stratified based on gender, age, race and education,
based on the 2023 Current Population Survey (CPS) to be repre-
sentative of US adults. Participants were pre-screened according
to their Internet and AI use. To qualify, respondents were required
to report using at least one of nine popular AI systems (ChatGPT,
Gemini, Copilot, Meta AI, Perplexity, Claude, DeepSeek, Grok, or
Apple Intelligence) at least once per month. Respondents who re-
ported less frequent use were screened out. 1,586 respondents were
screened out due to not meeting this criteria. A further 158 respon-
dents did not complete the survey, while 334 were omitted following
quality control checks and 225 were omitted due to already meeting
demographic quotas. Data collection took place in May 2025.
4.1.2
Survey Design. We administered a web-based survey exam-
ining how people assess the accuracy and trustworthiness of gen-
erative AI responses (see Table 2 for survey items). We collected
responses about people’s verification behaviours when using AI
chatbots, such as how frequently they verified AI information, what
steps (if any) they took to do so, and why they did so. We also
asked people about information that AI chatbots could provide
to help them evaluate their reliability. We also collected respon-
dents’ demographic information (e.g., age, gender). The survey took
approximately 5 minutes to complete.
4.2
Survey Results
4.2.1
AI chatbot usage contexts. People reported using AI for a
diverse range of tasks. The three most common were (1) research
& recommendations (49.9%), (2) writing & editing (38.6%), and (3)

5
RQ3: How do factuality indicators affect
perceptions of LLM chatbot factuality?
Finally, we conducted a large-scale experiment in order to system-
atically compare factuality indicators and their impact on people’s
perceived accuracy, trust, and overall impressions of an AI chatbot
across three different topics with varying stakes.
5.1
Method
5.1.1
Participants. The sample comprised 5,151 United States-based
adults recruited through YouGov’s online research panel aged 18-
89 (𝑀𝑎𝑔𝑒= 46.7, 𝑆𝐷𝑎𝑔𝑒= 15.8), who reported using generative AI,
on a monthly basis or more frequently. The sample was stratified
based on gender, age, race and education, based on the 2023 Cur-
rent Population Survey (CPS) to be representative of US adults. All
participants provided informed consent prior to the study.
5.1.2
Design. We designed a between-participants experiment in
which participants were tasked to evaluate an example of a con-
versation with a mobile AI chatbot (see Section 5.1.3 for details).
Participants were randomly assigned to conditions in which the AI
system they saw varied along three main factors: type of factual-
ity indicator (No indicator, Confidence-label, Confidence-percentage,
Confidence bubble, Source-label, Source-label + confidence language),
topic (gardening, math, medical), and response accuracy (correct or
incorrect). People assigned to a condition involving a confidence in-
dicator (Confidence-label, Confidence-percentage, Confidence-bubble,
Source label + confidence cue) saw either a high confidence or a
low confidence indicator. Each participant saw only one type of
example.
We measured participants’ perceptions of the AI chatbot, includ-
ing perceived accuracy and information sufficiency, as well as trust
and overall sentiment before and after the correct response was
revealed to them, see Table 3 for details.
5.1.3
Materials. We created a set of mobile chatbot conversation
scenarios that varied in the factuality indicators cues presented
to people and the objective correctness of the AI response. All
scenarios had the same structure: a user query followed by a chatbot-
generated response. We examined three different domains, which
we chose based on the results of our survey and interviews to
represent contexts in which people place high (medical advice),
medium (education or work; a mathematical calculation), and lower
(everyday task; gardening advice) importance on understanding
how accurate an AI chatbot is. The query and response belonged
to one of three domains (gardening, math, or medical), and the
response consisted of either a correct answer or an incorrect answer
(see Table 4 for details). We designed five different combinations
of factuality indicators based on prior work and insights from RQ1
and RQ2, alongside a control condition (see Fig. 6 for examples).
The Confidence-label condition displayed a high- or low-confidence
label directly beneath the chatbot response together with clickable
text that read “Learn more”. The Confidence-bubble condition pre-
sented the same confidence information (high/low) in a more visu-
ally prominent chat bubble beneath the response. The Confidence-
percentage condition showed numerical confidence estimates (85%
for high confidence, 30% for low confidence) beneath the response,
instead of confidence labels. In the Source-label condition, partic-
ipants were shown source icons beneath the model response. In
the Sources & Confidence Language condition, source icons were

chatbot and their trust in its responses. Data collection took place
in June 2025. The average completion time was 6m 27s.

6
Discussion
Our mixed-methods investigation reveals that while users express
a strong desire for factuality indicators to help them assess AI-
generated responses, the effectiveness of these cues is heavily mod-
erated by AI response correctness, task context, interface design,
and subjective interpretations of confidence.
6.1
The role of source and confidence indicators
in assessing factuality
Although our survey and interview participants expressed that fac-
tuality indicators for AI chatbots would be useful, our experiment
indicated that the impact of these interventions on people’s percep-
tions in the experiment was relatively small, and affected only initial
trust and sentiment towards the system response. We found that
people who were provided with source indicators gave higher judg-
ments of information sufficiency and initial overall sentiment than
people shown the Confidence-bubble indicator. Confidence-label and
Source-label indicators led to higher pre-revelation trust. However,
we also observed that any significant differences between indi-
cators in the pre-accuracy revelation measures disappeared once
participants were informed about whether the AI response was
correct or not. Perceived accuracy, trust, and sentiment also often
did not differ from the control condition in which no factuality in-
dicators were provided. Moreover, the sizes of the effects attributed
to factuality indicators were small, indicating that these interven-
tions may have limited impact in practice. This may have been due
to the between-participants design of the experiment, in which
participants only saw one type of indicator, rather than directly
comparing them, which might have elicited stronger preferences.
Together, these results highlight that while factuality indicators
may influence people’s initial impressions, response accuracy and
being able to access reliable information play the greatest role in
shaping people’s perceptions of AI systems.
6.2
The importance of aligning and
communicating confidence accurately
Our interview participants consistently stated that they wanted AI
systems to communicate confidence. In the experiment, confidence
indicators influenced pre-revelation judgments of trust, sentiment,
and perceived accuracy (although the effect sizes were small), with
high-confidence signals generally leading to more favourable evalu-
ations than low-confidence signals. However, in the experiment, we
also found that misaligned (high) confidence indicators for incorrect
responses led to more extreme drops in trust than for misaligned
(low) confidence for correct responses. This pattern echoes work
on algorithm aversion, which shows that people lose trust in an
algorithm quickly once they see it make mistakes [10, 12]. A confi-
dently presented error may be judged particularly harshly because
it breaks the expectation the indicator itself created. This asymme-
try also suggests that conservative confidence indicators may be
preferable when using chatbots for information-seeking, as people
perceive the consequences of following incorrect advice as more
severe than not following correct advice. This finding also under-
scores the importance of accurate confidence calibration for model
developers: including factuality indicators that are not reliable un-
dermines trust.
Moreover, our interviews surfaced that participants held diverse
and sometimes contradictory interpretations of what confidence
actually meant. Some interpreted confidence as the probability
of correctness, others as agreement among sources, and some as
evidence that the AI had already verified its answer. Several partic-
ipants shifted between interpretations during the same interview.
This finding highlights a significant transparency issue, as well
as a risk for overreliance: each interpretation implies a different
guarantee, e.g., a user who reads ‘85% confident’ as ‘already ver-
ified’ is likely to under-verify relative to a user who reads it as
‘85% likely correct.’ Our findings also align with broader research
on uncertainty communication, which has repeatedly shown that
numerical and verbal uncertainty expressions are interpreted in
multiple ways by different people [11, 36], and echoes recent calls
for closer alignment between human notions of uncertainty and
how it is typically operationalised in AI models [33, 37]. One solu-
tion to this could be to provide clearer explanations to users of what
given confidence scores represent and how to interpret them e.g.,
likelihood, or faithfulness to the underlying source. Another could
be to provide explanations of how confidence scores are derived or
calculated, by explicitly connecting confidence estimates to under-
lying evidence, agreement among sources, retrieval quality, or other
contributing factors. For example, several participants described
wanting an explanation of what confidence scores were based on,
and one participant explicitly proposed highlighting the specific
span of a response that was less certain rather than (or in addition
to) a single confidence score for the entire response. Prior work
has shown that highlighting can help people to validate reliability,
and have positive impacts on trust [14, 34]. Such approaches could
build on methods of explaining the uncertainty associated with an
output by highlighting the parts of the evidence that contribute to
it (e.g., [32]).
6.3
Sources are necessary, but not sufficient
alone
Across both the survey and interview studies, participants repeat-
edly emphasised the importance of sources. People reported re-
viewing sources more often than requesting confidence scores, and
interview participants frequently described sources as evidence
that could be independently inspected and validated. In our ex-
periment, participants who were shown source indicators along
with confidence-aligned language gave the highest information
sufficiency judgments. However, our findings also suggest that
merely providing sources alone is insufficient to support factuality
assessment. Some participants admitted that even when sources

are offered, clicking through to inspect them can feel like too much
effort, mirroring previous findings that, although people believe
that sources should be available, they can be reluctant to engage
with them [19, 20, 35]. Our findings also point to potential ways of
designing such systems to facilitate more verification. Presenting
source information on demand through integrated, dropdown in-
terface designs instead of pop-ups, which can appear disconnected
from the AI response, could reduce the friction often involved in ver-
ifying information [38]. Interview participants also suggested that
presenting direct relevant excerpts or pull-quotes from the source
material could reduce the cognitive effort required to verify infor-
mation and helped users easily identify potential hallucinations.
Further work in human-AI interaction, natural language processing,
and information retrieval is therefore needed to develop methods
of allowing users to verify AI sources, while managing the effort
needed to evaluate them.
6.4
Considering stakes when designing
factuality indicators
Finally, a recurring theme across our three studies was the role
of context in how AI responses are perceived and trusted. Sur-
vey respondents reported that knowing AI uncertainty was most
important for health, legal, and financial topics, and interview par-
ticipants repeatedly highlighted high-stakes domains as where fac-
tuality indicators were most critical. In the experiment, differences
between domains also emerged consistently: medical responses
elicited lower trust, lower sentiment, and lower perceived accuracy
than mathematics or everyday gardening questions. Furthermore,
confidence indicators also appeared less effective for medical topics,
with weaker effects than on other topics. On one hand, it seems
somewhat counterintuitive that factuality indicators appear to have
less impact for contexts in which people emphasise that they are
particularly important. One possible explanation for this may be
that people are often guided by intuition in high stakes scenarios
involving potential risk [30]. This finding indicates that people
weigh factuality indicators alongside perceived risk or stakes, and
that confidence indicators that may be sufficient for everyday ques-
tions may be inadequate for queries involving health, or finances.
Future work is required to examine in more detail how factuality
indicators should be designed and presented in context-sensitive
ways. For example, high-stakes contexts may warrant richer evi-
dence displays, and greater transparency about uncertainty, while
low-stakes contexts may benefit from more minimal, less disruptive
indicators.
6.5
Limitations and future work
Our findings are subject to several limitations, and point to some
promising areas of future work. First, all three studies recruited
only US-based participants, and required participants to already
use generative AI tools regularly, and so our findings may not
generalise to other populations. Although the survey (RQ2) and
experiment (RQ3) targeted a representative sample of US adults,
the participants in our interview sample were all relatively young
(under 30) and therefore our insights for this part of the study were
limited to the experiences of this younger demographic. Future
work is therefore needed to examine how people in different socio-
geographical contexts and those less familiar with generative AI
assess AI factuality and perceived factuality indicators and what
expectations they may have. Our participants were also drawn


Warren et al.
from opt-in online panels, which may be less representative of the
population than address-based sampling or other random methods.
Second, our experiment only showed participants static screen-
shots of an AI chatbot interaction, rather than facilitating more
realistic interactions with a chatbot. This limited the ecological
validity of our design in several ways, for example, potentially re-
ducing the effort and/or friction of actually clicking a source or a
confidence explanation in comparison to a real life scenario. We
also focused on participants’ perceptions of the system, rather than
behavioural measures (e.g., reliance [19, 20, 28]) or decisions (e.g.,
deciding to share or publish an AI-generated response [38]), as
other studies have done. This may have contributed to the small ef-
fect sizes we observed in the experiment; participants may have had
less reason to engage with the factuality indicators and responses,
as no follow up action or decision was required. Another limitation
of our design was that each participant saw only one example of a
conversation between chatbot and user, and so their judgments are
based on this single impression rather than repeated exposure over
multiple examples as they might in everyday life. Nevertheless, our
results shed light on how initial impressions are formed and shaped
by AI accuracy. Examining how various factuality indicators affect
how people interact and make decisions with AI chatbots in real-life
settings, and how perceptions and reliance are shaped over time is
a critical area for future work.
7
Conclusion
We examined how people assess AI factuality and how different
factuality indicators influence their perceptions through a mixed-
methods investigation comprising qualitative interviews, an obser-
vational survey, and a large-scale experiment. Our findings shed
light on how people integrate confidence cues, sources, and contex-
tual considerations when deciding whether information is reliable.
Our interview findings highlight how providing factuality indica-
tors such as confidence and sources can support this process, but
they must be clearly interpretable and easily accessible to users,
as well as being sensitive to contextual factors like topic and com-
plexity. Furthermore, our experimental results show that people
ultimately judge systems based on their accuracy, and suggest that
rather than maximising trust or sentiment, effective factuality in-
dicators should help people to form well-calibrated expectations
and make informed decisions about when and how to verify AI-
generated information.
