Abstract 

LLM-based annotation has advanced rapidly, yet existing benchmarks remain misaligned with real-world practice. Actual annotation of-ten involves lengthy, task-specific guidelines, and different annotators may produce divergent but reasonable judgments, especially on bound-ary cases, where single-labeled dataset fail to reflect this disagreement. This gap also lim-its existing single-agent and workflow-based annotation systems, which often lack adapt-ability to complex, evolving guidelines. To bridge this gap, we introduce ANT-A Bench-mark , a real-world multi-annotator benchmark spanning 7 datasets across domains and modal-ities, with task-specific guidelines and 5 inde-pendent human labels per instance. ANT-A enables a distribution-aware evaluation, using Fleiss’ κ to assess how closely model annota-tions align with human labeling distributions. We further propose ANT-OR , a multi-agent annotation framework that refines prompts by distilling contrastive decision rules from posi-tive and negative feedback. Experiments show that ANT-OR improves agreement@ ≥2 accu-racy by 4.5 points over the strongest baseline and achieves the highest alignment with human annotator distributions as measured by Fleiss’ 

κ within 2 points. Code and benchmark are available at https://github.com/Krueske/ ANT-OR .

2 Related Work 

LLM-based Data Annotation and Benchmarks. 

LLMs are increasingly used as scalable alternatives to human annotators (He et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2024; Gilardi et al., 2023), creating a need for reli-able benchmarks of their annotation behavior. How-ever, existing benchmarks often simplify real-world annotation in two ways: first, they lack the detailed guideline documents that capture the complexity of realistic annotation tasks; second, they provide only a single gold or aggregated label per sam-ple (Basile et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2026). These limitations make it difficult to evaluate whether LLMs can follow task-specific annotation proto-cols (Ke et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024a) and whether their outputs match the distribution of human judg-ments rather than a single reference answer (Liu et al., 2024b). Recent work shows that LLMs strug-gle with specialized guidelines (Tseng et al., 2025; Diao et al., 2025), and that annotator disagreement often reflects meaningful differences in perspective rather than noise (Santurkar et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025c; Muscato et al., 2025; Wang and Lee, 2026). Concurrently, a line of work models anno-tator disagreement as a first-class problem rather than noise to be averaged out. Fleisig et al. (2023b) and Xu et al. (2025) predict individual annotator labels or demographic-group distributions. In emo-tion recognition, Wu (2026) and Jia et al. (2026) model ambiguity directly from human label dis-tributions without demographic routing, yet their approaches are tied to task-specific architectures. These methods are valuable references, but they do not address the cross-domain, guideline-grounded setting we target, where disagreement persists on boundary cases even under explicit rules. Overall, prior work recognizes the importance of both guide-lines and disagreement, but existing benchmarks typically study them in isolation. We address this gap with ANT-A , a multi-annotator benchmark that pairs detailed annotation guidelines with multiple independent human annotations per sample.

3 ANT-A Benchmark 

As shown in Figure 2, we introduce ANT-A 

(Aggregated N-annotator Task Annotations), a multi-source benchmark that jointly provides de-tailed guidelines and 5 independent human judg-ments per sample that reflect the inherent ambiguity of tasks and subjectivity of human annotations ren-dering single labels unreliable (see Appendix B), enabling rigorous comparison between LLM sys-tems and human raters under realistic conditions. 

3.1 Dataset Preparation Data Collection. We collect domain-relevant query samples from public internet data sources for each dataset, anonymize potentially identifiable information, and filter exact duplicates and low-quality instances to obtain 1,500 samples with a mix of clear-cut and boundary cases. 

Guideline Document Design. We derive annota-tion guidelines from real-world requirements and refine them by clarifying ambiguous definitions, adding decision rules for boundary cases, and tai-loring to our data distribution. The resulting docu-ments specify fine-grained decision boundaries and edge-case handling rules. 

3.2 Human Annotation Process 

All annotations are conducted by professional anno-tators from an internal annotation vendor, and we present the relevant backgrounds of the annotators in Table 10. We use a pool of 10 annotators, and each sample is independently labeled by 5 of them. This setup reflects realistic annotation practice and reduces bias from a fixed small human group. To ensure quality, we adopt a three-phase protocol, with a total annotation cost of USD $4078. 

Phase 1: Pre-annotation. A small subset of each dataset (approximately 50 samples) is first anno-tated by all 10 annotators. This pilot phase fa-miliarizes annotators with the guideline document and task requirements, and surfaces ambiguities Figure 3: Workflow of ANT-OR . Agents construct contrastive knowledge bases (§4.2), perform retrieval-augmented annotation (§4.3), and optimize prompts via failure attribution with iterative knowledge updates (§4.4). 

or under-specified rules in the guidelines. Outputs from this phase are reviewed to identify systematic disagreements stemming from guideline ambiguity rather than genuine judgment differences. 

Phase 2: Rule Clarification. Based on the pre-annotation results, we collect questions and con-fusion points raised by annotators regarding cat-egory definitions, boundary cases, and decision rules. These are addressed through a structured Q&A process: annotator questions are compiled, unified answers are provided, and the guideline doc-ument is revised accordingly to eliminate identified ambiguities. This iterative clarification ensures that remaining disagreements in the formal annotation phase reflect genuine task difficulty rather than in-consistent rule interpretation. 

Phase 3: Batch Annotation. After guideline fi-nalization, each of the 1,500 samples in a dataset is independently labeled by 5 annotators selected from the pool of 10. Annotators label only their as-signed samples and cannot access others’ labels, en-abling meaningful inter-annotator agreement anal-ysis and comparison with LLM-based annotators. 

3.3 Benchmark Statistics 

Table 1 summarizes the key statistics of our bench-mark. Across the 7 datasets, we observe varying levels of inter-annotator agreement as measured by Fleiss’ κ (Fleiss, 1971), reflecting the inher-ent difficulty differences across tasks and domains. Across datasets, instances with five completely dif-ferent annotations are extremely rare (nearly 1%), suggesting that most samples retain partial human consensus. Further details on dataset construction, annotation guidelines, and annotator demographics are provided in Appendix C.

4.3 Knowledge-Augmented Reasoning Annotator–Verifier Architecture. Our ANT-OR adopts a hierarchical annotator–verifier archi-tecture (Hegazy et al., 2025; Ahtisham et al., 2026): three annotators independently produce labels and rationales using separately optimized prompts, and a verifier then compares their justifications to de-termine the final label. This design preserves pro-posal diversity while centralizing conflict resolu-tion, avoiding the costly multi-round deliberation of debate-based methods (Du et al., 2024) and the plan-induced error propagation of planner–worker alternatives (Wang et al., 2023a) (see Appendix A).

5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup Models and Benchmarks. Considering model performance, cost, and inference efficiency, we con-duct experiments using DeepSeek-V3.1-Terminus (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) for text-modality tasks and Qwen3-235B-A22B-VL-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) for vision-language tasks as the backbone models of MAS. Both models also serve as the op-timizers themselves. As mentioned in Section 3, we construct a benchmark covering multiple do-mains (medical, e-commerce, general) and multi-ple modalities (including text and vision-language tasks) to evaluate the performance of different opti-mization methods in data annotation scenarios. 

Baselines. We reproduce the relevant baselines in both single-agent and multi-agent system (MAS) scenarios. In single-agent scenarios, we compare with the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022) and In-Context Learning (ICL) via similarity-based example retrieval (Liu et al., 2022), as well as other single-agent optimization algorithms, includ-ing APE (Zhou et al., 2023), ORPO (Yang et al., 2024), ERM (Yan et al., 2025), and SPO (Xiang et al., 2025) (we reproduce the optimization logic of SPO in labeled scenarios). In multi-agent set-tings, we evaluate vanilla and ICL variants of the Annotator–Verifier architecture. We also adapt SPO, the strongest single-agent optimization base-line, to the same MAS architecture using the same batch schedule for a fair comparison. 

Implementation Details We configure ANT-OR with 3 LLM-based annotators and one verifier, all at temperature=0.1. Each annotator is initialized with the same prompt template, while the verifier uses a separate adjudication prompt. Following the optimization procedure of SPO (Xiang et al., 2025), we train on 200 samples per task with batch size 20, 3 iterations per batch over 6 epochs, and select the best epoch based on training set performance. We evaluate on 1000 test samples; the remaining 300 samples are unused. For retrieval, we encode text and images with Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025d) and Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B (Li et al., 2026). Prompts are detailed in Appendix D. 

Metrics. We evaluate annotation performance with two complementary metrics: agreement@ ≥2

measures whether an annotation is supported by at least two human annotators. For model predictions, we count a prediction as correct if it matches a label annotated by at least two of the five human anno-tators. For human performance, we use a leave-one-annotator setting: each human annotation is treated as the prediction and is counted as correct if at least one of the other four annotators gives the same label. Fleiss’ κ measures inter-annotator con-sistency. We compute the human baseline from the five human annotators, and compute model κ by treating the model as an additional annotator along-side the humans. 5.2 Main Results 

Table 2 presents the comprehensive evaluation re-sults comparing ANT-OR against single-agent and multi-agent baselines across all 7 datasets. 

Consistent Superiority Across Tasks, Domains, and Modalities. ANT-OR achieves the best over-all performance across all 7 datasets, outperform-ing the strongest baseline by 4.5 points in average agreement@ ≥2 accuracy. The gains are consistent across medical, e-commerce, and general-domain tasks, as well as both text and vision-language modalities. These results show that ANT-OR can effectively handle nuanced decision boundaries in diverse guideline-governed annotation settings. 

Narrowing the Gap to Human-Level Consis-tency. ANT-OR not only improves point-wise consensus coverage (agreement@ ≥2), but also achieves higher distributional agreement with hu-man judgments, as measured by Fleiss’ κ. Notably, ANT-OR achieves κ = 56.61 on ImageAR and κ =83.82 on BillSC, approaching the inter-annotator agreement among human annotators themselves (58.59 and 83.50 in Table 1). This validates that, compared to static agent systems, optimized multi-agent systems can narrow the gap to human-level consistency on complex annotation tasks.

This confirms that two positives are sufficient to stabilize decision patterns without unnecessary cost, making the 2:1 ratio the optimal trade-off.

6 Conclusion 

We present ANT-A, a multi-annotator benchmark and ANT-OR, a multi-agent framework, to advance automated data annotation toward human-level con-sistency. Our benchmark spans 7 datasets across multiple domains and modalities, providing 5 inde-pendent human annotations per sample alongside detailed guideline documents, thereby enabling rig-orous evaluation of inter-annotator agreement. To address the limitations of static agent architectures, ANT-OR introduces iterative prompt optimization with a structured knowledge base and contrastive learning over annotation samples, mining discrimi-native dimensions from contrastive prediction pat-terns to transform collective annotation experience into reusable decision rules. Extensive experiments demonstrate that ANT-OR significantly narrows the gap to human-level consistency, consistently outperforming single-agent and multi-agent base-lines across diverse tasks. 

Limitations 

Broader Benchmark Coverage. Although our benchmark spans multiple domains and modali-ties, it still represents only a subset of real-world annotation settings. Future work can extend this benchmark to cover a wider range of tasks, such as long-document annotation, open-ended judgment, and more specialized expert workflows, in order to further evaluate automated annotation systems under broader and more realistic conditions. 

More Efficient Optimization. ANT-OR im-proves annotation reliability through multi-agent collaboration and iterative optimization, but this design also introduces additional computational overhead. A natural direction for future work is to develop more efficient optimization and inference strategies that preserve the benefits of structured multi-agent refinement while reducing optimiza-tion and annotation cost. 

Stronger and More General Backbone Models. 

The current framework is built on the capabilities of the underlying LLMs for retrieval, reasoning, and prompt refinement. As backbone models con-tinue to improve, it will be important to study how ANT-OR scales to stronger models and how well it transfers across different model families, domains, and annotation settings. 

Ethics Statement 

This work follows the ACL Ethics Policy. Our benchmark is constructed for research on reliable data annotation, and we take care to remove or anonymize personally identifiable or sensitive in-formation before release. Human annotations are collected from professional annotators who are pro-vided with task guidelines and clarification oppor-tunities. Although ANT-OR improves LLM-based annotation reliability, it should not be used as a sub-stitute for human experts in high-stakes domains such as medicine, finance, or legal decision-making. LLM-generated annotations may still contain er-rors or biases, so users should conduct task-specific privacy, bias, and reliability assessments before de-ployment.

Table 6 compares different multi-agent system (MAS) architectures for data annotation. We ana-lyze the failure modes of alternative designs: 

Debate-based Architecture. Following Du et al. (2024), we implement a multi-agent debate where agents iteratively discuss and refine their predic-tions. While debate improves reasoning on open-ended tasks, it performs poorly for guideline-governed annotation: (1) debates often diverge from the original task into tangential arguments, amplifying rather than correcting initial errors; (2) the lack of explicit role separation leads to group-think, where agents converge on consensus rather than correct answers; (3) computational costs scale quadratically with debate rounds, making it eco-nomically impractical for large-scale annotation. 

Planner-Worker Architecture. We implement a Planner-Worker design following standard task-decomposition patterns (Wang et al., 2023a), where a planner agent divides inputs into sub-tasks and delegates to worker agents, then aggregates results. Contrary to expectations, this architecture underper-forms single-agent baselines across all tasks (-1.1% on MedEE, -3.7% on DisPA, -0.4% on BillSC). This failure may be attributed to coordination noise in guideline interpretation. Unlike simple task decomposition where sub-tasks are independent, annotation tasks require holistic understanding of interdependent category definitions and boundary rules. The planner’s decomposition forces prema-ture partitioning of decision logic: workers process truncated contexts that lack crucial disambiguat-ing information from the full guidelines, while the aggregation step (simple concatenation or voting) lacks the capability to perform guideline-grounded adjudication. This creates a compounding error ef-fect: the planner introduces interpretation errors at the decomposition stage, workers amplify these er-rors with incomplete context, and the aggregation fails to reconcile genuine disagreements. While costs are reduced compared to full debates (34-50% lower), the accuracy degradation renders this architecture unsuitable for high-stakes annotation tasks. 

Annotator-Verifier Architecture. Consistent with prior findings that annotator-verifier archi-tectures enhance reliability (Hegazy et al., 2025; Ahtisham et al., 2026), ANT-OR uses parallel an-notators to diversify perspectives while a dedicated verifier adjudicates conflicts through guideline-grounded reasoning. This design achieves 1-2% accuracy improvement over baselines with only 3-4 × cost increase (vs. 10-50 × for alternatives), demonstrating superior accuracy-cost efficiency for annotation tasks.

C.3 Annotation Process 

Our three-stage annotation process is designed to differentiate meaningful disagreement from ambi-guity caused by unclear guidelines. 

Phase 1–2: Pilot Annotation and Guideline Re-finement. We use approximately 50 pilot sam-ples to expose ambiguities and missing rules, which are resolved through Q&A and guideline revision before formal annotation begins. 

Phase 3: Formal Annotation. After guideline refinement, formal annotation is conducted by five independent annotators per sample. This process is further supported by the annota-tion statistics shown in Table 9: the proportion of instances where all 5 annotators give completely different labels is about 1% on average. If disagree-ments were primarily caused by unclear guidelines, we would expect a much higher rate of random di-vergence. The fact that full disagreement is rare in-dicates that most samples reach a consensus under the refined guidelines, and the retained disagree-ments are concentrated in specific boundary cases that resist unique adjudication. Thus, the disagree-ments in our experiments reflect differences that persisted after guideline refinement, not systematic ambiguity.

C.5 Qualitative Analysis of Prompt Optimization 

To complement the quantitative results, we provide a qualitative analysis of how the prompts evolve through the optimization process. Below is a repre-sentative example from the Billing Scenario Classi-fication task, illustrating how the annotation prompt improves across optimization epochs. 

Example: Merchant Name Specificity Thresh-old in Billing Scenario Classification. Epoch 0 (Simple condition): 

“When merchant name contains explicit industry keywords, classify by industry attribute.” 

Epoch 2 (Added anti-examples): 

“When merchant name is too generic (e.g., only contains ‘company’, ‘indus-try’, ‘group’ without industry indication) AND transaction description is ambigu-ous → classify as ‘Other’.” 

Epoch 5 (Balanced two-tier rule): 

“When merchant name contains ex-plicit industry keywords → prioritize industry over transaction nature; when overly generic + ambiguous content →

‘Other’.” 

Why it matters: This evolution prevents both false positives (over-eager industry classification) and false negatives (missing valid industry signals), allowing the model to better align with human un-derstanding. 

D Prompts 

We present all the prompts used in our paper in the following tables, including: • Prompts for Basic Annotation (Single An-notator): The prompt is used to perform stan-dard annotation when no contrastive samples or dimensions are available. The system pro-vides the base annotation rules and the query to be annotated, then the LLM generates the annotation result with step-by-step reasoning. • Prompts for Dimension-Aware Annotation with Contrastive Samples: The prompt is used to perform annotation when discrimina-tive dimensions and contrastive samples are retrieved from the example pool. The sys-tem provides the base rules, relevant dimen-sions (rule intent, decision boundary, applica-ble boundary), and reference samples (both correct and incorrect demonstrations) to guide the LLM in making more accurate annotations by learning from historical error patterns. • Prompts for Verification Agent: The prompt is used to select the best annotation result from multiple annotators by comparing their reason-ing logic. When annotators disagree, the LLM evaluates each annotator’s reasoning for logi-cal consistency, coherence, and errors, then se-lects the most sound reasoning. This serves as a meta-judge to resolve disagreements among the annotator ensemble. • Prompts for Contrastive Dimension Min-ing: The prompt is used to analyze contrastive triples (two correctly judged samples and one incorrectly judged sample that are semanti-cally similar) to uncover the fundamental in-tent behind rule formulation. The LLM iden-tifies surface differences, decision boundaries, rule intent, meta-rules, and applicable bound-aries, producing structured dimension defini-tions that capture the core logic distinguishing correct from incorrect judgments. • Prompts for Dimension Field Refinement: 

The prompt is used to integrate newly ana-lyzed contrastive cases with existing discrimi-native dimensions. The LLM determines the relationship between new cases and histor-ical rules (support, supplement, correction, or extension), extracts generalizable rule in-tent, resolves conflicts, and tests counterex-amples to produce refined rule_intent, deci-sion_boundary, and applicable_boundary defi-nitions. • Prompts for Verifier Prompt Optimization: 

The prompt is used to optimize the verifica-tion agent’s selection criteria based on error analysis. The system provides current verifica-tion errors, learned discriminative dimensions, and contrastive samples as auxiliary reference. The LLM analyzes root causes of verification failures and produces an improved verifica-tion prompt that can more accurately select the best annotator result by checking reason-ing logic. • Prompts for Annotator Prompt Optimiza-tion: The prompt is used to optimize the an-notation instructions based on error analysis. The system provides the existing annotation prompt, current error samples (core optimiza-tion basis), learned discriminative dimensions, and contrastive samples as auxiliary reference. The LLM analyzes root causes of annotation errors and produces an improved annotation prompt that directly addresses the identified problems while following optimization princi-ples.
