# PerSPL: Personalized Semi-supervised Preference Learning

## Abstract 

Personalized reward models require user-specific preference comparisons, but collecting such feedback is costly and provides only limited supervision for each user. At the same time, large pools of responses can be generated without user identities or preference labels, yet existing personalized reward models leave this data unused. Directly learning from these responses is difficult because pairwise objectives constrain relative scores within a prompt, whereas pseudo-labeling indi-vidual responses requires scores that are comparable across prompts for the same user. We propose PerSPL (Personalized Semi-supervised Preference Learning), a backbone-agnostic framework that uses pair-mean score centering to stabilize this cross-prompt score coordinate and iteratively turns unlabeled responses into user-specific training signals. Against seven representative baselines across four benchmarks—PersonalLLM, HelpSteer2, Reddit TLDR, and Chatbot Arena— PerSPL achieves the highest reported mean accuracy among the evaluated methods for both seen and unseen users. The two latter benchmarks provide real-user preference comparisons in summarization and open-domain chatbot interaction, respectively. These results indicate that unlabeled responses can provide additional supervision for personalized reward modeling when per-user preference labels are scarce. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models are expected to serve users with different goals, values, and communication preferences. The best response for one user may therefore not be the best for another Bakker et al. [2022], Durmus et al. [2023], Kirk et al. [2024], Ryan et al. [2025]. Alignment pipelines commonly learn what people prefer by asking them to compare two responses to the same prompt. A reward model learns to assign higher scores to preferred responses, and these scores guide model training or response selection Christiano et al. [2017], Ouyang et al. [2022], Bai et al. [2022]. Most reward models, however, pool all comparisons and learn a single, population-wide notion of a good response. When users disagree systematically, this aggregate reward can obscure their differences and poorly reflect individual preferences. Personalized reward models instead make the score depend on the user and their prior feedback Poddar et al. [2024], Chen et al. [2025], Bose et al. [2025], Cai et al. [2026]. Personalizing the score creates a more demanding data requirement. A population model can pool judgments from all annotators, whereas a personalized model must learn about each user from that user’s limited feedback. Every preference label requires the user to inspect and compare responses, so even seen users provide only short histories and a new user must be modeled from a handful of examples. Responses themselves are easier to obtain because they can be generated or collected without additional user feedback. This creates an opportunity to supplement scarce per-user labels with a much larger, shared pool of unpaired responses. Existing personalized reward models improve learning from sparse comparisons by sharing information across users Poddar et al. [2024], Chen et al. [2025], Bose et al. [2025], Cai et al. [2026], but they do not use this pool as additional supervision.

What is missing is a way to turn the same unpaired response into potentially different training signals for different users. Pairwise supervision alone does not determine how to pseudo-label individual responses across prompts. It requires a preferred response to score above the rejected response for the same prompt, but it does not fix where either score lies relative to scores for other prompts. Shifting both scores for a prompt by the same amount preserves the pairwise prediction while changing these cross-prompt comparisons. This ambiguity is irrelevant when ranking responses to the same prompt. It matters for an unpaired example, which contains one response and must be judged against a threshold fitted across prompts. Reliable pseudo-labeling therefore requires comparable scores across prompts for each user. Existing semi-supervised preference-learning methods use unlabeled data to train a population-wide reward or policy He et al. [2024], Kim et al. [2024], Lee et al. [2025]. However, these methods do not provide this user-specific score comparability. We address these challenges with PerSPL (Personalized Semi-supervised Preference Learning), which combines cross-prompt score stabilization with user-specific pseudo-labeling. To make scores more comparable across prompts, PerSPL encourages the two scores in each labeled pair to have mean zero, a mechanism we call pair-mean score centering. It then fits a per-user mapping from reward scores to preference probabilities, so the same response can receive different pseudo-labels across users. PerSPL retains confident pseudo-labels and iteratively trains on them alongside labeled data, extracting additional supervision without collecting new user feedback. Figure 1 shows how labeled and unpaired data interact in this process. We make three contributions: • Personalized learning from unpaired responses. We formulate semi-supervised personalized reward modeling, in which limited per-user comparisons are supplemented by a shared pool of responses without user identities or preference labels. PerSPL converts this pool into user-specific supervision for seen users and for new users given a small labeled support set. • Score centering for cross-prompt pseudo-labeling. We identify a score-coordinate ambiguity that prevents pairwise reward models from directly pseudo-labeling individual responses across 2prompts. We introduce pair-mean centering to address this ambiguity, connect it theoretically to cross-prompt and single-response risk, and show experimentally that it enables unpaired training to improve personalized reward learning. • Evaluation across complementary personalization settings. Against seven representative base-lines, PerSPL achieves the highest reported mean seen- and unseen-user accuracy among the evaluated methods in every primary setting across four benchmarks, including real-user preferences from Reddit TLDR and Chatbot Arena. Controlled generator-shift experiments show that the overall unlabeled-data benefit persists across all tested mixtures. 

## 2 Related Work 

Personalized reward modeling. Existing personalized reward models address per-user feed-back scarcity through architectural sample efficiency, falling into the three paradigms identified by MRM Cai et al. [2026]: (1) personalized input methods that condition a shared reward on user-specific signals such as personas or learned embeddings Zhao et al. [2023], induced personas Ryan et al. [2025], in-context preferences Singh et al. [2025], or a user-specific latent Poddar et al. [2024]; (2) personalized parameter methods that add user-specific modules over a shared backbone Li et al. [2024], Chen et al. [2025], Bose et al. [2025], deliberately keeping the per-user parameter count small to remain tractable on tens of pairs; and (3) meta-learning methods that learn an adaptable initialization for few-shot inner-loop updates Cai et al. [2026], with related downstream work on heterogeneous direct alignment at the policy level Shirali et al. [2025], Zhang et al. [2024]. All three paradigms attack per-user scarcity through architecture, sharing the implicit assumption that the only available signal is each user’s labeled preference pairs. In contrast, PerSPL attacks per-user scarcity at the data level, turning each user’s unpaired responses into pseudo-labeled supervision. 

Semi-supervised preference learning under limited feedback. A separate line, semi-supervised reward modeling, leverages unlabeled data to mitigate annotation cost, but in the homogeneous -reward setting. SSRM He et al. [2024] self-trains on unlabeled paired responses, SPA Kim et al. [2024] iteratively generates responses from the current policy and DPO-self-labels them, and SSPO Lee et al. [2025] extends pseudo-labeling to unpaired SFT responses around a global threshold KDE-estimated from labeled data (see also Park et al. [2022]). Each method learns one population-wide reward model or policy and applies one global pseudo-labeling rule, with no mechanism for cross-user preference variation. PerSPL is purpose-built for personalized reward modeling with unpaired data, a problem these homogeneous-reward methods cannot address; we therefore omit them as empirical baselines. 

## 3 Problem Setup 

Personalized preference learning. For a user u, prompt x, and response y, a personalized reward model assigns a scalar score rθ (x, y, u ). Among responses to the same prompt, the model predicts that the user prefers the one with the higher score. Each labeled comparison (x, y +, y −) records that user u prefers y+ to y− for the same prompt. Writing r± = rθ (x, y ±, u ), the Bradley–Terry model assigns the preference probability and loss 

Pr( y+ ≻ y− | x, u ) = σ(r+ − r−), LBT = − log σ(r+ − r−), (1) where σ(t) = (1 + e−t)−1.For each user u, DuL denotes the labeled comparisons available for that user. For a seen user, these comparisons are available during joint training. For a previously unseen user, they form the small support set used at adaptation, and evaluation uses disjoint comparisons. Thus, we study few-shot personalization, where every user provides some preference feedback, rather than zero-shot personalization. All users also share an unpaired pool DU of prompt–response examples (x, y ). Each example contains one response to a prompt and has neither a preference label nor a user identity. We focus on the regime in which this shared response pool is much larger than the labeled comparison set available for any individual user. Our goal is to use the shared pool to improve personalized ranking for both seen and unseen users without collecting additional user feedback. Because an unpaired example has no alternative response, using it requires interpreting its score relative to responses from other prompts. 

Cross-prompt score ambiguity. The BT loss constrains the score difference between two responses to the same prompt. It does not constrain their common score location. For any prompt–user offset 3c(x, u ), the transformed reward function 

r′(x, y, u ) = rθ (x, y, u ) + c(x, u ) (2) produces the same preference probability and BT loss. This invariance is harmless for same-prompt ranking because the offset cancels, but it changes how a score compares with responses from other prompts and whether it crosses a pseudo-labeling threshold. The pairwise objective therefore does not identify the cross-prompt score coordinate required for single-response pseudo-labeling. The usual held-out metric cannot reveal this ambiguity. 4.2 Per-user calibration and pseudo-labeling 

With this centering signal, PerSPL learns how each user’s reward scores relate to their observed preferences. For each labeled pair in DuL, we assign target 1 to the chosen-response reward and target 0 to the rejected-response reward. We fit a non-decreasing isotonic regressor fu : R → [0 , 1] 

to these labeled rewards with the Pool-Adjacent-Violators algorithm Zadrozny and Elkan [2002], Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana [2005]. Thus, fu(r) is a monotone empirical estimate of the probability that a score r appears on the chosen side of user u’s comparisons. The fit is a stop-gradient target and also defines the per-user score anchor 

δu = inf {s : fu(s) ≥ 12 }. (5) For an unpaired response, write r = rθ (x, y, u ) and choose τ ∈ (1 /2, 1) . PerSPL assigns z = 1 if 

fu(r) > τ , assigns z = 0 if fu(r) < 1 − τ , and otherwise abstains. The retained triples (x, y, z )

## 5 Experiments 

Our primary evaluation compares PerSPL with seven representative baselines on four benchmarks spanning synthetic and real responses and simulated and real users. We then use ablations and controlled studies to examine the contribution of unlabeled training, its interaction with pair-mean centering, and its behavior under different label budgets, preference noise, and response-distribution shift. 

5.1 Datasets 

The four benchmarks separate response realism from user realism. PersonalLLM Zollo et al. [2024] uses synthetic responses and Dirichlet-sampled simulated users. HelpSteer2 Wang et al. [2024] uses human-rated responses with simulated users defined by weights over rating attributes. Reddit TLDR Stiennon et al. [2020] contains real users and human preferences on summaries. Chatbot Arena Chiang et al. [2024] contains real users and human preferences over open-domain chatbot responses. PersonalLLM uses αdiv ∈ { 0.001 , 0.01 , 0.1}, while HelpSteer2 uses αdiv ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1}.Appendix A.1 gives the complete construction and split definitions. 

5.2 Baselines and Evaluation Protocol Baselines and architecture. We compare against a homogeneous Bradley–Terry reward model ( BT ), personalized-input methods ( GPO , VPL ), personalized-parameter methods ( PAL , LoRe ), and the meta-learning method MRM with its RPO objective Zhao et al. [2023], Poddar et al. [2024], Chen et al. [2025], Bose et al. [2025], Cai et al. [2026]. Each baseline uses its released implementation and a benchmark-level configuration shared across seeds. PerSPL uses the same reward-head architecture as LoRe, providing an architecture-controlled comparison between the two methods. Both operate on the same frozen 4,096 -dimensional final-token representation; PerSPL learns a low-rank linear basis and simplex-valued user weights. The default PerSPL configuration uses λr = 0 .01 across benchmarks; ablations explicitly mark deviations. Dataset construction, the exact reward head, optimizer settings, and stage-specific configurations for the main results are reported in Appendix A, including Table 5. 

Evaluation protocol. Seen users are trained jointly, whereas each unseen user is adapted only from its designated support set. All test comparisons are held out. To ensure consistent comparisons, methods use the same assigned splits, labeled budgets, and seed identities. Methods defined on frozen features (Skywork V2, BT, GPO, LoRe, MRM, and PerSPL) use the same cached 4,096 -dimensional representations; VPL and PAL retain the native GPT-2 and OPT-350M backbones prescribed by their released implementations. The supervised baselines receive the same labeled comparisons, while PerSPL additionally receives DU , whose responses carry neither user identities nor preference labels. We report within-prompt pairwise accuracy separately for seen and unseen users. Unless noted, results are mean ± population standard deviation ( ddof =0) over five matched seeds, while the controlled shift study uses ten. ## 6 Conclusion 

We introduced PerSPL to turn a shared pool of responses without user or preference labels into user-specific supervision. The method combines pair-mean score centering, per-user isotonic calibration, confidence filtering, and iterative self-training. Against seven representative baselines across four benchmarks, PerSPL has the highest reported mean accuracy among the evaluated methods for both seen and unseen users. Paired-bootstrap intervals support its overall gains in every evaluated HelpSteer2 and Reddit TLDR setting and on Chatbot Arena, which supplies real-user open-domain preferences. The controlled generator-shift study further defines the scope of the unlabeled-data benefit. Across all tested mixtures, the overall gain over regularized labeled-only training ranges from 0.66 to 3.21 points, with every paired interval above zero. This evidence applies to the tested generators. Per-user calibration requires a labeled anchor, so the current setting studies few-shot rather than zero-shot personalization. ## B Theory and Diagnostics for Pair-Mean Centering 

This appendix characterizes the score-space role of pair-mean centering and its effect on labeled-only reward-head training. We first relate pair means to cross-prompt risk. We then use an idealized Gaussian setting to exhibit how center covariance can change the optimized scoring direction. Finally, we derive the one-sided response of a fixed-budget Adam checkpoint and measure it on real data. The factorial ablation in Section 5.4 separately measures how centering interacts with iterative unpaired training.

## D Scope and Limitations 

Labeled anchors and information budgets. Per-user isotonic calibration requires labeled com-parisons for every user to whom the method is applied. Zero-label cold start is therefore out of 22 scope. Our seen-user histories and unseen-user support sets play different roles and must not be conflated: the former learn shared structure, while the latter adapt that structure to a target user. Mining user-generated content could reduce explicit annotation cost, but would introduce privacy and consent issues and would require an evaluation with a matched total information budget. 

Selective pseudo-labeling. Confidence filtering intentionally abstains on ambiguous responses and retains all original labeled comparisons. It can nevertheless overrepresent easy or well-calibrated response regions. We test the all-response alternative and report accuracy, but do not yet measure subgroup-specific pseudo-label precision and coverage; deployment should audit both. 

Distribution shift. The response-shift experiment holds labeled data fixed and varies a mixture of six in-domain and two held-out generators. It does not establish robustness to arbitrary semantic, demographic, adversarial, or temporal shift. Recalibration or abstention may be required when the unlabeled pool is far from the labeled support. 

Architecture and scale. Experiments use a frozen reward-model representation with a learned low-rank reward basis. The findings may change with end-to-end fine-tuning or a much larger personalized backbone; a capacity-matched full-fine-tuning control is not included. The user populations contain at most 200 users, so computational and statistical behavior at production scale remains uncharacterized. 

Language and tasks. The benchmarks are English-language and focus on instruction following, open-domain chatbot comparisons, and summarization. Cross-lingual transfer, multi-turn interaction, code, and long-horizon consequences are not evaluated.
