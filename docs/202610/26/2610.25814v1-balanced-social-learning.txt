# Balanced Social Learning 

## Andrew Koh ∗

## Columbia Ricky Li †

## MIT This version: October 19, 2026 

Abstract 

When is social learning efficient if agents have heterogeneous preferences over a set of actions? When myopic agents generate information only about the action they choose, efficiency requires balance such that all high-value actions are discovered. We characterize the value of policies that control the flow of past information to future agents. Under full transparency of past information, the probability of efficient out-comes is approximately one if and only if the prior and preferences are balanced and the log-likelihood ratio distribution satisfies a set of intuitive moment conditions. Noisy bad news is a canonical example. By contrast, approximate efficiency is obtainable on the set of arbitrary adaptive information policies if and only if the prior alone is balanced. Moreover, it suffices to use adaptive evidence policies which permanently and fully disclose subsets of past information. When the prior is imbalanced and ap-proximate efficiency is unattainable, we characterize the value of adaptive information and show that it is uniformly approximated by the value of adaptive evidence as the impact of each individual signal on beliefs vanishes.

Many social learning environments feature a tension between speed in aggregating dispersed information and balance in the composition of actions that society learns about. When social learning is fast, society quickly grows confident in the value of an action. When social learning is balanced, enough actions are sampled that society eventually learns about the value of all actions. Speed is preferred to balance when preferences are identical across society: identifying a single high-value action suffices for efficiency. However, balance may be preferred to speed when preferences are heterogeneous: to serve diverse individual tastes, society must learn about a broad set of actions. When and how is balanced social learning achieved? To answer this question, we develop a binary-action, binary-state model of multidimen-sional social learning. 1 Each action has an unknown value that is common across agents. Agents are uncertain about these values and have heterogeneous preferences over the two actions. An information policy controls the flow of information from past to future. At each time t, an agent arrives and chooses an action given their idiosyncratic preference and the public belief about each action’s value. The choice generates a signal from a statistical experiment about the chosen action. The information policy then determines what future agents learn from that signal. Two running interpretations illustrate the model: 1. Product markets : consumers choose between two products that differ in quality and in fit with individual taste. An information policy is a review system that determines what future consumers learn about past consumers’ experiences. 2. Technology adoption : adopters choose between two technologies that differ in qual-ity and in fit. An information policy determines how early outcomes are communicated to later adopters. A starting observation (Lemma 1) is that, under any informational environment in which infinitely sampled actions are correctly learned, long-run inefficiencies arise only from in-sufficient exploration in which too few high-value actions are learnt about. When society abandons a high-value action, agents with an idiosyncratic taste for that action forgo the payoff from a better horizontal match. This is the sense in which balance —high-value actions being learned about in tandem—is required for efficiency.

Efficiency under full transparency. We begin our analysis with the regime of full trans-parency. Lemma 1 establishes a key benchmark: under full transparency, the probability of efficient outcomes is strictly less than 1 for any fixed experiment. We therefore ask how closely almost-sure efficiency can be approximated. Theorem 1 shows that, along any se-quence of experiments whose high-state log-likelihood ratios are uniformly bounded above, the efficiency probability approaches 1 if and only if two conditions hold. 4 First, the prior and the preference distribution satisfy path balance . Second, conditional on a high-value ac-tion, the expected movement in the log-likelihood ratio (the drift ) vanishes and the variance of this movement (the noise ) is of smaller order than the drift. Intuitively, slowing learning down generates endogenous exploration. When each signal moves public beliefs only slightly, the dispersion in the horizontal preferences of arriving agents is large relative to the movement in beliefs, so both actions continue to be chosen. Using diffusion asymptotics, we show that in the noisy limit, where each agent’s signal has a vanishing impact on public beliefs, beliefs about high-value actions can be made to increase in tandem along a balanced learning path . Efficiency is then restored because no high-value action is abandoned. Notably, this exploration arises even though agents are myopic and never choose a myopically suboptimal action. A key insight of our analysis is that noise alone does not deliver asymptotic efficiency: the restriction that the high-state noise be of smaller order than the drift is essential. Noisy bad news , in which conclusive evidence that an action is low value arrives only rarely, is a canonical example: conditional on a high-value action, the upward drift in beliefs dominates the signal variance, and posterior beliefs deterministically rise in small steps. By contrast, whenever the signal variance remains of the same order as the drift, there always remains a possibility for beliefs to diverge from the balanced learning path, which bounds the efficiency probability away from 1. In product markets, Theorem 1 implies that approximately efficient limit outcomes re-quire that society learn from reviews slowly, and in a specific way. By contrast, in single-product environments faster learning from reviews always improves welfare (Acemoglu et al., 2022). In technology adoption, increasingly rare ‘breakdowns’ (Keller and Rady, 2015) are a canonical sufficient route to balanced social learning, whereas Proposition 1 shows that perfect-good-news ‘breakthrough’ experiments (Keller et al., 2005), in the sense of Defini-3tion 4, generate maximal imbalance. We briefly discuss the technical contribution of Theorem 1. In our setting, beliefs evolve as a multidimensional stochastic process with two sources of randomness: (i) the realization of idiosyncratic preferences among arriving agents; and (ii) the realization of signals about action values. Both sources of randomness are important features of the economic environ-ments we study, but together they make the evolution of beliefs difficult to analyze, because past beliefs determine which action is sampled and hence which belief is updated. We show that, for sufficiently smooth sequences of experiments in the noisy information limit, the law of the suitably rescaled belief process converges weakly to the law of the solution to a stochastic differential equation. 5 A separate argument establishing the arrival of rare signals handles the remaining “non-smooth” sequences. Together, the two arguments yield the tight conditions for asymptotic efficiency in Theorem 1. 

How quickly do efficiency gains obtain as learning slows? Theorem 1 gives tight conditions under which the efficiency probability approaches 1, but it requires signals to become arbitrarily noisy. We show (Theorem 2) that, when perfect bad news arrives with probability p, the efficiency probability under path balance converges to 1 at least exponen-tially fast as p → 0. We establish this by bounding the probability that the belief process escapes from the balanced learning path. The exponential bound suggests that, in practice, perfect bad news signals may not need to be very noisy: moderate noise can yield a high efficiency probability without sacrificing much speed. 

What is the value of design? Can a designer approximately obtain efficiency under less restrictive conditions than those of Theorem 1 by controlling the flow of information from past to future? Theorem 3 shows that adaptive evidence policies approximately achieve efficiency if and only if the prior satisfies static balance , which merely requires that both actions are chosen with positive probability under the prior. This is depicted by the green region in Figure 1. Approximately efficient adaptive evidence policies take the following two-stage form. The designer first commits to silence for a long deterministic accumulation period, during which both actions continue to be sampled. She then discloses the accumulated evidence and thereafter fully discloses all future evidence. When static balance fails, Theorem 4 characterizes the second-best value of all adaptive information policies. The key insight is that Bayes plausibility bounds the probability of lowering the belief about the favored action into the balanced region. A simple two-message garbling of a sufficiently informative designer posterior approximates the bound. Finally,

1.1 Related Literature. Our work contributes to the literatures on (i) learning from ban-dits; (ii) social learning from others’ outcomes; and (iii) dynamic or adaptive information design. We discuss each in turn. 

Myopic and contextual bandits. We contribute to a broad literature on multi-agent learning from bandits (in our model, actions with uncertain values). Our model shares with this literature the property that information externalities may cause insufficient exploration. 6

Within this literature, our paper relates most closely to myopic bandits (since our decision makers are short-run) and to contextual bandits, in which dispersion in horizontal preferences plays the role of “contexts.” One strand of this literature (Kannan et al. (2018), Bastani, Bayati, and Khosravi (2021), Raghavan et al. (2023), Slivkins, Xu, and Zuo (2025)) provides conditions under which myopic maximization (a so-called “greedy” algorithm) guarantees efficient learning outcomes in a contextual bandit setting. 7 Many of these papers fix the signal structure and study conditions which ensure that contexts are sufficiently diverse. 8

By contrast, we impose an upper bound on context diversity and provide a tight character-ization of the signal structures for which myopic subjective expected utility maximization (the Bayesian “greedy” algorithm) achieves approximate long-run efficiency. 9 Our results therefore complement the insight that sufficiently diverse contexts can induce efficient out-comes under greedy behavior: sufficiently noisy and asymmetric information can do so as well.

Social learning from others’ outcomes. A closely related paper is Acemoglu, Makhdoumi, Malekian, and Ozdaglar (2022) (henceforth, AMMO), who study learning about the quality of a monopolist’s product. In AMMO’s single-product environment, learning always suc-ceeds under their maintained conditions, and their focus is on how the ‘selection effect’ 10 

and the granularity of reviews affect the speed of learning. In our multi-action case, agents always choose some action, but this does not imply learning about all actions. Our focus is therefore on the composition of the actions that are chosen and learned about. We show that, in contrast to AMMO, fast learning can exacerbate imbalance and balanced learning can be restored by slowing learning down in a specific way. 11 

Several other papers model social learning from others’ outcomes in environments with some degree of competition or product selection (Vaccari, Scarsini, and Maglaras, 2016; Wolitzky, 2018; Vellodi, 2018; Kakhbod, Lanzani, and Xing, 2021; Banihashem, Hajiaghayi, Shin, and Slivkins, 2023). 12 Our full-transparency regime is closest to the environments in these papers. The main difference is our focus on horizontal preference dispersion, which is absent in these papers: we study how this dispersion interacts with the experiment to shape multidimensional limit learning outcomes. 13

learning environment (henceforth environment ) is a tuple ( σ, ϕ, μ 0, ν ) consisting of (i) the information policy σ; (ii) the experiment ϕ about values, which is common across actions; (iii) the prior μ0; and (iv) the distribution ν of idiosyncratic preferences. 4 THE VALUE OF DESIGN 

Theorem 1 gives tight conditions under which the efficiency probability approaches one under full transparency. We now ask whether—and if so, by how much—a designer who controls the information policy can do better. In product markets, the designer is a platform that designs a review system, e.g., by coarsening or censoring reviews to encourage balanced exploration and thereby cater to idiosyncratic tastes (e.g., for color or shape). In technology adoption, the designer is a government that decides how to disclose information about the efficacy of new technologies. 

When is efficiency attainable under design? We begin by stating a tight condition un-der which adaptive evidence design and adaptive information design can bring the efficiency probability arbitrarily close to one.

5 CONCLUSION 

When preferences are heterogeneous, efficient social learning requires balance such that every high-value action is discovered. This paper studies how the informational and preference environment affects the possibility of obtaining efficient outcomes. Under full transparency, we characterize when the efficiency probability can be made approximately one in terms of the noise and drift of experiments (Theorem 1). We then turn our attention to perfect good news and perfect bad news , documenting that the former lead to extreme cases of learning failure regardless of noise (Proposition 1), while the latter yield efficient outcomes at an 

exponential rate in the noise parameter (Theorem 2). Together, these results shed light on the key dimensions of the speed-efficiency tradeoff displayed in Figure 6. Next, we study the value of design in our setting. We find that efficiency can be achieved by adaptive evidence design (and more generally, adaptive information design) if and only if the prior is sufficiently balanced (Theorem 3). Moreover, when the prior is imbalanced, we characterize the second-best adaptive-information value and show that adaptive evidence approaches it uniformly as the impact of individual signals vanishes (Theorem 4 and The-orem 4B). We conclude with a few remarks on the broader economic implications of our findings. 

1. Fast vs slow learning. In environments with single-dimensional uncertainty (e.g., a single product or technology is either good or bad), a common prescription is to speed 

> 33 Analogous diffusion limits have been studied in single-agent settings (Liang et al., 2021).

29 up social learning. For instance, Acemoglu et al. (2022) show how finer reviews in online marketplaces can facilitate quicker learning about product quality. More recent work by Knoepfle and Salmi (2024) and Board and Meyer-ter Vehn (2025) reinforces this view, show-ing that a designer would want to be fully transparent about bad news, disclosing it as soon as it arrives. We offer a complementary view of how and why a designer might instead want to slow learning down: slow learning balances exploration across actions even though each agent is myopic and does not internalize the learning externality on future agents. However, slow learning may also be costly: in the interim, agents risk taking a low-value action. We make three remarks about speed. First, if the designer is patient, the efficiency probability is what matters for welfare. Second, even if the designer is impatient, Theorem 2 suggests that the experiment may not need to be very noisy in practice: under binary bad news, the efficiency probability converges to 1 at an exponential rate in the noise. A key takeaway is therefore that not all garblings are created equal, and slowing learning down in the right way can improve welfare even away from the patient limit. Third, how a designer should resolve the tradeoff between speed and balance remains an open question. The tools we use to show convergence of the belief process to the solution of a stochastic differential equation apply only in the noisy-experiment limit. Understanding how often efficient outcomes arise away from this limit is an exciting question for future work. 

2. Coarsening in theory and practice. In practice, the statistical experiment gen-erated by an action is often fixed rather than a design choice. For instance, the probability that a new technology generates breakthroughs and/or breakdowns is technology-specific. Similarly, the informativeness of a consumer’s experience is specific to the product in ques-tion. Theorem 3 offers a simple recipe for restoring balanced learning in such settings. Yet the associated policy is stark: it prescribes potentially long periods in which agents receive no information and public beliefs do not move. Such a policy might be undesirable for a de-signer (e.g., an e-commerce platform) who is patient and cares about long-run efficiency but, conditional on that, wishes to minimize the probability that low-value actions are chosen in the interim, i.e., at finite times. Theorem 1 instead points to coarsenings that approximate noisy bad news. Along the sequences covered by the theorem, these coarsenings permit interim learning while the efficiency probability approaches one. In product markets, where the exogenous experiment is typically not conclusive about quality (but might still be quite informative), one such coarsening is a simple upper-censorship 

policy, in which favorable signals above a threshold are pooled while lower-tail signals are dis-closed. 34 This policy might be operationalized by (i) offering consumers an option to report 

> 34 Such disclosure policies have been extensively studied in the literature on static persuasion. The reason they are useful here is distinct.

30 fraudulent products, which might then be taken off the platform—these reports correspond to the ‘bad news’ signals; and (ii) coarsening positive reviews. 35 In technology adoption, where the exogenous experiment might be conclusive ‘good news’ (breakthroughs) or ‘bad news’ (breakdowns), one such coarsening is to disclose bad news stochastically (thereby slowing down the Poisson arrival rate) and never to disclose good news.

B PROOF OF THEOREM 1 Outline. The proof of Theorem 1 is organized into the following blocks. 

• Housekeeping. Section B.1 verifies existence and uniqueness for the path balance ODE in the main text. Section B.2 verifies that the stochastic differential equation (SDE) has a unique solution and that the associated martingale problem is well posed. 

• Weak convergence. Section B.3 shows weak convergence in Skorokhod space to the solution of (SDE) when KL n → 0 and the stochastic smoothness condition holds. The condition holds automatically when drift dominates noise ( Var n/KL n → 0). 

• ‘If’ direction of Theorem 1. Section B.4 combines convergence to the path-balance ODE with the maximal inequality of Lemma 7. 

• ‘Only if ’ direction of Theorem 1. Section B.5 considers three cases. First, if KL n → 0and Var n/KL n → 0 but path balance fails, the ODE limit enters a herding region. Second, if KL n̸ → 0, finitely many signals reach the herding region. Third, if KL n → 0 but 

Var n/KL n̸ → 0, the stochastically smooth subcase has a diffusion limit with C > 0, while the nonsmooth subcase is handled by a direct finite-n rare-signal construction.
