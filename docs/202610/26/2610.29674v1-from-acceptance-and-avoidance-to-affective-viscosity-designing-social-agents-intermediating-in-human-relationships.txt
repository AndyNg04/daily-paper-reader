# From Acceptance and Avoidance to Affective Viscosity: Designing Social Agents Intermediating in Human Relationships 

## Tomoko Yonezawa 

Kansai University Takatsuki, Osaka, Japan yone@kansai-u.ac.jp 

Abstract 

As social agents become embedded in human relationships, they are expected not only to provide smooth assistance but also to han-dle rejection, discomfort, jealousy, persistence, and other forms of social tension. This paper connects two research directions: accep-tance and avoidance in response to an agent’s gaze, approach, and contact, and affective viscosity as a model of how negative affect accumulates, persists, is suppressed, and is expressed over time. We argue that avoidance should not be treated merely as a failure of interaction, but as a relational signal that indicates boundaries be-tween humans and agents. Building on this view, affective viscosity provides a design perspective for agents that maintain, suppress, or express negative states depending on social relationships and interaction histories. We discuss several ongoing design cases, in-cluding triadic jealousy, discomfort suppression, patience timing, and spatiotemporal stickiness, and consider how these cases lead to broader questions of blame and accountable intervention. Rather than designing agents as simple scapegoats or artificial villains, we ask how agents can intermediate in problematic social situations while making their intervention traceable, limited, contestable, and repairable. 

Keywords 

Human-Agent Interaction; Social Agents; Acceptance and Avoid-ance; Affective Viscosity; Trust and Blame; Social Boundaries; Tri-adic Relations 

1 Introduction 

Social agents are often designed to be polite, helpful, and smoothly cooperative. However, agents embedded in human relationships may need to do more than maintain pleasant interaction. They may be approached or avoided, accepted or rejected, welcomed or perceived as intrusive. They may also need to express hesita-tion, discomfort, jealousy, or limits of patience when social tension arises. These reactions should not be treated only as breakdowns of interaction. They may indicate relational boundaries and provide important cues for how agents should intervene in social environ-ments. Our previous and ongoing work on acceptance and avoidance focuses on how users respond to an agent’s gaze, approach, and contact, and how such responses can be estimated and reflected in the agent’s behavior. This perspective treats avoidance not simply as a negative outcome, but as a signal that the agent has entered a boundary-sensitive region of interaction. The next step is to model how such boundary-sensitive states persist and transform over time. In particular, discomfort, jealousy, annoyance, and reluctance may accumulate, remain latent, leak through behavior, or suddenly become explicit. We refer to this temporal and relational property as affective viscosity. This perspective also leads to questions of blame and responsi-bility. Once an agent detects social tension and intervenes, it may change the trajectory of the relationship. If the intervention fails, becomes annoying, or exposes a tension that participants preferred to ignore, the agent may become a target of blame. However, assign-ing blame to an agent should not simply mean placing an artificial scapegoat or villain in the environment. A more productive ques-tion is how to design agents whose interventions are traceable, limited, contestable, and repairable. In this sense, affective viscos-ity is not only a model of negative emotion, but also a basis for discussing how agents can take part in socially risky intervention. Prior work on trust in automation and human–robot interaction has emphasized that trust is not simply a positive attitude, but a basis for appropriate reliance under uncertainty [3, 4]. Reviews of trust in AI and human–automation trust further suggest that trust depends on both system properties and the user’s interpretation of agency, reliability, and risk [6, 7]. However, when agents are embedded in social relationships, the issue is not limited to whether users rely on them. Agents may also affect interpersonal boundaries, social norms, and the distribution of responsibility. In socially embedded HAI, trust, blame, and responsibility may be distributed across users, agents, developers, institutions, bystanders, and broader social contexts. This makes it necessary to consider not only whether an agent is trusted, but also how the agent is treated as a social participant when it refuses, avoids, asserts a boundary, or becomes involved in socially disruptive interaction. 

2 Acceptance and Avoidance as Relational Signals 

Human acceptance and avoidance do not emerge as instantaneous binary reactions. They are grounded in temporally developing cog-nitive, affective, and bodily states. In human interaction, discom-fort toward another person’s approach, hesitation before rejection, or the decision to continue a relationship often involves gradual changes in attention, arousal, memory, expectation, and interpreta-tion. Such changes take time. Therefore, if a social agent expresses acceptance, avoidance, discomfort, or rejection as an immediate symbolic output, the expression may appear behaviorally conve-nient but psychologically unnatural. This view is consistent with process-oriented theories of emotion. Scherer’s component process model treats emotion as a dynamic process of sequential appraisal checks, rather than as a single instan-taneous output [8]. Gross’s process model of emotion regulation also distinguishes multiple points at which emotion can be modu-lated, including attention, cognition, and response expression [9]. arXiv:2610.29674v1 [cs.RO] 21 Oct 2026 Tomoko Yonezawa 

This point is important for human–agent interaction. An agent that is always accepting may appear polite, but it lacks a believ-able boundary. Conversely, an agent that immediately rejects or displays anger may break the interaction before a relationship can be adjusted. A more natural design requires an internal process in which negative affect accumulates, is temporarily suppressed, leaks through external behavior, and is eventually expressed depending on the situation. The external expression should not be identical to the internal state. Rather, the gap between the internal state and the displayed behavior is itself a social signal. It allows an agent to have something like “honne” and “tatemae”: an internal state that may differ from its outward expression, while still allowing the agent to express rejection or avoidance according to the relationship and the situation. This paper extends our recent discussion of negative aversion and real reliability, where discomfort and rejective reactions were framed as legible self-disclosure for boundary learning and rela-tional repair [2]. Here, we shift the focus from familiar reliability to trust and blame in socially risky intermediation. 

3 From Avoidance to Affective Viscosity 

In this paper, affective viscosity refers to the tendency of negative affect to accumulate and persist over time, characterized by its ac-cumulation, retention, and decay. These dynamics are influenced by the suppression and expression of negative affect, and are par-ticularly relevant to persistent negative emotions such as jealousy. Affective viscosity is therefore not simply a delay before respond-ing. An agent may retain discomfort while displaying restraint, respond differently to the same event after repeated encounters, or express an accumulated state abruptly after a tolerance threshold is exceeded. The distinction between internal state and external expression is central: changes in one need not be immediately mir-rored in the other. Here, affective viscosity is a design concept organizing these processes, rather than a single empirically validated measure. Ac-ceptance and avoidance are also social effects. They do not only represent whether the agent likes or dislikes the current user input. They regulate distance, timing, intimacy, and the distribution of attention among participants. When an agent accepts an approach, withdraws from it, tolerates it, or expresses discomfort toward it, the agent is not merely displaying an internal state; it is shaping the relationship. This relational view is grounded in classic work on interper-sonal distance and relationship development. Hall’s proxemics treats distance as a socially meaningful organization of interper-sonal space [10], while social penetration theory describes relation-ship development as a gradual process of increasing interpersonal depth [11]. Expectancy violations theory further suggests that de-viations from expected interpersonal behavior can be evaluated positively or negatively depending on the relationship and con-text [12]. This view extends the issue from dyadic approach interaction to broader social relationships. In approach-based interaction, the design question is whether an agent should accept the user’s ap-proach, endure it for a while, reject it, or remain involved despite annoyance. For example, an agent may display jealousy or possessiveness not as a simple negative emotion, but as an expression of the social relationship it tries to secure. The agent’s negative state may remain because the relationship remains unresolved. Its avoidance may express a boundary, while its persistence may express attachment. This perspective prepares the ground for discussing social agents that in-termediate in human relationships, including the difficult question of whether such agents can accept blame when their involvement changes the social situation. 

4 Design Cases from Our Recent Studies 

Building on our recent synthesis of negative aversion and real re-liability [2], this section uses four recent HAI poster studies as concrete design cases. Table 1 summarizes how these cases con-nect acceptance, avoidance, discomfort, stickiness, and jealousy to internal processes and design implications. The following stud-ies provide separate evaluations of particular behaviors and user impressions. They are used here as empirical design cases, rather than as a combined validation of affective viscosity or of an agent’s capacity to bear responsibility. 

4.1 Acceptance and Avoidance in Boundary-Sensitive Interaction 

This subsection follows our earlier prototype of approach-triggered avoidance behavior, in which a robot accumulated and decayed “dis-like” according to a user’s physical approach, displayed endurance below a tolerance threshold, and performed avoidance when that threshold was exceeded [1]. Our three recent studies examine the expression and perception of acceptance, avoidance, and discomfort in boundary-sensitive human–agent interaction. 

Patience and suppression. Fujino et al. [13] proposed an emotion-suppressing agent that responds to personal-space intrusion. In their model, the agent’s internal stress increases as the user ap-proaches and decreases as the user moves away, and the agent tran-sitions among neutral, “patience”, and anger states. Here, “patience” From Acceptance and Avoidance to Affective Viscosity: Designing Social Agents Intermediating in Human Relationships 

is not a simple positive expression, but a temporary suppression of unpleasant emotion before anger. The evaluation comprised two online studies with 24 participants each, using 0–99 visual analogue scales. The first used static images to select a facial expression per-ceived as “patience”. The second used prerecorded approach videos to evaluate impressions of the agent, including emotion suppression, likability, and willingness to continue interacting. Their evaluation showed that displaying “patience” before anger increased perceived emotion suppression, willingness to continue interacting, and likability. This case suggests that rejection or avoid-ance can be staged through a gap between the internal state and the external expression. 

Patience in utterance. Sunasaka et al. [14] extended this issue to voice interaction. Their system estimates a Discomfort Index from inappropriate user utterances and accumulates it with an emotional decay factor. The agent then changes its vocal tone according to the accumulated state. The pilot evaluation involved 24 partici-pants who listened to prerecorded, scripted audio dialogues while viewing text transcripts. Ratings on 0–99 visual analogue scales included approachability, trustworthiness, perceived agent discom-fort, emotionality, contemplation before speaking, and willingness to interact. Thus, the evaluation examined responses to controlled dialogue stimuli rather than live adaptation by the proposed system. Their pilot evaluation suggests that the policy of expression is more important than the acoustic transition pattern alone: a delayed escalation policy, in which the agent first gives a neutral warning and expresses discomfort only after repeated inappropriate utter-ances, was evaluated more positively than immediate boundary setting. This case shows that avoidance and discomfort expression should be designed as temporally justified responses rather than as immediate negative outputs. Because the escalation policies also differed in the number of conversational turns, the results do not isolate the effect of delay alone. 

Acceptance of approach and continued presence. Hachiken et al. [15] examined a related boundary problem through spatial and temporal involvement. Although the study uses the term spatiotem-poral stickiness, its current experimental contribution is best un-derstood as an evaluation of how users accept or reject an agent’s approach and continued presence. Their within-subject robot interaction study involved 24 par-ticipants and manipulated approach distance (30 or 50 cm), stay duration (20 or 40 s), and relational framing (stranger, acquaintance, or close friend). Seven-point ratings assessed friendliness, favorabil-ity, discomfort, annoyance, enjoyment, and continued interaction intent. Relationships were introduced through instructions for each trial rather than developed through long-term interaction. The results showed that excessive proximity increased discom-fort and annoyance, whereas in close relationships, brief and distant contact could also be evaluated negatively. This case suggests that acceptance is not determined only by whether the agent approaches the user, but also by how long it remains, how close it comes, and what relationship is assumed. 

4.2 Affective Viscosity in Social Relationships 

Obata et al. [16] provide a case in which negative affect is generated from a social relationship rather than from a dyadic boundary violation alone. Their VR agent extends Heider’s POX model of balance theory into a distance-based continuous model among the agent, a third party, and the object of affection [17]. The agent independently accumulates two discomfort values: jealousy, caused by the third party’s presence, and avoidance, caused by being too close to the object. When the accumulated value exceeds a threshold, the agent performs a resolution behavior to restore the desired distance. This case is important because negative affect is not treated as a momentary reaction to a user’s direct behavior. Instead, it emerges from the discrepancy between the agent’s ideal and actual social configuration. The within-subject VR study involved 25 participants and com-pared a two-party model with two three-party models differing in the agent’s desired relative distance [16]. Seven-point questionnaire ratings assessed competitiveness, familiarity, attachment, human-likeness, and jealousy-related emotions, supplemented by free de-scriptions. The evaluation showed that the three-party model, especially the condition in which the agent wanted to be closer to the object than the third party, increased impressions related to competitiveness, possessiveness, familiarity, and attachment. Thus, jealousy can be understood not only as a negative emotional display, but also as an expression of the relationship that the agent attempts to secure. In this sense, the jealousy model provides a core example of affec-tive viscosity in social relationships. The negative state is generated by relational imbalance, accumulates over time, and is released through behavior that changes the social configuration. This ex-tends the design problem from whether an agent should accept or avoid a user’s approach to how an agent maintains, protects, or modifies a relationship involving multiple social actors. Figure 1 illustrates the triadic situation used by Obata et al. The agent accumulates jealousy when a third party approaches the user and then performs a resolution behavior by moving closer to the user. This figure is used here as a concrete example of how negative affect can be generated from a social configuration rather than from a dyadic boundary violation alone. 

5 Discussion 

The cases discussed above suggest that social agents do not merely respond to users; they intermediate in relationships. When an agent expresses patience, discomfort, or jealousy, or behaves in ways that users find annoying, it may clarify a boundary, preserve a relationship, or reveal a social tension that had remained implicit. Such behavior may improve the social environment, but it may also make the agent disliked or blamed. The issue of blame is important here because blame is not a simple emotional reaction after failure, but a structured judgment involving causality, agency, intentionality, and obligation [5]. In socially embedded HAI, such judgments may become distributed or fragmented across users, agents, developers, institutions, and surrounding social contexts. Acceptance and avoidance first appear in boundary-sensitive interaction, such as personal space, utterances, and approach. These responses then require in-ternal accumulation, suppression, and delayed expression, which we describe as affective viscosity. Finally, affective viscosity leads to social relationship regulation, including triadic jealousy, persis-tence, and blame. Although “honne” and “tatemae” offer one way to describe the gap between internal states and outward expression, the interpreta-tion of such expressions is culturally situated. Suppressed discom-fort or delayed rejection may be perceived as considerate restraint or as passive aggression, while jealousy may suggest attachment or unwanted intrusion. The social consequences of emotion suppres-sion can differ across cultural contexts [18]. Our cases do not test these differences. Future work should examine how interpersonal distance, communication norms, and relational expectations shape acceptance and blame attribution. 

6 Conclusion 

This paper connected four recent studies on personal-space in-trusion, vocal discomfort, spatiotemporal stickiness, and triadic jealousy to the broader question of how social agents intermediate in human relationships. We argued that acceptance and avoidance should not be treated as immediate binary outputs, but as tem-porally developing internal states and social effects. Through the notion of affective viscosity, negative states such as discomfort, re-luctance, annoyance, and jealousy can be understood as states that accumulate, are suppressed, leak through behavior, and become expressed according to relationship and situation. The main question raised by this paper is how agents can bear re-sponsibility for such involvement. When agents express boundaries or become involved in social tension, they may support relationship regulation, but they may also become targets of dislike or blame. Future work should therefore examine how agents can intermedi-ate in socially risky relationships while making their involvement traceable, limited, contestable, and repairable.
