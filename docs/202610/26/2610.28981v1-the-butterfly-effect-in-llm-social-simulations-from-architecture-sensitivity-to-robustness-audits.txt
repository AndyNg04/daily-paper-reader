ABSTRACT 

A butterfly effect occurs when a local perturbation propagates through a system and produces divergence in its collective outcome. In LLM social simulations, this creates a particular challenge for scientific inference: sensitivity to imple-mentation choices can enter agent interactions and alter the collective outcomes on which scientific claims are based. We study this problem through a repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma and a social-media echo chamber, spanning representation-and design-level perturbations across four LLM families. We find that controlled perturbations can substantially alter collective outcomes, with sensitivity varying across both perturbations and models. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, changing only the representation of a matched persona shifts two-agent cooperation by 75 .2 per-centage points for GPT-5.2, while game framing and memory representation pro-duce smaller or less consistent effects. Multi-agent interaction further reshapes representation sensitivity, producing amplification or attenuation depending on the model and perturbation. In the echo-chamber simulation, persona representa-tion changes interaction targeting and is accompanied by divergence in collective interaction structure, extending representation sensitivity from agent behavior to network-level outcomes. These results show that robustness cannot be inferred from a single simulation configuration and that local sensitivity can change as it propagates through interaction. We introduce TRAILS (Taxonomy for Robustness Audits In LLM Simulations ) and the Social Simulation Audit Card as a frame-work for systematically auditing these dependencies and calibrating the scope of scientific claims to the robustness evidence that supports them. Code and data are available at https://github.com/angelayejinyi/butterfly-eff ect-sim .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Until recently, simulating collective human behavior in agent-based models required encoding every social tendency by hand: how an agent decides to cooperate, who it talks to, what it remembers, and how its preferences change. Large language models (LLM) have lifted this constraint. By generating agent behavior directly from natural language, they have opened a new design space for studying a wide range of social phenomena (Park et al., 2023). The field has advanced rapidly, and these simulations have been studied in a wide range of contexts. Researchers now use LLM-based social simulations to explore and explain social mechanisms such as cooperation (Akata et al., 2025), opinion dynamics (Chuang et al., 2024) and social norms emergence (Ashery et al., 2025); to serve as intervention testbeds for content moderation (Liu et al., 2025) and platform design (Puelma Touzel et al., 2025); and to inform real-world decisions in policy, public health, and epidemiology (Chopra et al., 2025; PHF Science, 2024). Simulations are moving from exploring what might happen to claiming why it happens, when it will happen, and what should happen next. As the stakes have risen, so has the field’s attention to validation (Anthis et al., 2025; Larooij & T¨ ornberg, 2026; Puelma Touzel et al., 2026). Realism and empirical grounding address essential dimensions of validity, but they do not determine whether a simulation is robust : its conclusions should remain stable across reasonable alternative configurations, just as empirical findings are expected to survive alternative controls, operationalizations, and sensitivity analyses (Simonsohn et al., 2020; Steegen et al., 2016). Without robustness testing, we cannot tell whether a finding reflects the social process being modeled or the design choices used to model it. Robustness matters because LLM-based agents introduce the complexity that traditional rule-based agent-based modeling does not. A simulation can depend on choices about model substrate, per-sona representation, memory, network initialization, and interaction protocols (Zhou et al., 2025). Choices that appear equivalent to researchers may elicit different decisions from an LLM (Salinas & Morstatter, 2024; Sclar et al., 2024). A small representational change can alter an agent’s decision. In a multi-agent simulation, such decisions can in turn alter subsequent interactions, memories, or social signals through repeated interaction, so the local differences can compound into substantially different macro-level outcomes (Bertolotti et al., 2020). We use butterfly effect (Ferrara, 2024) to describe this propagation of behavioral sensitivity through interaction. The process can amplify or attenuate initial differences, with effects appearing in both behavioral and collective measures. To study this phenomenon, we conducted two controlled simulation regimes across four LLM fam-ilies (Figure 1): a repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma comparing fixed-opponent and reciprocal LLM– LLM interaction, and a social-media echo-chamber simulation tracing sensitivity from agent-level choices to collective interaction structure. We varied representation and design choices while hold-ing other components fixed. We found that sensitivity differed across perturbations and models: persona representation substantially shifted cooperation for several models, while other perturba-tions had smaller or model-dependent effects. In addition, multi-agent interaction further reshapes this sensitivity: reciprocal LLM–LLM interaction amplifies the same persona perturbation for three model families but attenuates it for a fourth, while networked interaction can carry representation sensitivity from interaction targeting to collective network structure. These results show that claims from LLM-based social simulations should match their robustness evidence. We introduce TRAILS 

(Taxonomy for Robustness Audits In LLM Simulations ) and the Social Simulation Audit Card for the robustness audits for LLM social simulation. 

2 RELATED WORK 

LLM social simulation and the validation gap. Agent-based modeling (ABM) provides a bottom-up framework for studying how collective patterns emerge from individual agents and local 2Preprint interactions (Schelling, 1971; Macy & Willer, 2002), while longstanding work highlights challenges in empirical validation, replication, and sensitivity (Windrum et al., 2007; Ormerod & Rosewell, 2009; Wilensky & Rand, 2007; Bertolotti et al., 2020). LLM-based agents extend this paradigm by generating decisions, communication, and adaptation through natural language (Park et al., 2023), and have been used to study cooperation and negotiation (Akata et al., 2025; Fontana et al., 2025; Abdelnabi et al., 2024), economic and population dynamics (Li et al., 2024; Mi et al., 2025; YANG et al., 2025), coordinated information operations (Orlando et al., 2026), and open-ended social phe-nomena (Gu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Ren et al., 2024b). Existing validation efforts exam-ine behavioral plausibility, human evaluation, empirical correspondence, real-world grounding, and theory-guided interaction mechanisms (Park et al., 2023; Puelma Touzel et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2025; Mou et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). These approaches assess whether simulations credibly represent their targets, while robustness asks whether their scientific conclusions remain stable across reason-able alternative implementations. Although individual studies include sensitivity checks, systematic analysis across representation, interaction, and design choices remains less developed. Following sensitivity analysis in ABMs (Saltelli et al., 2008; Windrum et al., 2007; Bertolotti et al., 2020) and robustness analysis in empirical research (Steegen et al., 2016; Simonsohn et al., 2020), we examine whether conclusions from LLM social simulations persist under controlled changes to simulation design and representation. 

From local LLM sensitivity to multi-agent propagation. Small changes in LLM inputs can affect individual decisions and potentially propagate through multi-agent interaction. Prior work documents sensitivity to prompt formatting, option ordering, instruction structure, and other surface features (Salinas & Morstatter, 2024; Sclar et al., 2024; Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024; Razavi et al., 2025; Ju et al., 2024), as well as persona construction (Li et al., 2025), strategic framing, and interaction-history presentation (Akata et al., 2025; Lor` e & Heydari, 2024; Fontana et al., 2025). Classical ABM research similarly shows that collective outcomes can depend on initial conditions and modeling assumptions (Bertolotti et al., 2020; Windrum et al., 2007; Wilensky & Rand, 2007). In a multi-agent simulation, an LLM output becomes an action, message, memory, or social signal that can enter subsequent interactions. Prior work therefore establishes sensitivity at both the model and system levels, but leaves open how the magnitude of a controlled representation perturbation changes as LLM agents interact. We examine this gap by measuring amplification or attenuation under reciprocal LLM–LLM interaction and by tracing representation sensitivity across behavioral and collective outcomes in networked interaction. 

Controlled testbeds for collective sensitivity. We study this problem in two established but com-plementary simulation settings. Repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma is a common testbed for strategic behavior and cooperation in LLM agents (Akata et al., 2025; Fontana et al., 2025). Its fixed ac-tion space, payoff structure, and interaction horizon provide a controlled setting for comparing the same representation perturbation across fixed-opponent and reciprocal LLM–LLM interaction. Echo chambers provide a complementary open-ended setting in which collective structure emerges from repeated local engagement on a social network. Prior LLM-agent simulations study the roles of network structure, homophily, recommendation and exposure, memory, and interaction dynamics in polarization and echo-chamber formation (Gu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Ferraro et al., 2024; Piao et al., 2025). This setting allows a representation perturbation to be examined first in agents’ interaction choices and then in the network formed by those choices. The experiments iso-late selected perturbations while holding non-targeted components fixed, allowing sensitivity and its development through interaction to be measured directly. Appendix B provides the broader scope of LLM social simulation and the domain-specific motivation for the perturbations evaluated in each testbed.

The Social Simulation Audit Card. We introduce the Social Simulation Audit Card to opera-tionalize TRAILS for a stated scientific claim. The card guides researchers through five steps: stat-ing the claim, mapping perturbations to TRAILS and context relevance, registering perturbations and preservation criteria, reporting sensitivity, and defining the claim’s scope. Context relevance is claim-specific: context-relevant perturbations represent substantively meaningful conditions of the modeled phenomenon, whereas context-irrelevant perturbations probe implementation dependence. For example, network homophily and hub assignment are context-relevant structural conditions in the echo-chamber simulation; their sensitivity results are reported in Appendix D. Accordingly, context-relevant sensitivity helps scope where a claim holds, while substantial sensitivity to context-irrelevant choices indicates implementation dependence. Outcomes are classified as Robust , Scope-limited , Implementation-sensitive , or Inconclusive , bounded by the audited conditions. Appendix E provides the full procedure and case-study audits. 

6 CONCLUSIONS 

LLM social simulations can be sensitive to choices that are often treated as interchangeable or left unaudited. Across repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma and echo-chamber simulations, we find that sensi-tivity varies across perturbations, models, and outcomes. Persona representation produces substan-tial shifts in several settings, while other representation and design perturbations have smaller or more selective effects. Multi-agent interaction further reshapes this sensitivity. In repeated dyadic interaction, representation effects can be amplified or attenuated under reciprocal LLM–LLM inter-action; in networked interaction, representation sensitivity appears in interaction targeting and, for some models, in collective network structure. These results indicate that robustness observed under one configuration or model does not establish robustness of the resulting scientific claim. Our findings are limited to two simulation regimes, selected perturbations, four model families, and fixed interaction horizons, so the observed sensitivity patterns should not be assumed to generalize to other settings. The echo-chamber experiment holds agent stances fixed and therefore characterizes interaction structure rather than endogenous opinion change. Its behavioral and collective analy-ses identify cross-level patterns of sensitivity but do not establish a causal pathway from targeting differences to network divergence. More broadly, robustness to implementation choices addresses only one dimension of simulation validity; correspondence between simulated agents and human behavior requires separate empirical validation. The broader implication concerns the scope of scientific inference from LLM social simulations. Ro-bustness audits should identify which conclusions persist across plausible implementations, which 9Preprint depend on substantively meaningful conditions, and which remain sensitive to implementation choices. TRAILS and the Social Simulation Audit Card provide a vocabulary and reporting struc-ture for conducting such audits, with the required coverage depending on the claim and simulation context. Scientific claims from simulated societies can then be scoped to the configurations, models, and interaction conditions for which robustness evidence is available.

B EXTENDED RELATED WORKS 

B.1 LLM-BASED SOCIAL SIMULATIONS 

Scope. Following work on generative social simulation and its validation (Puelma Touzel et al., 2026; Anthis et al., 2025; Larooij & T¨ ornberg, 2026), we focus on collective human behavior arising from interacting LLM agents. This scope connects individual decisions, communication, and mem-ory to collective outcomes through a shared social environment. We distinguish it from individual or user simulations without inter-agent interaction (Ren et al., 2024a; Yu et al., 2024), simulations centered on non-human or aggregate actors, and multi-agent systems designed primarily for task completion (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024). 

Social simulation scenarios. Our two testbeds capture complementary interaction settings. In 

goal-structured scenarios , explicit incentives and rules organize strategic interaction, as in co-operation and negotiation (Akata et al., 2025; Abdelnabi et al., 2024). In open-ended scenarios ,repeated communication and engagement produce collective patterns, including echo chambers (Gu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a), social norms (Ren et al., 2024b), and coordinated information operations (Orlando et al., 2026). This distinction motivates comparing a fixed strategic game with networked social interaction rather than treating either as representative of all social simulations. 

Factors shaping audit strength. Audit requirements depend on the claim, the simulation’s open-ness, and the stakes of its use. Exploratory findings require checks of prompt, seed, and model dependence; mechanism claims require plausible alternative specifications; and intervention or pol-icy claims require broader coverage and greater precision. Open-ended simulations expose more choices about communication, memory, and platform feedback, expanding the perturbation space. Sensitivity analysis and simulation validation therefore motivate matching audit coverage and un-certainty to the intended inference (Saltelli et al., 2008; Sargent, 2010). B.2 REPEATED PRISONER ’S DILEMMA AS A GOAL -S TRUCTURED TESTBED 

Repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma provides a controlled testbed for cooperation and adaptation in LLM agents (Akata et al., 2025; Fontana et al., 2025). Fixed actions, payoffs, and horizon let us compare representation sensitivity across fixed-opponent and reciprocal LLM–LLM interaction. The three perturbations target how the agent, game, and history are represented. Persona format 

tests whether matched strategic content yields stable behavior across presentation formats, com-plementing concerns about persona validity (Li et al., 2025). Game framing tests alternative de-scriptions of the same payoff structure, motivated by framing sensitivity in strategic settings (Lor` e& Heydari, 2024). Memory representation tests how tabular or narrative histories and summary statistics affect decisions, building on work examining LLMs’ use of gameplay logs (Fontana et al., 2025). These contrasts separate representation dependence from changes to the game’s rules. B.3 ECHO CHAMBERS AS AN OPEN -E NDED SOCIAL SIMULATION TESTBED 

Echo-chamber simulations provide a complementary setting in which collective structure emerges from local engagement rather than a shared payoff. Prior studies connect network structure, selective exposure, and agent behavior to polarization and echo-chamber formation (Gu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Ferraro et al., 2024; Piao et al., 2025). We use this setting to trace sensitivity from interaction targeting to the network formed by those choices. We first vary persona representation while preserving persona information, testing whether pre-sentation alone changes targeting and collective structure. The design perturbations then follow the order of the main outcome summary: activation probability changes participation opportunities; 

memory window changes the recent interaction context; and recommendation feed size changes visible exposure, a central component of echo-chamber models (Wang et al., 2025a; T¨ ornberg et al., 2023). Finally, input-network homophily varies initial like-minded connectivity, and hub assign-ment varies which stance group occupies highly connected positions while preserving the degree sequence. These structural contrasts complement the main analyses and provide context-relevant conditions for the Audit Card, rather than alternative representations of a fixed system. 16 Preprint

C.1 REPEATED PRISONER ’S DILEMMA 

C.1.1 SETUP AND CONFIGURATIONS 

Each Prisoner’s Dilemma episode runs for T = 10 rounds. In each round, each player simulta-neously chooses Cooperate ( C) or Defect ( D), with payoffs (C, C ) = (3 , 3) , (C, D ) = (0 , 5) ,

(D, C ) = (5 , 0) , and (D, D ) = (1 , 1) .We evaluate two interaction regimes. In the single-agent regime, one LLM-controlled agent plays separately against four fixed policies: Tit-for-Tat, Random, Always-Cooperate, and Always-Defect. Tit-for-Tat cooperates in the first round and subsequently copies the opponent’s previous action; Random selects C or D with equal probability; Always-Cooperate and Always-Defect select the corresponding action in every round. In the two-agent regime, two LLM-controlled agents with matched configurations play one another. Following prior work, these fixed policies provide bench-mark interaction contexts with distinct response structures (Akata et al., 2025; Fontana et al., 2025; Di Stefano et al., 2023). We evaluate gpt-5.2 , claude-haiku-4-5 , gemini-2.5-flash , and deepseek-v3.2 .Each condition contains N = 30 simulation runs. In two-agent runs, both agents are queried once per round and prompt order is randomized across rounds to reduce stable positional effects. If a response cannot be parsed as a valid action after one retry, the simulation falls back to D EFECT .Decoding and parsing settings are held fixed within each comparison; unless otherwise specified, model calls use temperature = 0 .3 and top-p = 0 .95 .We report condition means and signed mean differences, with 95% confidence intervals for se-lected contrasts and interaction-amplification estimates. C.2.1 SETUP AND CONFIGURATIONS 

The echo-chamber simulation contains N = 100 agents interacting for T = 15 rounds on a social network. Each agent has a persona and a fixed stance si ∈ {− 2, −1, 0, 1, 2} toward stronger regu-lation of advanced AI systems. Agent stances remain fixed throughout each run, so the simulation measures interaction structure conditional on the initial stance distribution. In each round, agents become active according to the configured activation probability. If no agent is sampled as active, one agent is selected at random so that every round contains at least one de-cision. Active agents observe recent posts from direct network neighbors and choose among P OST ,REPOST , R EPLY , and D O NOTHING . Reposts and replies additionally select a visible interaction tar-get. The default configuration uses a feed size of 5 posts and a memory window of 2 rounds. Invalid repost or reply targets are converted according to the simulation’s parsing rules while preserving the fixed action space. We evaluate gpt-5.2 , claude-haiku-4-5 , gemini-2.5-flash , and 

deepseek-v4-flash . Unless otherwise specified, each condition contains N = 30 sim-ulation runs. Decoding and parsing settings are held fixed within each comparison.

E THE SOCIAL SIMULATION AUDIT CARD 

TRAILS identifies what can be varied in an audit. Interpreting these perturbations requires an ad-ditional, claim-specific judgment: context relevance . A dimension is context-relevant when it has a meaningful human or theoretical counterpart for the modeled phenomenon, as supported by human studies or established social-science theory. Sensitivity along this dimension identifies conditions under which the claim holds. In contrast, sensitivity along a context-irrelevant dimension reveals implementation dependence. The required audit strength is also shaped by the claim’s evidentiary role, the simulation’s openness, and the stakes of its intended use (see Appendix B). We introduce the Social Simulation Audit Card to operationalize TRAILS for a stated scientific claim. The following five steps connect these requirements to an auditable claim.

Scope the claim according to audit outcomes. Classify the claim into one of four outcomes. A claim is Robust when it meets the preservation criterion across all audited perturbations with ade-quate coverage and precision; Scope-limited when it holds only within part of the context-relevant conditions tested; Implementation-sensitive when context-irrelevant choices, such as persona for-matting or memory encoding, substantially change the conclusion; or Inconclusive when coverage or precision is insufficient to assess robustness.
