Abstract 

Unified streaming speech language models aim to support speech recognition and synthesis within a single model, enabling real-time hu-man–computer interaction. However, jointly supporting these two directions of speech–text conversion while preserving task-specific per-formance remains challenging. We propose Uni-Stream, a unified streaming speech lan-guage model built on compact pretrained speech models. Uni-Stream processes inter-leaved text and speech in a streaming format and achieves performance comparable to task-specific models through two-stage training. We also develop a data annotation pipeline for streaming training and will publicly release the generated transcriptions and word-level times-tamps. We further examine Uni-Stream’s rep-resentations across tasks and modalities and find that speech and text residence in distinct latent feature space even when trained in a unified model architecture, while representa-tions within each modality become more task-dependent in later layers. 

1 Introduction 

Unified streaming speech language models inte-grate automatic speech recognition (ASR) and text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis within a single model, facilitating real-time human–computer interaction and low-cost model deployment applications. How-ever, jointly training a model on both tasks may potentially degrade performance relative to task-specific models in performing ASR or TTS. A cen-tral challenge is therefore to combine recognition and synthesis in a streaming setting with low la-tency while retaining the capabilities of each task-specific model. Prior work has pursued unification and stream-ing largely separately. Unified speech language models perform recognition and synthesis with a shared backbone (Yan et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026), but most operate offline and require complete speech or text inputs. Streaming recognition and synthesis sys-tems process their input incrementally, either by interleaving text and speech in one sequence (Du et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2026) or by delaying one stream relative to the other (Défossez et al., 2024; Zeghidour et al., 2025), with a separate model trained for each task. Chen et al. (2021b) developed a unified streaming model for TTS and ASR based on a neural transducer, but their eval-uation was limited to single-speaker data. To our knowledge, no prior work has demonstrated both recognition and synthesis with streaming input us-ing a single shared decoder-only language-model backbone. In this work, we develop Uni-Stream, a unified streaming speech language model, through three design choices: streaming speech–text interleav-ing, pretrained backbone selection, and two-stage training. For speech–text interleaving, we compare fixed-ratio and time-aligned formats in TTS. Time-aligned interleaving yields lower TTS word error rates in our streaming adaptation experiments, mo-tivating its use in the unified model. To build on pre-trained capabilities for both ASR and TTS, we se-lect the backbone from the VibeVoice family (Peng et al., 2025, 2026), whose TTS and ASR variants use similar architectures, especially for the identi-cal speech tokenizer. We first adapt separate TTS and ASR models to our streaming formats, then initialize Uni-Stream from the adapted TTS model and jointly train it on both tasks. We formulate task-specific distillation objectives to preserve syn-thesis capabilities and facilitate recognition learn-ing, and examine their contributions through train-ing ablations. Our evaluation shows that the uni-fied model achieves performance comparable to the task-specific models on both TTS and ASR. To sup-port training in the time-aligned format, we develop a transcript voting and word-level time-alignment pipeline, retaining approximately 40,000 hours of 1arXiv:2610.32187v1 [eess.AS] 23 Oct 2026 Emilia-EN for training after filtering. We will re-lease voted transcriptions for Emilia-EN and word-level timestamps for Emilia-EN, GigaSpeech, and LibriSpeech to support reproducibility and further research. Beyond task performance, we ask how the uni-fied model represents speech and text. Does learn-ing both speech-to-text and text-to-speech map-pings bring their internal representations closer to-gether? To investigate this question, we probe rep-resentations across tasks, modalities, and layers. We find that speech and text representations remain distinct across layers despite joint training in both directions. Across tasks, representations are similar in early layers but diverge in later layers. Our contributions are threefold: • We develop a unified streaming speech lan-guage model for both ASR and TTS with a set of shared weights, achieving performance comparable to task-specific streaming models on both tasks. • We show through representation probing that speech and text remain distinct, while TTS and ASR representations are similar in lower layers and diverge in upper layers. • We transcribe and have time-aligned segmen-tation for 40,000 hours of English data, fa-cilitating the future time-aligned streaming modeling research. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Speech Language Models 

Language-model-based speech recognition systems generate transcripts from encoded speech (Ma et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2026a; Peng et al., 2026). Language-model-based speech synthesis systems generate speech conditioned on text (Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2025). Unified models perform both tasks with a single language model, operating on dis-crete speech tokens (Cai et al., 2026) or on contin-uous representations (Guan et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Balancing the two capabil-ities remains delicate: recognition and synthesis place competing demands on speech representa-tions (Yan et al., 2025), and starting the genera-tion module from a synthesis-trained checkpoint improves synthesis but can reduce recognition ac-curacy (Li et al., 2026). The results reported for these models, some with progressive output, do not establish that both tasks run on streaming input. Streaming means processing the input as it ar-rives, with bounded lookahead, not only emitting output progressively. Serial interleaving places text and speech in one sequence: recognition alternates audio chunks with incremental transcripts (Tu et al., 2026), and synthesis alternates text and speech at a fixed ratio (Du et al., 2024) or at word boundaries with text lookahead (Bai et al., 2025); our time-aligned format follows the latter. Delayed-stream models instead keep text and audio in parallel and use separately trained recognition and synthesis models with opposite delay directions (Défossez et al., 2024; Zeghidour et al., 2025). Full-duplex dialogue models share a backbone for listening and speaking, with an emphasis on conversational interaction (Défossez et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). An early transducer, Speech-T, was trained jointly for synthesis and recognition with stream-ing in both directions on a single-speaker corpus (Chen et al., 2021b). We study how to extend a pre-trained speech synthesis model to both tasks with streaming input while retaining the quality of our separately adapted streaming models. 

2.2 Speech–Text Representations in Speech Language Models 

Speech shares linguistic content with text while carrying additional information about prosody, speaker identity, and acoustic context. Under-standing how these shared and modality-specific at-tributes are represented is important to joint speech– text modeling. Evidence from multimodal lan-guage models suggests that non-textual inputs can acquire language-aligned representations in inter-mediate or later layers (Wu et al., 2025; Neo et al., 2025; Nikankin et al., 2026). However, studies of cross-model alignment caution that semantic overlap and functional compatibility do not nec-essarily imply a common representational struc-ture (Koepke et al., 2026; Smith et al., 2025). The relationship between cross-modal alignment and effective information use remains an open question. In speech language models, this question spans both tokenization and language modeling. Codec probing reveals that phonetic, lexical-semantic, and accent information are encoded unevenly across representations, highlighting the importance of tok-enizer design (Shi et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026). Yet retaining information does not ensure its effec-tive use: acoustic attributes can remain recoverable 2from LM hidden states even when the output head fails to express them in task predictions (Jo et al., 2026). These findings motivate examining both what speech representations preserve and how the LM accesses that information. 

3 Uni-Stream: Unified Streaming Speech Language Model 

3.1 Overview 

We propose Uni-Stream, a unified streaming speech language model that performs text-to-speech syn-thesis (TTS) and automatic speech recognition (ASR) with shared weights. To this end, we de-sign the model from three key aspects: streaming speech–text interleaving data format, pretrained backbone selection, and two-stage training. As shown in Figure 1, each input sequence be-gins with a task token specifying TTS or ASR, fol-lowed by interleaved text and speech chunks. We build on the VibeVoice model family (Peng et al., 2025, 2026; Tu et al., 2026) for its synthesis and recognition capabilities. Our two-stage training procedure first adapts separate TTS and ASR mod-els to our streaming formats, then initializes Uni-Stream from the adapted TTS model and jointly trains it on both tasks. We introduce synthesis anchoring and recognition distillation into unified training objectives and evaluate their contributions through ablations. 

3.2 Streaming Speech-Text Interleaving

For stream-ing TTS, we consider fixed-ratio interleaving (Du et al., 2024) and time-aligned interleaving (Bai et al., 2025). We use Si and Ti to denote speech and text chunks. 3.3 Unified Streaming Backbone

Specif-ically, both VibeVoice-TTS (Peng et al., 2025) and VibeVoice-ASR (Peng et al., 2026) use acoustic and semantic tokenizers that encode 24 kHz speech into 7.5 latent frames per second. Using these combined speech representations, VibeVoice-ASR autoregressively generates text dur-ing inference, while VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming (Tu et al., 2026) extends it to streaming recogni-tion. 3.4 Two-Stage Training 

Stage I adapts separate TTS and ASR models to the streaming formats. These specialists provide task-specific performance references and distillation tar-gets for Stage II, which jointly trains Uni-Stream on synthesis and recognition. 

Stage I: Task-specific Streaming Adaptation. 

We first fine-tune VibeVoice-TTS and VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming independently to adapt them to our streaming interleaving formats while limiting deviations from their pretrained capabilities. For TTS, we train two separate models for each inter-leaving format: fixed-ratio and time-aligned, using diffusion loss Ldiff on target speech latents and cross-entropy LTTS -CE at frame-continuation and speech-ending positions. For ASR, we compute the cross-entropy loss LASR -CE over transcript tokens, text-chunk terminators, and the end-of-sequence token.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Data preparation. We use the English subset of Emilia (Emilia-EN) (He et al., 2024) for both TTS and ASR, supplemented with GigaSpeech XL (Chen et al., 2021a) and LibriSpeech train-960 (Panayotov et al., 2015) for ASR. To con-struct streaming training sequences, our pipeline produces transcripts with word-level timestamps. For Emilia-EN, considering its transcription is gen-erated by WhisperX (Bain et al., 2023) and not necessary to be perfect, we combine the origi-nal transcripts with two ASR hypotheses through majority voting to refine the transcription, then merge word-boundary estimates from three align-ment sources. We filter Emilia-EN utterances by transcript agreement, alignment quality, and chunk-length constraints, retaining approximately 40k hours for training with development and test speak-ers held out. For GigaSpeech and LibriSpeech, we normalize the provided transcripts and obtain word timestamps with Qwen3-ForcedAligner (Shi et al., 2026a). Word-end times determine speech-segment boundaries for time-aligned TTS and text-emission chunks for streaming ASR. Appendix A details annotation, filtering, and sequence construc-tion. 

Training. Stage I adapts VibeVoice-1.5B TTS and VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming-1.5B to our stream-ing TTS and ASR formats, respectively, on a 10k hours of speech subset from the full training set. Stage II jointly trains TTS and ASR for 300k steps, using the full training set with 40k hours of Emilia for TTS and a mixture of 28k hours of Emilia, 10k hours of GigaSpeech, and two passes over the 960-hour LibriSpeech training set for ASR, approx-imately balancing the two tasks by speech duration. Both stages use AdamW with a peak learning rate of 10 −5 and cosine decay, keeping the speech tok-enizers frozen. 

Streaming settings. Following Section 3.2, ASR uses m = 4 speech frames per step with ℓ = 3 

lookahead frames. Time-aligned TTS uses n = 2 

words per step with ℓ = 2 lookahead words, while fixed-ratio TTS pairs n = 5 text tokens with m = 6 

speech frames, following VibeVoice’s streaming TTS implementation. 1 TTS inference uses 10 dif-fusion steps with text and voice CFG scales of 3.0 and 4.0, respectively. ASR uses greedy decoding.

These results show that Uni-Stream supports both tasks within a single model while maintaining streaming responsiveness comparable to that of the Stage I specialists.

Table 4 compares Uni-Stream with offline unified models and task-specific streaming systems. Base-line results are taken from the corresponding pa-pers, technical reports, or model cards. Among the listed models, Uni-Stream uniquely supports streaming input for both recognition and synthe-sis with a set of shared weights. Compared with HoliTok-Unite, Uni-Stream achieves lower WER on both tasks while supporting streaming operation, although it uses a larger backbone. Ming-UniAudio achieves lower ASR and TTS WER, showing that unification can support stronger task performance at a larger model scale. These comparisons provide context rather than controlled evaluations, given differences in architecture and training data. Task-specific streaming systems still achieve bet-ter ASR performance, with the recognition gap particularly pronounced on LibriSpeech test-other. For TTS, Uni-Stream achieves speaker similarity comparable to Kyutai DSM-TTS, but its higher WER indicates room to improve intelligibility. The main result is therefore the combination of unified and streaming capabilities while retaining perfor-mance close to the Stage I specialists. The remain-ing gap to dedicated systems motivates further im-provements in recognition robustness and synthesis accuracy. Uni-Stream acquires streaming TTS and ASR capabilities by adapting a pretrained speech model with approximately 40k hours of training data per task. Table 4 describe Stage II adaptation, not total training data, since VibeVoice’s pretraining data are not reported. The results support a practical route toward unified speech interfaces: extending pretrained capabilities to both streaming directions within one model. Future work will focus on nar-8rowing the quality gap to specialized systems. 

5 Conclusion 

We presented Uni-Stream, which unifies stream-ing speech recognition and synthesis in a shared decoder-only language model. With time-aligned speech–text interleaving and two-stage training, Uni-Stream achieves performance comparable to the adapted task-specific models on both tasks. Our training ablations show that synthesis anchoring helps preserve TTS quality, while additional recog-nition distillation improves early ASR performance without a statistically significant final gain. We will make the accompanying transcriptions and word-level timestamp annotations public to support re-producibility and further research. Representation probing reveals a persistent modality gap: speech and text remain distinct under our similarity mea-sure, while representations within each modality become more task-dependent in later layers. These findings show that unified streaming capabilities can coexist with distinct modality and task rep-resentations. Future work will focus on further improving recognition and synthesis quality. 

Limitations 

While Uni-Stream presents the potential of a uni-fied streaming speech language model for speech synthesis and recognition, several limitations and future directions remain for improving model per-formance, experimenting with more training con-figurations, and extending the model to support other languages. 

Recognition and synthesis quality. Although Uni-Stream performs comparably to the adapted task-specific models, the strongest specialized sys-tems still achieve better results on LibriSpeech test-other and intelligibility in synthesized speech. Future work will investigate whether stronger pre-trained backbones and different joint training strate-gies can improve results on these tasks while main-taining unified streaming capabilities. 

Streaming configuration search. Computa-tional constraints limit our exploration of streaming and training configurations. We select chunk sizes, lookahead windows, and the text-to-speech ratio for fixed-ratio interleaving empirically or following prior work, which balances system latency and task-specific performance without an exhaustive search. In the future, a broader evaluation of these choices would help characterize the trade-offs between task performance, system latency, and training cost. 

English-only evaluation. Our data preparation, training, and evaluation are limited to English. The effectiveness of the proposed streaming formats and joint training approach in other languages re-mains untested. Future work will extend the data pipeline and model training to multilingual set-tings.

A Data Preparation Details 

A.1 Emilia-EN Annotation 

The Emilia-EN annotations pair voted transcripts with merged word boundaries. For GigaSpeech XL 11 and LibriSpeech train-960, timestamps correspond to the normalized provided transcripts. The anno-tation release described in the main text contains these derived texts and timestamps, without audio. 

Transcript voting. We combine the original Emilia transcript with hypotheses from Crisper-Whisper 2.0 (Wagner et al., 2026) and Nemotron-Streaming-ASR-0.6B (Noroozi et al., 2024; Rekesh et al., 2023). Before voting, we normalize com-parison units so that numbers, abbreviations, and filler variants can match equivalent spoken forms. We jointly align the three sequences with a beam-pruned edit alignment (beam width 32) and apply ROVER-style column-wise voting (Fiscus, 1997). Words supported by at least two hypotheses are retained; singleton insertions are removed. Other columns retain the entry with the most support and are marked disputed; utterances are rejected if disputed columns exceed 5% of output-producing columns. We prefer the Emilia surface form when it matches the selected entry, and use Emilia to break ties. Finally, we restore fillers detected only by CrisperWhisper and attach its timestamps wher-ever its words match the voted transcript. 

Word-boundary merging. We combine Crisper-Whisper timestamps with forced alignments of the voted transcript from MMS-FA (Pratap et al., 2024) and Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B (Shi et al., 2026a). Available predictions are averaged with weights proportional to inverse squared boundary-error scales. With three predictions, an outlier is identified when the other two agree within 80 ms and its value differs from their mean by more than 80 ms. Re-jection is shared across each word-end/next-word-start pair: the aligner with the largest qualifying deviation on either boundary is removed from both. Using the same surviving aligners and weights on both boundaries preserves their order when the in-puts are monotone. 

Alignment validation. Table 5 evaluates bound-ary accuracy on TIMIT’s 5,040 utterances with manual word boundaries (Garofolo et al., 1993). All aligners receive the reference word sequence, and CrisperWhisper uses its forced-alignment path for this diagnostic. For this evaluation, merge weights use the aligners’ measured boundary er-rors. The three-way merge achieves a mean bound-ary error of 24.4 ms, below that of each individual aligner.
