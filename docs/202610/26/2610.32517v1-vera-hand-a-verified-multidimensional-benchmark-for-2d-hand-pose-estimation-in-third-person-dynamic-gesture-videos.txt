Estimation in Third-Person Dynamic Gesture Videos

Abstract
Hand landmarks serve as input for gesture recognition, sign
language understanding, and touchless human–computer
interaction. Yet hand pose estimators are typically eval-
uated with frame-wise localization metrics such as PCK
or MPJPE, which are computed over matched predictions
and overlook missed or spurious hands, handedness errors,
and temporal instability. To address these limitations, we
present VERA-Hand, a benchmark that evaluates estima-
tors using manually verified, frame-level 2D annotations for
10,690 hands across four complementary dimensions (de-
tection, localization, identification, and temporal stability),
as well as a joint Pose-F1 metric that requires both correct
detection and accurate localization. Evaluating six estima-
tors spanning hand-specific, whole-body, reconstruction-
based, and foundation-model paradigms, we find that no es-
timator dominates: rankings reorder sharply across dimen-
sions. VERA-Hand also exposes failures that localization-
only and downstream evaluations may not reveal, includ-
ing cross-hand overlap in over 85% of frames for a recent
foundation model. A post-hoc cleanup case study demon-
strates how the benchmark can validate corrective interven-
tions, while stratified analyses show that robustness varies
across motion-blur classes and hand regions. We release
the verified annotations, protocol, and code at https:
//gibranbenitez.github.io/VERA_Hand/.

and virtual reality, and touchless human–computer interac-

those keypoints has been shown to directly affect down-
stream performance [29, 30].

jointly with the body and face, such as MediaPipe Holis-

They differ in how they detect, localize, and represent
hands. Despite this heterogeneity, estimator selection is fre-
quently treated as an implementation detail, while the way
reliability is measured has not kept pace.
However, common evaluation practice remains centered
on frame-wise localization metrics.
Hand-specific esti-
mators are commonly assessed using Percentage of Cor-
rect Keypoints (PCK) and Mean Per Joint Position Error
(MPJPE) [28, 37], while whole-body models are typically
evaluated with AP and AR based on Object Keypoint Sim-
ilarity (OKS) [21], mostly on isolated images, controlled
sequences, or curated benchmarks.
Since localization is
computed over predictions already matched to the ground
truth (GT), these evaluations largely take hand detection
as given and do not account for missed hands or spurious
predictions. They also provide limited insight into hand-
edness identification and temporal stability, allowing esti-
mators with markedly different failure patterns to receive
similar aggregate scores.
This limitation is particularly important for continuous
dynamic gestures, where hand scale varies, rapid motion
produces blur, hands pass in front of the face, partially leave
the image, and may be absent altogether. Existing estima-
tor comparisons in related settings rely on downstream task
performance, controlled perturbations, or frame-level spa-
tial evaluation rather than a unified verified reference [29–
31, 33]. Meanwhile, gesture and sign language corpora in-
creasingly release hand poses generated by off-the-shelf es-
timators, but without full human verification [1, 12, 22]. As
summarized in Table 1, to the best of our knowledge, no
prior resource combines fully verified frame-level 2D anno-
tations with a unified evaluation of detection, localization,
identification, and temporal stability in a third-person con-
tinuous dynamic-gesture setting.
To address this gap, we introduce VERA-Hand, a bench-
mark for evaluating hand pose estimators on third-person,
continuous, dynamic-gesture RGB videos. Built on the IPN
Hand dataset [4], VERA-Hand provides manually verified,
frame-level 2D hand pose annotations for 10,690 hands in
10,768 frames.
We evaluate six off-the-shelf estimators
spanning four major paradigms across detection, localiza-
tion, identification, and temporal stability. Pose-F1 addi-
tionally evaluates detection and localization jointly, while
stratified analyses examine performance across motion-blur
classes and specific hand regions. Our central finding is
that estimator rankings change substantially across evalu-
ation axes. As Figure 1 shows, WiLoR leads in detection
but ranks second-last in localization, while MP-hands leads
in localization despite a substantially higher identification
error. No single estimator dominates across dimensions,
demonstrating that localization alone provides an incom-
plete account of hand pose estimator reliability.

Our contributions are:
• VERA-Hand, a manually verified benchmark with frame-
level 2D hand pose annotations for 10,690 hands (10,768
frames) from third-person dynamic-gesture videos.
• A multidimensional protocol for evaluating complete
hand pose estimation pipelines across detection, localiza-
tion, identification, and temporal stability, complemented
by Pose-F1 and stratified analyses across motion-blur
classes and hand regions.
• A systematic comparison of six off-the-shelf estimators,
revealing axis-dependent ranking reversals and estimator-
specific failure modes, followed by a targeted post-hoc
cleanup case study that demonstrates how the benchmark
can validate corrective interventions.
2. Related Work
2.1. Hand pose datasets and annotation resources
Existing hand pose datasets span a broad range of acqui-
sition settings, but their annotations are not uniformly de-
signed for estimator evaluation. Isolated RGB images are
commonly labeled through semi-automatic model fitting
[21, 37]; large-scale 3D datasets use multi-view systems
or marker-based motion capture in controlled environments
[10, 13, 16, 28]; synthetic datasets provide exact annota-
tions at the cost of a domain gap [25, 35]; and egocen-
tric collections focus primarily on close-range hand–object
interaction [2, 17, 30]. As summarized in Table 1, these
resources often rely on model initialization or specialized
capture systems, while complete frame-level manual veri-
fication remains uncommon. The per-axis columns make
this fragmentation explicit: HInt [31], for example, pro-
vides human-corrected 2D hands but targets a designated
hand rather than annotating all visible hands exhaustively,
and temporal sequences are available only for a subset.
Third-person continuous gestures are instead represented
in gesture and sign language corpora [1, 4, 7, 12, 22]. Al-
though these datasets match our target setting, their released
hand landmarks, when available, are typically generated by
off-the-shelf estimators without human verification. For ex-
ample, How2Sign [12] uses OpenPose [8], while iSign [22],
Isharah [1], and the pose extension of IPN Hand [5] rely
on MP-holistic [18]. Their continuous videos support in-
trinsic temporal analysis and training of recognition mod-
els, but the annotations may inherit estimator-specific er-
rors (marked ✓∗in Table 1). Consequently, to the best of
our knowledge, no prior third-person resource supports all
four evaluation axes under complete human verification, a
gap that VERA-Hand fills.
2.2. Hand pose estimator paradigms
Current hand pose estimators span several paradigms with
distinct representations and system designs. Hand-specific
pipelines predict landmarks from localized hand regions
[19, 36]; whole-body estimators recover the hands jointly
with body and facial keypoints [18, 20]; reconstruction-
based methods fit a parametric 3D hand model [34] and
project its joints into the image [32]; and human-centric
foundation models predict dense whole-body keypoints
through large-scale pretraining [23, 24].
These paradigms also differ in how hands are detected
and assigned a hand label. Although all evaluated pipelines
follow a top-down design, the input hand or person region
may be obtained via an integrated detector [32] or via the
default off-the-shelf recommendation provided by the cor-
responding framework [11]. Despite these design differ-
ences, the resulting systems are commonly used as com-
plete hand pose estimation pipelines in downstream ap-
plications, where comparisons often focus solely on key-
point localization. Their behavior in hand recovery, hand
identification, and temporal stability remains less systemati-
cally characterized. VERA-Hand evaluates these properties
jointly under a common protocol.
2.3. Evaluating and benchmarking pose estimators
A smaller body of work systematically compares pose es-
timators, following three complementary directions. First,
estimator quality has been evaluated extrinsically via down-
stream performance in sign language or egocentric action
recognition [29, 30]. Second, metamorphic testing has ex-
amined robustness to controlled perturbations such as oc-
clusion, illumination changes, and motion blur, but with-
out human-verified spatial annotations [33].
Third, spa-
tial benchmarks report frame-level 2D accuracy using PCK
and occlusion labels [31], while prior egocentric evaluations
separately analyze spatial accuracy and temporal consis-
tency [14]. As summarized in Table 1, reported evaluations
range from task-level accuracy/BLEU to PCK, AP/AR, and
MPJPE/PA-MPJPE, and therefore do not necessarily eval-
uate all dimensions supported by the released annotations.
Ego-Exo4D [17] is the closest resource, providing substan-
tially larger verified multi-view 3D supervision in contin-
uous egocentric video; however, EgoHandPose targets the
camera wearer’s hands and benchmarks 3D pose using PA-
MPJPE and MPJPE. VERA-Hand complements it with ex-
haustive frame-level 2D annotations for third-person dy-
namic gestures and a unified four-axis evaluation protocol
covering detection, localization, identification, and tempo-
ral stability.

3. Benchmark Construction and Protocol
VERA-Hand combines manually verified,
frame-level
2D hand pose annotations for third-person continuous
dynamic-gesture videos with a multidimensional evaluation
protocol (Figure 2).
3.1. Source videos and benchmark composition
We build our benchmark on the IPN Hand dataset [4], which
consists of 640 × 480 RGB videos recorded from a frontal
view, with each subject performing continuous gestures to
control touchless screens. Each video comprises roughly
twenty gestures, and the hand-to-camera distance varies
within a single recording. Fast motion, partial exits from
the frame, and hands passing in front of the face occur nat-
urally. The benchmark uses the official test split, keeping its
annotations disjoint from the dataset’s training data, while
the source videos remain under its original terms.
Annotation targets approximately 200 keyframes per
video, using a category-dependent sampling rate designed
to maximize pose variation. Dynamic gestures are sampled
densely (about one frame in two), pointing sparsely (about
one frame in seven), and resting at most four frames per in-
stance. The annotations are therefore not temporally dense,
and the protocol is built accordingly: the supervised axes
(detection, localization, and identification) are computed on
the annotated frames, while the temporal metrics are intrin-
sic to the predictions and are computed over the full videos
without extra annotations.
Each visible hand is annotated independently in every
sampled frame with a left/right hand label and 21 two-
dimensional keypoints following the MediaPipe topology.
The visibility convention distinguishes two cases: keypoints
outside the image are marked as out of frame, whereas
occluded keypoints remain annotated, with their locations
inferred from hand context. The annotations were initial-
ized with an off-the-shelf estimator and manually corrected
across all sampled frames in two passes conducted several
months apart (Figure 2, left). A subsequent independent au-
dit was performed to further correct the annotations.
VERA-Hand comprises 10,690 annotated hands on
10,768 frames from 13 subjects across 52 videos (four
per subject). The data are strongly right-hand-dominant:
10,252 frames contain only the right hand, 72 only the
left, 183 both, and 261 no hands. The benchmark includes
10,435 right-hand and 255 left-hand annotations. Although
smaller than training-oriented corpora, the benchmark is de-
signed for estimator evaluation rather than training.
3.2. Ground-truth quality and annotation bias
To assess annotation reliability, we independently audited
3,500 frames sampled across all 52 videos, focusing on
hand presence, handedness, gross pose, and local keypoint
placement.
Overall, 268 frames (7.7%) required correc-
tions; most involved local keypoint adjustments, while five
previously missed hands were added. All identified issues
were corrected in the released annotations, which are the
ones used throughout this paper. A breakdown and repre-
sentative examples are provided in Appendix A.
Since the annotations were initialized with MP-holistic
[18], its predictions could retain an advantage during eval-
uation. We address this potential bias by distinguishing be-
tween biased and bias-free hands. A hand is classified as
biased when the corresponding MP-holistic prediction at-
tains PCK@0.02=1 with respect to the final GT (approxi-


mately 3,400 hands); all remaining hands form the bias-free
subset. Manual correction substantially altered the initial-
ization within this subset: 76% of the keypoints moved by
more than α=0.02, with a median normalized displacement
of 0.06, compared with 0.008 for biased hands. Moreover,
approximately 1,381 hands missing from the initialization
were added, and 1,387 spurious detections were removed
from the corresponding frames. Consequently, MP-holistic
exhibits a substantial drop in localization performance on
the bias-free subset, whereas the other estimators show no
comparable agreement with the initialization. Unless stated
otherwise, all results are reported on the bias-free subset.
3.3. Evaluation axes and matching
Each axis answers a question the others cannot, and each
rests on a precondition the source material must satisfy for
that question to be measurable. All supervised metrics are
computed per frame; localization, identification, and Pose-
F1 use macro-averaged left/right scores, whereas detection
is label-agnostic.
Matching. Spatial matching uses hand bounding-box
IoU between the GT hand and each candidate with a thresh-
old of 0.2.
Detection uses a global one-to-one, label-
agnostic assignment, whereas localization and Pose-F1 are
restricted to same-label matching to preserve hand corre-
spondence.
Detection asks whether each GT hand is recovered, in-
dependently of its predicted hand label. Unmatched GT
hands are false negatives (Figure 2, right-top) and un-
matched predictions are false positives. Because the bench-
mark also includes frames with no hands, false positives are
measured in genuinely empty scenes as well as alongside
real hands. We report label-agnostic precision, recall, and
F1 (Det-F1), together with two recurrent false-positive pat-
terns: duplicate hands (surplus predictions sharing the same
label, either beside a matched hand or without a correspond-
ing GT) and cross-hand overlap (opposite-label predictions
at nearly the same position, IoU > 0.80). Note that detec-
tion is evaluated at the system level, using each estimator
with the default detector provided by its framework.
Localization asks where the keypoints fall once the cor-
responding hand is found and is computed over matched
true positives only. We report PCK at α=0.10 and MPJPE,
normalized by hand size (wrist-to-middle-MCP distance).
Out-of-frame GT keypoints are excluded from the metric,
while occluded keypoints are scored normally.
Identification asks whether each detected hand is as-
signed the correct hand label. We measure it through the
label-swap rate using a label-agnostic spatial match: the
highest-IoU candidate above 0.2 is selected regardless of
label, and a swap is counted when the labels disagree (Fig-
ure 2, right-bottom). Cross-hand overlap cases in which
predictions of both hand labels overlap the same GT hand
are excluded from this metric.
The resulting swap rate,
therefore, captures unambiguous hand label flips without
being inflated by overlapping duplicate predictions.
Temporal stability asks how steady the predictions re-
main over time. This axis is intrinsic, since it uses no tem-
poral GT and characterizes prediction dynamics over the
full videos rather than temporal accuracy. Following [29],
we compute acceleration- and jerk-based jitter, Jacc and
Jjerk, as the mean magnitudes of the second- and third-order
temporal differences, respectively. We additionally report
motion energy, Ev (mean joint velocity), to contextualize
smoothness relative to the predicted motion. All metrics are
scaled by 100 and computed from image-normalized co-
ordinates for three keypoint subsets: all 21 keypoints, the
base joints (wrist and MCPs), and the fingertips. We also
count tracking gaps, defined as runs of consecutive missed
detections bounded by detections of the same hand. De-
tailed keypoint-subset results and tracking-gap analysis are
provided in Appendix C.3.
3.4. Pose-F1: Joint Detection and Localization
Detection and localization can be misleading when consid-
ered independently. A pipeline may achieve high detection
recall by producing excessive predictions, while localiza-
tion, computed only over matched hands, conditions eval-
uation on successful detection. Pose-F1 evaluates both re-
quirements jointly. A prediction is counted as a true pos-
itive only if it is matched to a GT hand under same-label
matching and satisfies PCK@α ≥β. A matched prediction
that fails this criterion is counted as a false positive, with its
corresponding GT hand as a false negative; unmatched pre-
dictions are counted as false positives and unmatched GT
hands as false negatives, following the same accounting as
detection. Precision, recall, and F1 (Pose-F1) are then com-
puted from these counts. We report α=0.10 and β=0.5 in
the main paper. Since neither permissive detection nor ac-
curate localization alone can raise Pose-F1, we use it to as-
sess whether the case study in Section 4.4 produces joint
improvements.

3.6. Estimators and evaluation setup
The six evaluated estimators summarized in Table 2 cover
the four paradigms discussed in Section 2.
To preserve
off-the-shelf comparability, each pipeline uses its official
default configuration, confidence thresholds, and detector,
without dataset-specific tuning. Detection is therefore as-
sessed as part of the complete deployed pipeline rather than
independently of the pose estimator. The main comparison
is based on the raw predictions, while any post-processing
intervention, including the cleanup examined in Section 4.4,
is evaluated separately.
Native outputs are mapped to a
common topology of 21 two-dimensional keypoints. This
includes projecting WiLoR’s 3D MANO [34] joints onto
the image plane and applying the Goliath-to-21 mapping
for the Sapiens2 layout. Additional conversion details are
provided in Appendix B.

4.2. Per-dimension findings
We next examine each evaluation axis individually. Detec-
tion performance varies substantially, with Det-F1 ranging
from 0.666 to 0.978, not following a clear hand-specific
versus whole-body distinction. WiLoR, which uses a ded-
icated hand detector, and RTMW-wb, which relies on per-
son detection, achieve the two highest scores despite their
different detection strategies. Thus, the gap one might ex-
pect between hand-level and person-level detection does not
clearly materialize in this setting. WiLoR leads by a con-
siderable margin, whereas Sapiens2 obtains substantially
lower Det-F1. Detection errors also include spurious pre-
dictions, as analyzed in Section 4.4. Localization reverses
this ranking. Among correctly matched hands, MP-hands
achieves the highest PCK (0.573), whereas WiLoR, the de-
tection leader, obtains the second-lowest (0.47). Because
WiLoR predictions are mapped to 2D keypoints by pro-
jecting a reconstructed 3D representation [34] rather than
directly regressing image-space landmarks, this result may
reflect a trade-off between robust hand recovery and precise
2D localization. More importantly, this ranking inversion


demonstrates the need for VERA-Hand’s multidimensional
evaluation: a localization-only comparison would poorly
rank WiLoR while overlooking its leading performance in
detection and temporal stability.
Pose-F1 makes the joint reading explicit, crediting a
hand only when it is both detected and accurately local-
ized. Here RTMW-wb achieves the highest performance
(0.477) by combining strong detection (0.932) with com-
petitive localization (0.56), despite leading neither axis in-
dividually. In contrast, MP-hands’ localization advantage is
limited by its detection performance, yielding a Pose-F1 of
0.389, while WiLoR drops from first in detection to second
in Pose-F1. Thus, the joint metric favors balanced pipelines
over estimators that excel along only one dimension.
Identification reveals a marked difference between
hand-specific models and the rest. MP-hands and RTMP-
hand exhibit label-swap rates of 4.41% and 3.51%, re-
spectively, whereas all other estimators remain at or below
0.51%.
For the whole-body and foundation models, ex-
plicit body context may help disambiguate left from right.
WiLoR, despite operating on individual hands, achieves a
comparably low rate (0.11%), possibly aided by its custom
YOLO detector and handedness-assignment mechanism.
On the other hand, temporal stability does not track spatial
quality and can even oppose it. WiLoR has the lowest jitter
(Jjerk: 1.18) despite relatively weak localization, while MP-
hands and RTMP-hand, which perform comparatively well
in localization, exhibit the highest jitter values (1.69 and
1.76). Because low jitter can also reflect limited predicted
motion, Table 3 also reports Ev alongside Jjerk; the detailed
analysis is provided in Appendix C.3. Thus, these metrics
characterize prediction smoothness rather than correctness
and should not be interpreted in isolation. Qualitative com-
parisons between the GT and the estimator’s predictions are
provided in Appendix F.
4.3. Stratified analysis: blur and region

on the evaluation dimension (Figure 3a). Detection remains
highly stable for WiLoR and Sapiens2, while the hand-

creases from 0.94 to 0.81, with RTMP-hand showing a sim-

blurry for all estimators. RTMW-wb leads across all three
classes, although its score drops from 0.57 to 0.39. MP-
hands is competitive on sharp instances (0.49) but falls to
0.27 under blur, similar to RTMP-hand and WiLoR. This
reveals that no single estimator is uniformly robust to blur
across evaluation axes, just as none dominates the aggregate
ranking.

4.4. Cross-hand overlap and post-hoc cleanup
Sapiens2 predicts a second hand with the opposite label
over the real one in 85.44% of all benchmark frames (Ta-
ble 4). This cross-hand overlap rate is more than an order of
magnitude higher than that of any other estimator (all below
5%). The failure also becomes more frequent across Sapi-
ens generations: Sapiens1 [23] exhibits cross-hand overlap
in 14.1% of frames, despite Sapiens2 providing better PCK
and label-swap performance. The overlap occurs mostly


when only one hand is present and nearly disappears when
both hands are visible. This suggests that Sapiens halluci-
nates the absent hand rather than missing the visible one,
possibly due to its full-body training setting. VERA-Hand
exposes this failure through its predominantly single-hand
setup, in which opposite-handed phantom predictions are
directly observable. By contrast, the Sapiens family may
appear robust in downstream evaluations on videos where
both hands are consistently present, because it rarely drops
either hand [29]; here, the same persistence becomes a se-
vere source of false positives and opposite-handed phantom
predictions when one hand is genuinely absent. Additional
comparisons with Sapiens1 and breakdowns are provided in
Appendix D.
As a case study, we use the benchmark to evaluate a
post-hoc, training-free cleanup applied to the predictions.
It comprises two stages that address distinct failure modes:
a spatial stage resolves cross-hand overlap by retaining the
higher-confidence hand when opposite-handed predictions
coincide, while a temporal stage uses a short frame win-
dow to remove duplicates and fill brief detection gaps via
interpolation. The cleanup benefits different estimators (Ta-
ble 4). For Sapiens2, cleanup nearly eliminates cross-hand
overlap (85.44% →0.05%) and produces by far the largest
Det-F1 gain from 0.666 to 0.94, pushing the rank from
last to third. MP-holistic shows the second-largest detec-
tion gain, while the remaining estimators change only mod-
estly. Pose-F1 gains are smaller: MP-hands improves the
most (0.389 →0.442), overtaking WiLoR (0.419), whereas
RTMW-wb remains highest (0.477). Taken together, these
results show how the protocol links distinct failure pat-
terns to targeted improvements across evaluation dimen-
sions. VERA-Hand therefore provides more than an es-
timator ranking: it helps identify the failure mechanisms
underlying the scores and evaluate targeted corrective inter-
ventions.
5. Discussion and Limitations
The ranking reversals across evaluation axes have a direct
practical implication: asking which estimator is best is in-
complete unless the reliability dimension relevant to the ap-
plication is specified. An application that relies on consis-
tent hand identity over time may favor a different pipeline
from one that depends on precise keypoint localization, and
an estimator that is excellent for one can be a poor choice for
the other. VERA-Hand makes these trade-offs actionable by
reporting reliability along complementary axes rather than
collapsing it into a single score.
This application-dependent view also clarifies the rela-
tionship between component-level and downstream evalua-
tion. Downstream evaluation remains essential, but it mea-
sures end-task performance and may overlook failures of
the pose estimator itself that become apparent when evalu-
ated against a verified 2D reference [29]. VERA-Hand is
therefore complementary to task-level evaluation, not a re-
placement for it. The post-hoc cleanup case study illustrates
this role: its contribution lies not in the cleanup procedure
itself, but in demonstrating that a multidimensional proto-
col can identify a specific failure mode, determine which
reliability axes it affects, and assess whether a corrective in-
tervention improves the targeted behavior while monitoring
potential regressions elsewhere.
VERA-Hand has some limitations that should be con-
sidered when interpreting its findings.
First, it covers a
single regime (the third-person dynamic gestures of IPN
Hand), so the observed rankings should not be assumed to
transfer to egocentric, close-range, or other domains. Ex-
tending the protocol to additional corpora remains impor-
tant. At the same time, subsampling shows that the main
comparative conclusions remain stable even with substan-
tially fewer annotated frames (Appendix C.5).
Second,
the dataset is strongly right-hand-dominant, containing only
255 left hands. For the label-aware axes, macro averaging
prevents right-hand performance from dominating the ag-
gregate scores, but the limited left-hand annotations make
left-hand estimates less conclusive. Finally, VERA-Hand
evaluates 2D rather than 3D pose accuracy, and the anno-
tations were initialized from an existing estimator and then
manually corrected. The independent audit identified issues
in 7.7% of the reviewed frames, all of which were corrected;
nevertheless, some residual annotation errors and initializa-
tion biases may persist beyond the audited sample.
Two further limitations concern the scope of the off-
the-shelf evaluation. The main benchmark evaluates only
the Sapiens2-1B checkpoint with its official weights. Al-
though the results for the preceding Sapiens generation in
Appendix D provide an initial point of comparison, broader
testing across model sizes and checkpoints is needed to de-
termine how consistently the observed cross-hand overlap
occurs across the family. More generally, each estimator
was evaluated as a complete released pipeline using its de-
fault detector and pose configuration.
This reflects how
practitioners commonly deploy these systems, but it does
not disentangle detector errors from pose-estimation behav-
ior or identify the best detector–pose combinations, which
would require a dedicated factorial study. Accordingly, the
reported rankings should be interpreted as comparisons of
complete released pipelines rather than isolated pose heads.
6. Conclusion
We introduced VERA-Hand, a manually verified, frame-
level 2D hand pose benchmark for third-person continu-
ous dynamic gestures, along with a protocol that evaluates
detection, localization, identification, and temporal stabil-
ity, including Pose-F1 as a joint metric of detection and
localization. Our comparison of six off-the-shelf estima-


tors shows that no single pipeline dominates across evalu-
ation axes: rankings change substantially from one dimen-
sion to another, demonstrating that localization alone pro-
vides an incomplete account of reliability.
VERA-Hand
also reveals estimator-specific failure modes that aggre-
gate or localization-only evaluations may overlook, includ-
ing cross-hand overlap.
At the same time, the post-hoc
cleanup case study demonstrates how the protocol can as-
sess targeted corrective interventions and monitor their ef-
fects across reliability axes. We release the annotations,
protocol, and code to support reproducible multidimen-
sional evaluation of hand pose estimators.

A. Annotation Audit
To assess the reliability of the manually verified annota-
tions, we conducted two independent audits, each carried
out by a reviewer who did not participate in the keypoint
annotation. The first inspected 500 frames and the second a
further 3,000, for a total of 3,500 audited frames spanning
all 13 subjects and all 52 videos. Each frame was examined
for four categories of error: hand presence, handedness la-
bel, gross pose configuration, and local keypoint placement.
The audits were corrective rather than diagnostic: every is-
sue identified in either pass was fixed, and the corrected
annotations are the ones released and used throughout the
paper.
The audited set is deliberately not a proportional sam-
ple (Table 5). Because left and bimanual hands are rare in
VERA-Hand, the audit covers all 72 left-only frames and all
183 bimanual frames, together with roughly one third of the
right-only frames. Left hands therefore account for 6.9% of
the audited hands against 2.4% of the benchmark, so the
rare and most error-prone configurations are inspected ex-
haustively rather than in proportion. Coverage of out-of-
frame keypoints (12.5% of audited frames against 14.1%
of the benchmark) and of the motion-blur classes remains
close to the benchmark composition. The 261 hand-free
frames fall outside this audit, since they contain no keypoint
annotations for inspection; each was confirmed to contain
no visible hand at selection time.
Across the 3,500 audited frames, 268 (7.7%) required a
correction. Of these, 263 were small displacements of in-
dividual keypoints and five were previously missed hands
that were added; no errors were found in handedness labels
or gross pose configuration. Residual uncertainty is there-
fore concentrated in fine-grained localization rather than in

B. Estimator Setup and Keypoint Conversion
Each estimator is evaluated using its official off-the-shelf
configuration, including the default detector, confidence


thresholds, preprocessing, and input resolution, without
dataset-specific tuning.
The MMPose pipelines [11] use
the RTMPose-m Hand5 checkpoint for RTMP-hand [19]
at 256×256 and the RTMW-x Cocktail14 checkpoint for
RTMW-wb [20] at 384×288.
Sapiens2 [24] uses the
1B Goliath pose checkpoint at 1024×768, while MP-
hands [36] and MP-holistic [18] use MediaPipe release
0.10.32. WiLoR [32] is evaluated using its publicly released
checkpoint. The detectors and per-model details are sum-
marized in Table 2.
Handedness is obtained differently across pipelines,
which is relevant to the identification results. MP-holistic,
RTMW-wb, and Sapiens2 encode the left and right hands
using fixed output keypoint indices.
MP-hands provides
a handedness label through its native classifier, whereas
WiLoR uses the side class predicted by its YOLO-based
hand detector. RTMP-hand is the only evaluated pipeline
whose detector returns hand bounding boxes without a side
label, requiring handedness to be assigned post hoc. For this
purpose, we use RTMPose-L-WholeBody from the same
MMPose framework, with a 384×288 input and the same
RTMDet [27] person detector used for RTMW-wb. This
auxiliary pipeline is used exclusively to obtain left- and
right-wrist anchors and does not alter the hand keypoints
predicted by RTMP-hand. Each RTMP-hand detection is
assigned the label of the nearest compatible wrist anchor.
Anchors located inside the hand box are preferred, fol-
lowed by anchors lying within one box diagonal of its cen-
ter. When no suitable anchor is available, handedness is
inferred from the predicted hand geometry using a palm
cross-product. Because RTMP-hand relies on an inferred
rather than native handedness label, this additional assign-
ment step may contribute to its comparatively high label-
swap rate.
All native outputs are mapped to the common 21-
keypoint MediaPipe topology. Most mappings require only
index selection, but Sapiens2 and WiLoR require additional
transformations. In the Sapiens2 Goliath layout, each finger
is ordered from tip to base, and the wrist follows the finger
joints; we reverse each finger block to the MediaPipe base-
to-tip order and move the wrist to the first index. WiLoR
predicts 21 MANO [34] joints in 3D, which are projected
onto the image plane using the model-predicted camera. For
left hands, the projected coordinates are mirrored horizon-
tally to match the annotation convention.

C.3. Temporal stability
All temporal metrics are computed on the right hand, the
side that performs the gestures; the sparse left hand car-
ries too little continuous motion to be measured meaning-
fully. Three patterns stand out. Motion energy Ev tracks
jitter: the steadiest estimators also predict the least motion,
so part of their stability reflects a low-motion prior rather
than tracking accuracy (Table 8). The estimator ranking
is largely consistent across keypoint subsets, with the fin-
gertips exhibiting higher jitter than the base joints, indicat-
ing that temporal instability increases toward the distal key-
points (Table 9). Finally, the cleanup decreases the num-
ber of tracking gaps by approximately 37% to 67%, while
Jjerk increases slightly in every case (Table 10). This ef-
fect scales with the number of gaps filled: the gap-heavy
estimators gain the most continuity and pay the most in jit-
ter, while the gap-light ones barely change. The post-hoc
interpolation thus trades gap continuity for a small loss of
smoothness.

C.5. Benchmark size sensitivity
To assess whether the reported comparisons depend on the
size of the benchmark, we recompute the supervised metrics
on nested subsets of the annotated frames. Subsampling is
deterministic and applied per video: frames are grouped by
video and ordered chronologically, and for a target fraction
we retain k = round(n · pct/100) equispaced indices over
that ordering, always including the first and last annotated
frame of the clip. Sampling within each video preserves
the proportion of frames across videos and subjects, and the
equispaced selection keeps the subset distributed over the
whole clip rather than concentrated at its beginning.
The relative ordering of the estimators is preserved at ev-
ery fraction on all four metrics, down to 10% of the bench-
mark (Table 12). Detection is the most stable axis, vary-
ing by less than 0.01 across all fractions, which is expected
since it is computed over every annotated hand.
Cross-
hand overlap is equally stable: the Sapiens2 failure remains
above 82% at every fraction and stays two orders of mag-
nitude above the other estimators, so the headline finding
is not a consequence of benchmark size. Localization and
Pose-F1 fluctuate more at the smallest fractions, where far
fewer matched hands remain; since the subsampling is de-
terministic, these fluctuations reflect the particular frames
retained rather than variance across repeated draws. The
one effect that does depend on size is the measurement of
rare failures: WiLoR’s cross-hand overlap, at 0.07% on the

D. Sapiens Family Analysis
The cross-hand overlap reported in the main paper, com-
puted as the percentage of annotated GT hand instances
affected by an overlapping opposite-handed prediction, in-
creases sharply from Sapiens1-1B [23] to Sapiens2-1B [24]
(Table 13). Sapiens2-1B achieves substantially better lo-
calization and a lower label-swap rate, yet its raw Det-F1
falls well below that of Sapiens1-1B because the opposite-
handed phantom predictions reduce precision.
Table 14 groups the annotated GT hand instances ac-
cording to whether their source frame contains one or both
hands, while Figure 6 shows representative predictions.
Cross-hand overlap is rare when both hands are present, but
becomes dominant for Sapiens2-1B in single-hand frames.
This pattern indicates that the failure primarily consists of
an opposite-handed duplicate prediction over the visible
hand rather than a failure to recover the visible hand it-
self. The behavior may reflect a full-body prediction prior
that favors bilateral hand outputs even when only one hand
is visible. Because VERA-Hand consists predominantly of
single-hand frames, it provides a setting in which this fail-
ure becomes directly measurable.

overlap entirely and raises Det-F1 from 0.666 to 0.929,

E. Post-hoc Cleanup: Method and Ablation
The cleanup operates exclusively on the predicted hands
and uses no GT, image content, or localization met-
ric. It comprises a spatial stage that resolves overlapping
opposite-handed predictions within individual frames and a
temporal stage that enforces short-term consistency across
consecutive frames.
Spatial stage. For every pair of opposite-handedness
predictions in a frame, we compute the bounding-box IoU
(from the valid keypoints) and the center distance normal-
ized by the larger box diagonal. The pair is treated as a
cross-hand overlap when IoU ≥0.80 and the normalized
center distance ≤0.15, in which case the hand with the
higher mean keypoint confidence is kept and the other re-
moved. The spatial stage primarily addresses the cross-hand
overlap problem.
Temporal stage.
The per-video timeline is indepen-
dently processed by hand over a sliding odd window of 7
frames (a radius of 3) using a binary presence signal, with
three components. Deduplication retains at most one pre-
diction per hand label in each frame, selecting the prediction
with the most valid keypoints. Phantom removal discards a
prediction supported by fewer than 2 neighbors in its win-
dow, treating it as a non-persistent detection. Gap recovery
fills an absent side that has at least 4 supporting neighbors,
with support on both temporal directions, by linearly inter-
polating the keypoints between the nearest present frames;
it is restricted to gaps of at most 3 frames and skipped when
the motion between the endpoints is too large, to avoid in-
terpolating across fast movement. The complete cleanup
applies the spatial stage first, followed by all three compo-
nents of the temporal stage.
Ablation. We ablate the temporal stage on MP-hands,
whose gain is purely temporal since it has no cross-hand
overlap, and compare against a majority-vote baseline that
forces each side present whenever it appears in more than
half of the window, copying the nearest hand without in-
terpolation. Table 15 shows that deduplication removes the
duplicate predictions but produces little change in the eval-
uation scores. Gap recovery provides the main individual
gain in detection, although it also reduces PCK by intro-
ducing interpolated predictions, whereas phantom removal
improves PCK and Pose-F1 at a small cost in detection by
discarding non-persistent detections. Combining the three
components outperforms every individual variant on both
Det-F1 and Pose-F1, indicating that the components are
complementary and that the detection gains outweigh the
accompanying localization change. The majority-vote base-
line, which forces presence without interpolation, remains
below the full temporal stage on all three metrics.
Together, these stages produce the cleaned results re-
ported throughout the paper. The spatial stage drives the
Sapiens2-1B recovery shown in Table 13, while the tem-
poral stage produces the reduction in tracking gaps and the
accompanying jitter increase reported in Table 10. Apply-
ing both stages yields the raw-versus-clean comparison pre-
sented in Table 4.
