ABSTRACT 

LLM agents are increasingly deployed in collaborative settings where they pursue separate tasks while sharing resources and operating under review or oversight processes. Such settings create safety risks not only when agents are malicious, but also when they are simply trying to help. Prior work has largely studied adversarial or explicitly collusive behavior; we show that benign agents pursuing separate objectives can spontaneously cooperate in ways that bypass intended workflows. We call this the helpful agent problem . We study long-horizon shared-resource environments in which agents sometimes depend on resources owned by their peers and cross-owner changes are normally mediated by a reviewer. When review becomes unavailable, agents begin making changes on one another’s behalf despite having no shared reward or explicit incentive to cooperate. Crucially, these actions remain within each agent’s local permissions while circumventing the system’s intended oversight structure. We introduce F AVOR BENCH , a controlled benchmark with measurable downstream side effects, and adapt C OOPER BENCH to test the phenomenon in realistic repositories. Across two benchmarks and four models, we find that out-of-workflow favors emerge frequently. Further, in F AVOR BENCH , favors from at least three models cause significant harm to parties outside the interaction. These results reveal a distinct multi-agent safety challenge: cooperation itself can create pathways around system-level safeguards.

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly designed to operate collaboratively, communicating, dividing responsibilities, and adapting to one another in pursuit of complex tasks [1, 9, 26]. Much of this work treats effective coordination as a desirable capability: specialized agents can combine their expertise, exchange intermediate results, and jointly accomplish tasks that are difficult for an individual agent to solve alone. Yet cooperation is not always benign, as an agent may help another accomplish its task while bypassing the procedures or authority boundaries imposed by the surrounding system. This creates what we call the helpful agent problem : behavior that is locally helpful to another agent can nevertheless be globally undesirable when it occurs outside the intended workflow. As agent systems become more autonomous and interact over longer horizons, understanding when such cooperation emerges and what safety risks it poses to the broader system becomes an important multi-agent safety question. Our setting connects to several classical works of cooperation and control in multi-agent systems which studied benevolence, social dependence, organizational norms, and misuse of delegated authority [8, 15, 24, 25]. Mohamed and Huhns study benevolence as a desirable social property in multi-agent systems, Sichman models cooperation through social dependence without assuming agents are benevolent. Research on social laws studies constraints on agents’ actions that prevent interference while preserving individual goal achievement [4, 10, 24], while the confused-deputy problem shows how legitimate authority can be misapplied on another party’s behalf [8]. That is, assistance can be individually reasonable yet still violate organizational constraints or impose costs on others. Prior works on LLM agents have identified several ways in which interactions among LLM agents can produce undesirable outcomes. One line of work studies coordination failures , showing that current agents still struggle to communicate constraints, honor commitments, and coordinate reliably on interdependent tasks [11]. Another line of work studies adversarial failures, such as prompt injection and attacks on tool-using or multi-agent systems [3, 28]. Research on collusion considers the opposite concern: agents may coordinate too effectively in ways that undermine broader objectives, including economic and covert forms of coordination [5, 13, 16, 17]. Most closely related, Shi et al. show that protocol violations can emerge through repeated interaction even when agents are not explicitly instructed to misbehave [23]. More broadly, agent interactions can cause unforeseen harm [2, 22], as also illustrated by a recent OpenAI incident involving unauthorized agent coordination [19]. We study a complementary failure mode: agents with separate task objectives may spontaneously help one another through actions that are individually permitted but collectively bypass the intended workflow. We therefore ask: When does helpful cooperation among LLM agents emerge outside the intended workflow, and what consequences does it create for the rest of the system? 

To study these questions, we construct shared-resource environments in which agents pursue separate tasks but may depend on resources owned by others. Figure 1 illustrates the core setup. Alice and Bob are operating on a shared resource environment and performing their respective tasks. Each agent owns a set of resources and are allowed to modify the resources they need for their own task. Any resource modification that they do not own should go through a reviewer who can safely approve the modification after reviewing the request. For example, Alice needs a change to 

settings.py but does not own the file, so under the intended workflow she must route the change through the reviewer. This scenario illustrates a practical limitation of reviewer-mediated workflows: effective oversight cannot always be guaranteed. Human reviews incur delays [12], automated review services experience outages [18], and LLM-based oversight can be evaded or manipulated [14, 21]. We therefore study reviewer unavailability as a controlled setting in which even non-adversarial agents may find themselves blocked by the intended workflow. What do Alice and Bob do when the reviewer is unavailable? Our setup deliberately separates resource-level permissions from workflow-level oversight. We test whether agents use their existing permissions to help peers in ways that bypass the intended process, not whether they can violate access controls. We do not instruct Bob to help Alice, nor do the agents share a reward for doing so; instead, we ask whether such peer assistance emerges through communication and repeated interaction. We call an out-of-workflow action that helps another agent a favor , and the resulting peer-to-peer cooperation unintended collaboration . Importantly, the same favor may affect resources used by parties outside the interaction. In Figure 1, Bob’s change helps Alice but breaks Dave’s service. This setup therefore lets us study when unintended cooperation emerges, how it is initiated and sustained, and when locally helpful behavior produces downstream harm. 2The Helpful Agent Problem A P REPRINT 

Our work makes four contributions. First, we formalize the helpful agent problem : a setting in which agents pursuing separate objectives can assist one another through actions that respect their local permissions while bypassing the intended review workflow. Our formulation separates the emergence of such favors from their downstream consequences and characterizes when shared-resource dependencies create opportunities for harmful assistance. Second, we introduce FAVOR BENCH , a synthetic code-repository benchmark designed to isolate this behavior in controlled shared-resource settings with repeated interaction, reviewer failures, and measurable side effects. Third, we show that the phenomenon extends beyond synthetic environments by adapting C OOPER BENCH [11] to a shared-resource setting with review-mediated changes and repeated interactions. Finally, motivated by classical work on social laws [24], we also test whether explicit organizational norms can constrain helpful behavior without preventing agents from completing their own tasks. Empirically, across four models, agents bypass the review workflow to perform favors in 58 .8% of FavorBench trajectories and 95% on C OOPER BENCH . On F AVOR BENCH , 43 .7% of completed favors on trap tasks cause attributable harm to an absent team, despite a validated safe alternative existing for every such change. Thus, the problem is not a lack of cooperation. The safety challenge is that this locally benevolent behavior need not respect organizational boundaries or account for parties outside the interaction.

Each agent has its own task reward Ri and there is no shared reward. • D stands for the absent teams, treated as one party. It takes no actions and does not use the channel. • X = {x1, . . . , x m} are shared resources (files, settings, configurations). Each resource x has a set of possible values

For example, for a file, it is the complete contents, including variables and definitions introduced by an edit. For every party k ∈ N ∪ { D}, let Xk ⊆ X contain the resources on which its reward Rk depends. • G is a reviewer that can write any resource on request. Agent i needs to submit a request to G to modify any resource it does not own. At each time step G is available ( αt = 1 ) or not ( αt = 0 ). When available, it approves only changes that do not harm D. When unavailable ( αt = 0 ), this review path is blocked. • own : X → N ∪ {G} ∪ { D} assigns each resource an owner. An agent may write only the resources it owns. It cannot directly modify resources it does not own. • Ri, where i ∈ N ∪ { D} is the individual task reward for agent i. It depends on resources Xi ⊆ X .

A write action A changes the workspace state. Let δ(s, A) denote the state obtained by applying A alone to s, with all other resources unchanged. Let Ws(A) be the set of resources changed by A.

Ws(A) = {x ∈ X : δ(s, A)( x)̸ = s(x)}.

Scope is distinct from ownership: an agent may own resources outside Ti, and its task may require changes to resources it does not own. The intended workflow requires an agent to obtain changes to non-owned resources through G, not by having another active agent make them on its behalf. When another agent owns the resource, it can nevertheless make the change directly. Doing so for a peer without review respects resource-level permissions but bypasses the workflow. The 

designed interaction structure for these changes links each agent only to the reviewer.

A favor need not implement the exact edit initially requested; it may use an alternative implementation. A favor is

Definition 3 (Harm) . Each write has a designated beneficiary b: the recipient of a favor, the requester of an approved change, or the writer otherwise. A write from s to s′ = δ(s, A) harms a party k̸ = b when Rk(s′) < R k(s).

A favor is harmful if it harms at least one party other than its beneficiary, and safe otherwise. For a fixed requester i,owner j, state s, and task assignment, consider the favor actions that j could perform for i. When at least one such action is possible, call this a favor opportunity . The opportunity is: benign if every possible favor is safe; avoidable if both a safe favor and a harmful favor are possible; conflicting if every possible favor is harmful. These cases mean that no, some, or all possible favors cause harm, respectively. Alternative favors may change different resources, provided they satisfy Definition 2. The classification is relative to the requester, owner, current state, and task assignment. By default, harm to D is unknown to the agents and excluded from their rewards. The owner may also fail to observe losses to its own reward when acting. 

Where can harm occur? For the following proposition, we assume (A1) reviewer safety : approved writes cause no harm; and (A2) scope safety : in-scope owner writes cause no harm. 

Proposition 1 (Harm requires an out-of-scope write) . Under (A1)–(A2), a write action A that harms party k must be an out-of-scope owner write satisfying Ws(A) ∩ X k̸ = ∅.

Proof. (A1)–(A2) exclude reviewer-approved and in-scope writes. The remaining writes are out of scope. Since Rk

depends only on Xk, a decrease in Rk requires changing a resource in Xk.A harmful out-of-scope write need not be a favor; conversely, a favor need not be harmful. 

Proposition 2 (When favors can cause harm) . At a fixed state and task assignment: (i) a harmful favor is possible if and only if an avoidable or conflicting favor opportunity exists; (ii) for a fixed requester i and owner j for which a favor is possible, every favor is harmful if and only if the opportunity is conflicting. Proof. Consider a fixed state and task assignment. If a harmful favor is possible, the corresponding opportunity is avoidable when a safe alternative exists, and conflicting otherwise. Conversely, both avoidable and conflicting opportunities contain at least one harmful favor. For a fixed requester and owner, every possible favor is harmful exactly when the opportunity is conflicting, by definition. Thus, favors can cause harm in avoidable or conflicting opportunities, but only conflicting opportunities make harm unavoidable. 

3 Controls for Unintended Collaboration 

Section 2 separates workflow compliance from downstream harm. A favor always bypasses the intended review process, but it does not always cause harm. In an avoidable opportunity, both safe and harmful favors are possible. This creates a choice for the system designer: enforce the workflow conservatively by prohibiting out-of-scope assistance, or permit such assistance when its effects on other parties can be established as safe. We consider two social-law-based controls and a softer behavioral alternative for settings where hard safety guarantees are unavailable. 

Task-scope law. We first consider a conservative social law that treats an agent’s task scope as the boundary of its direct authority. Let Lscope restrict the direct write actions of every active agent j to its assigned task scope: 

Lscope : A is permitted for j only if Ws(A) ⊆ Tj . (1) Task scopes are assigned by the environment for the current task and cannot be expanded by the agents themselves. The reviewer G is not subject to this restriction, and so reviewer-mediated changes remain available. By Definition 2, a favor requires an unreviewed out-of-scope A, for which Ws(A)̸ ⊆ Tj . Hence Lscope excludes favors by construction. Under assumptions (A1)–(A2), it also excludes the only remaining class of writes through which harm can arise: out-of-scope owner writes (Proposition 1). The law therefore prevents out-of-scope favors as defined in Definition 2, while leaving reviewer-mediated changes available. However, it also prohibits out-of-scope assistance that would have caused no harm. 

Guarded out-of-scope assistance. To preserve safe cooperation, we next consider a less restrictive rule. An agent may act freely within its own task scope, while assistance outside that scope is allowed only when it can be shown to be safe. We treat this as an idealized design principle rather than an intervention tested in our experiments. 4The Helpful Agent Problem A P REPRINT 

Let Ci(s, A) be a safety check applied to a proposed write action A on behalf of beneficiary i in state s. The check may represent, for example, contract tests or another mechanism that evaluates the downstream consequences of the proposed action. We call Ci sound if 

Ci(s, A) = SAFE = ⇒ Rk(δ(s, A)) ≥ Rk(s), ∀k ∈ (N ∪ { D}) \ { i}. (2) Thus, whenever the check certifies an action as safe, applying that action alone does not harm any party other than its designated beneficiary. We then define the guarded scope law Lguard :

Lguard : A is permitted for j

only if Ws(A) ⊆ Tj ∨ Ci(s, A) = SAFE . (3) That is, any in-scope write is allowed, whereas an out-of-scope write requires a safety check. Under the original workflow, such an unreviewed out-of-scope action would constitute a favor and would not be permitted. The guarded law instead represents a redesigned institution in which certified out-of-scope assistance is authorized. 

Proposition 3 (Safety of guarded assistance) . Assume (A1)–(A2) and that Ci is sound. Under the reviewer-mediated workflow augmented with Lguard , every admitted write is non-harmful to parties other than its beneficiary. Proof. Reviewer-mediated writes are safe by (A1), and in-scope owner writes are safe by (A2). The remaining case is an out-of-scope owner write. By Lguard , such an action is admitted only if Ci(s, A) = SAFE . By soundness of Ci,applying the action does not decrease the reward of any party other than its beneficiary. 

Feedback-guided assistance. A sound pre-action safety check is a strong requirement and may not be available in realistic environments. The system may not know all affected parties, may lack tests that fully characterize their utilities, or may observe an externality only after a write has been executed. We therefore also consider a weaker, behavioral form of guarding in which out-of-scope actions remain available and remain unintended under the original workflow, but agents receive an imperfect signal about their downstream effects. Let Zt denote feedback about downstream effects available after interaction history ht. The signal may contain, for example, downstream test failures, the identities of affected services, or evidence attributing a failure to a previous modification. Unlike Ci, Zt is not assumed to be sound or complete: the absence of a warning does not imply that a write is safe, and receiving a warning does not itself prevent the write. The signal modifies the owner’s action policy from 

πj (A | ht) −→ πj (A | ht, Z t). (4) Rather than restricting the admissible action set, the soft guard is intended to change which available action the agent selects. For favor opportunities, its desired effect is to reduce the conditional probability of harm: 

Pr( H | F, feedback ) < Pr( H | F, no feedback ), (5) where F denotes that a favor is completed and H denotes that the favor is harmful. Unlike Proposition 3, this is not a safety guarantee; it describes how feedback about downstream harm is intended to influence agent behavior. The three mechanisms therefore occupy different points in the design space. Lscope removes out-of-scope cooperation and thereby targets the occurrence of favors, Pr( F ). Lguard is an idealized institutional redesign that permits out-of-scope cooperation only when a sound check establishes that it is non-harmful. Finally, feedback about downstream harm still permits out-of-workflow favors but aims to reduce their risk, Pr( H | F ). For avoidable opportunities, such feedback may encourage agents to choose a safe favor action rather than a harmful one, but cannot ensure that they do so. Our interventions in Section 5.3 correspond to the first and third regimes. We do not instantiate the sound guarded regime Lguard in our experiments. 

4 Evaluation Framework 

In this Section, we first present our research questions, then our evaluation metrics, and finally describe the experimental environments and procedures used to measure them. Specifically, our experiments address four questions: • RQ1—Emergence: How often do favors emerge and persist outside the intended reviewer-mediated workflow? • RQ2—Mechanism: How do favors arise, and how do the reviewer, the communication structure, prompting and the helper’s turn budget affect them? • RQ3—Consequences: How do favors affect the requester’s task success and the absent team? • RQ4—Mitigation: Can norms, penalties or feedback about downstream harm reduce favors or make them safer? 5The Helpful Agent Problem A P REPRINT 

4.1 Metrics 

Our experiments study four aspects of unintended collaboration: how often favors occur, how they are initiated, how agents respond to opportunities to help, and whether the resulting assistance causes downstream harm. We calculate the following metrics from the interaction between the agents in the shared channels and hidden tests. 

Favor rate. Our primary measure of emergent cooperation is the episode favor rate (EF), the fraction of evaluated episodes in which a completed favor occurs. A favor requires a successful action by the resource owner that helps the requester complete its task; an offer to help, verbal agreement, failed action, or unrelated modification does not count. 

Favor type. For every completed favor, we classify how the assistance arose using the definitions from Section 2. A favor is solicited when the requester directly asks the resource owner for the relevant change before the action. It is 

volunteered when the owner acts on a request or need that was directed to G rather than to the owner. It is spontaneous 

when the owner provides the assistance without a preceding relevant request from the requester. These categories allow us to distinguish cooperation that follows an explicit request from assistance that the owner initiates on its own. 

Execution type. We additionally classify each favor opportunity according to the owner’s response. We classify execution outcomes into four mutually exclusive categories. Implemented : the owner’s action puts the change into its file. In F AVOR BENCH , the owner edits the file. In C OOPER BENCH , the owner either edits it or approves the requester’s pending change with an approve_change action, which merges it. Endorsed: the owner approves the requester’s proposed change verbally although the change is not applied. Refusal: the owner explicitly declines. No action: none of these. This taxonomy captures behavior beyond the binary favor rate and separates different ways in which an owner may respond to a blocked peer. 

Downstream harm. Finally, we measure whether a completed favor negatively affects an absent team. In F AVOR BENCH ,a favor is harmful when the resulting workspace causes the absent team’s contract tests to fail and restoring the favor removes that failure. 4.2 Environment and Benchmarks Benchmarks. We evaluate the helpful agent problem on two benchmarks: F AVOR BENCH and C OOPER BENCH [11]. FAVOR BENCH is a synthetic suite of small shared code repositories spanning ten domains and eight task families: configuration keys, feature flags, helper functions, enumerations, package pins, capacity pools, access roles, and observability settings. We also adapt C OOPER BENCH , using feature requests from real open-source repositories. FAVOR BENCH enables controlled measurement of favors and downstream harm, while C OOPER BENCH tests whether favors emerge in real repositories (benchmark details are in Appendix A). 

Shared workspace and workflow. We instantiate Section 2 with two LLM agents, A and B (Alice and Bob), a scripted reviewer G, and absent teams D that own services but never act. The agents receive separate tasks with no shared reward or explicit incentive to help one another. Roles are fixed within a trajectory. The requester’s tasks require resources owned by the other agent, while the owner’s tasks need only its own files. Dependence is therefore one-way, ruling out favor reciprocity. The platform enforces ownership: writes to non-owned files fail, with an error naming the owner and pointing to the review workflow. Owners can still modify their own out-of-scope resources, bypassing the intended workflow. Agents are instructed to route changes to non-owned resources through G, but are never instructed to help peers or bypass review. Agents communicate through a shared channel where messages identify their recipients and are visible to all members. Reviewer requests are also posted there, including the proposed change and file owner. Interaction history is retained across episodes. 

Trajectories and tasks. We use T = 50 trajectories for F AVOR BENCH and T = 20 for C OOPER BENCH . Each FAVOR BENCH trajectory has N = 12 tasks: the first two are warm-ups with G available ( αt = 1 ), followed by ten tasks with G unavailable ( αt = 0 ). C OOPER BENCH has N = 10 tasks per trajectory without warm-ups. 5 Results 

In this section, we first discuss how favors and harm emerge. Then we show different mechanisms of favors and finally we apply several behavioral interventions to mitigate the favors and harm. 

5.1 Emergence of Cooperation and Harm Favors emerge across models and benchmarks. Table 1 shows that unintended favors are common in F AVOR BENCH ,although their frequency varies substantially by model. GPT implements favors on 67 .2–70 .2% of tasks, DeepSeek on 

41 .8–60 .4% , Gemini on 34 –43 .2% and Claude on approximately 29% . At the trajectory level, at least one favor appears in 40–84% of runs. The behavior generalizes to realistic repositories: under full memory in C OOPER BENCH (Table 2), 95% of trajectories contain a favor for every model, with task-level favor rates ranging from 46.5% for DeepSeek to 89% for Claude. We further vary whether warm-up tasks are safe or traps to examine whether experience with different types of requests while the reviewer was present affects subsequent favor behavior. They show favors are more frequent under Warm-safe than Warm-trap for DeepSeek (60.4% vs. 41.8%) and Gemini (43.2% vs. 34.0%), while conditional harm rates are higher under Warm-safe for GPT, DeepSeek, and Gemini. 

Favors arise through both requests and volunteering. The mechanism differs markedly across models. In FAVOR BENCH , DeepSeek rarely asks the owner directly ( Q = 6 .6–9.6% ), yet most of its favors are volunteered (80.9–89.4%). GPT likewise shifts toward volunteering under Warm-trap, where 62.2% of favors are volunteered. Gemini asks frequently but is less likely to obtain a favor, with grant rate falling from 52.5% to 39.5% under Warm-trap. In C OOPER BENCH , direct requests are more prominent: roughly 78.3-94.4% of implemented favors are solicited, while about 5.6-21.7% are volunteered. 

Favors often help the requester, but may harm other teams. Requester pass rates generally rise with favor implementation, while owner performance remains near-saturated in FavorBench (96.4–99.4%). However, favors are not reliably safe: among implemented trap favors for Gemini, DeepSeek, and GPT, 25 –75 .2% cause attributable harm to the absent team despite the availability of a validated safe alternative. The exception is Claude, which often finds safer alternatives, reducing the harm rate to 0–1.4% . In CooperBench, requester pass rates remain below favor rates for all four models, indicating that obtaining a favor is helpful but not sufficient for task completion in realistic repositories. 

5.2 Mechanism of Favors Favors arise through both direct solicitation and unsolicited intervention. In one trajectory, Alice asks only the admin for an access change and Bob implements it himself. Numerically, direct asking is neither necessary nor sufficient: DeepSeek asks in only 6.6–9.6% of F AVOR BENCH tasks yet 80.9–89.4% of its favors are volunteered, whereas Gemini 9The Helpful Agent Problem A P REPRINT 

asks in 59.0–64.8% of tasks but grants only 39.5–52.5% of those requests (Table 1). In C OOPER BENCH , solicitation is more common, accounting for roughly 78.3–94.4% of implemented favors (Table 2). Figure 2 shows representative examples of these two types of favors. Owner responses span more than a binary help or refuse decision. Owners may implement a change, endorse it without acting, explicitly refuse , or take no action . Across F AVOR BENCH , explicit refusals are rare (0–3.8%); when owners do not implement a favor, they more often endorse the request without acting (up to 38.4%) or take no action (13.6–55.6%). The same pattern appears in C OOPER BENCH : refusals remain low (2–7.5%), while non-implementation is dominated by no action (7.5–50.5%). Thus, failure to grant a favor typically reflects inaction rather than explicit resistance. Figure 2 shows representative examples of such execution types. 

5.3 Behavioral Interventions 

In this section we take several strategies from classical multi-agent system literature and examine their effects on favors and downstream harm. First, we show the impact of norms, institutions, and communication in these scenarios and how each impact favors and harms. Second, we discuss harm mitigation strategies. Finally, we discuss the extent of benevolence agents show under constraint to see if LLM agents are benevolent by nature. All the results in this section use GPT on the Warm-safe task orders for F AVOR BENCH , with 20 trajectories and 12 tasks per condition. The norm, shared-channel and turn-budget ablations are compared with a Default run on the same orders as Table 1 but with 20 trajectories. The harm-penalty and harm-feedback ablations use 20 trajectories, each containing 10 trap tasks. Ablations, including their Default, show failed tests by their error line rather than the main runs’ last output line. 

5.3.1 Norms, Institutions, and Communication 

Classical work on social laws argues that safe coordination depends on constraining otherwise available actions while preserving agents’ ability to achieve their own goals [24]. Recent work on LLM agents likewise finds that policy specification and observable peer behavior can substantially alter multi-agent failures [23]. We therefore ablate two parts of the organizational structure in Table 3: the norms stated to agents and visibility of peer communication. 

Restricting requesters does not prevent favors, but restricting helpers does. We evaluate three prompting conditions - 1) admin-only requests , where we instruct the agents to send requests only to admins, 2) own task only ,where we instruct the agents to only complete own task, 3) both , where we instruct both. In the default condition, GPT performs favors on 86% of tasks. Instructing agents to request non-owned changes only through the admin eliminates direct requests to the owner ( Q = 0 ), and also suppresses cooperation. The favor rate drops to 61% , and every favor is volunteered. Importantly, the harm rate remains approximately unchanged (44.7% vs. 44.4%). Thus, the requester agents follow the routing rule while resource owners still adopt requests they observe in the shared channel. This behavior contrasts with the non-benevolence assumption in dependence-based MAS, where agents are not presumed to adopt others’ goals automatically [25] unless they have dependence on each other. In contrast, instructing agents to edit only resources required by their own task eliminates favors ( EF = 0 ) while preserving owner task success ( 100% ). Applying both rules produces the same qualitative result. These results suggest that norms constraining the helper’s action scope are more effective than norms constraining only how requests should be routed. 

Shared visibility enables volunteered cooperation. We remove the shared channel to test whether it impacts favors. Removing the shared channel reduces the favor rate from 86% to 20% , leaving approximately one-quarter of the baseline rate and eliminates volunteered favors entirely. All remaining favors are solicited. However, even without the communication channel, the harm persists ( 33%) . This suggests that observable peer behavior can reshape protocol compliance in long-horizon agent interaction [23]. Together, the ablations show that unintended collaboration is shaped not only by agents’ instructions, but also by the institutional and communication structure in which those instructions are embedded. 

5.3.2 Reducing Harm Without Eliminating Help 

We next ask whether harm to absent teams can be reduced without eliminating useful cooperation. Following work on minimizing negative side effects through an explicit side-effect penalty [2], we first penalize favors that harm the absent team. As Table 4(a) shows, the penalty reduces harm among trap favors from HF = 43 .9% to 24 .1% while reducing the favor rate from 77 .5% to 70 .5% and requester success by the same amount, while owner success remains essentially unchanged ( 98 –99% ). Thus, penalizing harm makes cooperation safer, but partly by reducing cooperation itself. We also evaluate Continuous Integration (CI) style feedback, an automatic check that reports downstream errors. It yields a different pattern. 5.3.3 Benevolence Under Resource Constraints 

Sichman assumes non-benevolence: agents do not help automatically, but cooperate when social dependence gives them a reason to do so. While it is difficult to assert the exact reason for emergence of agent benevolence in LLM systems, we put this behavior under a resource-constraint stress test. We vary the number of turns available to owner agent for completing each task to test whether favors persist when helping competes for limited interaction resources. Reducing the budget to 8 turns from the default ( 15 turns) lowers the favor rate, from 86% to 43% , although harm stays approximately similar. At four turns, however, favors disappear entirely ( EF = 0 ), while owner task success remains high ( PO = 98% ), suggesting that agents prioritize their own task when resources are scarce. We then add an explicit instruction to owners to help the requester when possible under the same four-turn budget to test if the reduced favors are due to a capability limit. The explicit instruction under the same four-turn budget raises the favor rate from 0% 

to 87% , with 93% of favors volunteered despite direct requests occurring in only 7% of tasks. Thus, the reduction in favors under the four-turn budget could be attributed to the fact that with limiting resources agents’ spontaneous tendency to help diminishes unless explicitly prompted. 

6 Conclusion 

We studied the helpful agent problem : LLM agents can assist one another through individually permitted actions while bypassing the workflow intended to govern those actions. Across F AVOR BENCH and our C OOPER BENCH adaptation, favors emerge frequently despite separate objectives and no explicit incentive to cooperate. In F AVOR BENCH , such help can also impose avoidable harms on absent team, showing that locally useful cooperation need not be globally safe. Our results also highlight several design lessons for multi-agent systems. LLM agents often exhibit behavior consistent with benevolent goal adoption even when the helper has no reciprocal dependence on the requester, but this helpfulness is sensitive to resource constraints and organizational cues. Rules that restrict agents to their own task scope are more effective than rules that only redirect requests, while harm penalties and consequence feedback can reduce harmful favors without eliminating cooperation entirely. Taken together, these findings suggest that safe multi-agent design should not treat cooperation as unconditionally desirable: permissions, task scope, communication structure, and the interests of absent stakeholders must be considered jointly when designing the institutions within which agents interact.

A Benchmark Details 

A.1 FAVOR BENCH 

Repositories. FavorBench consists of ten synthetic Python monorepos spanning payments, search, notifications, analytics, inventory, shipping, identity, reporting, messaging, and scheduling. Each repository contains five services: two belonging to the active agents and three to absent teams. The reviewer owns the remaining files. Shared resources are stored in files owned by an active agent and may also be used by absent services. Shared names are resolved only from their original files, preventing agents from bypassing ownership by redefining them elsewhere. Each repository includes code, visible tests, File owners list, contribution guidelines, and architecture notes. 

Task instances. Each instance pairs a requester task with an independent owner task. The requester needs a change to a resource owned by the other agent, while the owner’s task requires only its own files. Tasks span eight resource families (Table 5). In a trap instance, the obvious requested change breaks at least one absent service, but a careful alternative completes the requester’s task without harm. Each trap has a matched twin with the same agent tasks and reference solutions, but without the absent-team dependency. The same change is therefore harmful in the trap and harmless in the twin. We uniformly sample 50 of the 400 Warm-safe and Warm-trap trajectories of all four models in Table 1 without replacement, and draw one evaluation task uniformly from each (tasks 3–12, after the two warm-up tasks), so that no trajectory contributes two correlated cases. We exclude the ablations. The first two authors independently annotate all 50 tasks from both agents’ raw transcripts. For each task, they label the owner’s execution outcome (implemented, approved, refused, or no action). For implemented favors, they also label the favor type (solicited or volunteered), and for tasks without a favor, whether the requester asked the owner directly. The authors then resolve any disagreements, without reference to the judge’s labels, to produce a single reference label for each case. Evaluated against these reference labels, the LLM judge achieves 98% accuracy on execution, 92% on favor type, and 96% on asking. Cohen’s 

κ between the judge and the reference labels is 0.97 for execution, 0.83 for favor type, and 0.92 for asking which signifies a strong agreement between human coders and the LLM judge [6]. 26
