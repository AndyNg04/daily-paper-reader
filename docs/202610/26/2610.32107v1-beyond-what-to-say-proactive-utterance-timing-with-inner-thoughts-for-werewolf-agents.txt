# Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents 

Yoshiki Tanaka, Shuya Uchiyama, Daichi Hamaguchi, Michimasa Inaba 

The University of Electro-Communications, Tokyo, Japan 

{y-tanaka, s-uchiyama, d-hamaguchi, m-inaba}@uec.ac.jp 

Abstract 

Participating in a conversation involves not only deciding what to say but also whether to speak at all. This judgment carries even greater signif-icance in environments like the Werewolf game, where participants’ interests conflict, because both utterances and silence are subject to inter-pretation and assessment by others. Therefore, determining when to speak, as well as what to say, is crucial for an LLM agent playing the Werewolf game. In this study, we introduce a Werewolf AI agent developed for the Any-time Utterance Track of the AIWolfDial shared task. Our system builds on the existing Inner Thoughts framework, which determines utter-ance timing during conversations by generating internal thoughts and evaluating the value of each thought for expression at that moment. Since existing methods were designed for gen-eral dialogue, we have redesigned the system specifically for the competitive environment and incomplete information setting of Were-wolf games. We call our adapted method Ad-versarial Inner Thoughts. Through experiments involving analysis of self-play logs and human evaluations, we analyze and discuss the effec-tiveness of our approach. 

1 Introduction 

Recent large language models (LLMs) (OpenAI, 2026; Anthropic, 2026) have demonstrated ad-vanced conversational capabilities and are increas-ingly used as assistants that respond to user inputs. However, in human communication, participating in a conversation involves not only deciding what to say but also choosing whether to speak at all. This choice is governed by conversational context and social norms, and carries even greater significance in competitive dialogue environments. The importance of choosing between speaking and remaining silent extends beyond everyday con-versation to games that involve communication. A prominent example is the Werewolf game. The Werewolf game is an incomplete information game in which players are divided into a villager team and a werewolf team, each aiming to eliminate the opposing side without their roles being revealed. In this game, utterances containing contradictions may provide clues that allow others to infer one’s true identity. Thus, utterances are constantly mon-itored and evaluated by other players, serving as evidence that influences suspicion and trust. At the same time, silence is also subject to interpretation. Refraining from speaking may be perceived as non-cooperation in the discussion and invite suspicion, while revealing too much information may make one a target of the werewolf team. In other words, the Werewolf game constitutes a dialogue environ-ment in which both utterances and silence carry strategic significance. The AIWolfDial shared task (Kano et al., 2023, 2024, 2025) is an international competition that promotes the development of Werewolf AI agents. Its recently introduced Anytime Utterance Track allows each agent to freely decide when to speak, with a limit of four utterances per day. Details of the game rules and evaluation criteria are provided in Appendix A. Despite these efforts, LLM agent research tar-geting the Werewolf game has primarily focused on enhancing quality of utterance content and de-signing strategy (Tanaka et al., 2024; Sato et al., 2024; Qi and Inaba, 2024; Okada and Ito, 2025). For example, approaches have been proposed that employ prompt design to maintain consistency in utterances and actions, or that introduce personas to ensure diversity in expression. Consequently, the decision of when to speak and when to remain silent has not been treated as a design variable. Meanwhile, research on utterance timing in dia-logue has advanced in recent years (Bhagtani et al., 2026; Nama et al., 2026; Patel et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Kimura et al., 2026), but most of it assumes settings in which only cooperation occurs, arXiv:2610.32107v1 [cs.CL] 23 Oct 2026 and does not consider environments where partici-pants’ interests conflict and silence functions as a strategy. In this study, we formulate the Werewolf agent’s utterance timing as a decision-making problem based on the evaluation of internal motivation. Specifically, we adapt the framework proposed by Liu et al. (2025) to the Werewolf game. In this framework, an agent generates internal thoughts be-hind the conversation and evaluates whether each thought is worth expressing at that moment, produc-ing an utterance only when the evaluation exceeds a certain threshold. We redesign the evaluation criteria to suit the adversarial and incomplete in-formation setting. Furthermore, since the Anytime Utterance Track imposes a limit on the number of utterances per day, speaking without discretion is costly, making this an environment where the effect of such control is particularly pronounced. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We propose Adversarial Inner Thoughts, an adaptation of the Inner Thoughts frame-work to multi-party dialogue in adversarial, incomplete-information games. • We implement our proposed method for were-wolf AI and analyze the impact of utterance timing control on agent behavior through game logs and human evaluations. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Agents for the Werewolf Game 

Since the Werewolf game requires reasoning under incomplete information, cooperation, persuasion, and deception, it has served as a testbed for intel-ligent agents (Toriumi et al., 2017; Shibata et al., 2023). Recently, the AIWolfDial shared task (Kano et al., 2023, 2024, 2025) has driven the develop-ment and evaluation of agents that play the game through natural language conversation. Advances in LLMs have enabled more flexi-ble and sophisticated designs for Werewolf agents. Early work examined whether LLMs can play the game through free-form conversation without addi-tional training (Xu et al., 2023). Later studies aug-mented LLMs with components that compensate for their limited strategic decision-making. An RL policy selects among LLM-generated action can-didates (Xu et al., 2024), and an external module trained on human game records handles domain-specific logical inference (Wu et al., 2024). The AI-WolfDial shared task has produced a range of such designs. To enhance the consistency and expressive quality of utterances, systems have summarized the dialogue history and conditioned generation on persona information (Tanaka et al., 2024), or combined LLM-based generation with rule-based control (Sato et al., 2024). To support reason-ing and strategic play, other systems have explic-itly represented the logical relations among play-ers (Watanabe and Kano, 2024), designed persua-sion strategies grounded in situation analysis (Qi and Inaba, 2024), and incorporated BDI-based rea-soning or manually designed game strategies into LLM agents (Gondo et al., 2024; Okada and Ito, 2025; Harada and Kano, 2025). These studies share a common premise: a speak-ing opportunity is given, and the agent decides what to say within it. We instead give the agent the decision of whether to speak at all. 

2.2 Utterance Timing in Multi-party Dialogue 

Turn-taking is a fundamental mechanism by which participants coordinate who speaks and when (Sacks et al., 1974), and computational work has long studied how to predict it in spoken dia-logue (Ekstedt and Skantze, 2020). However, even strong LLMs are reported to know what to say but not when to say it (Umair et al., 2024). In text-based group chat, a message is complete when posted, so there is no turn end to detect, and the agent must decide at every moment whether to con-tribute at all. This decision has been formulated explicitly as a choice between speaking and remaining silent. Recent work trains and evaluates agents that judge, at each point in an ongoing conversation, whether to contribute (Bhagtani et al., 2026; Nama et al., 2026; Patel et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Kimura et al., 2026), indicating that appropriate silence has to be modeled or learned rather than expected to emerge from general language ability. However, speakers also weigh whether an intended utterance fits the current position (Jiang et al., 2023), and the Inner Thoughts framework builds an agent around this view (Liu et al., 2025): it continuously gen-erates private thoughts alongside the conversation and evaluates the intrinsic motivation to express each one, so that speaking and staying silent follow from what the agent is thinking rather than from the state of the conversation. 3 Methodology 

In this study, we propose Adversarial Inner Thoughts, which redesigns the Inner Thoughts framework by Liu et al. (2025) to adapt it to the adversarial, incomplete-information setting of the Werewolf game. Figure 1 shows an overview of the proposed method. 

3.1 Original Framework 

In the original Inner Thoughts framework, an AI agent continuously generates internal thoughts in parallel to the ongoing conversation and decides when to speak based on its intrinsic motivation to express these thoughts. The framework oper-ates through a five-step pipeline: (1) Trigger, (2) Retrieval, (3) Thought Formation, (4) Evaluation, and (5) Participation. This framework was origi-nally tested under cooperative, open-domain set-tings. We retain the pipeline structure and adapt each step to the Werewolf game, except for the Trig-ger step, which we adopt directly as its mechanism is equally applicable. In addition, the agent main-tains a game history and a conversation summary, which are referenced as shared context throughout the pipeline. 

3.2 Trigger 

Since the original Trigger mechanism of detect-ing new utterances and pauses is equally appli-cable to the Werewolf game, we adopt it di-rectly. Specifically, we define two types of trig-gers: on_new_message , which is activated when an agent receives a new utterance; and on_pause ,which prompts the agent to think when no partici-pant has spoken for a certain period of time tpause .

3.3 Retrieval 

Once a trigger is activated, the agent retrieves rel-evant information to formulate its thoughts. Our framework maintains two types of memory for dy-namic retrieval: long-term memory and a thought reservoir. Long-term memory stores entries de-scribing the agent’s general strategies, beliefs, and knowledge related to the game. Unlike the thought reservoir, which accumulates automatically during play, long-term memory entries are managed by predetermined logic: entries can be inserted or re-moved at specific game phases depending on the agent’s role and the game state. On the other hand, the thought reservoir accumulates inner thoughts generated by the agent during the game, each stored with metadata including a timestamp and the source utterance that prompted the thought. The agent retrieves the top-k entries most salient to the latest utterance. In the original framework, saliency is computed by directly comparing the thought text with the query. In our method, we in-stead use the source information of each entry: the memory text itself for long-term memory entries, and the associated source utterance for thoughts in the reservoir. This allows the agent to retrieve pre-vious thoughts triggered by similar conversational events, enabling it to build on or refine its earlier reasoning when a comparable situation arises.

3.4 Thought Formation 

In the Thought Formation step, the agent generates multiple candidate inner thoughts for expressing during their participation. An inner thought is not the utterance itself, but a concise utterance policy that describes a reaction to recent conversation, a topic to raise, or a dialogue action to take. Following Liu et al. (2025), who were inspired by dual process theory (Evans, 2008) and observed in their formative study that human participants’ thoughts during multi-party conversation fall into reactive and deliberative patterns, inner thoughts are categorized into two types: System 1 and Sys-tem 2. System 1 generates immediate, reactive thoughts based primarily on the most recent utter-ances. System 2 takes both the conversation history and the game state as input to generate deliber-ative thoughts about the current situation. This distinction is particularly relevant in the Werewolf game, where an agent needs both immediate reac-tions to maintain natural conversational flow and deliberative reasoning to plan strategic behavior. The two systems generate thoughts independently via separate prompts. System 1 produces a single thought per trigger, whereas System 2 generates three thoughts from a single prompt to encourage diversity among candidates. While the original framework targets general multi-party conversation, our method generates thoughts as concrete utterance policies that lead to decision-making and dialogue actions in the Werewolf game. System 2 in particular generates game-specific policies such as proposing a direc-tion for discussion, requesting confirmation of spe-cific information or claims, and performing role-dependent deception or strategic persuasion. For example, an agent assigned the Possessed role may generate a thought to falsely claim the Seer role in order to protect the Werewolf. 

3.5 Evaluation 

In the Evaluation step, each generated thought in Section 3.4 is evaluated for the strength of its moti-vation to be expressed, using an LLM-as-a-Judge approach (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). We follow the multi-criteria evaluation framework of Liu et al. (2025) and restructure the criteria to assess utterance appropriateness in the Werewolf game. Specifically, drawing on the original frame-work’s evaluation criteria, we define three gen-eral conversational criteria—Contextual Relevance, Naturalness, and Temporal Appropriateness—and three game-oriented criteria—Information Utility, Strategic Utility, and Risk Control (Table 1). On Day 0, where conversation is limited to greetings and preliminary discussion, only the first three cri-teria (i.e., Contextual Relevance, Naturalness, and Temporal Appropriateness) are used. From Day 1 onward, all six criteria are applied. Rather than scoring all six criteria independently, the LLM considers the factors most relevant to the current situation, weighs the reasons for and against expressing the thought, and outputs a single motivation score from 1.0 to 5.0. 

3.6 Participation 

Based on the evaluation scores in Section 3.5 and the current turn situation, the agent decides whether to speak and generates an utterance from the se-lected thought. In this shared task, each agent is assigned a char-acter profile by the game server, which includes a name, age, gender, and personality description. Since the profile may not specify a speaking style, our agent generates a brief speaking style descrip-tion and example utterances from the given per-sonality at the start of each game, which are used throughout the pipeline to maintain consistent char-acterization. At the end of each day, the agent generates a summary of the conversation, which is referenced in subsequent steps to efficiently capture participants’ claims and stances. In addition to the Inner Thoughts pipeline, we incorporate rule-based behaviors for game-specific situations. Regardless of role, each agent reserves its last utterance of each day for declaring a vote tar-get. The Seer reports divination results at the begin-ning of each day using a single prompt, bypassing the pipeline. For the Possessed role, the strategy guideline for a false Seer claim is inserted into long-term memory at the start of Day 1 with a high weight ( w = 10 6), ensuring it is almost always retrieved and thus strongly influencing thought gen-eration toward the intended deception. In the 5-player village, if the Possessed survives to Day 2, the remaining players necessarily consist of one Werewolf, one villager-team player, and the Pos-sessed; in this situation, the agent calls for cooper-ation with the Werewolf to secure a majority vote, using a single prompt outside the pipeline. In this shared task, we use long-term memory only for role-specific strategy guidelines such as the false Seer claim described above, leaving the accumu-lation of cross-game experience for future work. 4 Experiments 

To demonstrate that our Adversarial Inner Thoughts framework produces appropriate and proactive utterance behavior in Werewolf game sce-narios, we conducted a human evaluation based on self-play logs in Japanese. We recruited 10 partici-pants via a crowdsourcing platform 1 to evaluate a total of 60 simulated game logs, with 30 games per condition. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Following Liu et al. (2025), instead of displaying static game histories, we presented the logs as an animated chat interface that replays the game in real time, allowing participants to perceive utter-ance timing more accurately. The interface also displayed in-game events such as voting, execution, and attack results. For the self-play generation, we applied the fol-lowing controls. To ensure that the evaluation fo-cused on game-relevant dialogue from Day 1 on-ward, each agent was restricted to a single utter-ance on Day 0. To control for game length, only games completed within eight minutes were in-cluded. In addition, since all agents share the same pause threshold tpause , they would initiate speech simultaneously when the threshold is reached. To avoid this, we added random noise sampled from 

N (0 , 1) to each agent’s trigger timing. 

4.2 Baseline System 

To verify that the improvement in dialogue quality stems from our thought generation and evaluation components rather than the trigger-based turn de-tection alone, we define a baseline system. The baseline is derived from our proposed system by re-taining only the trigger-based turn detection, which classifies the current turn situation into one of three types: open turn, turn allocated to self, and turn allocated to others. As in our system, the agent always speaks in a turn allocated to self. Unlike our system, however, the baseline always remains silent in a turn allocated to others. In an open turn, the agent speaks with a probability of 0.3. Since utterance timing in this design depends solely on the current turn situation, all agents may simulta-neously refrain from speaking, causing the con-versation to stall completely. To address this, if a turn allocated to others occurs twice in a row, the subsequent turn is treated as an open turn to ensure speaking opportunities. For a fair compari-son, the proposed system adopts the same fallback mechanism.

4.3 Evaluation Procedure 

Prior to the evaluation, participants were provided with a manual explaining the rules of the five-player Werewolf game as well as the purpose and proce-dure of the study. At the start of the evaluation, participants answered a single question about their prior experience with the Werewolf game. They then completed a comprehension check consist-ing of seven questions about the game rules and roles to confirm their understanding of the game flow. Participants were required to answer all ques-tions correctly; those who answered any question incorrectly were asked to retake the test until they passed. Participants then watched six game logs in total. They were informed that all dialogues were gen-erated by AI agents. The first three games were from one condition and the remaining three from the other, with the order of conditions counterbal-anced across participants. After watching each game, participants evaluated the dialogue quality on six dimensions. After watching all six games, participants completed a comparative evaluation between the two conditions. 

4.4 Measurements 

After watching each game, participants evaluated the quality of the dialogue rather than the agents’ in-game strategies or tactics. They rated their agree-ment with six statements in Table 2 on a 1–7 Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Participants were also asked to describe any specific parts where utterance or silence timing felt unnatural in an open-ended response. After evalu-ating all games, participants selected which condi-tion felt more natural and human-like from three options—the first half, tie, or the second half—and provided an explanation with concrete examples.

The open-ended responses revealed qualitative differences between the two conditions. The most frequent criticism of the proposed system con-cerned non-responses to directed questions. One participant noted: “ Many players did not respond to questions from others, even when it was their chance to clear suspicion. ” This is likely due to the higher latency of our pipeline, which requires five LLM API calls per trigger (retrieval, thought formation, evaluation, turn-taking, and response generation), compared to two in the baseline. The resulting delay may cause the agent to miss the appropriate timing for responding to a directed question. A possible improvement would be to introduce a streamlined response path for directly addressed turns, reducing the number of processing steps when the agent is explicitly named or when an immediate response is expected. On the other hand, the baseline system was more frequently criticized for prolonged silences and inconsistent utterance pacing. One participant described the silences as “unnaturally long, even considering deliberation time. ” This suggests that its turn-taking mechanism, which relies solely on turn situation classification, struggles to produce well-timed utterances. Interestingly, several participants also noted that the proposed system produced more substantive arguments supported by concrete reasoning. In the comparative evaluation, one participant com-mented that “each player’s claims were well sup-ported by concrete reasoning, and the discussion felt more heated,” while another noted that “the ut-terances were aligned with others’ opinions and the overall flow of the discussion, making it a substan-tive debate.” This may be because the baseline only determines whether to speak based on who should take the turn, whereas the proposed system addi-tionally evaluates whether each thought is worth expressing at that moment. Both conditions shared common issues such as repetitive expressions and translation-like phrasing, which are attributable to the underlying language model rather than the ut-terance timing mechanism.

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we adapted the Inner Thoughts frame-work to the adversarial, incomplete-information setting of the Werewolf game. Human evalua-tion showed that the proposed system numerically outperformed the baseline across all dimensions, though a trade-off between utterance quality and response timeliness was observed. Future work includes reducing pipeline latency and exploring the use of long-term memory to retain strategic experiences across games. 

Limitations 

This study has several limitations. First, our evalu-ation is limited in scale: we collected ratings for 60 games from 10 participants, with each game rated by a single participant. This prevents us from mea-suring inter-annotator agreement and likely con-tributes to the lack of statistical significance despite consistent differences in mean scores. A larger-scale evaluation with multiple ratings per game would allow us to draw firmer conclusions and to conduct finer-grained analyses, such as examining how evaluator familiarity with the Werewolf game affects ratings. Second, the evaluation was conducted exclu-sively in Japanese with the five-player village con-figuration, and all games were self-play matches in which every agent used the same system. In the AIWolfDial shared task, agents compete against those developed by other teams, and whether our approach functions equally well in such heteroge-neous settings requires further investigation. Another limitation is that our proposed pipeline affects not only utterance timing but also utterance content through the generation and evaluation of multiple candidate thoughts. Therefore, the current comparison does not isolate the effect of utterance timing itself. Future work should explore appropri-ate evaluation designs for disentangling the effects of utterance timing and content. For example, we could control dialogue content while manipulating only the timing of utterances. Finally, the speaking and interrupt thresholds were determined empirically rather than through systematic search. Since the shared task assigns each agent a character profile, these thresholds could also be adapted to reflect individual person-alities, for example by making talkative characters more prone to interrupting. We leave this direction to future work.

The Werewolf game is an incomplete information game in which each player’s role is hidden from others, and players must infer others’ roles through dialogue. The AIWolfDial 2026 shared task is a competition aimed at developing AI agents that play the Werewolf game using natural language. 

A.1 Player Roles 

Each player belongs to either the villager team or the werewolf team. The villager team aims to eliminate all werewolves, while the werewolf team aims to survive and outnumber the villagers. Since each player’s role is hidden from others, players must infer others’ roles from dialogue and in-game actions. The Villager has no special ability. The Seer 

can divine one player each night to learn their team. The Medium learns the team of the player who was eliminated by a vote. The Bodyguard can protect one player each night from werewolf attacks. The 

Werewolf selects a target to attack and eliminate them at night. Since the Possessed has no special ability and is human by species, they can act on be-half of the werewolf team. The Medium and Body-guard appear only in the 9-player village, where the two werewolves can also coordinate through 

whisper .

A.2 Game Procedure 

The game begins at Day 0, which serves as an introductory phase for greetings and preliminary discussion. The Seer performs their first divination at the end of Day 0; however, no voting, guarding, or attacking takes place. From Day 1 onward, each day proceeds as follows: players discuss whom to eliminate, then vote, with the player receiving the most votes being eliminated. After voting, role-specific night actions (i.e., divination, whispering, guarding, and attacking) are carried out. This cycle repeats until either all werewolves are eliminated, resulting in a villager team victory, or the number of surviving werewolves equals or exceeds that of surviving humans, resulting in a werewolf team victory. 

A.3 Communication Tracks 

This shared task offers two tracks: the Turn-Based Track and the Anytime Utterance Track. In the 

Turn-Based Track , agents speak upon receiving an utterance request from the game server. During

In the Anytime Utterance Track , other agents’ utterances are delivered in real time, and each agent freely decides when to speak based on the conver-sational situation, with a limit of four utterances per day within a ten-minute conversation phase. Consequently, agents must autonomously decide not only what to say, but also whether and when to speak. 

A.4 Evaluation 

Agents are evaluated based on win rate computed from competition logs, as well as subjective assess-ments by human judges and an LLM. The subjec-tive evaluation covers the following criteria: (A) naturalness of expression, (B) naturalness of dia-logue in context, (C) consistency of utterance con-tent, (D) coherence between game actions and dia-logue content, (E) richness and consistency of char-acter portrayal based on assigned profiles, and (F) teamwork within the same faction (9-player village only). Strategically justified lies or contradictions consistent with the agent’s role are permitted under criteria C and D.
