# Tactile In-Hand Object Rotation: A Comparative Study of Hand Morphology in Simulation and Hardware 

Wenhao He, Max Yang, Chenghua Lu, Yijiong Lin, David A.W. Barton, Nathan F. Lepora, 

Abstract — Continuous in-hand rotation requires coordinated contact maintenance and renewal, linking manipulation per-formance to both hand geometry and tactile feedback. We in-vestigate these relationships through rotational progress, finger participation, and fingertip loading on two versions of the low-cost LEAP Hand with anthropomorphic and symmetric finger arrangements. Both hands are integrated with TacTip vision-based tactile sensors and use a shared reinforcement learning framework. Simulation evaluates full tactile, binary touch, and proprioception-only observations on 12 unseen objects; hard-ware experiments evaluate full tactile policies on ten objects under multiple hand orientations. In simulation, full tactile observations increase mean normalized rotation per episode over proprioception alone by 94.8% for the anthropomorphic hand and 66.9% for the symmetric hand, with longer mean episode durations and lower mean total fingertip loads. Hard-ware contact measurements reveal more differentiated finger participation in the anthropomorphic hand, while cumulative loading is more evenly distributed across the symmetric hand’s fingers. Overall, the symmetric hand achieves a 32.0% higher mean rotation count and a 25.8% higher aggregate rotation rate, together with longer manipulation duration. Additional hardware force recordings show a 19.9% lower mean total fingertip load for this layout. These findings relate finger arrangement to contact organization and load sharing, and show that richer tactile information supports greater task progress at lower mean loading in both simulated embodiments. 

I. INTRODUCTION A robotic hand’s geometry determines where its fingertips can reach and how they approach an object. During sustained rotation, these geometric constraints interact with the need to support the object, permit motion, and renew contact under different gravity directions. The forces carried by the fingertips and the distribution of contact changes across fingers characterize how these interactions develop during manipulation. Learning-based controllers have enabled con-tinuous fingertip rotation using proprioceptive and tactile observations [1]–[3], providing an experimental basis for examining these relationships across hand layouts. The question considered here is how finger arrangement changes both the organization of fingertip contact and load-ing and the benefit obtained from tactile feedback. Similar task scores can arise from different distributions of persistent contact, contact-state changes, and accumulated fingertip load. Examining these quantities together provides a basis for interpreting how each layout performs the task. Comparing full tactile, binary touch, and proprioception-only obser-vations then establishes how task progress, duration, and

We study anthropomorphic and symmetric configurations of the low-cost LEAP Hand [4], each with 16 actuated joints and 4 integrated vision-based (TacTip) tactile fingertips [5]. We adopt the task and reinforcement learning framework of AnyRotate [3] as a shared experimental protocol. The comparison uses a common tactile observation interface and learning procedure to examine differences across finger ar-rangements and observation conditions. Simulation evaluates the three observation conditions on 12 unseen objects with randomized hand orientations and rotation axes. The results associate the two layouts with distinct patterns of finger participation and cumulative loading. On hardware, the anthropomorphic thumb and middle finger maintain contact more persistently and contribute a larger share of accumulated load, with more frequent contact changes at the index and little fingers. The symmetric hand distributes participation more evenly, and its cumulative loads are more uniformly shared in both simulation and hardware. In the simulation comparison, richer tactile observations improve mean normalized rotation and manipulation duration while reducing mean fingertip loading in both hands. The main contributions of this work are: 1) We compare two hand morphologies under three obser-vation conditions, quantifying changes in normalized rotation, manipulation duration, and mean fingertip loading on unseen simulated objects. 2) We relate fingertip workspace arrangement to hardware contact persistence and transitions, and to cumulative load sharing in simulation and hardware, identifying differentiated and more evenly distributed patterns of finger contribution. 3) We implement two low-cost LEAP–TacTip hand con-figurations and evaluate their continuous fingertip ro-tation on ten physical objects under multiple hand orientations, combining manipulation outcomes with complementary contact and loading measurements. Hand Design and Comparative Evaluation. Compara-tive studies assess in-hand dexterity through manipulation tasks and measures of hand capability. The Elliott and Connolly Benchmark provides a common set of in-hand manipulation tasks [16], while KaRMA compares feasible translation and reorientation through rolling contact in a two-finger pinch across 16 hand designs [17]. Kinematic capability and executed manipulation provide complementary views of hand design. Reachable motions describe the opportunities afforded by geometry; contact persistence, contact changes, and accumulated loading describe how the fingers participate as a task is performed. This distinction motivates assessing workspace arrangement together with measured interaction and task outcomes. III. M ETHODS 

A. Task Definition 

We consider the AnyRotate task [3], which requires the hand to continuously rotate an object about a commanded rotation axis through fingertip manipulation under different hand orientations relative to gravity. Starting from an initial grasp, the policy sequentially tracks orientation targets sepa-rated by 30°. Target attainment is evaluated using six virtual keypoints located along the positive and negative directions of the three axes of the object coordinate frame. A subgoal is considered completed when the sum of Euclidean distances between the six corresponding object–target keypoint pairs does not exceed 0.015 m, thereby accounting for both posi-tion and orientation. Upon completion, the target advances without resetting the grasp. An episode terminates when the pose error becomes excessive, the rotation-axis deviation exceeds 45 ◦, or all fingertip contacts are lost. B. Hand Embodiments 

To study how hand morphology affects tactile in-hand rotation performance, we compare two customized LEAP Hand embodiments equipped with TacTip fingertips: an anthropomorphic hand and a symmetric hand (Fig. 1). Both have four fingers and 16 actuated joints, but differ in finger arrangement and fingertip orientation. 

1) Anthropomorphic LEAP Hand: The Anthropomorphic LEAP Hand (top left of Fig. 1) is derived from the orig-inal CMU LEAP Hand [4]. It preserves the approximately anthropomorphic arrangement of three adjacent fingers and an opposing thumb, while adding TacTip tactile sensors to all fingertips. The fingertip sensing surfaces are oriented toward the central manipulation workspace: the index and little fingertips are yawed inward toward the middle finger, the middle fingertip remains aligned with the palm-forward direction, and the thumb fingertip is oriented between the thumb-forward and palm-forward directions. Together, these fingertip orientations define a partially enclosed manipulation region above the palm. 

2) Symmetric LEAP Hand: The Symmetric LEAP Hand (bottom left of Fig. 1) is a task-oriented reconfiguration of the same LEAP platform. Its palm adopts an octagonal geometry, and the four fingers are mounted on four non-adjacent long edges so that opposing finger pairs are approximately orthog-onal in the palm plane. Unlike the Anthropomorphic LEAP Hand, all fingertip sensing surfaces are aligned with the palm-forward direction. This arrangement distributes the four fingers more symmetrically around the central manipulation region.

3) Teacher–Student Learning and Curricula: We use the teacher–student structure for policy training. The teacher combines policy observations with an eight-dimensional encoding of privileged simulator information. The MLP encoder and policy are jointly trained using proximal policy optimization (PPO) [19]. Performance-based curricula scale gravity and auxiliary reward weights from zero to their full values as rotational performance improves. The student inherits the teacher’s actor parameters and estimates the latent from a 30-step rolling history of ob-servations and commands using a temporal convolutional encoder. The teacher remains frozen while the student actor and history encoder are jointly trained on student-generated rollouts using latent and action distillation with entropy regularization. Student training uses full gravity and auxiliary reward weights. 

4) Domain Randomization: Object geometry and dimen-sions are sampled from the training object collection, while object mass, center-of-mass offset, and PD gains are random-ized across parallel environments. At episode reset, initial hand–object states are sampled from cached grasps, hand orientation is randomized, and commanded rotation axes are sampled over the unit sphere. Random noise is added to joint-position and tactile contact-pose observations. Intermittent mass-scaled Gaussian forces with exponential decay further perturb the object during rollouts.

A. Simulation Training and Experiments 

We train teacher and student policies in Isaac Gym [21] with a simulation timestep of 1/60 s and a policy control frequency of 20 Hz. The training objects comprise cuboids and ellipsoids of varying sizes, and domain randomization is applied following Sec. III-C. To reduce the sim-to-real discrepancy in hand dynam-ics, we perform trajectory-based system identification. The identification recordings include joint trajectories collected under multiple hand orientations relative to gravity. For the symmetric hand, they include configurations with each of the four fingers pointing downward, covering the corre-sponding changes in gravitational loading. Recorded joint commands are replayed in simulation, and the covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) [22] opti-mizes PD gains, link masses, joint friction, and armature parameters to minimize the discrepancy between simulated and recorded joint trajectories. Simulation evaluation uses 12 held-out object models excluded from training (Fig. 3). For each hand and observation condition—full tactile, binary touch, or proprioception only—the trained student policy is evaluated in 64 parallel environments with randomly sampled objects, hand orientations, and commanded rotation axes. Reported means are cumulative averages over completed episodes lasting more than 20 control steps, pooled across the 64 parallel environments. We report the values after approximately 200 evaluation iterations, when the monitored means have stabilized. Episodes last up to 600 control steps (30 s), subject to the termination criteria in Sec. B. Real-World Experiments 

Both hands are mounted on a common fixture supporting repeatable orientations relative to gravity (Fig. 2) and use the tactile perception pipeline described in Sec. III-D. Full tactile policies are evaluated on ten physical objects spanning dif-ferent shapes, sizes, aspect ratios, and masses (Fig. 3), with five trials per object and hand orientation. Trials command rotation about the palm-aligned axis and last up to 30 s. Rotation count is estimated visually in revolutions. Ma-nipulation duration is the time until the object is dropped, becomes stuck, or the rollout otherwise becomes invalid; trials reaching the horizon are assigned 30 s. Results are reported as mean and standard deviation. Overall rotation count and duration are calculated by equally weighting the orientation-group means, and overall rotation rate is their ratio.

D. Implications for Hand Design and Tactile Control 

These findings suggest an evaluation sequence for hand design. Workspace maps first describe the regions reachable by the fingertips. Finger-level recordings then identify how the controller distributes persistent contact, contact changes, and loading within that workspace. For example, a modi-fication to finger placement can be assessed by whether it alters these distributions and whether the altered interaction accompanies greater rotational progress or longer manipula-tion. This connects a geometric design change to observable behavior and a task outcome. Selection of tactile inputs can be evaluated in the same way. For the evaluated task, the results support retaining contact-state information and show an additional benefit from the combined contact-pose and force features for both layouts. Similar gains in normalized rotation can accompany different reductions in mean load. Comparing progress, du-ration, and loading together provides a basis for assessing what a sensing choice contributes on each hand. VII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This study compared two LEAP-based finger arrange-ments using a common tactile interface and teacher–student learning framework. In simulation, binary touch improves mean normalized rotation and manipulation duration over proprioception alone, and full tactile observations provide further gains while reducing mean fingertip loading. The additional gains in mean normalized rotation from contact-pose and force features are similar across the two layouts, despite their different patterns of finger participation. Hardware contact measurements reveal more persistent contributions from the anthropomorphic thumb and middle finger, with more frequent contact changes at the index and little fingers. The symmetric hand shows more evenly distributed contact participation on hardware and cumulative load sharing in both domains. It also achieves a higher rotation count and rotation rate, together with longer ma-nipulation duration, in the hardware performance evaluation. Additional hardware force recordings show lower mean total fingertip loading for this layout. These findings motivate evaluating geometric reachability together with observed finger contributions, achieved task progress, and loading during manipulation. Future work will extend the comparison to additional hand layouts and tasks, evaluate reduced tactile observations on hardware, and combine object-pose tracking with contact-resolved force measurements to examine how contact re-newal contributes to rotational motion.
