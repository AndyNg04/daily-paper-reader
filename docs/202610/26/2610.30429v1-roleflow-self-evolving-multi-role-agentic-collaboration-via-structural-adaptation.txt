Abstract 

Large language model (LLM) based multi-agent systems often rely on predefined col-laboration structures, limiting their adaptabil-ity to evolving task requirements. Although recent approaches enable dynamic orchestra-tion, trajectory-level rewards they apply as-sign one outcome to each decision, ofen strug-gling to identify the contributions of individual structural changes. We propose RoleFlow , a self-evolving multi-role agentic system that re-fines collaboration structures through feedback-driven atomic edits and on-demand role syn-thesis. To make structural adaptation learnable, we introduce Single-Edit Counterfactual Pol-icy Optimization (SECPO) , which evaluates each edit against its no-edit counterpart at the same chain state and assigns targeted credit to edit-specific tokens. Across seven benchmarks spanning four domains, RoleFlow achieves the optimal average performance, surpassing the strongest competitor by 4.7 points. It leads on all three multi-hop QA benchmarks by 3.2– 7.5 F1 points and also shows supremacy on mathematical reasoning, web interaction, and code generation. Further analyses demonstrate cross-benchmark transferability, training-data efficiency, and cross-executor generalization. 

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) based multi-agent systems have shown capabilities in solving com-plex tasks through specialized agent collabora-tion (Durante et al., 2025; Ghareeb et al., 2026). However, existing approaches typically rely on pre-defined or pre-optimized collaboration structures that remain fixed during execution. Such structures struggle to accommodate evolving task require-ments, where effective collaboration may demand adding, removing, or reorganizing specialized roles.

This raises a fundamental question: How can multi-role agentic systems autonomously evolve their col-laboration structures during task execution? 

Recent studies have explored reinforcement learning for dynamic agent orchestration (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Nie et al., 2025). How-ever, learning structural adaptation introduces a critical challenge: credit assignment for individual structural decisions . Conventional methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) rely on trajectory-level rewards, assign-ing the same advantage to decisions within a roll-out regardless of their individual contributions. As shown in Fig. 1, when collaboration structures un-dergo successive modifications, this coarse-grained feedback cannot reliably identify which edits im-prove performance and which undermine it. 

Our key insight is that reliable structural evolu-tion requires making each structural modification individually assessable and learnable. Rather than treating an evolving collaboration structure as a 1arXiv:2610.30429v1 [cs.AI] 21 Oct 2026 sequence of decisions optimized only through its final outcome, we decompose structural adaptation into atomic edits and evaluate each edit against an unchanged counterpart under the same chain state. This isolates the immediate contribution of a struc-tural decision and provides a localized learning signal, allowing the system to learn not only how to modify collaboration structures but also which modifications are worth making .Motivated by this insight, we propose RoleFlow ,a self-evolving multi-role collaboration framework that continuously adapts agent structures through execution feedback. A trainable Global Router (GR) constructs task-specific role chains and re-fines them through atomic structural edits, while synthesizing specialized roles on demand. To op-timize these decisions, we introduce Single-Edit Counterfactual Policy Optimization (SECPO) ,which compares executions with and without each candidate edit under the same chain state and incor-porates the resulting advantage into edit-specific token optimization. RoleFlow further maintains a return-gated role library to retain useful syn-thesized specialists during training. Experiments across seven benchmarks spanning four domains demonstrate the effectiveness of RoleFlow, achiev-ing the highest average performance among evalu-ated multi-agent systems. 

Our contributions are threefold: 

• We introduce RoleFlow , a self-evolving multi-role agentic system that enables feedback-driven structural adaptation through atomic edits and on-demand specialist synthesis. • We propose SECPO , a structure-aware pol-icy optimization method that estimates the counterfactual contribution of individual struc-tural edits and assigns targeted credit to edit-specific tokens. • Experiments across seven benchmarks span-ning four domains show that RoleFlow achieves the highest average performance. Further analyses validate structural adapta-tion, edit-level counterfactual learning, cross-benchmark transferability, training-data effi-ciency, and cross-executor generalization. 

2 Related Work 

Handcrafted Multi-Agent Structure and Content-Level Adaptation. Existing multi-agent systems often rely on fixed collaboration structures, such as the predefined pipeline in ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024), sequential agents in Chain-of-Agents (Zhang et al., 2024b), and graph-based retrieval in LinearRAG (Zhuang et al., 2025). Other approaches adapt the information processed within a fixed structure through retrieval or tool use (Lewis et al., 2020; Schick et al., 2023; Song et al., 2025). While effective at content-level adaptation, these methods do not learn to modify agent roles or their interactions in response to execution feedback. 

Self-Evolving Agents and Workflow Search. 

Existing self-evolving agents primarily optimize model behavior within fixed roles, as in Multi-Agent Evolve (Chen et al., 2025). Alternatively, workflow optimization methods search for effec-tive collaboration structures through Monte Carlo tree search (AFlow) (Zhang et al., 2024a), learned graph connections (GPTSwarm) (Zhuge et al., 2024), offline workflow generation (W4S) (Nie et al., 2025), or task-specific architecture genera-tion (MaAS and MAS-GPT) (Zhang et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025). However, these methods deter-mine the collaboration structure before execution, limiting their ability to address emerging task re-quirements through online structural refinement. 

Online Structural Adaptation and Credit As-signment. Recent approaches incorporate ex-ecution feedback into agent orchestration, in-cluding AgentFlow’s learned planner (Li et al., 2025), AgentConductor’s dynamic topology (Wang et al., 2026), and FlowSteer’s atomic workflow ed-its (Zhang et al., 2026a). However, their trajectory-level rewards provide coarse-grained credit across structural decisions (Shao et al., 2024). Coun-terfactual credit assignment has been explored in COMA (Foerster et al., 2018), while COLLA-TOR (Chen et al., 2026), SERO (Ge and Guo, 2026), and EvoSteer (Zhang et al., 2026b) eval-uate individual decisions using different references. Unlike these approaches, RoleFlow evaluates each structural edit against its no-edit counterpart at the same chain state. Its SECPO assigns the resulting counterfactual advantage specifically to edit-related tokens, enabling fine-grained credit assignment for evolving collaboration structures.

RoleFlow evolves a chain within a task rather than fixing its structure before execution. The GR first writes a plan that decomposes the task and names the roles of RFC 0. After each rejected output, it uses the deficit dt and accumulated memory σt to propose one structural edit. The edited chain is then executed in the next cycle, and the process stops upon validation success or after Tmax adap-tation cycles (Figure 2). This one-edit-per-cycle design ensures that consecutive chains differ by at most one edit; applying several edits at once would confound their individual effects. The available roles are not restricted to a fixed predefined set. If the GR names a role absent from

At inference, each task starts with the domain’s seed library rather than the library grown during training, and the required specialists are synthe-sized for that task. Each chain is executed once per cycle; the additional no-edit executions described next are used only during SECPO training. 

3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment 

Structural adaptation presents a credit-assignment problem: the terminal reward reflects the outcome of several decisions, even though an individual edit may improve or worsen the chain. GRPO’s group-relative advantage also mixes rollouts with differ-ent structures, while a learned value critic would need to track changing chain states. Counterfac-tual methods such as COMA (Foerster et al., 2018) require evaluating alternative actions at each step. SECPO instead measures a candidate edit against the unchanged chain at the same state, while retain-ing GRPO’s critic-free group of K rollouts (Shao et al., 2024) and PPO’s clipped surrogate with KL regularization (Schulman et al., 2017).

4 Experiments 

We investigate whether feedback-driven struc-tural evolution improves multi-agent collaboration, which components make this evolution effective, and whether the learned adaptation transfers across tasks and executors. In particular, we assess overall performance (§4.2), isolate key components and credit signals (§4.3), and examine transfer, effi-ciency, and executor generalization (§4.4; §4.5). 

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We evaluate RoleFlow across four task domains: (1) Multi-hop QA with HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020) and MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022); (2) Mathemat-ical reasoning with MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023); (3) Code generation with MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) and HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021); and (4) Web interaction with WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) under the AgentBench protocol (Liu et al., 2024). We train a domain-specific GR on the Hot-potQA, MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021), MBPP, and WebShop training sets. We evaluate 2Wiki, MuSiQue, and HumanEval without retraining, us-ing the GR trained on HotpotQA or MBPP, respec-tively. Dataset details appear in Appendix B.

4.2 Main Results 

As shown in Table 1, RoleFlow achieves the high-est average score of 67.1 across seven benchmarks, outperforming the strongest competing multi-agent system, W4S, by 4.7 points and ranking first on five benchmarks. Notably, RoleFlow consistently improves upon its underlying gpt-4o-mini execu-tor across all four task domains, demonstrating the effectiveness of learned multi-role collaboration beyond individual model capabilities. The gains are particularly substantial on multi-hop QA (10.0– 19.0 F1), where coordinating complementary rea-soning roles is essential for handling interdepen-dent evidence and reasoning steps. Unlike prede-fined or pre-optimized workflows, RoleFlow com-bines feedback-driven structural refinement with edit-level counterfactual learning, enabling collab-oration structures to evolve in response to task requirements. These results establish the overall effectiveness of self-evolving collaboration, with subsequent ablations isolating the contributions of structural adaptation and SECPO.

This comes at a cost: realized chain executions rise from 9.1 to 13.5 per training question relative to standard GRPO. 

Atomic Edits Preserve Credit Granularity. 

Training with up to two or four edits per cycle low-ers QA performance relative to single-edit SECPO (Table 3). A shared counterfactual for multiple simultaneous edits measures their joint effect, ob-scuring individual contributions. Since these vari-ants are evaluated with one edit per cycle, train– test mismatch also contributes to the comparison; the evidence supports the single-edit configuration without isolating that mechanism. 

4.4 Structural Adaptation and Transfer Structure and Specialization Are Complemen-tary. Preserving synthesized roles’ names and positions while replacing their descriptions with generic ones retains roughly half to three-quarters of their benefit (Table 6). Much of the gain there-fore comes from inserting a useful step into the chain, while tailored role instructions contribute further. Role synthesis improves both collabora-tion structure and specialist behavior. 

Adaptation Transfers Across Tasks. The HotpotQA-trained GR transfers to 2Wiki and MuSiQue without retraining (Table 1). Its initial chains vary little, but final chains diverge almost three times as much; on MuSiQue, about a quar-ter of questions finish with a chain absent from the initial proposals. Almost all additional OOD specialists are synthesized after rejection (Table 6). The transferable behavior is therefore not merely an initial template, but the ability to revise collabo-ration in response to task feedback.

5 Conclusion 

We presented RoleFlow , a self-evolving multi-role agentic system that adapts collaboration structures through feedback-driven edits and on-demand role synthesis. We further introduced SECPO to en-able structure-aware learning through edit-level counterfactual credit assignment. Experiments across seven benchmarks demonstrate superior av-erage performance and strong transferability. Over-all, these findings highlight the value of structure-aware credit assignment in enabling effective and adaptive multi-agent collaboration. 8Limitations 

RoleFlow requires a task validator that returns a pass or reject verdict and a deficit for every cycle, so a new domain needs such a check before the GR can be trained or run. Inference costs more than training-free pipelines and SECPO training executes one additional chain per edit.

D Chain Evolution Case Studies 

Five cases show how the chain evolves within a task and what each part of RoleFlow does. Case 1 follows RoleFlow on a MATH-500 problem that twelve of the eighteen compared methods answer incorrectly: the validator rejects the first answer, and the chain edited with a role synthesized for the reported deficit answers correctly. Cases 2 to 5 each isolate one component of RoleFlow. In Case 2 the validator accepts the output of cycle 0 and no edit follows, in Case 3 a rejection opens an adaptation cycle where the GR edits the chain, Case 4 compares the chain before and after training, and Case 5 compares the GR trained with and without SECPO. Cases 2 and 3 are from QA benchmarks, and Cases 4 and 5 share one repository-level coding task. 

Case 1: a role synthesized for a deficit. This case illustrates one adaptation cycle where the GR’s plan names the null edit. On the MATH-500 prob-lem of Figure 7, twelve of the eighteen methods compared in Table 1 give a wrong answer, among them GPT-4o-mini prompted directly, which an-swers 5040 in each of its three runs. RoleFlow’s submitted answer, the majority answer of twenty sampled chains, is 10080 and correct for all three trained GRs. The figure shows a replayed chain: one plan that a trained GR sampled for this problem is executed once more, separately from the twenty chains, with every role output recorded. The chain of cycle 0 computes 7! = 5040 , and the validator 

V rejects this answer with the note that it does not account for equivalent rotations properly. Since the plan names the null edit, deficit-driven synthe-sis (§3.2) creates a role for the reported deficit, RotationEquivalenceEvaluator, and adds it before the OutputFormatter. The edited chain is executed from its first role, and its roles receive neither the rejected answer nor the note. The Planner’s strat-egy divides 8! by the four rotations, the roles before the new one compute 10080, the new role confirms this result, and V accepts it. 

Case 2: a RoleFlowChain against a direct an-swer. This case shows how the roles of a Role-FlowChain split a two-hop question, and that no edit follows an accepted output. On the HotpotQA question of Figure 8, GPT-4o-mini prompted di-rectly names another film by the same author and is incorrect. The GR plans InformationExtractor, Rea-soner and OutputFormatter, and the chain runs with the Planner in front and the Validator role at the end. The Planner states what has to be found, the InformationExtractor recalls that the actress starred in a film based on the author’s novel and names it, the Reasoner confirms the link between the film and the novel, and the OutputFormatter emits the answer in the required form. The Validator role accepts the output of cycle 0, so the chain runs once. The trajectory comes from a logged run of an earlier GR checkpoint not reported in Table 1. On average such cycle-0 chains score at the executor’s level on HotpotQA (§4.2), and the improvement of Table 1 comes from the adaptation cycles that follow a rejection, as in Case 3. 

Case 3: adaptive routing with the GR. This case shows why the GR has to act during the task. On the question of Figure 9, the chain of cycle 0 answers Norwood and the validator rejects it. Without the GR (left), the same roles run again: the Answerer moves to Beulah Park, the validator accepts it, and the answer is still incorrect. With the GR (right), cycle 1 applies A DD (Reasoner ): the Reasoner doubts Beulah Park, places College Park inside the district, and the chain answers correctly. Removing the GR on the QA benchmarks, as in Table 2, leaves the chain the GR proposes most often to run without edits.
