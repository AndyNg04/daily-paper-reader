The growing population of orbital debris has intensified interest in active de-bris removal and on-orbit servicing, for which dual-arm space manipulators offer improved grasping stability and load distribution relative to single-arm systems. However, coordinating two manipulators under inter-arm collision avoidance, ori-entation alignment, and sensing uncertainty remains challenging. This paper de-velops a coordinated reinforcement learning framework for cooperative motion planning of a dual-arm space manipulator in a target-reaching task with inter-arm collision-avoidance constraints. A centralized state representation combines joint-space, end-effector, and target-relative information, while a multi-objective reward function promotes target-reaching accuracy, orientation alignment, motion smoothness, synchronized task completion, and collision avoidance. Within this framework, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) are compared as representative on-policy and off-policy algorithms, and the learned policies are further evaluated for generalization to previously unseen target con-figurations. Across five random seeds, SAC substantially outperforms PPO within the considered fixed-base simulation scenarios: it reaches an 80% success thresh-old in every seed (median 1.6M environment steps) whereas PPO does not reach it within a 4M-step budget, and it attains a higher mean success rate (78.6% versus 7.4%) with lower position and orientation errors and tighter bimanual synchro-nization. Both algorithms degrade on unseen boundary targets, but SAC retains most of its performance while PPO’s collapses. The results also expose a fail-ure mode of PPO: under the two-phase shaped reward, it tends to converge to a reward-loitering local optimum rather than committing to the precision task. This behavior highlights the sensitivity of on-policy learning to reward shaping. 

1 INTRODUCTION Background and Motivation 

Orbital debris now poses a major threat to the long-term sustainability, safety, and economic viability of space operations. The accumulation of non-functional satellites, spent launch-vehicle stages, and fragmentation debris has substantially increased the collision risk in low Earth orbit, where a single high-energy collision could trigger a cascading fragmentation process that compro-mises the usability of critical orbital regions. These concerns have brought active debris removal, on-orbit servicing, and autonomous capture to the forefront of space-safety research. 1, 2 

Robotic manipulators have emerged as one of the most practical enabling technologies for these tasks. Unlike human-crewed approaches, they interact directly with non-cooperative targets, per-form capture and stabilization maneuvers, and operate without exposing astronauts to risk. Dual-arm configurations are particularly promising, since coordinated manipulation distributes contact

However, coordinating two manipulators in orbit is difficult. Meeting these demands reliably is therefore central to making dual-arm space manipulators practically viable. 

Related Work 

Classical nonlinear, robust, and optimal control strategies have been applied to many dynamical systems to mitigate disturbances and modeling uncertainties. 4–10 However, these methods typi-cally rely on accurate models and carefully tuned controllers, which limits their adaptability to the highly uncertain, contact-rich capture scenarios encountered in dual-arm operations involving non-cooperative targets. These limitations motivate learning-based approaches, and in particular deep reinforcement learn-ing (DRL), for cooperative motion control of dual-arm space robots. DRL methods can learn coor-dination policies directly from interaction data, capturing complex contact dynamics, uncertainty, and redundancy without requiring explicit analytic controller design. Among DRL methods, Prox-imal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) represent two widely used paradigms with distinct learning mechanisms, sample-efficiency properties, and exploration strategies. PPO has attracted considerable attention in space-robotic motion planning owing to its stable pol-icy updates, implementation simplicity, and suitability for continuous control. Wang et al. applied a PPO-based strategy to multi-target trajectory planning for a six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) free-floating space robot, demonstrating improved tracking performance and robustness under external disturbances, base-parameter variations, and joint-failure scenarios. 11 Reinforcement learning has also been investigated for dual-arm robotic capture of non-cooperative dynamic targets, where sim-to-real transfer improved the practical applicability of the learned capture policies. 12 More recent studies have extended learning-based motion planning toward dual-arm free-floating space robots and non-cooperative target capture, where coordinated planning must address dynamic coupling, high-dimensional action spaces, and end-effector pose constraints. 13, 14 Nevertheless, because PPO is an on-policy algorithm, it often requires substantial interaction data, and its performance can be sensitive to reward design, network architecture, and hyperparameter tuning. In contrast, off-policy actor–critic methods offer a promising alternative for improving sample efficiency in continuous control. SAC, in particular, incorporates entropy regularization to pro-mote exploration and stabilize learning, making it well suited to high-dimensional continuous action spaces. SAC-based approaches have been applied to robotic path planning and obstacle avoidance, including dynamic obstacle avoidance, multi-arm manipulator path planning, and dual-arm trajec-tory planning in configuration space. 13, 15–17 

The most closely related space-robot study jointly incorporates collision avoidance, orientation alignment, and observation-noise robustness for a free-floating manipulator, but does so for a single 

arm using Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG). 18 More broadly, prior dual-arm space-robot studies each commit to a single learning algorithm, predominantly off-policy DDPG 19, 20 or PPO alone, 21 and none contrast on-policy PPO with maximum-entropy SAC under an identical dual-arm formulation. 2Contributions

Rather than focusing solely on algorithm selection, the proposed approach constructs a task-specific cooperative learning architecture tailored to space-robotic capture operations. The objec-tive is to enable both manipulators to reach their assigned targets while maintaining safe inter-arm motion, satisfying task-level position and orientation requirements, and producing smooth, physi-cally feasible joint-space trajectories. To this end, a centralized state representation combines joint-space states, end-effector kinematics, and target-relative observations, and a multi-objective reward function balances target-reaching accuracy, orientation alignment, motion smoothness, inter-arm collision avoidance, and synchronized task completion. Within this framework, PPO and SAC are employed as representative on-policy and off-policy algorithms to evaluate how different policy-optimization paradigms realize the proposed formulation. The main contributions are summarized as follows: • A reinforcement-learning-based formulation is developed for cooperative motion planning of a dual-arm space manipulator in a target-reaching task with inter-arm collision-avoidance constraints. • A centralized state representation is constructed by incorporating joint-space information, end-effector kinematics, and target-relative observations to support coordinated dual-arm pol-icy learning. • A multi-objective reward function is designed to promote target-reaching accuracy, orienta-tion alignment, smooth joint motion, synchronized task completion, and inter-arm collision avoidance. 3• PPO and SAC are compared as representative on-policy and off-policy DRL algorithms to assess their convergence behavior, task performance, and suitability for the proposed cooper-ative motion-planning problem. • The learned policies are evaluated on unseen target configurations to examine their general-ization capability within the considered simulation scenarios. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the dual-arm robotic system and its reachable workspace. Section 3 reviews the reinforcement learning preliminaries and the PPO and SAC algorithms. Section 4 formulates the cooperative motion-planning problem as a Markov decision process, including the state and action spaces and the multi-objective reward design. Section 5 presents the comparative simulation results for PPO and SAC. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper and outlines future work. 

2 PROPOSED SYSTEM 

The dual-arm space manipulator considered in this study consists of two manipulators, each equipped with four revolute joints, three rigid links, and a two-finger gripper, as illustrated in Figure 1. This configuration supports repeated contact and reliable grasping while reducing the likelihood of inadvertently pushing the target away during cooperative manipulation. All motion is confined to a plane: each end-effector position is a two-dimensional coordinate and its orientation a single planar heading angle. The task is target reaching; a completed grasp is not required for success, and the grasp bonus introduced in Section 4 is an auxiliary shaping signal.

For simplicity, the spacecraft base is treated as fixed in this work. Under this assumption, the problem is formulated as a fixed-base dual-arm motion-planning task, while the extension to a free-floating base is left for future work.

3 TECHNICAL PRELIMINARIES 

This section formulates space-manipulator motion planning as a Markov decision process (MDP) for model-free deep reinforcement learning and reviews the two algorithms compared in this study. The MDP is defined as a tuple ⟨S , A, P, R, γ ⟩, where S denotes the state space, A the action space, P the transition dynamics, R the reward function, and γ the discount factor. Together, these elements define the agent–environment interaction and the balance between immediate and future rewards. The agent aims to learn a policy that maps observed states to actions so as to maximize the expected cumulative reward. In DRL, this policy is represented by a neural network and optimized through repeated interaction with the environment. 5Reinforcement Learning Algorithms 

On-policy methods such as PPO update only from data collected by the current policy, whereas off-policy methods such as SAC reuse stored past experience through a replay buffer. The former typically offer higher training stability, and the latter generally achieve better sample efficiency in high-dimensional continuous action spaces. To compare these two paradigms within an identical dual-arm task formulation, one representative algorithm from each category is investigated.

4 PROBLEM FORMULATION Markov Decision Process Formulation 

Building on the MDP defined in Section 3, the dual-arm space-manipulator motion-planning problem is instantiated through the agent–environment interaction, comprising the state representa-tion, action space, transition dynamics, and reward function. This formulation provides the basis for learning coordinated motion policies that enable both manipulators to reach their assigned targets while satisfying task-level and safety constraints. 

State and Action Spaces 

The centralized state representation includes the joint angles of each arm, the end-effector posi-tions and velocities, the relative position between each end-effector and its target, and the heading error of each arm. These components collectively define a state vector s ∈ R24 , organized into six groups: 

s = [ θr, θ l, p ee r , p ee l , ∆pr, ∆pl, v ee r , v ee l , ψ r, ψ l, ∆ψr, ∆ψl] , (11) where θr, θ l ∈ R4 are the absolute joint angles of the right and left arms; pee r , p ee l ∈ R2 are the right and left end-effector XY positions in the world frame; ∆pr = ptarget r −pee r and ∆pl = ptarget l −pee l ∈

R2 are the target-relative positions between each end-effector and its target; vee r , v ee l ∈ R2 are the right and left end-effector velocities; ψr, ψ l ∈ R are the right and left end-effector orientations; and 

∆ψr, ∆ψl ∈ R are the right and left heading errors, computed as the normalized angular difference between the target orientation and the current end-effector heading. 7The action space has dimension a ∈ R8, where each action corresponds to an incremental change in a joint angle, representing a relative adjustment from the current configuration rather than an absolute joint position. The action vector is defined as: 

a = [∆ θr, 0:3 , ∆θl, 0:3 ] , (12) where ∆θr, 0:3 ∈ R4 and ∆θl, 0:3 ∈ R4 denote the incremental joint-angle adjustments (rad) applied to the right and left arms, respectively, at each control step. 

Transition Dynamics 

The normalized policy output a ∈ [−1, 1] 8 is scaled by 0.05 and added to the current joint angles, so each control step applies an incremental change of at most 0.05 rad per joint; the updated angles are clamped to the joint limits of each manipulator. Each control action is held for four simulation substeps before the next observation is taken. The dynamics are deterministic: no process or ob-servation noise is injected in the experiments reported here. The success and collision conditions, together with the episode horizon Tmax = 1000 control steps, are specified in Section 4 (Episode Termination and Success Criteria). 

Reward Function Design 

To train the centralized policy for cooperative dual-arm manipulation, a multi-objective reward function is designed to promote accurate target reaching, orientation alignment, smooth motion, inter-arm collision avoidance, and timely task completion. The total reward at each time step is defined as: 

rt = rtask + rsmooth + rprox + rcollision + rgrasp + rasync + rtime + rsuccess , (13) where each term corresponds to a distinct behavioral objective, described below. 

Two-phase task reward. The primary task reward rtask is decomposed into two sequential phases for each arm, mirroring the two-stage nature of the manipulation task: first reaching the target position, then aligning the end-effector orientation. In Phase 1 (position not yet reached), the reward is dominated by a position term that drives the end-effector toward the target, supplemented by a small proximity-weighted orientation hint that biases the approach direction. In Phase 2 (the end-effector has entered the success region), the position term is disabled and a stronger orientation term takes over, providing a clear signal for wrist rotation. 5 SIMULATION RESULTS 

To assess the effectiveness of the proposed framework and to compare on-policy and off-policy learning for the target-reaching task with inter-arm collision avoidance, PPO and SAC are evaluated within the same task formulation. The objective is not only to verify that the framework can learn the cooperative motion-planning task, but also to examine its generalization to previously unseen target configurations. Unless stated otherwise, all comparative statements are scoped to the considered fixed-base dual-arm simulation scenarios. 

Simulation Setup 

The environment is implemented in PyBullet and the policies are trained with Stable-Baselines3. Each episode is capped at Tmax = 1000 control steps. During training, targets are resampled every 10 episodes from the interior of the common reachable region (with a 3 cm erosion of the boundary) with inter-target separation ∼ U (0 .07 , 0.20) m; during evaluation, targets are resampled every episode from a fixed, shared seed sequence so that episode-level comparisons across models are paired. Success and collision are defined as in Section 4: both arms must satisfy the position and heading tolerances before Tmax , and an episode is flagged as a collision when the minimum inter-link clearance falls below dsafe = 0 .02 m. All ten runs (five seeds per algorithm) share an identical MDP configuration (verified by a common configuration hash), so that only the algorithm-side hyperparameters differ. The simulation environment is shown in Figure 1(a). 

Training Configuration 

The final hyperparameters for PPO and SAC are summarized in Table 2. Both algorithms are trained for an identical budget of 4M environment steps over five random seeds; the fair-comparison currency is environment steps rather than wall-clock time or episodes. Following best practices for reproducible deep reinforcement learning evaluation, 24, 25 per-seed point estimates are aggregated across seeds and reported with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval (10,000 resamples), using the across-seed mean for the success and collision rates and the across-seed median for the continuous error metrics; headline PPO-vs-SAC gaps are assessed with the Mann–Whitney U test and Cliff’s 

δ effect size. Because n = 5 yields low statistical power, differences are reported as distinguish-able/indistinguishable at n = 5 and supported by non-overlapping confidence intervals rather than by a p-value alone. Both algorithms optimize the identical MDP (verified by the shared configuration hash), so the differences in Table 2 are algorithm-side; two are disclosed for completeness. First, PPO uses obser-10 vation and reward normalization (with clipping at ±10 ), a standard preprocessing step for on-policy PPO, whereas SAC uses raw observations; this normalization preserves the ratio between the ter-minal success bonus and the dense shaping reward, and the normalized success bonus remains well within the clipping range. Second, the network sizes, batch sizes, and number of parallel envi-ronments follow common per-algorithm defaults. The hyperparameters were obtained by manual tuning of each algorithm rather than a systematic equal-budget search, which we note as a limitation of the comparison.

Training Convergence 

The learning curves in Figure 3 report the deterministic success rate, the policy evaluated with exploration disabled, using the mean action, versus environment steps (median ± bootstrap 95% CI over five seeds). SAC reaches the 80% success threshold in all five seeds, at a median of 1.6M steps [1.5M, 3.0M], whereas PPO does not reach that threshold in any seed within the 4M-step budget (a right-censored outcome, not a finite value). The normalized area under the success-versus-steps curve is 0.497 [0.426, 0.510] for SAC and 0.032 [0.019, 0.052] for PPO. Figure 4 shows the individual per-seed success curves, smoothed with an exponential moving average (weight 0.8), in the style of a TensorBoard visualization. All five SAC seeds rise to a substantial success level, whereas all five PPO seeds remain near zero, confirming that the separation is consistent across seeds rather than driven by an outlier.

PPO Failure-Mode Controls 

To identify why PPO fails, we examine the in-distribution episodes and run three control ex-periments. A success funnel over the 500 in-distribution episodes (five seeds pooled) shows that PPO does not fail at reaching: about half of its episodes bring both end-effectors within 5 cm of the targets. Instead, it attrits at the precision latch: only 14.8% of PPO episodes ever latch both arms, versus 79.8% for SAC, and two-thirds of one-arm latches never complete the second arm, consistent with the large inter-arm completion gap. The following control experiments (single seed) isolate candidate causes. 

Discount. Because PPO uses a higher discount factor ( γ = 0 .99 ) than SAC ( γ = 0 .97 ), the discounted value of prolonged loitering is larger for PPO, raising the concern that its behavior is merely a rational response to its own discount. Rather, the shaped reward admits a high-return loitering optimum, and the successful precision latches occur at a near-zero rate in the rollout data. PPO’s on-policy updates, which reuse each batch for only a few epochs before discarding it, cannot amplify these rare events, whereas SAC’s replay buffer reuses each rare success many times and propagates the terminal success bonus through its Q-function. Discussion 

Within the considered fixed-base dual-arm simulation scenarios, SAC converges faster (reaching 80% success in all five seeds while PPO never reaches it within 4M steps), attains substantially higher task success, produces more accurate and better-synchronized motions, and generalizes far better to unseen boundary targets than PPO. These tendencies are consistent with the reported sta-bility advantage of maximum-entropy off-policy methods on manipulation benchmarks 26 and with PPO’s known sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning; 27 however, the differences reported here are attributed to the present multi-seed measurements rather than to any universal ordering of the two algorithms. PPO’s failure mode is specific and should not be read as general PPO inferiority: as the control experiments above show, PPO converges to a reward-loitering local optimum that persists across discount, budget, and reward-shaping variations. Because episode return is not monotone in task performance under this reward, success rate is used as the primary metric throughout. 

Limitations. The study is confined to a fixed-base formulation evaluated only in simulation, with 

n = 5 seeds per algorithm. Robustness to observation noise, listed among the target objectives, was not evaluated in this study and is left for future work, as are the extension to a free-floating base and sim-to-real transfer. 

6 CONCLUSION 

This paper presented a coordinated reinforcement learning framework for cooperative motion planning of a dual-arm space manipulator in a target-reaching task with inter-arm collision-avoidance constraints. The proposed formulation combined a centralized state representation with a multi-objective reward function to promote target-reaching accuracy, orientation alignment, smooth mo-tion, synchronized task completion, and inter-arm collision avoidance. PPO and SAC were com-pared as representative on-policy and off-policy algorithms within the same task formulation, and 15 the learned policies were further examined for generalization to previously unseen target configura-tions. Across five random seeds, SAC substantially outperformed PPO within the considered fixed-base simulation scenarios, reaching an 80% success threshold in every seed (median 1.6M environment steps) while PPO did not reach it within a 4M-step budget, and achieving a higher mean success rate (78.6% versus 7.4%) together with more accurate and better-synchronized motions. On unseen boundary targets, SAC retained most of its performance whereas PPO’s largely collapsed. The comparison also revealed that, under the two-phase shaped reward, PPO tends to converge to a reward-loitering local optimum. Control experiments showed that this behavior persisted under a matched discount factor, a 12 .5× larger training budget, and an alternative reward design, pointing to a limitation of on-policy sample reuse rather than a general deficiency of PPO. Future work will evaluate robustness to observation noise, which was not assessed in this study, and will extend the current fixed-base formulation to free-floating space-manipulator dynamics, incorporate more realistic contact and grasping interactions, and investigate sim-to-real transfer and hardware validation.
