Abstract
Hazard maps are essential tools for public risk communication,
preparedness, and evacuation planning, yet effective cartographic
design is not always ensured due to limited access to cartographic
expertise across local governments and communities. We present
Agentic Hazard Mapper, a multi-agent system that automates hazard-
map production by emulating professional cartographic workflows
and iterative design trade-offs while adapting design decisions
to different data, locations, and hazard contexts. We conducted a
formative study with map experts to inform the system design, fol-
lowed by a user study and interviews with map experts and hazard
practitioners. The generated maps improved participants’ spatial
understanding and evacuation-planning performance, reduced per-
ceived workload and task completion time, and were generally
preferred over corresponding municipal maps. We further discuss
human oversight, adaptation across users and communication con-
texts, and opportunities to extend agentic support across broader
stages of hazard-map production and risk communication.

Introduction
Natural hazards can cause substantial loss of life, damage to infras-
tructure, and disruption to communities. Japan is particularly sus-
ceptible to a wide range of natural hazards, including earthquakes,
tsunamis, floods, and landslides, which occur across the country
on a recurring basis [4]. In recent years, large-scale disasters have
occurred, such as the 2016 Kumamoto Earthquake, Typhoon No.
14 in 2022, and heavy rainfalls caused by the Baiu Rainy Season


Wu et al.
Front in 2023. To mitigate the damage caused by natural hazards,
effective risk communication is necessary for the general public to
understand and prepare for the hazards. Hazard maps offer an im-
portant instrument for risk communication, as they present spatial
information and intensity of potential hazards, which help people
quickly gain knowledge about hazard extent, evacuation routes,
and local terrain, compared to text-only messages [23, 25]. As such,
hazard maps must ensure that the general public can understand
and correctly interpret the presented information [25].
However, hazard-map production in Japan is decentralized across
more than 1,700 municipalities, where the access to professional car-
tographic expertise can be limited or uneven, leaving many design
decisions to practitioners whose primary expertise lies in hazard
analysis or disaster management rather than cartography and vi-
sualization. This results in considerable variation in map design
and generally limited attention to cartographic representation [26].
Such variation can substantially affect how people interpret hazard
information and, in some cases, increase the likelihood of misinter-
pretation [27]. This gap is also reflected in residents’ experiences
with hazard maps. According to a nationwide survey conducted in
2021, only 23.2% of respondents who had viewed their municipal
hazard map considered it very useful for evacuation planning, while
56.4% considered it somewhat useful [30]. Respondents who found
the maps less useful reported difficulties in map reading such as
identifying the type and severity of hazards affecting their homes,
deciding on the evacuation destination, and determining which
evacuation routes to take. These responses highlight persistent
challenges in translating technical hazard information into visual
representations that support effective risk communication.
Addressing these challenges calls for an automated system that
can incorporate cartographic expertise in contexts where such ex-
pertise is limited. At the same time, rather than applying a single
uniform design standard, such a system should remain flexible
enough to make context-dependent adaptations, as data availabil-
ity, hazard characteristics, geographic conditions, and local com-
munication needs vary considerably across municipalities. These
requirements are difficult to capture in a static workflow, where
design decisions are predefined. Recent advances in AI provide
new opportunities for supporting this type of adaptive reason-
ing. Agentic AI is particularly promising because it can address
more open-ended and context-sensitive problems beyond narrow,
well-defined tasks. Such systems coordinate specialized large lan-
guage model (LLM) agents, decompose complex tasks into multiple
stages, dynamically adapt strategies to changing conditions, and
iteratively refine their decisions based on intermediate results and
feedback [6, 10, 40]. These capabilities make agentic systems well
suited to hazard map-making.
In this paper, we propose an agentic system for automatically
producing hazard maps for effective risk communication. We focus
on printed maps, as they remain one of the most widely used media
for hazard communication in Japan. The system is designed to
iteratively refine map quality, adapt to different data, locations,
and hazard characteristics, and remain grounded in established
cartographic practices, rules, and preferences. We use insights from
a formative study with map experts to inform the system design, and
then conduct a user study as well as expert interviews to investigate
how the cartographic expertise is operationized through the system,
how the resulting maps are perceived, and how such a system could
be used in actual hazard-preparedness contexts.
To summarize, our contributions are:
• We conducted a formative study with map experts to char-
acterize expert map-making practices, including explicit
cartographic guidelines and tacit design preferences for
effective visual communication, and used these insights to
inform the design of our agentic system.
• We designed and implemented an agentic map-making sys-
tem that incorporates expert cartographic knowledge and
practices to automatically generate hazard maps, with the
goal of supporting map production in contexts where car-
tographic expertise is limited or unavailable.
• We conducted a user study to evaluate how effectively the
generated maps support spatial understanding, risk com-
prehension, and evacuation planning.
• We conducted interviews with map experts and hazard
practitioners to assess the cartographic quality and practical
utility of the generated maps, and to identify opportunities
and challenges for integrating agentic map-making systems
into real-world hazard-preparedness workflows.

Related work
2.1
Risk communication and hazard maps
Broadly speaking, there are two types of hazard risk communica-
tion: warnings of incoming threats and communicating long-term
risks for early preparedness and mitigation [39]. For the latter, maps
have long been recognized for pre-disaster preparation and evac-
uation route planning [23]. However, the visual communication
of maps is not always effective. For example, for the case of hurri-
cane forecasting maps, the general public can sometimes struggle
with interpreting geographic risk areas or uncertainty levels [3].
More generally, substantial variation exists in how people com-
prehend hazard maps and draw appropriate conclusions from the
spatial information presented [22]. Recent reviews [48] continued
to highlight challenges related to readability, visual complexity,
color conventions, and risk perception.
The effective visual design of hazard maps has long been stud-
ied in cartography and risk communication. Prior work [28] has
examined how flood-risk communication through maps can in-
crease awareness and support preparedness. Other works have
investigated how hazard information can be represented with an
appropriate balance between simplicity and complexity [13], how
cartographic variables such as color, symbols, size, typography,
and basemap styles influence the readability and interpretation of
flood and evacuation maps [12, 21, 27, 41]. Works like [21, 34] have
investigated cartographic recommendations and explicit design cri-
teria for effective risk communication for flood and seismic hazards.
Rather than a uniform design scheme, Meyer et al. [29] proposed
user-specific adaptations in map content and visualization. Beyond
visual design, Koesten et al. [20] showed that crisis-map interpreta-
tion can also be hindered by insufficient explanatory information,
limited personal relevance, and distrust in the visualization. To-
gether, these studies provide an established body of cartographic
knowledge for hazard maps and inspire the design of our system.

Communication of imminent hazards, in contrast, often extends
beyond maps alone and combines textual warnings, alerts, visual
information, and other modalities to support timely protective ac-
tion [23]. Prior work has examined how emergency organizations
communicate with the public through online and social-media chan-
nels during emerging disasters [17], as well as how technologies
can support professionals in authoring effective severe-weather risk
messages [18]. Such warning scenarios involve rapidly changing
conditions, greater temporal pressure, and immediate protective
actions. In this work, we focus primarily on map-based communica-
tion for longer-term hazard preparedness and evacuation planning,
where cartographic design can support spatial understanding and
planning before an emergency unfolds.
2.2
LLMs and agentic systems for automated
visualization
Automating visualization design has long been a goal of the vi-
sualization community. Traditional approaches typically formal-
ize visualization knowledge as explicit rules, design guidelines, or
optimization constraints, such as Voyager [45], Draco [31], and
VizML [16]. While these approaches can produce effective visual-
izations, they often rely on predefined design spaces and are less
flexible in handling complex natural-language requests, ambiguous
user intentions, and diverse data characteristics.
Recent advances in generative AI have enabled a new paradigm
for automated visualization. Early approaches such as Data2Vis [9]
learned visualization specifications from a given dataset, which
are later transformed to visualization plots. More recently, LLMs
have extended this paradigm by interpreting natural-language re-
quests, reasoning about visualization choices, and generating ex-
ecutable visualization code. They been applied to various visual-
ization tasks, including visualization recommendation and design
assistance [36, 44], visualization code generation [5, 50], and visu-
alization evaluation and critique [37]. Compared with traditional
rule-based systems, LLMs provide greater flexibility in interpreting
user requests and synthesizing visualization designs from hetero-
geneous sources of knowledge. Beyond visualization, LLM-based
agents have demonstrated the ability to perform complex planning
and decision-making tasks in open-ended environments. For ex-
ample, [43] introduced an embodied LLM agent for Minecraft that
continuously acquires skills, interacts with the environment, and
accumulates knowledge through iterative exploration.
More recently, researchers have explored multi-agent systems in
which multiple specialized LLM agents collaborate to solve complex
tasks. Such systems have been applied to code generation [15], soft-
ware engineering [32], and general-purpose agent frameworks [46].
Rather than relying on a single model, multi-agent systems de-
compose problems into specialized subtasks and coordinate the
reasoning processes of multiple agents, often leading to improved
robustness, planning, and error recovery. Within visualization re-
search, several agentic systems have recently emerged such as
MatPlotAgent [47], CoDA [6], and VisPath [35]. Given user require-
ments in natural language and input datasets, these systems can an-
alyze tasks, select visualization strategies, generate executable code,
verify outputs, and iteratively refine results. For example, CoDA [6]
adopts a collaborative multi-agent architecture consisting of special-
ized agents for metadata analysis, task planning, code generation,
debugging, and self-reflection. Our agentic map-generation sys-
tem can be situated within the broader landscape of LLM-powered
visualization and multi-agent systems.
2.3
AI-assisted map-making
Early AI-assisted map-making primarily explored map generation
and style transfer through image synthesis. These approaches in-
clude GAN-based methods [7, 19], diffusion-based generation [1],
and direct use of off-the-shelf models such as DALL·E [8, 11]. Al-
though these methods can produce visually compelling results,
pixel-based generation may introduce hallucinated or geograph-
ically inaccurate content and offers limited flexibility and fine-
grained control over cartographic elements during map generation.
More recently, LLMs have been integrated with GIS and carto-
graphic tools to support more structured map-making workflows.
MapGPT [49] uses an LLM to interpret natural-language mapping
requirements, decompose user requests into subtasks, and invoke
specialized cartographic tools to manipulate individual map ele-
ments. By grounding the generation process in structured geo-
graphic data and tool-based operations, MapGPT provides greater
control over map content and reduces the risk of geographic hallu-
cinations. While MapGPT uses a single, centralized agent for map
generation, CartoAgent [42] employs a multi-agent framework for
map styling and restyling, consisting of preparation, design, and
evaluation agents. The preparation agent extracts style informa-
tion, such as color palettes, from user-provided reference maps; the
design agent proposes color schemes and map symbols; and the
evaluation agent assesses whether the generated map is visually ef-
fective and informative. The design is iteratively revised until it sat-
isfies the evaluation criteria. Its workflow is mainly structured as a
staged pipeline for map styling and evaluation, with limited empha-
sis on persistent memory, targeted reflection, or richer inter-agent
collaboration across the broader cartographic workflow. Building
on this trajectory, our system models a broader cartographic cycle
spanning data processing, design and layout, evaluation, and revi-
sion, with specialized agents grounded in cartographic knowledge,
practices, and expert preferences.
3
Formative Study
To understand professional map-making practices and the chal-
lenges surrounding current hazard-map visualization in Japan, we
conducted a formative study with map experts. We focused on
how experts approach the map-making process, the cartographic
principles and constraints that guide their decisions, the challenges
they perceive in existing hazard maps, and the evaluation criteria
to assess the map quality, in the context of hazard mapping and risk
communication. The goal of the study was to elicit both explicit de-
sign knowledge and tacit professional judgments that could inform
the design of our agentic hazard-mapping system.
3.1
Method
We first reviewed literature on cartographic design, with particular
attention to hazard mapping and risk communication. From the
literature [12, 13, 21, 24, 27, 33, 34, 38, 48], we extracted a set of


Wu et al.
fundamental cartographic principles and identified 16 criteria de-
scribing what an effective hazard map should achieve and how they
could be operationalized through cartographic variables such as
hue, lightness, symbol size, line style, texture, and layer order. Re-
quirements prescribed by official hazard-mapping guidelines were
treated separately as fixed constraints.
We recruited five experts (E1–E5) with between 3 and 40 years
of professional experience in cartography, including two with par-
ticular expertise in hazard visualization. During structured, on-site
interviews, experts first described their typical cartographic work-
flows. They then assessed three existing Japanese hazard maps
with respect to cartographic principles, risk communication, and
opportunities for improvement. Finally, experts evaluated the 16
literature-derived criteria, ranked their importance, selected the
eight they considered most critical, and explained their choices.
Afterward, they discussed how the design variables of these crite-
ria should be adapted to different hazard types, data complexity,
geographic characteristics, and communication needs.
3.2
Insights and findings
Cartographic Workflow Experts described map-making as an it-
erative rather than linear process. A typical cartographic workflow
starts by deciding determining the map’s purposes and intended au-
dience, followed by collecting and checking data, selecting relevant
information, and establishing a visual hierarchy to decide what
important features stand out from the background. Cartographers
then design colors, symbols, labels, and layout, scrutinize the result
for mistakes or confusing details, and revise their earlier decisions
as needed before publication.
Problems in Current Hazard Maps The experts identified five
main issues in current hazard maps: 1) Information overload. All
experts noted that hazard maps often retain excessive geographic
detail, including dense roads, buildings, labels, and contextual fea-
tures that are not always relevant to the communicated hazard.
Such information can compete with critical risk information and
reduce readability. Experts also noted overly detailed classifica-
tions (E1, E2, E4) or legends containing unnecessary or unused
categories (E3), which can increase users’ interpretive burden: 2)
Weak visual hierarchy and confusing visual encoding. E3 and
E5 observed that hazard-related information is not always visually
distinguished from basemap information used primarily for ori-
entation and context. They also noted confusing visual encoding:
multiple hues within the same hazard type can make it difficult to
perceive the ordering of severity levels, while similar colors or fill
patterns across hazard types can make different hazards difficult to
distinguish. 3) Limited interpretability of legends and symbols.
E1, E2, and E3 noted that legends often lack clear structure and
provide limited explanation. Rather than relying heavily on exten-
sive legends, E3, E4, and E5 suggested using more intuitive color
schemes and self-explanatory symbols can provoke users’ existing
visual associations and reduce the need for repeated legend consul-
tation. 4) Insufficient emphasis on meaningful labels. E4 and
E5 emphasized the importance of labels for shelters, communities,
landmarks, and other recognizable locations that help residents
orient themselves and plan evacuation routes. Such information
should be selectively emphasized rather than treated as equivalent
to less relevant geographic annotations. 5) Inadequate represen-
tation of topography and elevation. Most experts (E1, E3, E4, and
E5) emphasized the importance of terrain and elevation particularly
for floods and landslides. However, this information is either absent
or difficult to interpret in existing maps. For example, conventional
contour lines can convey precise elevation information but may
be less intuitive for the general public. Experts also identified use-
ful existing practices. E1, E2, and E3 valued illustrations linking
flood-inundation depths to affected building storeys, as they make
abstract depth values easier to interpret. E2 and E4 also noted that
historical hazard information can strengthen risk awareness by
connecting risks to past experience, although it is less useful for
assessing current conditions.
Aspects for Effective Risk Communication Across the inter-
views, four broader communication goals emerged. First, hazard
maps should support risk awareness by making affected areas and
relative severity readily perceivable (E1–E5). Second, they should
support spatial awareness by helping users identify shelters, neigh-
borhoods, roads, terrain, and potential evacuation routes (E1–E5).
Third, maps should establish personal relevance, allowing users
to understand how hazards may affect their own circumstances
and possible actions (E1–E5). Finally, E3 and E5 emphasized multi-
hazard awareness, to distinguish and associate hazards that overlap
spatially or interact in ways relevant to evacuation decisions.
Quality criteria for hazard map design Experts prioritized eight
criteria for guiding design and evaluation: information density, haz-
ard differentiation, visual hierarchy, symbol interpretability, basemap
clarity, orientation and localizability, label readability, and legend
clarity. Information density received particularly strong emphasis,
with all experts advocating the simplification or removal of less
relevant information when it competes with hazard-related con-
tent. Experts also emphasized clear differentiation between hazard
types and severity levels through appropriate color encoding and
intuitive symbols. They also emphasized a strong visual hierar-
chy that can be established through variables such as size, font,
color, and transparency. At the same time, basemap information
should remain sufficiently legible to support orientation, particu-
larly major roads and topographic features. Labels important for
localization and evacuation, such as shelter names, community
names, landmarks, and facility names, should remain identifiable
and unobscured by competing visual elements. Finally, legends
should be concise, clearly organized, and use plain-language de-
scriptions where possible. By contrast, criteria related primarily to
aesthetics and color harmony received lower priority. We derived
weights for each criterion based on how many experts have voted
for this. Importantly, experts stressed that these criteria cannot be
applied through a single fixed design scheme. Their implementa-
tion depends on factors such as map scale, spatial extent, available
data, terrain, and hazard characteristics. Design choices can also
conflict with one another, requiring trade-offs and iterative adjust-
ment. This makes flexible professional judgment central to effective
hazard-map design.

3.3
Design implications
Based on these findings, we made the following design decisions
for our system:
Risk-centered information organization and visual hierarchy.
The system should organize and simplify information according
to the communication goal, prioritizing content that directly sup-
ports risk understanding and evacuation planning while secondary
information can be reduced or omitted when appropriate. This pri-
ority should also be reflected visually, with the dominant hazard
visualization and still sufficiently legible contextual information to
support orientation and localization.
Adaptive and flexible approach. Rather than applying a uniform
visual template, the system should adapt to hazard type and sever-
ity, available data, terrain, urban structure, map scale, and locally
relevant landmarks or evacuation resources. Established criteria
and expert heuristics should guide decisions without prescribing
a single fixed solution, thereby preserving flexibility in selecting
appropriate visual variables, parameters, and representations.
Semantically and spatially grounded representation. Colors,
symbols, labels, and other visual variables should preserve clear
semantic distinctions between hazard types, severity levels, and
contextual information. At the same time, maps should preserve
meaningful spatial anchors for orientation and localization, and
provide interpretable terrain information when topography is rele-
vant to risk understanding.
Iterative and multi-objective map-making. Hazard-map design
should be treated as an iterative, multi-stage process in which deci-
sions made at one stage can affect later stages and improvements
in one quality dimension may conflict with another. The system
should therefore evaluate intermediate results, coordinate decisions
across stages, and iteratively revise the resulting map to balance
competing cartographic objectives.
4
The Agentic Hazard Mapper
4.1
System Overview
The overview of our proposed agentic system is shown in Figure 2.
Mimicking real-world cartographic practice, we design multiple
specialized agents, each focusing on a well-defined area of expertise.
Given the input data from various sources, the Data agent sum-
marizes, selects, processes and prioritizes the data based on their
relevance to the target hazard. The Design agent determines visual
encoding for each data layer, while the Layout agent organizes
the map layout, legends, and supplementary explanations. Each
agent produces a lightweight configuration file that can be easily
passed to other agents and to the renderer to generate maps. The
rendered map is then accessed by the Evaluation agent across mul-
tiple quality dimensions according to expert-derived cartographic
criteria and provides feedback for refinement. The Revision agent
interprets the evaluation feedback and determines the appropriate
improvements for the corresponding upstream agents. The system
iteratively regenerates and evaluates the map until the predefined
acceptance criteria are satisfied or the maximum number of itera-
tions is reached.
4.2
Knowledge and Constraint Grounding
Based on the formative study and collected cartographic and hazard-
management documents, we derive a set of hard and soft constraints
to guide map generation. Hard constraints capture requirements that
must always be satisfied. These include fundamental cartographic
rules, such as appropriate visual encoding for different geometry
types and the inclusion of essential map elements, as well as offi-
cial hazard-mapping requirements. For example, the geographical
extent, hazard zones, hazard categories, and their meanings must
remain unchanged throughout the generation process. Soft con-
straints capture cartographic recommendations, design heuristics,
and common mapping practices that allow for contextual interpre-
tation and flexibility. They include considerations such as adapting
color schemes to local characteristics, determining the appropriate
level of background detail when hazards overlap with other fea-
tures, and incorporating terrain information when it is relevant
for risk communication. Rather than prescribing a single design
solution, these constraints provide contextual knowledge and rec-
ommendations that guide design decisions. Within our multi-agent
framework, hard constraints are enforced through deterministic op-
erations and validation checks that prevent violations, whereas soft
constraints are encoded as system instructions and recommenda-
tions that inform the reasoning and decision-making of individual
LLM agents.
4.3
Data Agent
Given geospatial data from heterogeneous sources, such as hazard
information, building footprints, street networks, rivers, shelter
locations, points of interest (PoIs), community names, and terrain
characteristics, the Data agent first extracts lightweight metadata
summaries from the input files using Python-based geospatial tools.
These summaries describe properties such as geometry types, statis-
tics, spatial distributions, and relevant attributes. The agent then as-
sesses the relevance and importance of each data layer with respect
to the target hazard and recommends appropriate data-processing
operations based on the amount and characteristics of the available
information. The operations include:
• Layer selection: remove irrelevant or redundant layers.
• Feature filtering: retain only salient or representative fea-
tures, such as major roads, large buildings, or well-known
landmarks.
• Prioritization: estimate the relative importance of layers
and features for risk communication, such as prioritizing
shelters for flood preparedness.
• Ordering: determine the rendering order across layers and
among features within a layer based on geometry type,
semantic importance, and relevant attributes. For example,
polygon layers are typically rendered beneath line and point
layers, while features within a layer may be ordered by size,
class, or importance to reduce occlusion and maintain visual
hierarchy.
The resulting data summaries and processing decisions are stored
explicitly in a structured JSON configuration, allowing downstream
agents to reason over both the selected information and the trans-
formations applied to it.

4.4
Design Agent
The Design agent receives the configuration produced by the Data
agent and determines the design operations, which include:
• Visual encoding: assign visual properties such as color,
symbology, size, line width, typography, and transparency
to each data layer and their subclasses according to geom-
etry type, semantic meaning, and category type such as
nominal, ordinal or quantitative.

• Compound encoding: combine multiple visual channels
to represent different attributes simultaneously. For ex-
ample, color intensity may encode hazard severity or im-
portance, while symbol shape distinguishes semantic cate-
gories.
• Cross-layer coordination: coordinate visual styles across
layers and subclasses to maintain semantic consistency
while preserving perceptual distinction. For example, fea-
tures belonging to the same semantic family may share a
common base color, while subcategories are differentiated
through variations in lightness, saturation, shape, or size.
The resulting visual-design decisions for each data layer are
stored in the shared JSON configuration.
4.5
Layout Agent
The Layout agent receives the visual design decisions from the
Design agent, together with the original data characteristics and pri-
orities identified by the Data agent, and determines the organization
of the map. Its decisions include:
• Legend organization: group and arrange legend items
according to their semantic relationships, topic, and impor-
tance.
• Legend refinement: improve the clarity and readability
of legend labels and descriptions using concise, plain lan-
guage.
• Explanatory content: add and arrange supplementary
panels with annotations that explain key map elements,
hazard information, or recommended interpretations.
• Layout adjustment: adjust spacing, text wrapping, and
the placement of map components to reduce unused space
and avoid crowding, particularly when adapting the lay-
outs to languages with different text lengths and writing
characteristics, such as English and Japanese.
The layout decisions, together with the refined legend labels and
explanatory text, are stored in a shared JSON configuration file.
4.6
Renderer
The Renderer takes the configurations produced by the Data agent,
Design agent, and Layout agent and executes the specified data-
processing, visual-design, and layout operations in QGIS1, a widely
used open-source platform for processing, analyzing, and visual-
izing geospatial data and producing maps. The renderer interacts
with QGIS through the Model Context Protocol (MCP), which ex-
poses QGIS functionalities as callable tools for programmatically
manipulating geospatial data, applying cartographic styles, arrang-
ing map elements and layouts, and exporting the final map image.
Unlike the LLM-based agents, the renderer does not make indepen-
dent cartographic decisions. Instead, it deterministically executes
the operations specified in the shared configurations, providing a
clear separation between agent reasoning and map rendering.

4.7
Evaluation Agent
Given a rendered map image, the Evaluation agent uses a vision-
language model (VLM) to assess map quality according to evalu-
ation criteria derived from expert input. To reduce the ambiguity
from broad description and improve the consistency of VLM-based
evaluation, each criterion is decomposed into a set of explicit sub-
conditions. The agent evaluates whether each sub-condition is sat-
isfied and aggregates these judgments into a criterion-level score.
For example, basemap clarity considers whether contextual layers
such as buildings and roads remain visually subordinate to haz-
ard information, whether they can be distinguished from hazard
layers, and whether important structures remain legible beneath
hazard overlays. The complete evaluation rubric is provided in
Appendix ??.
The overall quality score is computed as a weighted combina-
tion of the individual criteria, with weights derived from expert
input collected during the formative study. In the first iteration, the
system evaluates the rendered map independently and produces
an absolute score for each criterion together with a justification. In
subsequent iterations, the Evaluation agent compares the current
map with the previous generation and estimates the change (Δ)
for each criterion. This pairwise comparison helps mitigate vari-
ability in VLM judgments across iterations by focusing on relative
improvements or degradations between successive generations,
rather than relying solely on independently calibrated absolute
scores. For each criterion, the score at iteration 𝑡is updated as
𝑆𝑡= 𝑆𝑡−1 + Δ𝑡, where Δ𝑡denotes the VLM-estimated change rela-
tive to the previous generation. The criterion-level scores and their
corresponding justifications are stored in a single configuration file
for each iteration.
4.8
Revision Agent
Given the feedback from the Evaluation agent, the Revision agent
decomposes identified issues and routes them to the corresponding
upstream agents for refinement. For example, if different hazard
classes are not sufficiently distinguishable, the Revision agent directs
the issue to the Design agent; if the legend or explanatory panels are
overly text-heavy, the feedback is instead routed to the Layout agent.
Similarly, issues related to excessive map content are assigned to the
Data agent. To prevent the Revision agent from proposing changes
that cannot be executed by the system, the supported capabilities
of each upstream agent are explicitly provided as part of its context.
This ensures that revision suggestions remain actionable within
the capabilities of the current system.
4.9
Multi-Agent Coordination and Iterative
Refinement
Beyond the responsibilities of individual agents, the system main-
tains a shared state that enables agents to build on earlier decisions
and coordinate across stages. Within each generation cycle, agents
are executed in a sequence: Data Agent →Design Agent →Lay-
out Agent →Evaluation Agent →Revision Agent. The outputs of
upstream agents are written to the shared state that stores their
configurations as structured JSON files and become part of the
context available to downstream agents, allowing later decisions to
remain consistent with earlier ones.


Wu et al.
At the end of each iteration, the system records criterion-level
evaluation scores, corresponding justifications, and revision sug-
gestions targeted at specific agents. These records are accumulated
in shared memory and summarized before the next iteration into
two forms of guidance:
• What to improve. The system identifies the iteration with
the highest overall evaluation score and summarizes its
four highest-impact weaknesses together with the corre-
sponding revision suggestions and responsible agents. This
allows subsequent iterations to build on the strongest pre-
vious result rather than restarting from the most recent
output.
• Lessons from prior attempts. The system summarizes
changes across previous iterations and identifies revisions
associated with increases or decreases in evaluation scores.
This provides a history of prior attempts and helps reduce
oscillation, such as repeatedly introducing and reverting
the same design changes.
These summaries are passed to the relevant upstream agents as
contextual guidance for the next generation cycle. Distilling the
interaction history in this manner reduces redundant contextual
information and token consumption while retaining the most ac-
tionable information from previous attempts. The system continues
the generation–evaluation–revision cycle until either the prede-
fined acceptance criteria are satisfied or the maximum number of
iterations is reached. If the stopping threshold is not reached, the
highest-scoring map generated across all iterations is retained as
the final output. For the individual agents, we use Claude Sonnet
4.6 for the Data Agent, Claude Opus 4.8 for the Design Agent, and
GPT-5.5 for the Layout, Evaluation, and Revision Agents.
5
User Study
To evaluate the effectiveness of risk communication in the maps
generated by our system, we conducted a user study with 24 partic-
ipants. We examined whether the generated maps support spatial
and hazard understanding, facilitate evacuation planning, reduce
perceived workload and task-completion time, and are preferred
over the corresponding municipality-produced hazard maps.
5.1
Method
Participants. We recruited 24 participants (15 male, 9 female; ages
21-31, mean=25) who has been living in Japan for more than 1 year.
All participants have minimal or no prior experience with hazard
mapping and risk communication.
Study design. We employed a within-subjects experimental design
to compare two conditions: an original municipality map and a
map generated by our AI system. Each participant experienced
both conditions using maps from different study areas within the
same municipality to reduce memorization from repeated exposure
to the same location. Condition order was counterbalanced across
participants.
Task and materials. We selected hazard maps from two Japan-
ese municipalities representing different geographic and hazard
contexts: Odawara, characterized by mountainous terrain and com-
pound exposure to river flooding and sediment-related hazards, and
Ube, a more densely populated coastal city with prominent storm-
surge risk. The two municipalities have different flood mechanisms
and types. Twelve participants were assigned to each municipality,
and two administrative divisions were selected within each munici-
pality for the two experimental conditions. For both conditions, we
prepared A1-sized PDF maps displayed on a 14-inch MacBook and
provided an A4 printout for drawing tasks. Participants could freely
zoom and pan the digital maps up to the full-resolution A1 output,
ensuring comparable access to map detail across conditions. Par-
ticipants completed a set of map-reading tasks designed to assess
four dimensions identified in our formative study:
• Spatial awareness: locating communities, hazard zones, and
shelters; comparing elevation; and identifying routes be-
tween specified locations.
• Risk awareness: identifying hazard types and affected areas
and comparing risk severity across locations.
• Personal relevance: determining risks affecting a hypotheti-
cal home location, identifying an appropriate shelter, and
planning an evacuation route.
• Multi-hazard awareness. identifying multiple hazards affect-
ing the same area and reasoning about their implications
for evacuation.
Procedure. Each study session lasted approximately one hour
and was conducted in person. Participants received a small gift as
compensation. At the beginning, participants were given a brief
explanation of the study purpose before they filled out the informed
consent form. Participants then completed a pre-study question-
naire on demographics, prior familiarity with hazard maps, and
perceptions of existing hazard maps Next, they completed the map-
reading tasks under two map conditions. We recorded completion
time for each condition, and participants completed the NASA-TLX
questionnaire after each condition. Finally, in the post-study ques-
tionnaire participants rated both map conditions on 7-point Likert
scales, indicated their overall preference, and provided open-ended
feedback on perceived strengths, limitations, and opportunities for
improvement.
5.2
Results

questionnaire, most participants had some prior familiarity with
hazard maps: 10 reported being moderately familiar, 10 slightly fa-
miliar, and four not at all familiar. Among the 20 participants with
some familiarity, most had encountered flood hazard maps, while
approximately half were also familiar with tsunami or earthquake
hazard maps. Despite this prior exposure, 21 of the 24 participants
reported that they rarely or never used hazard maps in practice.
Participants also described several challenges with existing hazard
maps. These included difficulty understanding the maps (3 partici-
pants), limited prior instruction in school on how to use them (1),
difficulty locating useful information because of excessive detail
(3), and uncertainty about how to translate mapped information
into concrete actions or decisions (4). When asked what makes a
hazard map effective for risk communication, ease of understanding
was mentioned most frequently (10 participants). Related responses

6
Expert Interview
The expert interviews aimed to assess the quality of the generated
maps, examine the system’s practical utility within hazard-mapping
workflows, and identify opportunities for further improvement. We
recruited four map experts (P1–P4), who primarily focused on
cartographic quality and system design, as well as one hazard prac-
titioner (P5) and one hazard expert (P6), who primarily evaluated
the system from the perspective of real-world hazard-preparedness
practice.
6.1
Procedure.
The interviews consisted of two parts. First, we presented partic-
ipants with paired examples of official municipality hazard maps
and the corresponding maps generated by our system. Map experts
(P1–P4) were asked to assess whether the generated maps followed
established cartographic principles and to compare the two map
conditions in detail, focusing on aspects such as visual hierarchy,
symbolization, readability, and information organization. Hazard
practitioner (P5) and expert (P6) were asked to provide a broader
assessment of the generated maps, with particular attention to
their usefulness and appropriateness for hazard communication
and preparedness. Second, we presented the system architecture,
intermediate outputs, and iterative refinement process. Map experts
were asked whether the system reflected a reasonable cartographic
workflow, whether the coordination and refinement mechanisms
were appropriate, and how the system could be further improved.
The hazard practitioner and expert were asked how, and under what
conditions, the system could be integrated into practical hazard-
mapping workflows, as well as what limitations or requirements
would need to be addressed before real-world deployment.
6.2
Insights and Findings
Here we summarize the main insights from the expert interviews.
The generated maps are consistent with cartographic princi-
ples and generally higher in quality. All four map experts agreed
that the generated maps followed key cartographic principles, in-
cluding appropriate information generalization and simplification,
a clear visual hierarchy that emphasized critical information, in-
terpretable terrain representation, appropriately sized labels, and
well-structured legends. When compared with the corresponding
municipality maps, all four experts generally preferred the maps
generated by our system. Experts also favored the system-generated
color schemes for communicating relative risk levels, although their
preferences varied across specific designs. For example, P2 and P3
noted that the system’s tendency to use darker colors for the most
severe hazard levels could make high-risk areas visually heavy or
less comfortable to view. P2 additionally observed that symbols
were occasionally enlarged too aggressively to establish visual hier-
archy, which could obscure useful spatial information underneath.
Similarly, P4 reported instances of inconsistent prioritization of
overlapping hazard layers and variations in legend organization
across generations, which sometimes could reduce readability and
interpretability.
The system workflow matches the cartographic practice and
the iterative process is reasonable. All four map experts agreed
that the system broadly reflects their professional map-making
workflow, although P2 noted that the system makes some design
choices more stochastically than a human cartographer would. The
evaluation feedback generated by the VLM also generally aligned
with the experts’ own assessments (P1–P4), supporting the ratio-
nale of the subsequent refinement process. P2 further noted that the
improvements across iterations were consistent with their overall
professional judgment, even though cartographers do not neces-
sarily evaluate maps by explicitly decomposing quality into a fixed
set of criteria during everyday practice. Experts particularly appre-
ciated that the system produces an editable QGIS project through
MCP rather than generating maps solely as raster images. This
allows map-makers to inspect and directly modify individual map
layers, symbols, labels, and other design elements when further
adjustment is needed.
Future systems could better support interpretable pictogram
and symbol design. Map experts P2 and P3 suggested that future
versions of the system could provide stronger graphical support for
designing symbols that are intuitive and culturally appropriate, par-
ticularly for elements such as shelters. As P3 put it, well-designed
pictograms can communicate information more efficiently than text
alone and may also be used in explanatory panels to reduce lengthy
texts (“a picture is worth a thousand words”). Beyond symbol cre-
ation, P4 also highlighted opportunities for more sophisticated
symbol manipulation and composition, such as combining symbols
or deriving composite symbols through union or intersection op-
erations. Such operations could help manage visual conflicts and
reduce visual complexity, particularly in areas where multiple haz-
ard layers overlap.
Future systems could generate multiple map outputs adapted
to different users and situations. Given the relatively low cost
and rapid generation of maps, experts suggested that future versions
of the system could produce multiple outputs tailored to different
needs. First, maps could be adapted to different user groups (P3).
For example, land-use planners may require detailed, fine-grained
information to support policy and planning decisions, whereas the
general public may benefit from more simplified, generalized, and
aggregated representations that support rapid evacuation decisions.
Second, maps could be adapted to different spatial and situational
contexts (P1, P4). For instance, a community-scale view may be
more appropriate when an entire neighborhood is exposed and evac-
uation requires moving to another area, while a more local-scale
view may be preferable when nearby safe areas remain available.
Future systems could incorporate both expert knowledge
and user preferences. While expert preferences provide impor-
tant guidance for maintaining cartographic quality, P2 suggested
that future systems could also incorporate user preferences to better
accommodate the needs of the general public. However, because
end users may not have the same domain expertise as professional


Wu et al.
cartographers, P2 argued that user control should be more con-
strained than expert control. For example, if future versions support
interactive specification through prompts, users could adjust high-
level preferences or communication needs, while expert-derived
principles and constraints continue to govern critical cartographic
decisions.
Broadening access, human oversight, and the scope of AI
support. Hazard practitioner P5 and hazard expert P6 both recog-
nized the potential of the system to support municipalities with
limited cartographic expertise. At the same time, they emphasized
the importance of maintaining human involvement in AI-assisted
hazard-map production. P6 particularly raised concerns about re-
sponsibility and liability for the final output, stressing the need for
human supervision and accountability. P5 valued the editable QGIS
output, which allows local hazard-map makers to inspect and adjust
generated maps, and suggested additional LLM-based interaction
for those unfamiliar with QGIS, allowing them to specify desired
edits through natural language. Both P5 and P6 also envisioned
broader roles for AI beyond the current system. P5 distinguished
preparedness-oriented hazard communication from warnings and
alerts during an emerging or ongoing emergency. While the cur-
rent system focuses on preparedness maps that communicate what
could happen, future systems could extend toward warning and
alert communication that conveys what is happening and what ac-
tions people should take, supported by dynamic information such
as real-time hazard conditions, infrastructure status, route accessi-
bility, and recommended actions. P6 further noted that expertise
across municipalities may not be limited to cartographic design, but
also at earlier stages such as data collection, processing, and physi-
cal hazard modeling, suggesting opportunities for AI support across
a broader hazard-mapping lifecycle. He also suggested that extend-
ing to an interactive system could support public understanding
and hazard education, particularly among younger audiences.
7
Discussion
We now discuss about the main findings from user study and expert
interviews, limitations and outlook.
7.1
Main Findings
Incorporating expert cartographic knowledge can support
risk communication. By incorporating cartographic rules, quality
criteria, and expert heuristics, our system can output maps that
improve risk communication, particularly in spatial understanding
and evacuation planning. Participants frequently attributed the use-
fulness of the generated maps to characteristics such as selective
abstraction, information prioritization, visual hierarchy, basemap
clarity, and terrain representation. These design features can help
users to locate information easily, therefore support hazard under-
standing while reducing the effort required to interpret the maps.
Our system can emulate expert cartographic practice. By
mimicking key stages of the cartographic workflow, the system can
generate maps that satisfy core cartographic requirements while
iteratively adjusting design choices to balance competing quality
criteria, guided by the experts’ preferences. This provides a prac-
tical foundation for map production in contexts where access to
cartographic expertise is limited, while still supporting expert in-
tervention through editable QGIS outputs enabled by MCP. More
broadly, our findings suggest that agentic systems can operational-
ize cartographic expertise by separating hard and soft constraints,
grounding in established rules while preserving the ability to adapt
concrete design choices to different data, communities, and hazard
contexts.
“Less is more”, but simplification depends on the use case.
While people generally preferred simplified AI-generated maps due
to their ease of interpretation, they also expressed concerns that
excessive abstraction could omit or suppress information relevant
for decision-making. The optimal level of simplification should
depend on context and the intended use case. While aggressive
abstraction can support rapid comprehension for public hazard
communication, fine-grained information can provide a reference
for detailed assessment and planning-oriented tasks.
“Automate the map, not the responsibility.” Although the sys-
tem can automate substantial parts of municipal hazard-map pro-
duction, responsibility, liability, and accountability remain impor-
tant concerns for both users and practitioners. This suggests that
human review and transparent provenance remain important for
establishing AI-generated maps as authoritative public information.
“No one-size-fits-all map”, toward user-adaptive design. A sin-
gle representation may not satisfy all users as features considered
peripheral by one group may be critical for another. Therefore,
rather than universally optimized maps, user-centered personal-
ization on map content, level of detail, scale, and design can be
investigated. In the context of risk communication, aesthetic prefer-
ence may matter less than interpretive needs and prior knowledge.
For example, users with lower map literacy may benefit from sim-
plified representations, explicit annotations, and familiar symbols,
whereas more experienced users may prefer denser information
and greater technical detail.
7.2
Limitations and outlook
Here we also discuss about limitations and point out to future
directions.
Covering different stages of risk communication. Our study
primarily focused on hazard preparedness and evacuation planning
rather than near-disaster warnings or emergent actions during an
ongoing disaster. This setting allowed us to examine how users
interpret and plan with AI-generated maps under relatively stable
information conditions, but does not capture the temporal pressure
and rapidly changing information characteristic of an unfolding
emergency. But since our system can output maps rapidly at rela-
tive low expense, future work could extend it across different stages
of disaster management, from preparedness and early warning to
active evacuation, by incorporating real-time hazard conditions,
infrastructure status, traffic conditions, and accessible evacuation
routes, paired with textual warning, alerts, and other modalities.

Learning implicit cartographic preference. The current map-
quality scoring function was derived from a limited number of ex-
perts and relied primarily on explicit and fixed preference weights.
In practice, however, cartographic preferences are often contextual,
implicit, and difficult to fully articulate. Experts may make differ-
ent design decisions depending on the task, audience, geographic
setting, and communication goals. Future work could investigate
preference-alignment approaches that learn expert preferences
from ranked pairs of map designs [31] and larger collections of car-
tographic examples, enabling more flexible and context-sensitive
cartographic reasoning.
Enabling a broader cartographic design space. The current sys-
tem provides relatively limited functionality of manipulating map
features and design parameters, primarily focusing on information
selection and prioritization, colors schemes, and symbol and font
sizes. Future systems could broaden the design space by enabling
the generation of pictogram or symbols that are intuitive within
specific cultural context or supporting more complex symbol op-
eration, to better support visual communication and storytelling.
Additional map-generalization functions, such as shape simplifica-
tion and aggregation could further improve the communication of
complex spatial information [2, 14]. Beyond symbolization, future
systems could also support more advanced layout design by intel-
ligently arranging map, legend, and explanatory panels, allowing
greater flexibility in the organization and presentation of informa-
tion.
Extending to other hazard types and locations. The current
study focused on flood and sediment hazards. Despite variation in
their mechanisms, impacts, and interactions, the findings may not
generalize to other hazard types of different spatial and temporal
attributes. Future work could investigate hazards such as tsunamis,
earthquakes, wildfires, and extreme weather events, where near-
real-time information, uncertainty communication, and rapidly
evolving conditions play a more central role. Furthermore, due to
data availability constraints, the system was evaluated in a limited
number of cities. Additional studies are needed to assess its gener-
alizability across diverse geographic settings, data conditions, and
community needs.
Supporting local map makers through accessible interaction.
For maps intended for official use, municipal map makers may still
need to inspect and revise AI-generated outputs before publication.
Although the current system produces editable QGIS projects that
support further manual refinement, directly editing these outputs
still requires familiarity with the software. This may limit acces-
sibility for municipal staff responsible for hazard-map production
who possess local and hazard-domain knowledge but limited Geo-
graphic Information System (GIS) or cartographic expertise. Future
systems could therefore support natural-language interaction, al-
lowing local map makers to inspect the generated design, specify
desired changes, and modify the map without directly manipulating
complex GIS interfaces. Such interaction could retain human over-
sight while lowering the technical barrier to adapting generated
maps to local needs.
Extending AI support across the hazard-mapping lifecycle.
The current system focuses primarily on cartographic production,
although expertise may also be limited at earlier stages such as data
collection, processing, and physical hazard modeling. Future work
could investigate how AI support can extend across this broader
lifecycle through a more comprehensive agentic framework that
coordinates specialized agents across different stages.
Adapting to users, situations, and communication needs. Be-
yond supporting map makers in reviewing and approving a map for
official use, future systems could also adapt the resulting represen-
tations to different audiences and communication contexts. Impor-
tantly, such adaptation should concern how authoritative hazard
information is communicated rather than altering the underlying
hazard data, categories, or meanings. Beyond static printed maps,
interactive systems could adapt map content and presentation ac-
cording to users’ interpretive needs, prior knowledge, local commu-
nity contexts, and personal preferences, while remaining grounded
in expert cartographic guidance and official hazard-mapping re-
quirements.
8
Conclusion
We presented Agentic Hazard Mapper, a multi-agent system for
automated hazard-map production that emulates key stages of pro-
fessional cartographic practice and iteratively refines its outputs
through evaluation and revision. Grounded in cartographic rules,
hazard-mapping requirements, and expert-derived practices, the
system coordinates specialized agents for data processing, visual
design, layout, evaluation, and refinement while producing ed-
itable outputs through QGIS. Across a formative study, user study,
and expert interviews, our findings show that incorporating carto-
graphic expertise into an agentic workflow can improve the effec-
tiveness of hazard maps for risk communication. Compared with
corresponding municipality maps, the generated maps supported
higher task accuracy and faster completion, reduced perceived
workload, and were generally preferred by both users and map
experts. Participants particularly valued clearer information priori-
tization, stronger visual hierarchy, improved basemap readability,
and more interpretable terrain representation. At the same time,
our studies highlighted trade-offs between simplification and in-
formational completeness, differences in needs across users and
communication contexts, and the continued importance of human
oversight for maps intended for official use. These findings suggest
that the goal of automated hazard-map production should not be to
produce a single universally optimal map, but to make cartographic
expertise more accessible while enabling context-sensitive and user-
adaptive representations within authoritative hazard-mapping con-
straints. More broadly, our work points toward agentic systems as a
promising approach for supporting complex visual-communication
tasks that require both domain expertise and flexible, iterative de-
sign judgment.
