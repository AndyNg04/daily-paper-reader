# Learning Which Device to Call: Online Tool Grounding for LLM Agents in IoT 

Yichen Liu 1, Xiaochong Jiang 2, Haoran Yu 1, Lifei Liu 1

> 1

Independent Researcher, Seattle, WA, USA 2Independent Researcher, Boston, MA, USA Email: yil160@ucsd.edu, jiang.xiaoc@northeastern.edu, haoranyu889@gmail.com, lliu.lifei@gmail.com 

Abstract —The Internet of Things (IoT) gives large language model (LLM) agents access to physical observations, but one requested capability is usually offered by several devices, and the agent’s plan does not say which one to call. Existing work retrieves relevant tools and checks plan feasibility, without learning which eligible device returns a useful observation. Unlike previous works, we study online tool grounding: binding an already specified tool call to one physical endpoint under service cost and device participation constraints. We propose a framework that learns which physical device should execute each agent-issued call. It formulates selection as a contextual bandit problem and combines adaptive context partitions with hierarchical endpoint trees, so that execution feedback is reused across related requests and devices. Selection balances task success against service cost while respecting device capability, authorization, battery reserves, and availability. Gaussian reward aggregation provides an optional mechanism for protecting individual feedback values. Theoretical results establish a finite-horizon regret bound and differential privacy for released reward statistics under fixed, reward-independent assignments. In a simulated loading-dock workload, the proposed router achieves higher step success and better cost-adjusted service, at lower simulated device energy and latency, than nearest-device, advertised-quality, UCB1, and LinUCB selection. 

Index Terms —Agentic AI, Internet of Things, large language models, tool grounding, contextual bandits, differential privacy. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Large language model (LLM) agents provide a way to interact with Internet of Things (IoT) systems through natural-language requests. By combining reasoning with tool calls, an agent can gather observations, assess the returned evidence, and determine its next action [1]. The agent, however, reasons about capabilities, whereas the deployment consists of individual devices. Consider an agent asked whether a loading dock is blocked. The agent can issue capture_image(area) , but this call does not identify a unique camera. A fixed camera and a robot-mounted camera may both cover the area, yet differ in viewing angle and image quality, so the system must choose which device to invoke. We call the assignment of an already specified tool call to one physical device endpoint device-bound tool grounding , and we refer to each device–capability pair as an 

endpoint .Existing approaches address tool selection and physical feasibility from complementary directions: retrieval methods match requests to tool descriptions and adapt from interaction feedback [2], while spatial and resource-aware planners verify that a sensing plan is executable [3]. However, three problems remain. First, identifying a relevant capability or a feasible plan does not determine which eligible endpoint will deliver the most useful observation. A high-resolution camera may have an obstructed view, whereas a camera with lower resolution may provide sufficient evidence from a clearer angle [4], [5], and sensor outputs also degrade with device faults and operating conditions [6]. Usefulness is therefore a property of the request– endpoint pair and has to be learned from execution outcomes. Because only the selected device produces feedback, the router must balance using established endpoints against exploring alternatives. Second, the choice must reflect the devices’ current operating conditions. A mobile camera may move out of range, a battery-powered device may lack sufficient energy, and even a useful observation may arrive too late for the deadline. Selecting devices solely by past success therefore cannot ensure that the next call is feasible, and it depletes the reserves of the endpoints it favors. Third, the execution history used to improve these decisions is itself sensitive. A sequence of feedback records describes when a particular device at a particular location was called and how it responded, which reveals activity patterns of the monitored space. Releasing individual rewards or unprotected aggregates, for example to an operator dashboard, therefore discloses more than routing requires, and the learning process needs a disclosure-limiting mechanism with a defined scope. To address these challenges, we propose an online tool-grounding framework for LLM agents in connected IoT systems. We formulate each routing decision as a contextual bandit problem: the requested capability, target, quality requirements, and deadline define the context, and each eligible endpoint represents a candidate arm. The router checks compatibility, authorization, and predicted feasibility, selects one endpoint, and updates its utility estimate from a reward that combines task success with latency and energy cost. The LLM remains fixed: it chooses the operation and its target, and the router chooses the device. The proposed learner uses adaptive context partitions and hierarchical endpoint trees to organize this experience. Context cells group requests with similar requirements, while the end-point tree of each cell groups devices by their operating features, so that nearby request–endpoint pairs can share feedback under the stated smoothness assumptions. Refinement creates finer partitions as evidence accumulates. For privacy preservation, an optional Gaussian mechanism perturbs temporal-tree reward aggregates [7]. The guarantee protects individual reward values arXiv:2610.25563v1 [cs.AI] 18 Oct 2026 in released statistics when metadata, record assignments, and release schedules are fixed independently of rewards, and it does not extend to the complete adaptive routing interaction. The contribution of this paper lies in the device-bound tool-grounding setting and its formulation, not in new partition or aggregation components. The context and endpoint parti-tions follow the two-sided structure of [40], and the private aggregation follows tree-based private learning and Gaussian summation [7], [37]. What is new is how these parts are combined for agent tool calls: eligibility is checked as a hard constraint before learning, the reward comes from verified tool-step outcomes and service cost, and the privacy statement names exactly which released statistics it covers. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. LLM Tool Use and Learning from Execution Feedback 

LLM agents interleave reasoning with tool use [1], [9], organize the evidence and memory that such workflows accumulate [10]–[12], and generate or retrieve candidate tool calls [13], [14]. All of these decide which operation to perform; our setting begins once the operation and target are fixed. Another line of work improves these decisions from out-comes, by adapting retrieval during deployment or offline [2], [15], or by optimizing agent workflows, exploration, skill reuse, and self-evolving training loops [16]–[20]. These methods adapt the agent or its retriever; our learner keeps the LLM fixed and adapts only the choice of physical endpoint. Runtime security studies are complementary to per-call eligibility [21], [22]. 

B. Physical Grounding and IoT Scheduling 

SayCan, IoT-Brain, and intent-aware frameworks add skill affordances, spatial relationships, and capability checks to physical plans [3], [23], [24], and adaptive environmental monitoring selects sensors under energy constraints [25]. These approaches determine which plans are executable; we instead learn how useful each eligible provider is for a given request. Following studies that show aggregate scores can hide regime-specific failures [26], [27], we state the scope of our evaluation explicitly in Section IV. 

C. Contextual Bandits for Provider Selection 

Contextual bandits have been used to learn IoT service utility and crowdsourcing participation [28], [29], to scale to large candidate sets through hierarchical and similarity-based partitions [30], [31], and to route prompts among LLMs [32]. We carry this local learning to physical endpoints, where eligibility is a hard constraint rather than a feature. 

D. Private Online Learning for IoT 

Privacy in IoT learning has been studied mainly for feder-ated model training [33]–[36]. These methods protect model updates; our learner protects the reward statistics that drive routing. Private tree-based IoT service selection is the closest methodological precedent [37], and private bandit studies show that guarantees depend on which records are protected and what an observer can see [38], [39]. Our aggregation follows these principles and claims no privacy for raw observations or for the complete adaptive transcript. III. P ROPOSED METHOD

B. Locating the Context and Endpoint Partitions 

Within each capability class, a binary tree ΛE partitions the request space, and each active context cell C carries its own endpoint tree ΛPC . This two-sided structure [40] shares experience among similar requests while keeping provider estimates specific to the conditions under which they were observed, so a camera that is reliable for obstruction detection is not credited with the same utility for reading a label. E. Updating Private Statistics and Refining the Trees 

A trusted feedback service receives the verified reward and appends it once to the selected cell pair’s stream. A temporal binary tree stores the reward sums over intervals, each completed node j receives one independent perturbation 

Zj ∼ N (0 , σ 2) that is reused in subsequent queries, and any prefix combines at most J = 1 + ⌈log 2 N ⌉ nodes. We write

Proposition 1 (fixed-assignment reward privacy). Fix a reward-independent schedule containing metadata, stream assignments, local record order, and release times. Neighboring histories differ in one reward in [0 , 1] and share that schedule. Two mechanisms plausibly account for this ranking: context partitions separate the request-dependent utility that UCB1 pools, while local estimates capture relationships that are nonlinear in LinUCB’s features. The comparison does not isolate features, tuning, or the two partitions, so these remain explanations rather than attributions. 

2) Reward and the Success–Cost Balance: The same order-ing holds once service cost is taken into account. Fig. 3(b) reports mean rewards of 0.598 for Ours and 0.563 for Ours private, the latter exceeding UCB1’s 0.470 by approximately 19 .8% and LinUCB’s 0.496 by 13 .5% , and also exceeding Highest quality, the strongest baseline on this measure. Because success and reward rise together, the additional successes are not obtained by spending more time or energy, as expected from the combined objective in (4). 

3) Device Energy Efficiency: Fig. 3(c) shows energy per successful step of 1.61 J for Ours and 1.79 J for Ours private. LinUCB is the most efficient baseline at 2.05 J, so the noisy variant reduces the reported metric by 12 .7% ; against Highest quality at 2.91 J the reduction is 38 .5% .Highest quality combines a strong success rate with the highest energy cost, which is why advertised quality alone is an incomplete objective; the energy penalty instead favors useful providers with lower service cost. Under total-attempt accounting fewer failures also lower the ratio, and the averages do not separate this from lower per-call energy. 

4) Tool-Call Latency: Fig. 3(d) reports latencies of 0.341 sfor Ours and 0.356 s for Ours private, placing the noisy variant 

10 .6% below LinUCB’s 0.398 s and 4.3% below Nearest, the fastest baseline at 0.372 s. Proximity need not minimize response time, since device processing and communication also contribute. The noisy variant is slightly slower than Ours, likely because aggregation noise keeps it trying less-tested endpoints, some of which respond more slowly. The measure says nothing about deadline misses or tail latency, and it excludes routing computation, whose cost is bounded in Section III-F. 

D. Learning Behavior over the Request Sequence 1) Cumulative Utility Loss: The advantage persists across the request sequence. At the final round in Fig. 3(e), cumulative regret is approximately 6.1 × 10 3 for Ours and 9.7 × 10 3 for Ours private, against 1.63 × 10 4 for LinUCB, so the noisy variant loses roughly 40% less utility overall while still paying for early exploration against the feasible per-call oracle. These finite-horizon curves do not by themselves establish vanishing average regret or convergence to that oracle. 

2) Recent Reward and Recovery: Fig. 3(f) shows both proposed variants improving during the early rounds, with Ours settling near 0.60 and Ours private near 0.57 –0.58 . The gap between them is the continuing utility cost of noisy aggregation. After the transient near request 45 ,000 , both return to higher rolling rewards than the baselines, and Ours recovers earlier. 

E. Privacy–Utility Tradeoff and Discussion 

Collecting the two comparisons above, aggregation noise costs 3.1 percentage points of step success and 5.9% of mean reward, the expected shape of a privacy–utility tradeoff. The price is modest here, since the noisy variant still has better means than all four baselines on every reported measure. Taken together, the results suggest that learning request-dependent provider utility is useful for this simulated loading-dock workload, at a utility cost when reward aggregates are protected. 

F. Limitations 

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation uses our own simulator, in which success labels are sampled directly. It does not include real devices, images, LLM inference, or a verifier, so the results describe router behavior in this workload rather than gains in deployment. Second, the comparison covers four baselines over five seeds. It does not include contextual zooming [31], Thompson sampling, or private tree-based selection [37], and it does not separate the effects of the context partitions, the endpoint trees, and the feature design. Third, the regret bound relies on the smoothness and stationarity assumptions of Section III, can be vacuous when cells stay coarse, and was not tested for sensitivity to Le and Lp. Under the simulated recharge rate, batteries stayed close to full charge, so the reserve check in (1) did not remove endpoints in practice and the energy side of eligibility was not exercised. Fourth, Proposition 1 covers released reward statistics under a fixed, reward-independent schedule. It does not cover the adaptive router used in our runs, which we evaluated only at ε = 8 , and we report no sweep over ε or empirical leakage test. V. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we presented an online tool-grounding frame-work for selecting physical device providers for LLM agents in connected IoT systems. Adaptive context and endpoint partitions reuse execution feedback to learn request-dependent device utility while accounting for authorization, battery reserves, availability, and deadlines. The analysis characterizes finite-horizon regret under the stated assumptions. Gaussian reward aggregation provides differential privacy for released statistics under fixed, reward-independent assignments. In the simulated loading-dock workload, both proposed variants achieve higher mean step success and mean reward and lower cumulative regret than the four evaluated baselines, with lower reported device energy per successful step and tool-call latency. Adding noise reduces success and reward, showing the performance cost of reward-value protection. Future work will calibrate the success model with a vision-language model on real images, test the router on trace-driven and physical device data, add matched component studies and stronger baselines, and extend the privacy accounting to the full adaptive transcript.
