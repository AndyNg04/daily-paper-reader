## Abstract 

Sustained cooperation among self-interested learning agents is essential in many shared environments. Reputation systems are among the best-studied mechanisms for promoting such coopera-tion. Existing multi-agent reinforcement learning approaches, how-ever, typically assume that reputation is either globally available to all agents or derived solely from an agent’s own observations, leaving largely unexplored how reputational information propa-gates between agents. We study gossip, the exchange of information about third parties, as a mechanism for reputation diffusion over dynamic communication networks. Agents form private opinions from limited observations and exchange them with their neighbors following DeGroot opinion dynamics, while the communication network changes as agents interact and move. A tunable broadcast radius controls the reach of information, spanning private repu-tation, local gossip, and globally shared reputation. We study this framework in two 𝑛 -player social dilemmas with distinct network dynamics: a Prisoner’s Dilemma with randomly resampled inter-action groups and Harvest, where communication neighborhoods emerge from agents’ movements. Our results show that the reach of reputational information shapes cooperation in both environments. Moreover, in Harvest, reputation diffusion increases environmental rewards and leads to more peaceful and sustainable behavior. 

## Keywords 

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, Reputation, Gossip, Opinion Dynamics, Social Dilemmas, Dynamic Networks 

## 1 Introduction 

Cooperation among self-interested agents is central to deploying learning systems in shared environments and has been studied across disciplines, including evolutionary biology [27, 32, 33] and multi-agent systems (MAS) [35, 52]. For example, autonomous vehi-cles share road space with other vehicles and human drivers, while energy and bandwidth markets allocate finite resources among participants with competing demands. Such interactions are of-ten mixed-motive: agents’ objectives are neither fully aligned nor strictly opposed, and individually rational behavior can lead to outcomes that are worse for everyone [8, 17, 37, 38]. This tension between individual and collective interests characterizes a social dilemma, in which self-interested learning can favor defection over sustained cooperation. Understanding which mechanisms can sus-tain cooperation among learning agents is therefore a fundamen-tal challenge for deploying populations of autonomous agents in shared environments [10, 41]. Reputation is among the best-studied mechanisms for promoting cooperation. Under indirect reciprocity, an agent’s past conduct informs a social standing that others use to determine how to interact with it, allowing defection to carry costs through the sub-sequent withdrawal of cooperation by others rather than through direct retaliation [15, 30, 50]. MARL has incorporated this idea in several forms, ranging from reputations assigned by third-party ob-servers according to fixed social norms [1, 36] to learned evaluation policies [45]. These approaches demonstrate how tracking agents’ behavior over time can promote cooperation among agents [26]. In these approaches, however, reputation either reaches every agent at once or remains confined to the agent that observes it, leav-ing largely unexplored how reputational information can spread through second-hand communication [13, 47]. When a single ob-server or a fixed norm maintains one score per agent that is available to all participating agents [1, 36], reputation is globally available by design. Conversely, when reputation is formed from first-hand interaction [45], information remains private to the observer, limit-ing the reputational consequences of behavior that others do not witness [9, 29, 40, 53]. Gossip sits between these extremes: agents exchange assessments of third parties, allowing information ac-quired through direct observation to reach agents who did not witness the underlying behavior. Gossip introduces two challenges for reputation systems. First, observers are not interchangeable [18, 20, 45]. Agents form judg-ments from limited and potentially different information, including partial views of the environment, distinct interactions, and private histories. As a result, agents may hold conflicting opinions, and propagating these opinions may lead to disagreement or even con-sensus around an inaccurate assessment. Second, the communica-tion network itself may not be fixed. Who can inform whom changes as agents interact [39, 44, 49]. Existing gossip-based approaches, however, typically assume a fixed communication graph [48]. To address these challenges, we study how reputational informa-tion propagates among learning agents that form different opinions from limited observations and communicate over networks that change over time. We introduce a reputation framework that sepa-rates three processes: private assessment, reputation aggregation, and information diffusion. First, each agent forms private opinions of the agents it directly observes according to a fixed assessment rule. Second, these private opinions are aggregated into a reputa-tion signal that shapes each agent’s reward. Third, agents exchange opinions with their neighbors following the DeGroot model of opin-ion dynamics [5], allowing information acquired through direct observation to propagate to agents that did not witness the underly-ing behavior. A tunable broadcast radius 𝜌 controls the reach of this information, spanning private reputation, local gossip, and globally arXiv:2610.22674v1 [cs.MA] 15 Oct 2026 , , 

shared reputation. The communication network is redrawn at every step, either through the resampling of interaction partners or as a consequence of agents’ movement. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to study how reputation diffusion through opin-ion dynamics shapes cooperation among reinforcement learning agents over dynamic communication networks. Our contributions are as follows: 

• We introduce a decentralized reputation framework that explicitly models how reputational information propagates among learning agents. The framework separates private assessment, aggregation, and gossip, using DeGroot opin-ion dynamics to exchange private opinions and a tunable broadcast radius to span private, local, and global reputation. 

• We couple reputation diffusion with dynamic communica-tion networks and study two distinct forms of network evo-lution: exogenous changes through random regrouping in an 𝑛 -player Prisoner’s Dilemma, and endogenous changes induced by agents’ learned movement in Harvest [21]. 

• We characterize how the reach of reputational information shapes cooperation and collective outcomes. In both games, it encourages cooperation, and in Harvest, it additionally encourages more peaceful and sustainable behavior. 

## 2 Related Work 

Cooperation in Mixed-Motive Games. In matrix games, the Pris-oner’s Dilemma is the canonical model of the tension between in-dividual and collective interests, motivating a broad range of mech-anisms for promoting cooperation among self-interested agents. Opponent-shaping methods promote cooperation by influencing how other agents learn [14, 23], while reciprocity-based approaches incorporate responses to an opponent’s past behavior directly into the agent’s policy [22, 43]. Another class of methods introduces partner selection, allowing agents to choose whom to interact with and thereby creating incentives for cooperative behavior [1, 2]. Sequential social dilemmas extend matrix games to Markov en-vironments, where cooperation and defection correspond to tempo-rally extended behaviors rather than individual actions [21]. Pérolat et al. [42] instantiate this framework in common-pool resource (CPR) appropriation and introduce Harvest, which serves as our second testbed. Subsequent work has developed several mecha-nisms for promoting cooperation in Harvest. One class augments agents’ rewards with social preferences, including inequity aver-sion [19] and altruism [25]. McKee et al. [25], for example, show that introducing altruistic agents can increase collective return, but at the cost of equality, as altruistic agents effectively subsidize the consumption of their self-interested peers. A second class of studies allows agents to directly influence one another’s incentives through mechanisms such as gifting [24] and binding contracts [3]. While these approaches promote cooperation by modifying agents’ incentives or preferences, our work instead studies how coopera-tion emerges when information about agents’ behavior propagates through reputation and gossip. 

Reputation in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning. Agrowing body of work incorporates reputation into MARL as a mechanism for promoting cooperation. Existing approaches differ primarily in how reputation is constructed and how reputational information is made available to other agents. One class assigns each agent a reputation, often represented as a discrete label, that is maintained by a third-party observer or centralized mechanism and made available to other agents [1, 26, 36]. In these approaches, reputation is public by construction: each agent is associated with a shared reputation value rather than potentially different assess-ments held by different observers. Consequently, the propagation of reputational information is not explicitly modeled; agents do not need to exchange their assessments, and access to reputation does not depend on the communication network. In our work, by contrast, agents form private opinions from limited observations, and reputational information propagates through communication with neighboring agents before being aggregated into reputation. Closest to our work are LR2 [45] and COOPER [48], which simi-larly allow agents to maintain private assessments of one another. In LR2, however, agents interact within fixed neighborhoods, and their assessments do not propagate beyond the agents they directly en-counter. COOPER allows agents to interact with randomly selected opponents and exchange assessments through a social network, but considers two-player games and assumes a static communi-cation graph. Our work extends these settings to 𝑛 -player social dilemmas in which both interaction partners and communication neighborhoods can change over time. We consider both exogenous network dynamics, where agents are randomly regrouped between rounds, and endogenous network dynamics, where communication neighborhoods emerge from agents’ learned movement. 

Evolutionary Biology. Reputation has long been studied in evolutionary biology as a mechanism for indirect reciprocity [27, 32, 33], with subsequent work characterizing the assessment rules that sustain cooperation [34] and the design of reputation sys-tems [6]. Much of this theory assumes that reputations are publicly shared. Relaxing this assumption introduces private assessments and, consequently, disagreement. Such disagreement can under-mine cooperation [18], and no cooperative norm is evolutionarily stable when individuals’ opinions are statistically independent [31]. Gossip provides a mechanism to overcome this informational frag-mentation by allowing individuals to learn about others beyond their own direct observations [13, 47]. Kawakatsu et al. [20], for example, characterize how much gossip is required for a population to reach sufficient consensus to stabilize cooperation. These results are developed primarily for evolutionary populations, where strate-gies spread through selection across generations. We study a related question in reinforcement learning agents by examining how the diffusion of private assessments through gossip shapes cooperation as agents learn over dynamic communication networks. 

Opinion Dynamics and Evolving Networks. We model gos-sip using the DeGroot model [5], in which agents repeatedly update their opinions by averaging those of their neighbors. Repeated av-eraging provides a simple mechanism for aggregating distributed information and, under appropriate conditions, leads opinions to-ward consensus [7, 16]. Separately, a large body of work has shown that network dynamics can shape the emergence of cooperation. Cooperation can change qualitatively when social ties are rewired over time [39, 49], and experiments with human groups show that cooperation is more readily sustained when participants can modify their connections [44]. To the best of our knowledge, these two strands have not been brought together in MARL to study how , , 

opinions propagate over evolving communication networks and, in turn, shape cooperation among learning agents.

## 3.2 Sequential Social Dilemmas 

Sequential social dilemmas extend social dilemmas from matrix games to Markov environments, where cooperation and defection are expressed through temporally extended behaviors rather than individual actions. We focus on Harvest, a partially observable gridworld in which 𝑁 agents move through a 2D environment pop-ulated with apples. An agent collects an apple by stepping onto its tile, receiving a reward of 1. Apples regrow at a rate determined by the local density of remaining apples, but cease to regrow in regions where they are completely depleted. They also cannot regrow on tiles occupied by agents. These dynamics create a social dilemma: aggressive harvesting provides immediate individual rewards but can deplete apple fields, producing a Tragedy of the Commons, whereas sustainable harvesting preserves the shared resource and yields higher long-term returns. Agents can also temporarily re-move one another from play for 𝑠 steps using a time-out beam, while their observations are restricted to a fixed view radius 𝑟 .Prior work on Harvest identifies a three-stage dynamic [42]. Agents initially enter an exploration phase, followed by a Tragedy of the Commons phase in which widespread overharvesting de-pletes the field and causes collective returns to collapse. At the end, agents learn to use the time-out beam strategically, reducing the number of active agents and allowing the remaining population to harvest the regrowing resource. Sustainability in Harvest therefore emerges not through voluntary restraint, but through the exclu-sion of competing agents. This dynamic motivates reputation as an alternative mechanism for inducing sustainable behavior. 

## 4 Methods 

We are interested in the behavior that emerges from standard reinforcement learning agents, and particularly in whether they can learn to cooperate when reputational information is acquired through direct observation and gossip. We do not hand-engineer prosocial strategies into the agents. Instead, agents form opinions of others using simple, fixed assessment rules based on their di-rect observations, exchange these opinions with their neighbors through gossip, and learn from the reputational information that results. An agent’s own reputation shapes its reward, while its opinions of others can inform its decisions. Prosocial strategies must therefore be discovered by the RL algorithm in response to these reputation signals. To study how the diffusion of reputational information affects this learning process, we introduce a flexible, observation-based reputation framework in which the reach of gos-sip is controlled by a tunable parameter, spanning no information sharing, local gossip, and population-wide broadcast. We describe the framework in the following subsection. 

## 4.1 Reputation Framework 

Our framework consists of three core mechanisms: local observa-tion, parameterized broadcasting, and temporal aggregation. First, agents form private opinions from local observations, allowing dif-ferent agents to hold different assessments of the same individual. Second, agents exchange these opinions through a parameterized broadcasting mechanism, where the broadcast radius controls how far reputational information propagates. Varying this radius spans settings ranging from private, first-hand information to local word-of-mouth and population-wide broadcasting. Third, the private opinions from the first step are aggregated over time into reputa-tion signals, providing a persistent memory of past behavior while allowing reputations to adapt as agents’ behavior changes. ## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate our reputation framework under the three broadcasting protocols introduced in Section 4: No Broadcast , Local Broadcast ,and Broadcast All . We compare these methods against a No Repu-tation baseline, in which agents are trained using only the extrinsic environment reward and receive no reputational information. This baseline corresponds to standard independent deep Q-learning, al-lowing us to isolate the effect of introducing reputation and its diffusion through gossip.

## 5.2 Opinion Formation 

We formalize the opinion formation rule introduced in Section 4 for the two games below. , , 

5.2.1 Prisoner’s Dilemma: 

(1) If 𝑖 observes 𝑗 cooperate, then 𝜎 𝑡 𝑖 𝑗 = +1(2) If 𝑖 cooperates and observes 𝑗 defect, then 𝜎 𝑡 𝑖 𝑗 = −1Because tablemates play against one another, an observer’s as-sessment reflects how a neighbor’s action affected the observer. The rule therefore condemns exploitation rather than defection. A player who defects against a cooperating observer exploits that observer and is assessed negatively, whereas mutual defection re-sults in no assessment. Cooperation is always assessed positively because it does not exploit the observer. Consequently, the same ac-tion can produce different assessments across tablemates: a player who defects against the entire table is assessed negatively by those who cooperated but receives no assessment from those who also defected. Opinions therefore depend on agents’ local experiences. 

5.2.2 Harvest: 

(1) If 𝑖 observes 𝑗 consume one of the last two apples within 𝑖 ’s view, then 𝜎 𝑡 𝑖 𝑗 = −1. If 𝑖 observes 𝑗 adjacent to such an apple but choosing not to consume it, then 𝜎 𝑡 𝑖 𝑗 = +1. (2) If 𝑖 observes no apple around, then for all neighbors 𝑗 within observation 𝜎 𝑡 𝑖 𝑗 = −1. (3) If 𝑖 observes 𝑗 firing the tagging beam at any agent 𝑘 with reputation 𝐸 𝑡 𝑘 ≥ 0, then 𝜎 𝑡 𝑖 𝑗 = −1. The first rule treats exploitation of the shared resource as antiso-cial and restraint as prosocial. The second rule treats resource deple-tion as a shared, global responsibility. The third treats aggression (i.e., beaming) toward agents in good standing as antisocial, reflect-ing social norms against unprovoked harm [4]. In contrast, tagging a low-reputation agent carries neither a reputational reward nor a penalty, allowing agents to sanction antisocial behavior without external compensation. This design resembles altruistic punish-ment [11, 12]: an agent bears the opportunity cost of sanctioning a defector, forgoing apple collection while firing the time-out beam, without receiving a direct reward for doing so.

5.4.1 Prisoner’s Dilemma: Figure 3 shows how cooperation evolves throughout training in the Prisoner’s Dilemma under three broad-casting settings: Broadcast All and Local Broadcast with 𝜌 = 5and 𝜌 = 10. Each curve reports the mean across 10 independent runs, with shaded regions indicating the variance across runs. The vertical axis reports the average number of rounds per episode (out of 1000) in which an agent cooperates. Reputation promotes cooperation across all three broadcasting settings, in contrast to the No Reputation baseline, where agents consistently defect. The broadcast radius has little effect on the overall level of co-operation, with all three settings converging to similar outcomes. Differences in convergence speed are small relative to the variation across runs. Local Broadcast with 𝜌 = 10 converges slightly faster than with 𝜌 = 5, whereas Broadcast All converges more slowly and exhibits the greatest variation, both during training and after con-vergence. Consequently, agents can accumulate first-hand information about much of the population within relatively few rounds, reducing the additional information provided by gossip. 

5.4.2 Harvest: Figures 4–8 show the evolution of utility, peaceful-ness, equality, sustainability, and punishment accuracy throughout training. Each curve reports the mean across 10 independent runs, with shaded regions indicating the variance across runs. The three reputation-based methods differ only in the broadcast radius 𝜌 . No Broadcast (𝜌 = 0) restricts agents to opinions formed from their own observations; Local Broadcast (𝜌 = 5) allows agents to ex-change opinions within their local communication neighborhoods; and Broadcast All (𝜌 = ∞) allows opinions to be shared across the entire population. The No Reputation baseline removes the reputation mechanism entirely. , , 

Figure 4 shows that all three reputation-based methods substan-tially outperform the No Reputation baseline, converging to utility values above 110. In contrast, the baseline peaks near 67 early in training before declining to roughly 50 as agents learn to deplete the shared resource. Among the reputation-based methods, utility increases with the broadcast radius. Broadcast All improves more slowly early in training but eventually surpasses the other two methods after approximately 5000 episodes. Sustainability exhibits the clearest effect of broadcasting (Fig-ure 7). The No Reputation baseline declines steadily from approxi-mately 480 to 260, indicating that agents collect rewards progres-sively earlier in the episode as they deplete the apple patches. Reputation-based methods remain substantially above the base-line, with sustainability increasing with the broadcast radius. No Broadcast, however, exhibits considerable variance across runs. One possible explanation is that agents rely exclusively on their own ob-servations, and thus the emergence of sustainable harvesting varies substantially across runs. In contrast, both broadcasting methods exhibit lower variance, suggesting that opinion sharing leads to more consistent sustainable behavior, in addition to improving av-erage sustainability. Interestingly, utility varies much less across No Broadcast runs, indicating that agents collect similar numbers of apples but at substantially different times within episodes. Figure 5 shows that reputation also promotes peacefulness, with all reputation-based methods outperforming No Reputation. Local Broadcast and No Broadcast perform similarly, while Broadcast All achieves slightly lower peacefulness. A different pattern emerges for equality (Figure 6). No Broadcast converges to a level comparable to the baseline, whereas Broadcast All achieves higher sustainability and utility at the cost of lower equality. One possible explanation is that population-wide opinion sharing makes an agent with a poor reputation identifiable to all other agents, increasing its exposure to repeated time-outs and potentially limiting its ability to recover its reputation. The punishment accuracy results in Figure 8 are consistent with this interpretation. Broadcast All most consistently directs time-out beams toward agents with negative reputation, whereas Local Broadcast and No Broadcast converge to similar levels of punishment accuracy. The baseline, in which agents do not use reputation, exhibits the lowest punishment accuracy. Taken together, these results suggest that Local Broadcast pro-vides the most balanced performance across metrics. The two ex-tremes exhibit distinct trade-offs. No Broadcast preserves equality and peacefulness but achieves the lowest sustainability among the reputation-based methods and exhibits substantial variation across runs. Broadcast All achieves the highest utility, sustainability, and punishment accuracy, but with lower equality, slightly lower peace-fulness, and slower improvement early in training. Local Broadcast largely avoids these trade-offs: it maintains the high equality and peacefulness of No Broadcast while recovering most of the sus-tainability benefits and consistency associated with Broadcast All. These results suggest that limiting the reach of opinion sharing can retain much of the benefit of reputation diffusion without the lower equality associated with population-wide broadcasting. 

## 6 Conclusion & Future Work 

We introduced a reputation framework in which agents form pri-vate opinions from local observations, exchange these opinions with their neighbors through DeGroot averaging, and receive re-wards shaped by their resulting reputations as the communication network evolves over time. We studied this framework in an 𝑛 -player Prisoner’s Dilemma with randomly regrouped interaction partners and in the Harvest sequential social dilemma, using the broadcast radius to control the reach of opinion sharing. Across both environments, agents learned cooperative strategies without those strategies being explicitly prescribed. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, agents without reputation converged to defection, whereas agents with reputation cooperated in approxi-mately 95% of rounds across all broadcast radii. The broadcast radius had little effect on average cooperation, although population-wide broadcasting produced greater variation across runs. In Harvest, reputation more than doubled utility relative to the baseline while also promoting more peaceful and sustainable behav-ior. Increasing the broadcast radius further improved sustainability and reduced variance across runs. However, broader opinion shar-ing was not uniformly beneficial: population-wide broadcasting achieved the highest utility and sustainability but the lowest equal-ity, whereas local broadcasting retained most of these benefits while preserving equality and peacefulness. These findings show that the reach of reputation diffusion is itself an important design choice, shaping not only cooperation and collective performance but also how their benefits are distributed across agents. One limitation of our work is that we fix two components that prior work has shown can be learned. Agents assess one another using fixed rules and aggregate received opinions through simple averaging, whereas LR2 [45] and COOPER [48] learn assessment rules and, in the case of COOPER, how agents weigh information received from their neighbors. We deliberately fix these compo-nents to isolate the effect of reputation diffusion, but this leaves open whether learned assessment and aggregation remain effective as communication networks evolve. Extending our framework to jointly learn these mechanisms is a natural direction for future work. We also assume that agents communicate their opinions truthfully. Strategic agents could instead manipulate the information they share, for example, by spreading false assessments of competitors or concealing unfavorable information about themselves. Studying strategic or noisy gossip would therefore help establish how robust our findings are to imperfect communication.
