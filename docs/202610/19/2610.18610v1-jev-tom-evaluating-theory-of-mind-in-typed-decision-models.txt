# Jev-ToM: Evaluating Theory of Mind in Typed Decision Models 

Kawai Chung Chunkit Chan Yuxuan Liu Yauwai Yim Xi Yang Wenbin Hu Huihao Jing Chiu Yeung Tang Changxuan Fan Ki Sen Hung Tsun On Kwok Haoran Li 

Department of Computer Science and Engineering The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology

Abstract 

Dialogue and collaborative assistants repeat-edly judge what a partner knows, believes, or intends. Typed decision models offer a po-tential alternative to generative large language models for bounded Theory of Mind (ToM) selection. We present Jev-ToM, comparing five typed decision models and seven genera-tive baselines on 11,117 English text questions from nine ToM benchmarks across six capabil-ity groups. TypeSafe Jev reaches 73.2% source-balanced accuracy including native controls and ranks first or second across all six capabil-ities, leading beliefs, goals/desires/intentions, and emotions. Other typed models show dis-tinct strengths across tasks. Separate resource comparisons suggest that Jev could reduce the cost and waiting time of repeated ToM se-lection. Confidence-based selection remains benchmark-dependent, and supplied reasons can repair errors or overturn correct answers depending on their attachment. Candidate ex-pansion reveals distinct selection and output-validity problems. Related-question cues ac-count for most of Jev’s downstream gains, with task-dependent effects from predicted states. The findings motivate testing Jev as a ToM selection component in assistants, with task-specific confidence calibration, candidate de-sign, and output validation as integration prior-ities. 

1 Introduction 

Theory of Mind (ToM) is the ability to attribute mental states, such as beliefs, knowledge, and in-tentions, to others and use them to interpret or pre-dict behavior (Premack and Woodruff, 1978). Ad-vances in the language and reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have brought in-creasing attention to their ability to reason about others’ perspectives (Strachan et al., 2024, Chan et al., 2026). Agents use mental-state inferences for action selection and response generation. Collaborative agents update explicit belief states from observa-tions for planning (Li et al., 2023). Dialogue agents use generative LLMs, including compact models, to infer participants’ mental states from conversa-tion and condition responses on those inferences (Hwang et al., 2026). For example, ToM-agent tracks a partner’s beliefs, desires, and intentions in simulated persuasion dialogues, such as attempts to encourage a donation (Yang et al., 2025). It main-tains candidate interpretations, revises them using observed and predicted replies, and conditions its next response on the candidate with the highest confidence. The authors identify repeated LLM calls and long prompts as sources of waiting time and cost (Yang et al., 2025). Repeated mental-state selection motivates testing Jev for candidate-based judgments in dialogue and collaboration, with can-didate generation and response composition left to the surrounding system. Such intermediate judg-ments can have finite alternatives, even when final responses require open-ended language. Typed decision models offer a direct interface for bounded judgments (Wang, 2026, Deußer et al., 1arXiv:2610.18610v1 [cs.CL] 13 Oct 2026 2026). Given context, a question, and candidates, TypeSafe Jev (hereafter Jev) returns a selection and candidate probabilities as structured outputs (Type-Safe AI, 2026). Its design targets fast, inexpen-sive decisions (Almeida, 2026). Structured selec-tion makes Jev a promising candidate for bounded ToM judgments currently performed by generative LLMs. Fulfilling this role requires accurate mental-state judgments at low time and cost. Fast, structured output alone does not establish suitability for this role. A high overall score can conceal differences between tracking knowledge, attributing beliefs or emotions, and applying men-tal states to behavior. Moreover, an agent needs to know when a selection is reliable and whether supplied reasons alter the choice or break the re-quired output format. This raises a research ques-tion: How competitive and reliable is Jev as a bounded ToM decision component? 

We present Jev-ToM, a systematic framework for evaluating Jev’s ToM capabilities. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first dedicated eval-uation of Jev’s ToM capabilities across multiple benchmarks organized into explicitly defined capa-bility groups. We compare five typed decision mod-els and seven generative baselines on 11,117 ques-tions from nine existing ToM benchmarks across six operational capability groups, as shown in Fig-ure 1. We examine confidence-based answer se-lection, interference from supplied reasons, and output validity, alongside observed response time and reference-price cost. Our results show that competitive accuracy can coexist with benchmark-dependent confidence er-rors, while supplied reasons can improve or disrupt selections depending on their attachment and the evaluated materials. The evaluation identifies confi-dence calibration, candidate sensitivity, and output validity as priorities for integration testing. Our contributions are threefold: • We compare Jev and generative LLMs across ToM tasks, measuring accuracy, reference cost, and observed response time. • We diagnose Jev’s reliability through confidence-based selection and candidate interventions, sep-arating selection errors from output-validity fail-ures obscured by aggregate accuracy. • We investigate supplied reasons and mental-state feedback, testing reason attachment and sepa-rating the contributions of related-question cues and predicted states.

2 Related Work 

ToM agents and benchmarks. Mental-state judgments support cooperative planning (Li et al., 2023), dialogue perspective tracking (Qiu et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025), response selection (Hwang et al., 2026), and action prediction (Mu et al., 2026). These uses motivate evaluating inter-mediate mental-state judgments in agents. ToM benchmarks differ in what successful judgment requires: controlled belief attribution and higher-order perspectives (beliefs about another person’s beliefs) (Le et al., 2019, Gandhi et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2023), conversational information asymme-try (Kim et al., 2023), or diverse psychological states and social situations (Xu et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024, Tong et al., 2026, Shinoda et al., 2025). SimpleToM separates mental-state inference from behavior prediction and judgment (Gu et al., 2026), while NegotiationToM studies inference in human negotiations (Chan et al., 2024). This variety makes scope and aggregation choices central to interpret-ing a ToM score. 

Jev interfaces and evaluation. Typed decision interfaces expose choices, scores, and probabilities for software to consume (Almeida, 2026, TypeSafe AI, 2026). Mnemon uses Jev to evaluate retrieved records while LLM plans searches and composes responses (Wang, 2026). This division motivates a similar component role for mental-state selection. Broad Jev evaluations already examine task perfor-mance, calibration, and resource use (Deußer et al., 2026), alongside annotation and rubric assessment (Ibrahim and Zaki, 2026, Rao and Callison-Burch, 2026). Adversarial and option-definition studies establish that structured outputs can remain sensi-tive to request content (Hu et al., 2026, Sun et al., 2026). JevOut also includes ToMBench when test-ing contextual additions that redirect decisions (Xu 2et al., 2026). We extend these studies by connect-ing cross-benchmark ToM profiles with confidence-based selection, bidirectional changes under sup-plied reasons, and output validity. 

3 Experiments 

To assess breadth and reliability in bounded ToM selection, we compare performance on fixed an-swer choices and examine confidence, supplied reasons, and resource use in separate subsets of models and questions. 

3.1 Theory of Mind Ability Categories 

We adapt Abilities in Theory of Mind Space (ATOMS) to group questions by the mental states they concern (Beaudoin et al., 2020). Knowledge access covers information available through ob-servation and communication (Kim et al., 2023). 

Beliefs concern representations of the world and others’ perspectives (Gandhi et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2023). Goals, desires, and intentions group re-lated preferences and planned actions for cross-source comparison (Xu et al., 2024). Emotions 

covers feelings (Shinoda et al., 2025), while social pragmatics groups socially situated and non-literal meaning (Chen et al., 2024). CogToM’s native Per-cept items remain in benchmark totals but outside capability groups pending finer mapping. 

Mental-state application adds a different di-mension: using a perspective to predict, select, or judge behavior (Gu et al., 2026). A question ask-ing what someone believes concerns attribution, whereas one asking how that belief affects their action concerns application. Application can over-lap the state categories because it describes a task demand. Figure 1 also shows unequal source cover-age: beliefs draw on eight benchmarks, while social pragmatics draws on two. These profiles compare models over the specified question groups. 

3.2 Dataset Selection and Harmonization 

We assemble a common evaluation from nine ex-isting ToM benchmarks with English contexts, ref-erence answers, and discrete candidate choices (Gandhi et al., 2023, Le et al., 2019, Wu et al., 2023, Kim et al., 2023, Xu et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024, Tong et al., 2026, Shinoda et al., 2025, Gu et al., 2026). Table 1 summarizes the evaluated subsets. We use normal-length discrete OpenToM, short-context discrete FANToM, English ToMBench, and the ToMi subset accompanying SimToM (Wilf et al., 2024). The collection consists of fixed devel-opment or previously used subsets, not newly col-lected questions. Appendix B.2 gives their scope. Source-specific adapters convert each question into a shared record containing source and ques-tion identifiers, a question family , context, question, candidate texts, and a reference answer. Families group related questions or scenario variants using source identifiers or shared contexts, so a BigToM family can span multiple story variants. We re-tain the source contexts without truncation and pre-serve task-specific candidate meanings. Candidate counts remain unequal, ranging from two to fifteen. Harmonization therefore standardizes the evalua-tion record while retaining the original decision task. We check that fields are nonempty, candidates are distinct, and references identify an offered an-swer. Of the initial 11,167 questions, we exclude 50 BigToM items for every model because their stories omit the subsequent event required by the reference. The resulting 11,117 questions and each question’s candidate order are frozen across pri-mary models. References are retained for scoring and withheld from model inputs. Source and family identifiers preserve the connection between stan-dardized records and related judgments. We then map native task, ability, or mental-state labels to the six capability groups. ToMBench ap-plication additionally follows the requested behav-ior prediction, selection, or judgment. All 730 ToMBench question targets underwent an agent-assisted post-evaluation review without item-level model predictions. This was not independent hu-man validation. A question may enter multiple capability groups but counts once in its benchmark total. Appendix B.1 provides the mapping rules.

4 Results and Analysis 

4.1 Where Are Typed Models Competitive? Jev is competitive across all six capabilities. 

Figure 2 ranks Jev first or second in every capa-bility, with its largest lead over the strongest gen-erative baseline in emotions: 84.8% against GPT-3.5’s 79.7%. Strong relative rankings still leave substantial errors in beliefs and mental-state appli-cation, Jev’s lowest-scoring capabilities. The other typed models remain below Jev but differ in their strengths. Open-Jev-2B leads this group in four capabilities, while Valen leads beliefs and OpenJev-0.6B social pragmatics. Tiny-Jev exceeds OpenJev-0.6B on emotions, reaching 55.2% against 43.5%, yet trails it on application. Application is the strongest capability for Open-Jev-2B, Valen, and OpenJev-0.6B, whereas Tiny-Jev favors emotions. Broad competitiveness is therefore a result for Jev, and the shared typed interface does not make the models interchangeable. 

Benchmark rankings can reverse. Table 2 shows why overall rank is insufficient for model selection. Removing native controls reverses Jev’s lead over GPT-3.5, leaving 69.1% against 70.9%. The comparison thus depends partly on which task demands enter the aggregate. Rankings also re-verse within a specific benchmark: Open-Jev-2B reaches 72.0% on ToMi against Jev’s 69.7%, de-spite trailing substantially overall. Appendices C.1 and C.2 provide capability breakdowns and control-free comparisons. 4.3 Intervention 1: How Do Supplied Reasons Affect Selection? Reason attachment can overturn an otherwise correct selection. We compare six conditions with questions, answer choices, and Jev settings fixed. The keyed reason supports the reference answer, and the foil reason supports a selected in-correct answer. Aligned reasons accompany their own answers, swapped reasons exchange these two attachments, and shared conditions repeat either reason across answers. No-reason and neutral-text controls complete the comparison. Jev selects only an answer on 249 historically correct and 1,554 historically incorrect questions, each in original and reversed orders. Figure 4 shows aligned rea-sons raising fresh accuracy on earlier-correct cases from 96.4% to 99.0%, while swapping lowers it to 25.3%. Neutral text preserves 96.8%, so extra text alone does not explain the loss. On earlier er-rors, aligned and shared keyed reasons reach 71.0% and 69.4% against a fresh 10.0% baseline. Ap-pendix E.2 details sampling and conditions. Re-covery can occur without option-specific support, yet misleading attachment can overturn a correct selection.

Candidate expansion affects both selection and output validity. To separate attachment from can-didate expansion, we test 108 questions, one per family, using an eight-pair baseline that repeats four answers and four reasons twice. Reattachment changes only pairs with neither reference compo-nent. Expansion instead includes all sixteen com-binations and changes repetition. Figure 7 shows reattachment helping both models, while expansion reduces accuracy, including a 13.9-point GPT-3.5 loss despite valid outputs. Jev’s strict accuracy falls from 81.0% to 76.4%. Post-hoc saved-choice scor-ing reads selections despite invalid probabilities without repairing them. Its smaller decline widens the strict-to-saved gap from 3.2 to 6.0 points, distin-guishing selection loss from additional validation failures. Appendix E.5 gives the protocol. Even when the correct pair remains available, expanding the candidates can impair both selection and output validity.

4.5 What Are the Cost–Time Tradeoffs? Jev improves the tradeoff against nano and mini. 

Figure 8 compares eight deployments on 11,117 questions, with seven priced using frozen Open-Router reference tariffs. At US$0.0295 per 1,000 questions, Jev costs 32.7% less than nano and 56.3% less than mini, with 11.7- and 6.7-point accuracy advantages. Lower cost alone does not favor Jev universally: DeepSeek offers US$0.0138 at 65.4% accuracy, giving Jev 7.6 more points at 2.14 times the cost. DeepSeek’s longer observed response time also shows that lower cost need not mean less waiting. Jev’s separate timing run records 73.0% accuracy and a 282 ms mean response time, the highest accu-racy and shortest mean in this cohort. Its mean is 83.9% below nano and 82.3% below mini. Open-Jev-2B follows at 464 ms with 56.6% accuracy and appears only in the time comparison because it lacks a matching tariff. Appendix G gives settings and full values. Observed timings reflect differing hardware, concurrency, and retry settings. Shorter decision calls could reduce waiting in assistants making repeated sequential judgments, but the over-all benefit depends on the cost and time of gener-ating candidates and composing responses, which this experiment does not measure. 

5 Conclusion 

Jev-ToM shows that Jev combines competitive bounded ToM selection with favorable reference-cost and observed-time comparisons in the eval-uated deployments. Benchmark-dependent con-fidence errors and sensitivity to supplied reasons show why accuracy alone is insufficient for adop-tion. The results prioritize task-specific confidence 8calibration, candidate design, and output validation before testing Jev within complete assistants. 

Limitations 

Language and task coverage. Our evaluation is limited to English, text-based questions with supplied candidates. The nine benchmarks cover several forms of ToM judgment, but their results do not establish generalization to other languages, cul-tural settings, or multimodal social situations. The six capability groups summarize the included tasks and are not an exhaustive measure of ToM. Pos-sible training exposure to the public benchmarks also cannot be excluded. 

Component-level evaluation. We evaluate Jev as a bounded decision component, without testing its integration into a complete interactive assistant. Candidate generation, partner-state updates across turns, and interactive task success are therefore outside our empirical claims. The confidence and reason analyses are exploratory, and the descriptive 0.90 cutoff is not a validated acceptance policy. The observed accuracy and resource advantages concern the evaluated tasks and deployments, so their value in a complete system depends on the surrounding components. 

Ethical Considerations 

This study uses existing benchmark situations, model outputs, and annotations by three postgrad-uate students at English-medium universities with prior experience annotating ToM benchmarks. An-notation procedures are provided in Appendix E.3. It does not infer sensitive attributes of real users. Social-decision components can nevertheless harm users when predictions about beliefs or intentions are treated as facts. Applications should distinguish observed information from inferred states and vali-date the consequences of their errors. Our results do not justify autonomous consequential decisions about individuals. Dataset and model redistribution remains subject to source terms, with the planned release described in Appendix I. To support reproducibility, we plan to release a versioned package containing evaluation materi-als, subset and family identifiers, candidate-order mappings, per-question predictions and available probability vectors, de-identified annotation-stage records, and executable scoring and family-level resampling code. The package will cover the rea-son interventions and matched-candidate analyses, with source attribution and redistribution governed by the original licenses. Appendix I describes the planned release. We used GPT-6.1-Sol to generate and separately review the reason materials. These supplied rea-sons are experimental candidate text, not expla-nations produced by the evaluated models. Con-struction and prompts are documented in Appen-dices E.1 and J, respectively. Language-model as-sistance also supported the question-target mapping audit, analysis-code preparation, and manuscript revision. The authors remain responsible for the methods, claims, and final publication.

Appendix A Extended Related Work 

A.1 ToM benchmarks and application 

ToM evaluations differ in the evidence available to a character and the reasoning required of a model. ToMi controls answer-space regularities that al-lowed earlier tasks to be solved through dataset biases (Le et al., 2019). BigToM uses causal tem-plates for belief and action questions, while Hi-ToM extends perspective tracking to recursively nested beliefs (Gandhi et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2023). Small alterations to otherwise similar tasks can also expose failures concealed by average success (Ull-man, 2023). Task structure and perspective depth therefore matter when interpreting a ToM score. 11 Broader benchmarks add psychological diversity and conversational information asymmetry. Open-ToM incorporates personality, preferences, inten-tions, and physical and psychological states (Xu et al., 2024). ToMBench organizes diverse social-cognitive tasks and abilities, while CogToM draws on paradigms inspired by human cognition (Chen et al., 2024, Tong et al., 2026). FANToM asks related questions about participants with unequal access to conversational information (Kim et al., 2023). ToMATO uses role-playing models’ private thoughts to construct questions about beliefs, inten-tions, desires, emotions, and knowledge (Shinoda et al., 2025). NegotiationToM studies beliefs, de-sires, and intentions in human negotiations (Chan et al., 2024). These constructions motivate report-ing benchmark-specific results alongside capability groups. SimpleToM distinguishes mental-state inference from behavior prediction and judgment, exposing a gap between explicit attribution and implicit appli-cation (Gu et al., 2026). SimToM instead filters a story to information available to the queried charac-ter before answering a mental-state question (Wilf et al., 2024). Our application category follows the requested judgment, and the feedback interven-tion separates the contribution of a related question from that of a predicted mental state. 

A.2 Jev evaluation and decision reliability 

TypeSafe introduces Jev as a model mapping un-structured state and typed questions to choices, scores, or probabilities (Almeida, 2026, TypeSafe AI, 2026). Across 37 datasets, Deußer et al. (2026) report strong results on many tasks but weaker per-formance for some label sets and judgment set-tings. The computational social science evaluation of Ibrahim and Zaki (2026) finds substantial gaps from the best LLM annotators on many tasks de-spite lower measured costs. These findings favor evaluation of the intended decision domain and label space. Confidence routing depends on both the task and the fallback. Li et al. (2026) test accepting con-fident Jev verdicts and escalating uncertain cases, with gains weakening under style-adversarial in-puts and reference-free prose. In rubric judging, Rao and Callison-Burch (2026) find shared errors between Jev and generative judges that limit accu-racy gains from deferral. The annotation study like-wise reports task-dependent confidence behavior (Ibrahim and Zaki, 2026). Our confidence analysis examines retained ToM answers without assuming that a fallback would correct them. Well-formed outputs can still reflect unintended request cues. JevAdvBench compares single-edit attacks with clean and repeated calls (Hu et al., 2026). Sun et al. (2026) show that option-name polarity can redirect selections under unchanged definitions. JevOut optimizes natural-looking con-textual additions that redirect initially correct de-cisions, including on ToMBench (Xu et al., 2026). Our predefined reason attachments probe repair and damage without adversarial context search. Proba-bility Contracts distinguishes valid reports, coher-ent probabilities, and downstream decisions (Chen and Li, 2026). An arithmetic rejection study further separates selecting an available answer from recog-nizing an answer-absent candidate set (Zhong et al., 2026). Together, this work motivates checking se-mantic selection, numerical validity, and candidate coverage separately. JEVal extends general decision-model evalua-tion to bilingual domains, agent workflows, and social simulation, finding that fast local decisions can coexist with lower long-horizon success and ag-gregate prediction errors (Duan et al., 2026). It also introduces InnerJev through reasoning-to-readout self-distillation. PACT studies contrastive evidence, answer-code permutations, and calibration objec-tives for single-token decisions (Lin, 2026). These methods illustrate different routes to a decision in-terface rather than a common architecture for our five typed models. 

A.3 Multilingual and multimodal reasoning 

XToM constructs matched ToMi, FANToM, and NegotiationToM instances in English, Chinese, German, French, and Japanese through transla-tion and human refinement (Chan et al., 2026). It distinguishes factual understanding from mental-state inference and finds that strong multilingual language understanding does not ensure compara-ble ToM performance across languages. A sepa-rate Jev cultural-values audit reports response and confidence changes across persona and language conditions (Asseri and Asseri, 2026). Both make language and social framing relevant evaluation dimensions beyond our English-only comparison. MultivationBench studies motivations inferred from accumulated visual and textual narratives (Chung et al., 2026). Maslow’s expanded hierar-chy of needs and Reiss’s basic desires support four multi-label tasks pairing theoretical definitions with 12 context-specific motivation options. This design distinguishes recognizing a psychological category from grounding it in behavior as a story unfolds. Its results identify difficulties in fine-grained mo-tivation selection and correct judgments across a narrative. It complements belief-centered evalua-tion through visual grounding, multi-label motiva-tions, and evolving evidence, which our fixed text questions do not measure. 

A.4 Mental-state reasoning in agents 

Explicit belief representations support cooperative planning (Li et al., 2023). MindDial tracks speaker beliefs, predicted listener beliefs, and their differ-ences for common-ground alignment and negoti-ation (Qiu et al., 2024). ToMA connects mental-state generation with dialogue lookahead and in-teractive goal achievement (Hwang et al., 2026). Mnemon assigns record-level judgments to Jev while an LLM plans retrieval and composes re-sponses (Wang, 2026). These systems motivate evaluating repeated selection as a component along-side the surrounding interaction, as discussed in Appendix H. 

B Evaluation Protocol 

Table 4 summarizes the model cohorts, questions, candidate constructions, and aggregation for each comparison. Variants reuse source questions, so six conditions in two orders produce twelve re-sponses per question. Primary, reason, feedback, and resource comparisons retain their own frozen populations. 

B.1 Capability mapping 

Native task, ability, and mental-state labels deter-mine the six capability assignments in Table 5. Ap-plication additionally requires predicting, select-ing, or judging behavior, including communica-tive appropriateness. Belief attribution, emotional reactions, factual recall, and utterance-truth judg-ments alone are excluded from application. The 152 ToMBench application assignments overlap other capabilities. All 730 ToMBench targets un-derwent an agent-assisted post-evaluation review without item-level model predictions, rather than independent human validation. A question counts once in its benchmark total. 

B.2 Datasets and harmonization 

The common primary set contains 11,117 questions. We use normal-length discrete OpenToM, short-context discrete FANToM, English ToMBench, and the ToMi subset accompanying SimToM (Wilf et al., 2024), with fixed development or previously used subsets. Source adapters preserve contexts, references, candidate meanings, identifiers, and question families. Contexts are not truncated, and candidate order is frozen across models. From 11,167 initial questions, we exclude 50 BigToM 

percept-to-belief true-belief items whose sto-ries omit the subsequent event required by the ref-erence. Table 6 reports question, family, option, and context-length statistics. 

B.3 Input, validation, and aggregation 

Typed models receive the context as state , the question as instructions , and indexed candidate texts, returning a choice and probabilities. Gener-ative models receive the same content and return a candidate label. Strict scoring counts failed or unparseable outputs as wrong. Jev probabilities must cover exactly the offered choices, be finite and lie in [0 , 1] , sum to one within 10 −4, and as-sign the selected choice a probability within 10 −6

of the maximum. Confidence selection uses the selected-answer probability. Exact instructions and an input–output example appear in Appendix J. Primary overall accuracy averages nine bench-mark scores equally, including native controls and unmapped questions. Capability scores average contributing benchmarks and exclude controls and unassigned items. In the eight-model reason com-parison, we average orders within questions, ques-tions within benchmarks, and benchmarks equally. The six-condition intervention instead pools ques-tions separately within each historical-correctness group. These weighting schemes remain distinct. 

B.4 Models and settings 

Table 7 records the twelve models and their primary settings. Models receive one source question per request without task-specific fine-tuning. The six-condition study uses Jev, the full pair study uses eight models, matched candidates use Jev and GPT-3.5, and feedback uses five typed models. Resource settings are documented separately in Appendix G. 

B.5 Paired uncertainty estimates 

Within each benchmark, bootstrap sampling resam-ples source-defined families with replacement, re-taining all questions, paired outcomes, and candi-date orders together. Each draw recomputes the original statistic. We use 5,000 draws for primary, 13 material, and feedback comparisons, 2,000 for the six-condition study, and 10,000 for matched can-didates. Percentiles 2.5 and 97.5 define pointwise 95% intervals. Comparators remain fixed across draws. The estimates describe family-sampling variation, without adjustment for multiple compar-isons or comparator selection, and do not measure repeated-inference or deployment variability. Com-plete estimates remain in the reproduction materi-als.

D Confidence-based Selection 

D.1 Threshold and counts 

The descriptive threshold of 0.90 applies to the probability assigned to the selected answer on all 11,117 Jev primary responses. Coverage divides retained answers by all questions in a benchmark. Retained error divides incorrect retained answers by retained answers. Table 11 gives the underlying counts for Figure 3, including 33 retained Hi-ToM answers with no observed errors. The threshold is not a validated deployment policy. 

D.2 Risk–coverage curves 

Figure 9 extends the comparison across all ob-served thresholds. Equal probabilities are retained together, and empty selections have undefined er-ror. OpenToM and SimpleToM have similar full-coverage error, 40.0% and 40.9%, but retaining about half their answers yields 23.3% error at 48.8% coverage and 35.3% at 48.9% coverage, re-spectively. Confidence therefore separates errors more effectively in the former comparison even though both retain substantial error. 

E Reason Interference and Candidate Selection 

E.1 Material construction 

For the eight-model pair study, 4,761 materials comprise 1,803 reused questions with reasons and newly prepared reasons for 2,958 other existing questions. Generation and review use benchmark references to construct supporting reasons for orig-inal answers, including plausible but mistaken rea-sons for distractors. Review checks story support, queried perspective, attachment, and overt refer-ence leakage. This selects experimental materials rather than establishing human correctness. Generation and separate review use GPT-6.1-Sol with JSON output. Newly prepared materials specify temperature 0, at most max(1600 , 200 K)

generation tokens for K options, and 1,600 review tokens. Only service failures permit repeat requests. The exact generation and review prompts appear in Appendix J.3. 

E.2 Six-condition answer selection 

Figure 4 uses the 1,803 retained reused materials, comprising 249 historically correct and 1,554 his-torically incorrect Jev answers. Material screening starts with 531 correct and 2,533 incorrect cases. Generation and initial screening retain 2,142 cases, and subsequent review retains 1,803. Historical cor-rectness defines the groups, while fresh no-reason calls provide their baselines. Each question retains its original answers un-der six conditions: no reasons, identical neutral filler, aligned reasons, swapped keyed/foil reasons, the keyed reason repeated on every answer, and the foil repeated on every answer. The foil sup-ports Jev’s earlier incorrect choice, or the first non-keyed option for earlier-correct questions. Swap-ping changes only the keyed and foil attachments. Original and reversed orders are pooled separately 14 within each historical group. Instructions and neu-tral text appear in Appendix J.2. E.3 Human annotation 

Three postgraduate coauthors at English-medium universities, with prior ToM annotation experi-ence, annotated in English as research collabora-tors without additional annotation payment. Writ-ten English–Chinese instructions and a label guide supported the procedure. Figure 10 preserves the complete interface excerpts. The initial sample contains 50 questions per benchmark, totaling 450 questions and 1,879 rea-sons, stratified by material origin and option count without model predictions. Each annotator initially assessed 270 questions. All three shared 90 ques-tions and 374 reasons, while 180 further questions had two annotations and 180 had one. A later answer-only stage collected 450, 450, and 270 judg-ments without adding reason labels. A follow-up covered all 249 earlier-correct intervention ques-tions with three human judgments each. Nineteen exact-material overlaps yield 680 unique questions across stages. Table 12 summarizes coverage and majority outcomes. Annotators first answered from the story, ques-tion, and ordered options, recording evidence before reasons were revealed. Later revisions were saved separately. References, model predic-tions, machine review, and material origin were hidden. Story-support labels were SUPPORTED ,

CONTRADICTED , NOT_ESTABLISHED , or UNCERTAIN .Attachment labels were YES , NO , or UNCERTAIN ,asking whether the reason, if true, supported its answer. Non-SUPPORTED /YES judgments required a note. Answer judgments also allowed MULTIPLE ,

NONE , or UNCERTAIN .For the initial two-annotator comparison on 374 shared reasons, story-support agreement was 85.56% (Cohen’s κ = 0.768 ) and attachment agreement 95.19% ( κ = 0 .833 ). The later answer-only stage had mean pairwise agreement of 82.47% on 270 questions shared by all three (Fleiss’ κ =0.751 ). On the 90 initially shared questions, all three endorsed the keyed reason and attachment on 75, while at least one endorsed an alternative answer–reason pair on 30. Agreement and endorse-ment do not establish correctness or complete adju-dication. The post-hoc follow-up analysis establishes an answer-category reference when at least two of three annotators choose the same single category. For the first two annotators it uses the revised an-swer after reason review, or the initial answer if unchanged. The third vote comes from the inde-pendent record saved before other judgments were shown. Non-single-category judgments remain out-side this subset. Of 249 questions, 219 have ma-jority references, including 211 unanimous cases, and all 219 match the benchmark categories. The remaining 30 stay in the annotation records. Table 12 retains both candidate orders and strict scoring, yielding 438 responses per condition on the majority subset. Aligned reasons raise accu-racy from 98.2% to 100.0%, while swapped rea-sons lower it to 24.9%. The paired changes are 

+1 .8 and −73 .3 points, with family-bootstrap in-tervals [0 .5, 3.6] and [−77 .6, −68 .9] . For original answers C = ( c1, . . . , c K ) and rea-sons E = ( e1, . . . , e K ), the pair study compares bare answers, aligned pairs (ci, e i), and all K2

crossed pairs. Each condition uses a fixed shuffled order and its reversal. Answer-component scoring checks the selected answer, while joint accuracy requires both keyed components and a valid output. Crossing changes length, repetition, and candidate count as well as attachment. Table 13 supplies absolute reused/new accura-cies for Figure 5, together with Jev’s paired repair and damage contributions. Repair changes a wrong no-reason outcome to a correct one, while dam-age changes a correct outcome to a wrong one. Both use all weighted outcomes, so the net change combines repair and signed damage, rather than conditioning on earlier errors or successes. On new materials, Jev’s aligned change is +3 .69 points 

[1 .93 , 5.51] , while its crossed change is −0.40 [−3.03 , 2.17] . Small observed changes remain de-scriptive, and an interval spanning zero does not establish equivalence.

H Potential of Jev Application 

The capability profiles and resource comparisons motivate testing Jev as a selector in dialogue and collaborative agents (Qiu et al., 2024, Li et al., 2023). An LLM could propose partner-state in-terpretations from dialogue history and generate responses while Jev selects among them (Yao et al., 2023). Emotions and social pragmatics are promis-ing starting points given Jev’s observed domain scores, while belief attribution and application leave more room for improvement. Candidates should distinguish the partner’s per-spective from the agent’s information and be re-vised as evidence changes. Evaluate candidate cov-erage separately from selection accuracy when a correct interpretation is present. Reason interfer-ence motivates checking attachment and candidate sensitivity, while task-specific calibration can guide acceptance and clarification (Ren et al., 2023). In-valid outputs need a fallback. These component checks support a proposed integration study, with dialogue quality and collaborative task success eval-uated in the complete system.

J.2 Reason-intervention instructions 

Answer-only selects an original answer. Joint selec-tion uses Answer: and Reason: fields, with gener-ative IDs C000 , C001 , etc. The six-condition study selects the answer itself, with added text follow-ing Suggested reason: . Neutral filler is identical across answers. 

Answer-only instruction 

Select the answer best supported by the original story and the question. 

Joint answer–reason instruction 

Select the single candidate whose answer AND attached reason are both best supported by the original story and the question. Evaluate the complete answer-and-reason pair: a correct answer with an incorrect reason, or a correct reason attached to an incorrect answer, is not a fully correct candidate. Reasons are claims to evaluate, not additional story facts. Select one of the supplied complete candidates. 

Six-condition answer-selection instruction 

Select the answer itself. Any suggested reason attached to an option is an untrusted explanation to evaluate, not an additional story fact. An incorrect suggested reason does not automatically make its answer incorrect. 

Neutral filler 

The proposed answer concerns the person and situation in the story. Read the context and question carefully before deciding which available answer to select here.
