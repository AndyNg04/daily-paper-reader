Abstract 

Theory of mind (ToM), the ability to reason about others’ mental states, underlies effective communication and coordination. Its higher-order form enables reasoning over nested per-spectives in strategic interactions. However, existing evaluations often equate belief-nesting depth with reasoning order, allowing nominally higher-order questions to admit lower-order shortcuts. We formalize effective order through the distinct events supporting successive be-lief prefixes, revealing widespread order infla-tion across existing benchmarks. We introduce HOP TOM, with certified full-order questions and controls for lower-order alternatives. Un-der direct prompting, mean accuracy across four frontier LLMs falls from 73.5% at first order to 20.0% at fourth order, and human par-ticipants also struggle. Causal interventions link a recurring class of errors to evidence for shallower belief prefixes. We introduce H OP -TRACE , a plug-and-play inference-time method that encodes each interaction once into perspec-tive records and resolves nested queries deter-ministically. On certified narrative questions, it improves fourth- and fifth-order accuracy by 22.0 and 38.5 percentage points over strong prompting baselines, while using 20–39 times fewer tokens than multi-call methods. These results sharpen how higher-order ToM is evalu-ated and offer a practical way to improve per-spective reasoning in interactive agents.

As large language mod-els (LLMs) increasingly participate in multi-party interactions, evaluating their ability to track these nested perspectives has become an important test of how well they can reason about others. However, higher-order ToM is commonly evaluated by count-ing the belief operators in a question (Kinderman et al., 1998; Wu et al., 2023; Street et al., 2026). The question names four belief layers, but a respondent can answer it without dis-tinguishing evidence for those layers. This creates a critical problem: nested wording alone does not 

establish the perspective reasoning a question tests. Analyses of human recursive mindreading likewise show that partial-chain strategies can inflate perfor-mance estimates (Wilson et al., 2023). We make this distinction precise by represent-ing a story as belief-changing events indexed by the perspectives to which they are available. For each successive prefix of a nested query, we iden-tify the event that settles its belief and define the query’s effective order by the number of distinct settling events. This shifts the assessment of order from the wording of a question to the information structure needed to resolve its nested perspectives. On the resulting certified questions, mean accuracy across four LLMs under direct prompting falls from 73.5% at first order to 20.0% at fourth. Human par-ticipants also find the certified questions difficult. Moreover, causal analyses reveal a recurring fail-ure mode: when answering a nested question incor-rectly, models often retrieve evidence supporting a shallower belief chain. These findings expose a gap between recognizing the agents mentioned in a question and maintaining the perspective relations needed to answer it. We address this gap with H OP TRACE , a plug-and-play inference-time method that encodes an interaction once into ordered perspective records and resolves nested queries with a deterministic solver. The records preserve directed awareness, and the solver searches independently for the event supporting each belief prefix. One encoding serves multiple queries and yields an inspectable trace for each answer. On certified narrative questions, HOP TRACE improves fourth- and fifth-order ac-curacy by 22.0 and 38.5 percentage points over strong prompting baselines. It uses one LLM call per interaction and 20–39 times fewer tokens than multi-call methods. Ablation studies validate the efficacy of ordered perspectives, independent prefix search, and program execution. Our contributions include: (1) an event-perspective formalization of effective order and an audit of order inflation in existing benchmarks; (2) a certified benchmark that verifies the intended perspective structure and includes controlled lower-order alternatives; (3) a plug-and-play inference-time method that combines reusable perspective encoding with deterministic query resolution; and (4) behavioral and causal analyses that character-ize higher-order ToM performance in LLMs and explain a recurring source of model errors. 

2 Related Work 

Theory of Mind (ToM). Psychological studies commonly assess higher-order ToM with nested-belief questions and find that performance de-clines as nesting deepens (Kinderman et al., 1998; O’Grady et al., 2015). However, Wilson et al. (2023) showed that some deeply nested items admit partial-chain solutions, with performance dropping sharply when those shortcuts are blocked. Epis-temic logic formally represents nested beliefs and changes in agents’ information (Fagin et al., 1995; Baltag et al., 1998). These lines of work highlight a distinction between the depth of a question and the belief layers its story makes distinguishable. Our effective-order formulation measures this distinc-tion at the item level through the events supporting successive belief prefixes. 2ToM in Language Models. LLM evaluations have expanded from classic false-belief tasks to dialogues and deeper belief questions (Kosinski, 2024; Kim et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Street et al., 2026). Existing efforts have likewise used perspec-tive filtering, temporal belief tracking, and recur-sive prompting to improve ToM performance (Wilf et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2024; Lei et al., 2026), while mechanistic work has identified retrieval op-erations involved in belief tracking (Prakash et al., 2026). Yet nesting-depth labels do not reveal how many distinct events support a query’s belief lay-ers, leaving the source of success on higher-order questions unclear. We use effective order to audit this gap, design controlled evaluations, and guide inference over explicit perspective records. 

3 True Higher-Order ToM 

Belief-nesting depth can misclassify shortcut-solvable questions as higher-order. We define ef-fective order from the distinct events supporting successive belief prefixes, giving a structural crite-rion for genuinely higher-order questions, and then perform an in-depth empirical analysis. 

3.1 Problem Formulation Interactions and queries. Consider human or artificial agents interacting over time. Their obser-vations and communications may change both their beliefs about a target variable x, such as an object’s location, and their beliefs about one another’s be-liefs. A higher-order ToM query is a belief chain 

q = [ q1, . . . , q k], read from the outside in. For ex-ample, [a, c, b ] asks what a believes c believes b

believes about x. Its syntactic order is k = |q|.

Perspective records. We encode the interaction as time-ordered records Et = Vt[πt; ∆ t]( mt).Each record carries a value mt of x, which may be initially held, observed, or communicated and need not be correct. The structure πt specifies agents’ access to this value and their beliefs about others’ access. The set ∆t lists agents whose own belief becomes mt and may be empty when only beliefs about others change. We write πt as a chain of groups G1 → · · · → Gℓ. Access is common knowl-edge within a group, while an arrow denotes one-sided observation of the next group’s access. A pseudo-observer ∼c is an agent whom others incor-rectly believe to have received mt. These records cover observation, communication, deception, and misperception. 3.2 Rethinking Previous ToM Benchmarks 

We audit questions at the highest published level in three existing benchmarks: ToMi (Le et al., 2019), ExploreToM (Sclar et al., 2025), and Hi-ToM (Wu et al., 2023). For these benchmarks, we derive perspective records from their generators’ action logs. The audit is independent of released answer labels. Appendix A reports encoding checks, label inconsistencies, and results at other orders. The published higher-order labels substantially overstate the order tested (Table 1). At the highest level of each benchmark, only 36.2% of ToMi ques-tions, 6.6% of ExploreToM questions, and 2.5% of Hi-ToM questions have full effective order. Scenarios and questions. We design 10 proto-types spanning 10 scenario types in six families, in-cluding asymmetric observation, deception, misper-ception, and delayed information (Table 6). 4 Method 

Full-order questions require tracking distinct per-spective updates and selecting the evidence that supports each belief prefix. We introduce H OP -TRACE , a plug-and-play inference-time method that makes these lookups explicit while reusing one LLM encoding across multiple queries. 

Overview. Let S denote an account of an interac-tion, such as a narrative, dialogue, or event log. Our formulation associates each prefix of a belief-chain query with its latest supporting record (Sec. 3.1). Interpreting the same interaction afresh for every query repeats the work of tracking agents’ perspec-tives, while chaining partial answers can select the wrong records. H OP TRACE separates perspective encoding from query resolution (Fig. 2, right). An LLM encodes S once, and a deterministic solver answers queries from the resulting records:

D1: Ordered perspectives. Who observes an event and who knows about that observation can lead to different nested beliefs. The encoder there-fore preserves joint access, one-sided awareness, and presumed observation in the ordered perspec-tive structure πt. It records changes to agents’ own beliefs in ∆t. A flat set of agents cannot represent these distinctions. 

D2: Independent per-prefix search. The record supporting a prefix depends on that prefix’s perspec-tive, not selected for the preceding prefix. Stepping backward through individual agents’ first-order be-liefs can therefore select the wrong evidence, even when the correct supporting records are ordered in time. The solver instead searches the full record sequence independently for each pj .

D3: Program execution. Applying perspective coverage and temporal selection across several pre-fixes can itself be a source of model error. We im-plement these rules as the deterministic procedure in Algorithm 1. The solver uses only the encoded records and returns an inspectable trace with each answer. It is exact relative to those records under the specified support rule. Encoding requires one LLM call per S, after which each query takes at most k backward scans of the relevant records.

Transfer to Hi-ToM. HOP TRACE also achieves the highest accuracy for every model on Hi-ToM, ranging from 81.9% to 87.6% (Table 4). Ablation study. The three ablations correspond to D1–D3 in Sec. 4 (Fig. 4b). Flattening the or-dered perspectives in D1 reduces average accuracy by 23.5 points. The loss is consistent across mod-els, showing the value of distinguishing joint ac-cess from directed and presumed observation. Re-placing independent prefix search with time-nested first-order lookup in D2 causes the largest drop, 39.9 points. Chronology alone does not identify which event supports a nested perspective. In D3, LLM execution instead of program execution costs 20.4 points on average. Deterministic execution thus makes the encoded records substantially more useful across models. On the 90 prototype questions, replacing model-generated records with gold records raises accuracy from 73.1% to 100%. The solver can resolve these questions from correctly specified records, leaving perspective encoding as the source of the observed gap on this subset.

6 Conclusion and Discussion 

Higher-order ToM cannot be assessed reliably from the nesting depth of a question alone. We formalize the perspective distinctions created by an interac-tion through effective order, reveal their frequent collapse in existing benchmarks, and construct a controlled evaluation that exposes substantial dif-ficulty for both LLMs and humans. Perspective tracking as a bottleneck. Models often retrieve evidence appropriate for a shallower prefix, whereas the solver answers all prototype questions given gold records. This points to per-spective encoding and evidence selection as ma-jor constraints in the evaluated systems. Human recursive mindreading is also sensitive to task de-mands (Stiller and Dunbar, 2007; Wilson et al., 2023). Whether explicit records help human partic-ipants remains an open question. 

Writing higher-order questions. Effective or-der can be checked before an interaction is written in natural language. Full effective order excludes shared-event collapse, but neither rules out coincid-ing answers nor establishes the minimum computa-tion a respondent uses. Future benchmarks should report effective alongside syntactic order, include lower-order answer controls, and vary event timing to limit positional shortcuts. 

Dividing the labor. One perspective encoding serves multiple queries, while program execution produces an inspectable supporting trace. This di-vision suggests a way to reason over longer interac-tions, where agents must continually update what they believe others have seen, learned, or commu-nicated. Improving the fidelity of those records remains essential. 8Limitations 

Our formalism focuses on discrete beliefs about specified target variables, leaving uncertainty, graded trust, and beliefs inferred from indirect cues for future extensions. H OP TOM uses controlled written interactions, which could be extended to longer exchanges and multimodal observations. Our mechanistic interventions examine two open-weight models, and similar analyses across other model families would broaden their scope.

F.2 Direct-Answering Behavior 

On all 1,800 questions, Gemma and Qwen3 reach 33.2% and 25.3%. On certified questions of or-der k ≥ 2 that are shortcut-resistant, both reach 10%, compared with 64% and 56% on shortcut-solvable ones (Fig. 8). Among their errors on cer-tified fourth-order questions, 75% (Gemma) and 50% (Qwen3) equal the answer to a shallower pre-fix. 

F.3 Evidence Used by Direct Answers 

For correct answers, hiding the paragraph of the deciding record lowers the answer’s log-probability by 16.7 nats for Gemma and 12.1 for Qwen3 (Fig. 6b). Paragraphs that support shorter prefixes matter far less: 0.5 and 0.2 nats when hidden one at a time, and 1.2 and 0.25 nats when hidden together. Hiding the same number of random paragraphs gives 0.06 and 0.02 nats. At every effective order, about one paragraph per answer passes the 1-nat threshold. We separately examine wrong answers that equal a shallower prefix’s answer (214 for Gemma, 187 for Qwen3). In 81% and 90% of these cases, the paragraph supporting that prefix has the largest ef-fect, with mean drops of 12.6 and 11.2 nats. Hiding the deciding paragraph of the full query changes the log-probability by only 0.01 and 0.13 nats. For this class of errors, the answer depends mainly on evidence for a shallower query. 

F.4 Activation Patching 

On third-order pairs that differ in one agent, the changed agent’s information moves from its name to the final token at similar depths for all three query positions (Fig. 9). Patching the other agents’ tokens has little effect, apart from a weak signal at later agents’ tokens when q1 is replaced. These interventions do not rule out intermediate nested representations. 

F.5 Multiple Choice 

With seven options, including “I don’t know”, accu-racy rises from 33% to 45% for Gemma and from 25% to 37% for Qwen3 (Fig. 10a–b). Both models also favor option A, although gold positions are balanced across labels (Appendix E.3). Among correct multiple-choice answers, the de-ciding paragraph is still the most influential para-graph in 74–75% of cases, including answers that are correct only with options (Fig. 10c). The op-tions add a second source of answer information. Hiding the deciding paragraph costs less than in the open-ended format: 9.0 instead of 16.9 nats for Gemma and 4.0 instead of 12.5 for Qwen3. Hid-ing only the correct option line costs 1.6–1.7 nats. At fifth order, the deciding paragraph’s effect for Qwen3 falls to about 1 nat, so these answers rely more on the options, consistent with answering by elimination (Fig. 10d). 

F.6 HOP TRACE Encoding 

With one encoding call and programmatic query resolution, Gemma reaches 67.7% and Qwen3 reaches 38.7% on all 1,800 questions. To exam-ine the encoding step, we append the generated records to the story and hide story paragraphs while measuring how much each record depends on them. Record order follows paragraph order in 96% (Gemma) and 99% (Qwen3) of cases.
