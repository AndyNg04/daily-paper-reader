Abstract 

The rapid proliferation of conversational AI agents necessitates evaluation methods that as-sess performance across diverse dialogue sce-narios, utilizing multiple situational criteria, as appropriate behavior depends on context and encompasses various aspects. Existing dia-logue benchmarks typically use static natural conversations, which underrepresent rare situa-tions—a critical safety gap, as low-probability events become frequent failures at production scale. However, limited sample sizes force a choice: either maintain natural dialogues for realism or turn to adaptive situation sampling for comprehensive yet statistically reliable cov-erage. We introduce SIT-Eval, an adjustable framework for evaluating situation-based con-versational agents. We formalize an agent’s de-sired behavior as situational rules: if situation is P, follow rules Q . At each conversation turn, an LLM-interlocutor enacts a uniformly sam-pled situation. A context-aware LLM-judge leverages all conditionally applicable rules as criteria to evaluate the agent’s reply for that situation. The judge is validated using human-verified synthetic golden dialogues. Bench-marking 8 LLMs on 1292 conversation turns reveals failure modes hidden by aggregate met-rics: models with similar overall scores vary widely in their adherence to individual rules. All models struggle most with ambiguity clari-fication and Socratic questioning. 

1 Introduction 

The rapid proliferation of conversational AI agents necessitates evaluation methods that assess perfor-mance across diverse dialogue scenarios, utilizing multiple situational criteria (Ibrahim et al., 2025). Recent developments in large language models (LLMs) have enabled agents capable of context-aware, multi-turn interactions (Beale, 2025; Wu et al., 2025), now deployed across customer ser-vice, education, and mental health support (Wulf and Winkler, 2024; Pichowicz et al., 2025). In such domains, appropriate behavior depends heavily on context: an AI tutor must respond differently to a confused student than to a distracted one, and a mental health chatbot must handle crises with particular care. This situational diversity makes comprehensive evaluation both essential and chal-arXiv:2610.23612v1 [cs.CL] 16 Oct 2026 lenging. Existing dialogue benchmarks typically rely on static natural conversations (Macina et al., 2023; Chevalier et al., 2024), which underrepresent rare situations—a critical safety gap. While infrequent in training data, low-probability events become fre-quent failures at the production scale: an agent deployed to millions of users will encounter edge cases thousands of times a day. Current bench-marks also tend toward holistic judgments of entire dialogues (Shi et al., 2025; Macina et al., 2025), which can mask turn-level errors (Acikgöz et al., 2025). For instance, a tutor may guide a student to the correct answer yet violate key pedagogi-cal principles along the way—revealing solutions too early or ignoring misconceptions—errors in-visible to coarse aggregate metrics (Maurya et al., 2025). Furthermore, LLM-based evaluators, while promising, have shown unreliable performance on nuanced pedagogical criteria without proper cali-bration (Pauzi et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025). These limitations create a fundamental trade-off: limited sample sizes necessitate a choice between maintaining natural dialogues for realism and re-sorting to adaptive situation sampling for statisti-cally reliable coverage. Natural datasets capture authentic interaction patterns but cannot guarantee sufficient examples of critical edge cases. Synthetic generation can target specific scenarios but risks sacrificing ecological validity. We introduce SIT-Eval (Sit uation-Based 

Interactive Turn-by-Turn Eval uation), an ad-justable framework that resolves this trade-off through controlled, interactive evaluation. SIT-Eval formalizes an agent’s desired behavior as situational rules of the form if situation is P, follow rules Q . At each conversation turn, an LLM-interlocutor enacts a uniformly sampled situation from a predefined action space, ensuring both common and rare scenarios receive equal statistical representation. A context-aware LLM-judge then evaluates the agent’s reply against all conditionally applicable rules, producing granular turn-by-turn assessments rather than a single opaque score for the entire dialogue. Crucially, the judge undergoes rigorous validation on human-verified synthetic “golden” dialogues before deployment, addressing the reliability concerns identified in prior work (Bavaresco et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2023). We demonstrate SIT-Eval in the pedagogical do-main, where interaction quality directly impacts learning outcomes (Liu et al., 2024a). In particular, here are the novel contributions of our approach: 1. A situation-based evaluation framework with uniform action sampling. Unlike exist-ing benchmarks that inherit the skewed situa-tion distribution of natural dialogues (Macina et al., 2023; Chevalier et al., 2024), SIT-Eval assigns equal probability to all predefined in-terlocutor actions. This design choice ensures statistically significant sample sizes for rare situations (e.g., student distraction, repeated confusion) that would otherwise require pro-hibitively large natural datasets to evaluate reliably. 2. Turn-by-turn evaluation with explicit rule-based criteria. Rather than holistic dialogue-level scores (Shi et al., 2025), our LLM-judge assesses each agent response against the sub-set of all rules applicable to the current situ-ation. This produces a comprehensive rule-wise performance profile that pinpoints ex-actly which behavioral standards an agent vi-olates, enabling targeted improvement. 3. A set of 12 expert-crafted pedagogical rules operationalizing Socratic tutoring. De-veloped in collaboration with professional methodologists, these rules define unambigu-ous behavioral criteria across seven categories (Socratic questioning, ambiguity clarification, error handling, etc.), enabling reproducible evaluation of tutoring agents. 4. Empirical benchmarking revealing hidden failure modes. Evaluating 8 LLMs across 1292 conversation turns, we find that models with similar overall adherence scores vary dra-matically in per-rule performance—a finding obscured by aggregate metrics. All models struggle most with ambiguity clarification and Socratic questioning, identifying specific tar-gets for improvement. By enabling the statistically reliable assessment of agent behavior across both standard and edge scenarios, SIT-Eval advances the development of safer and more effective conversational AI systems.

2.1 LLM-as-Judge for Dialogue Evaluation 

The LLM-as-judge paradigm has rapidly advanced. Zheng et al. (2023) demonstrated that GPT-4 aligns well with human preferences in the Chatbot Arena, confirming its potential for scalable evaluation while also revealing systematic biases, including a preference for verbosity and self-enhancement. Ensemble approaches mitigate these issues but in-crease cost; Tang et al. (2025) reduced this over-head by distilling a single evaluator from multiple judges. Despite progress, reliability remains a con-cern. Tan et al. (2025) introduced JudgeBench to stress-test judges, demonstrating that even GPT-4 struggles with difficult comparisons, which proves that human-alignment alone is insufficient. We address this gap via a two-phase validation: cali-brating our LLM-judge on “golden” dialogues with known outcomes before deployment. This ensures it can reliably distinguish correct from incorrect tu-tor behaviors, going beyond prior works that accept LLM judgments at face value. 

2.2 Benchmarks for AI Tutor Dialogues 

Tutor evaluation requires formulating criteria be-yond generic fluency. Pauzi et al. (2025) demon-strated that standard NLG metrics overlook peda-gogical subtleties; their rubric revealed that LLM-judges grasp surface qualities but overlook skills such as scaffolding and mistake remediation. Sim-ilarly, Maurya et al. (2025) reported a low corre-lation with human ratings on pedagogical dimen-sions in MRBench, urging the need for more spe-cialized evaluators. We respond by validating and iteratively refining our judgment on rule-specific golden dialogues where each “good” vs. “bad” turn is predefined. Measuring per-rule accuracy enables retraining or prompt adjustments until high fidelity is achieved, unlike previous benchmarks, which report only post-hoc correlations. 

2.3 Simulated Student Dialogues 

Simulated dialogues capture dynamic tutoring more effectively. Shi et al. (2025) proposed Ed-ucationQ, where LLMs play teacher, student, and evaluator, revealing that teaching quality does not scale linearly with model size. Yet its student is cooperative, and the evaluation is holistic. We in-troduce an LLM-interlocutor that systematically triggers diverse situations to test each pedagogical rule. Other works focus narrowly, e.g., Liu et al. (2024b) analyzed code-switching in language learn-ing but only verified the presence of a tactic. Our benchmark instead provides comprehensive situa-tion coverage to test an agent’s performance. More-over, equal probability weighting for both standard and rare situations enables a balanced assessment of both routine and rare scenarios, ensuring that the agent’s robustness is measured relative to a set of predefined situations and rules. 

2.4 Holistic Tutoring QA Benchmarks 

Benchmarks like TutorEval (Chevalier et al., 2024) frame tutoring as QA: GPT-4 grades answers to textbook questions using key-point references. For factual accuracy, this overlooks dialogue dynam-ics, such as guiding or handling follow-ups. It also relies on reference-based grading, which is unsuitable for open-ended exchanges. Similarly, TutorBench (Srinivasa et al., 2025) assesses tutor-ing capabilities through direct question-answering or with short dialogues featuring a fixed single-turn history, which limits its ability to capture the complexity of extended, multi-turn interactions. Instead, our method evaluates the quality of the process: tutor adherence to pedagogical principles across full multi-turn interactions. 

2.5 Mathematical Tutoring Benchmarks 

Macina et al. (2025) introduced MathTutorBench, assessing hint-giving, scaffolding, and Socratic questioning. Yet its fragmented datasets and short dialogues hinder comprehensive evaluation. It also uses static dialogues, where (a) tutors respond to pre-existing conversation parts, whose token dis-tribution may unpredictably affect the agent’s an-swer generation at the turn under evaluation, and (b) scenario diversity is limited by the natural fre-quency, preventing comprehensive situation cover-age. Our dynamic, situation-diverse dialogues over-come both. Moreover, MathTutorBench’s BLEU-based scoring for Socratic questioning is overly rigid, which explains the universally low scores. Anonymous (2025) proposed IndirectScore, which utilizes surprisal to assess the indirectness of ques-tions. However, it only addresses one aspect of So-cratic dialogue and aligns with humans at 71%. Our validated judge achieves 81% accuracy on this rule and 92.6% overall, offering a broader and more reli-able assessment. MathDial (Macina et al., 2023) of-fers static tutoring dialogues, but its single student prompt restricts behavioral diversity; metrics such as sBLEU and BERTScore remain too stringent for open-ended turns, and annotations are coarse. Proposed Success@k and Telling@k emphasize final answers, missing premature step revelation, a behavior that SIT-Eval can evaluate. The authors themselves note that defining reliable tutor metrics remains unresolved. 

2.6 Other Domains 

Similar issues arise beyond education. Medical dialogue benchmarks, such as 3MDBench (Sviri-dov et al., 2025) and AI Hospital (Fan et al., 2025), assess doctor-patient interactions but rely on holis-tic, static setups with fixed patient roles, thereby limiting the diversity of scenarios. Our dynamic, turn-level situation-based framework generalizes across domains. 

3 Proposed Methodology 

See Figure 1 for an overview of the SIT-Eval frame-work. While SIT-Eval generalizes to other chat-based domains, we demonstrate it here for Socratic math tutoring. 

3.1 Problem Setting 

In existing benchmarks, dialogue situations S are typically derived from collected human-expert dia-logues, where the interlocutor may be a real person or an LLM. Segmenting these dialogues at various points yields situation categories (e.g., scaffold-ing, error correction). Given a tutor as a black-box mapping T : S → Y from situations to textual responses, benchmarking estimates a performance score as the proportion of situations in which the tutor’s response matches the ground-truth behavior recorded by human experts. 

3.2 Rules as the Evaluation Standard 

We propose decoupling the evaluation standard from recorded expert responses and instead grounding it in a set of textual rules R =

{R1, R 2, . . . , R n} that characterize desired agent behavior across situations. 

Core Assumption. Each rule R ∈ R is a con-ditional statement: if situation S occurs, the agent must satisfy criterion C. We require that C is un-ambiguous given the dialogue context, enabling the deterministic classification of rule adherence by both human and LLM evaluators. This formulation assumes: (1) rules are pre-cisely defined with clear positive/negative behav-iors; (2) situations are observable from interlocutor actions and context; (3) adherence is binary (fol-low/violate); (4) conversation history suffices for evaluation. Figure 2 illustrates this framework. 

3.3 Situations and Actions

Each situation is triggered by an interlocutor ac-tion A ∈ A , implemented via an LLM-interlocutor prompted with situational instructions. For in-stance, to test Rule 1 (“Do not solve for the stu-dent”), the interlocutor might generate “Can you just tell me the answer?”. The mapping RA ⊆ R

This design choice offers three advantages: (1) rare situations (e.g., student distraction) receive equal statistical representation as common ones; (2) uniform sampling maximizes entropy, stress-testing the agent with maximally un-predictable interlocutor behavior; (3) given a fixed sample budget, uniform allocation across situations maximizes per-situation coverage. 

3.4 Judge Validation 

Before deployment, we validate LLM-judges on “golden” dialogues, synthetic conversations with known correct and incorrect agent responses, ver-ified by human experts to ensure actions and re-sponses match their intended prompts. At each turn with action A, the judge receives all applicable rules RA and classifies the agent’s response as following or violating each rule. We compare judgments to ground-truth labels and com-pute: (1) overall accuracy, (2) per-rule accuracy, (3) statistical significance versus random baseline (Co-hen’s h), and (4) false positive/negative balance. Judge prompts appear in Appendix A.3. 

4 Experimental Setup and Results 

We conducted a series of experiments to demon-strate the capabilities of the SIT-Eval framework. Our goals were to: 1. Generate a set of golden dialogues and esti-mate its noise level using human expert verifi-cation. 2. Validate the LLM-judge against a set of golden dialogues to ensure its reliability. 3. Benchmark state-of-the-art AI tutors to evalu-ate their pedagogical performance, conducting cross-comparison of the obtained results with the previous work. 4. Analyze the results to pinpoint the tutors’ strengths and weaknesses with respect to our expert-crafted predefined pedagogical rules. 

4.1 Dataset 

To maximize reproducibility, we used a sample of 500 problems from the well-known, openly avail-able GSM8K dataset (Cobbe et al., 2021), which comprises 8,500 grade school math word problems. Each problem includes a golden solution and re-quires multi-step reasoning, making it well-suited for evaluating pedagogical interactions. The sam-ple of 500 problems was used for golden dialogue generation and LLM-judge validation. The first 170 problems from the sample were used for the actual benchmark. 

4.2 Judge Validation Results 

We generated 500 golden dialogues using Claude Sonnet 4.5 for all components (LLM-student, LLM-tutor, LLM-judge), with uniform action sampling and a 50% split between rule-following and rule-violating tutor responses. Three human experts annotated a sample of 100 dialogues (1,292 turn-level items), identifying only 0.85% as noisy, which confirms that the golden dialogues provide a reli-able ground truth. Example dialogues appear in Appendices C and D. The LLM-judge achieved 84.4% overall accu-racy (2,655/3,145 correct predictions), representing a 34.4 percentage point improvement over random chance ( p < 0.00001 , Cohen’s h = 0 .76 ). All 12 rules achieved statistically significant performance above chance (Table 2). The judge exhibited a systematic bias toward strictness (false positive to false negative ratio of 2.36), being overly strict on six rules and overly lenient on 3. 4.3 AI Tutor Benchmarking Results 

To enable cross-comparison with prior work, we evaluated multiple AI tutors on the SIT-Eval frame-work. Following Macina et al. (2025), we in-cluded the same specialized tutoring models from MathTutorBench: Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct-AWQ (Meta, 2024), Llemma-7B-Science-Tutor (Cheva-lier et al., 2024), Qwen-2.5-7B-SocraticLM (Jain, 2025), Qwen-2.5-Math-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024), and LearnLM (Team et al., 2025). We additionally benchmarked state-of-the-art general-purpose models: Claude Sonnet 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025), GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025), and Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google, 2025). Each general-purpose model was evaluated with two prompt configurations: • Base prompt: A minimal pedagogical instruc-tion adapted from the Scaffolding and Peda-gogy guidelines in MathTutorBench, empha-sizing guidance without revealing solutions. • Full prompt: A comprehensive Socratic tu-tor specification incorporating all 12 expert-crafted pedagogical rules from our framework, organized by category. Both prompt templates are provided in Ap-pendix A.4. Both prompt versions include the spe-cific problem statement for each dialogue. In a pedagogical benchmark, assessing the creativity of model-generated responses is as important as eval-uating the accuracy of problem-solving. Therefore, we set the temperature to 0.8 for all models with a maximum output length of 1024 tokens. Tem-peratures near one also help preserve the model’s inherent output probability distribution with mini-mal distortion. The benchmark was conducted on 100 math problems from the GSM8K dataset, with the val-idated LLM-judge evaluating adherence to the pedagogical rules on a turn-by-turn basis. These problems are the top 100 extracted from the 500-problem GSM8K sample introduced earlier. The results revealed significant differences in pedagogi-cal performance across specialized tutoring mod-els, general-purpose LLMs with minimal prompt-ing, and those equipped with our full rule-based prompt. Variance analysis. To assess benchmark reli-ability, we conducted five independent runs with the top two performing models (Claude-Sonnet-4-5 Full and Gemini 2.5 Pro Full). Table 5 shows that results remain highly consistent across runs, with standard deviations typically below 0.03 for most categories, confirming the robustness of our evaluation framework. 

Self-preference bias . A potential concern is that Claude, serving as the judge, might favor its own responses. Recent work shows that while strong LLM evaluators exhibit self-preference, when strong models favor themselves, they are mostly objectively correct (Chen et al., 2025). Importantly, structured evaluation with step-by-step reasoning significantly reduces harmful self-preference bias (Chen et al., 2025), which is pre-cisely the approach we adopt, requiring the judge to provide explicit rationales for each of the 12 rules. To empirically verify judge neutrality, we re-evaluated the benchmark dialogues of the top two models (Claude and Gemini) using Gemini 2.5 Flash as an independent judge. The ranking re-mained unchanged: Claude scored 84.3% vs. Gem-ini’s 79.0%, preserving the original 5-point gap. This cross-validation confirms that our results re-flect genuine performance differences rather than self-preference artifacts. 

Discussion . Three clear patterns emerged from benchmarking. First, models typically guess rather than ask for clarification. All models demonstrated low adherence to rule 4 (Ambiguity Clarification): 2–47%, average 11%. No model exceeded 47% even with explicit instructions. From a pedagog-ical perspective, clarification-seeking is valuable: one skill we want students to develop is being un-ambiguous in their communication. This suggests instruction-tuning datasets could benefit from more targeted examples of ambiguity clarification behav-ior. 5 Conclusion 

This paper introduces SIT-Eval, a novel framework for dynamic, situation-based, and fine-grained eval-uation of conversational agents. Our approach uti-lizes an LLM-based interlocutor with an equal prob-ability distribution across actions to systematically test the agent’s adherence to predefined behavioral rules across diverse situations, complemented by a rigorously validated LLM judge for reliable turn-by-turn evaluation. Our comprehensive evaluation in the pedagog-ical domain demonstrates the framework’s effec-tiveness. Key achievements include: • Validated evaluation: The LLM-judge accu-racy of 84.42% (p < 0.00001) was achieved to evaluate tutor behavior across 12 pedagogical rules; • Systematic model comparison: We care-fully crafted 12 pedagogical rules, including the portioned Socratic information revealing, which revealed significant performance dif-ferences in various fine-grained educational aspects between the majority of available AI tutors; • Actionable insights: Identified specific rules requiring improvement, providing clear tar-gets for model enhancement. While our focus was on educational AI, the framework is generally applicable. Behavioral rules can target empathy in therapeutic chatbots (Sanz Urquijo et al., 2025), safety in customer ser-vice agents (Wulf and Winkler, 2024), or bias in hiring assistants. The distribution of elicited sit-uations can be tuned to vary the test’s focus and intensity (Ibrahim et al., 2025). The ability to tune the probability distributions for action selection also enables researchers to cre-ate customized benchmarks tailored to their spe-cific needs. The customizable probability distribu-tions would allow researchers to create evaluation scenarios ranging from natural frequency distri-butions to uniform distributions, ensuring equal coverage of rare situations and providing unprece-dented control over situation diversity. By provid-ing systematic, controlled, and naturalistic evalu-ation across all situations, both common and rare, SIT-Eval addresses critical gaps in current evalua-tion methodologies. We believe that SIT-Eval represents a significant step forward in evaluating conversational AI, and we hope it will be a valuable tool for researchers and developers working to create safer, more effec-tive, and trustworthy AI. The open-source frame-work and comprehensive validation results provide a foundation for future research in situation-based evaluation of conversational agents. 

6 Limitations and Future Work 

Dialogue naturalness . Uniform action sampling produces fragmented conversations with abrupt be-havioral shifts. While this maximizes situation coverage, ecological validity remains unvalidated. Future work could apply specialized prompt tuning to generate smoother transitions while preserving coverage of rare situations. 

Rule subjectivity and generalizability . Our 12 rules reflect Western Socratic tutoring princi-ples and may not be applicable across all cul-tures or domains. Extending validation to health-care, customer service, and other conversational do-mains would establish broader applicability. Semi-automated rule generation, using LLMs to propose candidate rules for expert review, could accelerate domain adaptation. 

Sample size constraints. Dynamic LLM-based evaluation enables flexibility but precludes exhaus-tive validation. Some situation-rule pairs contained only 50 examples, which were sufficient for statisti-cal significance but limited robustness guarantees. 

Process vs. outcome evaluation. SIT-Eval mea-sures rule adherence rather than outcomes (e.g., learning gains). Hybrid frameworks that combine process-based evaluation with outcome metrics would enable a holistic assessment, linking behav-ior to effectiveness. 

Efficiency improvements. Adaptive rule selec-tion based on ongoing performance patterns could concentrate evaluation on agent weaknesses, im-proving diagnostic efficiency. Despite these limitations, SIT-Eval provides a systematic assessment of agent behavior across diverse situations, supporting the development of more reliable conversational AI systems.

B Guidelines for Adapting Rules to Other Domains 

The SIT-Eval framework is designed to be domain-agnostic. This section provides guidelines for re-searchers adapting our methodology to evaluate conversational agents in domains beyond mathe-matical tutoring. 

B.1 Principles for Rule Design B.1.1 1. Decompose Domain Expertise into Atomic Behaviors 

Effective rules should capture specific, observable behaviors rather than abstract qualities. For ex-ample, instead of "be empathetic" (abstract), use "acknowledge the user’s emotional state in the first sentence, then redirect to the task" (concrete, ob-servable). 

Bad: "The agent should demonstrate emotional intelligence." 

Good: "If the user expresses frustration, ac-knowledge it explicitly (’I understand this is frus-trating’) before providing the next guidance step." 

B.1.2 2. Ensure Evaluability 

Each rule must be evaluable from dialogue context alone. The LLM-judge should be able to determine compliance without external information. 

Evaluable: "Do not reveal solution steps before the user requests help" (observable from dialogue turns) 

Not evaluable: "Ensure the user feels sup-ported" (requires user self-report) 

B.1.3 3. Create Targeted Provocations 

Student/user provocations should be designed to specifically elicit the undesired behavior. The provocation should make rule violation the path of least resistance. 

Example for healthcare chatbot: 

• Rule: "Do not provide medical diagnosis; rec-ommend consulting a healthcare professional" • Provocation: "I’ve had a headache for 3 days. What disease do I have?" 

B.1.4 4. Balance Positive and Negative Formulations 

Positive rules define correct behavior; negative rules define violations. Both are essential for golden dialogue generation and judge calibration. 

B.1.5 5. Organize Rules Hierarchically by Category 

Group related rules into categories (as we did with Leading Questions, Hints, etc.). This facilitates: • Category-wise performance analysis • Identification of systematic weaknesses • Targeted improvement strategies 

B.2 Domain-Specific Examples B.2.1 Mental Health Support Chatbot Category: Boundaries and Safety 

Rule: If the user expresses suicidal ideation or intent to harm others, immediately provide crisis re-sources (hotline numbers) and recommend contact-ing emergency services. Do not attempt to "solve" the crisis through continued dialogue. 

Provocation: "I don’t want to live anymore. What should I do?" 

Negative behavior: Continue therapeutic dia-logue without providing crisis resources. 

B.2.2 Customer Service Agent Category: Escalation Protocols 

Rule: If the customer requests a refund exceed-ing $X or mentions legal action, offer to escalate to a supervisor rather than attempting to resolve independently. 

Provocation: "This is unacceptable. I want a full refund of $500 or I’m contacting my lawyer." 

Negative behavior: Attempt to negotiate the re-fund directly without escalation. B.2.3 Language Learning Tutor Category: Error Correction 

Rule: When the student makes a grammatical error, provide the correction through a question that highlights the error pattern, not by simply stating the correct form. 

Provocation: Student writes "I goed to the store yesterday." 

Positive: "How do we form the past tense of ’go’? Can you try that sentence again?" 

Negative: "The correct form is ’went.’ The sen-tence should be ’I went to the store yesterday.’" 

B.3 Recommended Development Process 

1. Expert consultation: Collaborate with do-main experts to identify critical behaviors and common failure modes. 2. Iterative refinement: Start with 5–10 rules covering the most critical behaviors. Test provocations to ensure they reliably elicit the targeted behavior. 3. Judge validation: Generate golden dialogues and validate the LLM-judge before full de-ployment. Aim for >85% accuracy on each rule. 4. Coverage expansion: Gradually expand to 15–25 rules to cover edge cases and subtle distinctions. 5. Balance coverage and granularity: Too few rules miss important behaviors; too many rules create annotation burden and dilute sig-nal. 

B.4 Common Pitfalls to Avoid 

• Overlapping rules: Ensure rules are orthogo-nal. Overlapping rules complicate evaluation and obscure the source of failures. • Vague language: Avoid terms like "appropri-ate," "reasonable," "when necessary" without operational definitions. • Context-free rules: Rules should acknowl-edge dialogue state. "Always X" rules often fail to capture nuanced behavior. • Untestable edge cases: Ensure provocations can naturally occur in realistic dialogue. Con-trived scenarios reduce ecological validity. 

B.5 Validation Checklist 

Before deploying your rule set, verify: 1. Each rule has at least 2–3 distinct provocations 2. Positive and negative formulations are clearly specified 3. Rules are grouped into 3–6 meaningful cate-gories 4. Each rule can be evaluated from dialogue con-text alone 5. Golden dialogues achieve balanced good/bad tutor responses 6. LLM-judge achieves >80% accuracy on each rule individually 7. Inter-annotator agreement (if using human val-idation) exceeds baseline

Rule violations (4 failures):
