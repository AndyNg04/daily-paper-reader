(Dated: October 12, 2026) The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in physics research is increasingly common, yet few guide-lines exist for its effective use. Starting with a principles-based approach, we outline a set of ten requirements for collaborative AI agents in experimental physics, spanning scientific norms, effec-tive agent use, and domain expertise. We take a human-centered approach in which agents act as collaborators that amplify rather than replace the expertise of a human researcher (user) who directs the work and is responsible for its results. We then present a framework designed to enforce these requirements, detailing methods for improving off-the-shelf behavior of large language models (LLMs) with custom scaffolding and describing general-purpose scientific workflows and skills. In particular, we highlight the power of closed-loop campaigns where agents work iteratively toward a well-defined goal. We demonstrate the potential of this mode of research through six case studies in experimental physics, covering device design, hardware control, neural network optimization, phys-ical model identification, and data analysis. All six case studies result in useful work products that add utility to our research, including a neural network architecture and solid-state quantum defect photodynamic model that substantially outperform our published work; a microwave waveguide de-sign that extends a published design concept to a fabrication-ready device meeting our experimental constraints; a quantum control pulse sequence candidate that improves simulated performance by more than an order of magnitude; commissioning of an NMR spectrometer to ∼1 Hz frequency pre-cision on live hardware; and finally a data-fitting pipeline that achieves, to our knowledge, the first reconstruction of the Ising microstate of an artificial spin-ice array from diffraction-limited magnetic imaging. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) underpin a growing set of powerful, general-purpose artificial intelligence (AI) tools for coding and problem-solving [1]. Equipping mod-els with structured instructions, tools, and file access enhances their capabilities and allows semi-autonomous work [2]; these arrangements are directly realized in pop-ular harnesses such as Claude Code [3] and Codex [4]. Custom tools and workflows can enhance performance further still. Such LLM-based agents 1 have been em-ployed in a wide variety of applications across physics and engineering, including symbolic reasoning and physical-model identification [5], analysis of real experimental data [6, 7], construction and automation of simulation workflows [8–10], design of devices and instruments [11– 13], experimental protocol creation [14–16], and hands-on operation and tuning of real apparatus [17–22]. A partic-ularly powerful mode is the closed-loop agentic workflow, in which a model proposes candidate solutions, scores them with a frozen evaluator, reviews results, and plans the next iteration [23–27]. However, fully realizing the promise of agentic re-search in experimental physics presents several chal-lenges. First, models of physical systems often involve some degree of approximation whose correctness must be determined through empirical measurements and sci-entific judgment, unlike in fields such as pure mathemat-ics and software development, where a formalized proof checker or test suite can quickly evaluate a proposed so-lution. Research problems in physics often involve com-plex sets of physical, experimental, and logistical con-straints that require human judgment rather than the judgment of an agent acting alone. Experimental data add a unique challenge, as statistical noise, systematic effects and bias, and apparatus-specific information not available to the agent can cause errors and complicate the analysis. Second, even if a scientific result is correct, a useful research product requires careful tracking of the entire data collection and analysis pipeline, in contrast to other fields where a final product such as a proof or an algorithm may stand apart from the process that pro-duced it. Appropriate verification and disclosure require well-documented code and methodology from all project phases, clearly delineated assumptions and sourced val-ues, and an organized supporting file structure that a human can navigate and review. These key elements are not consistently preserved without specific guidance arXiv:2610.17193v1 [physics.comp-ph] 12 Oct 2026 2and structure to the agent. Finally, agentic research in physics is vulnerable to common failure modes of LLMs, including hallucinated numbers [40], metric gaming [41– 43], premature commitment [5, 44], spatial reasoning er-rors [8, 9], and promotion of unphysical candidate models [45]. As agentic research transitions from an object of study to a research tool, it is important to create replica-ble frameworks that address these issues and stream-line useful work in experimental physics. Previous ef-forts in agentic research have demonstrated impressive proofs of concept [14, 16, 18], but the prompts or frame-works employed are often highly specialized for a single class of problems or subfield, and offer few generalizable lessons. At the other extreme, some AI research has fo-cused on autonomous research agents meant to handle the entire paper creation process from idea generation to manuscript preparation, with minimal human involve-ment [46–48], rather than on collaborative tools that sup-port existing research efforts [49, 50]; see Ref. [51] for a review. Such “fully-AI” systems often exhibit high self-reported error rates and lack domain-specific skills, fail-ing to address the particular challenges of experimental physics [40, 48]. From a practical perspective, scientific infrastructure is built around human accountability and verification, and most journals and preprint servers only accept human authorship [52–55]. Thus, real-world AI-assisted scientific work requires human researchers to de-duce physical principles from AI outputs, test their va-lidity and the range over which they hold, and connect them to independent experimental and computational evidence, resulting in a scientific finding that a human author can publicly endorse. Inspired by these challenges and our extensive explo-ration of customized, closed-loop workflows, this paper serves as a practical tutorial on conducting collaborative, LLM-driven agentic research in experimental physics (fundamental and applied). Instead of focusing on a cus-tom framework aimed at a single problem, we begin by formulating ten behavioral principles that we want a col-laborative AI agent to follow in physics research, span-ning scientific norms, effective agent use, and domain-specific knowledge (Sec. II). We then detail methods to implement these principles, including system prompts, skills, workflows, templates, and programmatic gates and structures for interactive and closed-loop work (Sec. III) with the goal of providing a guide that can be applied to a wide range of problems; these methods are instan-tiated in our research framework, PhysKit [56], which we present as a concrete example. Finally, we present six case studies showcasing the capacity of structured agentic physics research across simulation, hardware-in-the-loop control, device design, symbolic reasoning, and neural network optimization (Sec. IV). Among the re-sults discussed below, a collaborative AI agent identi-fied a photodynamic model of a spin defect in hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) that explains experimental data bet-ter than published models; discovered candidate quan-tum control sequences that improve simulated perfor-mance by more than an order of magnitude relative to published sequences; commissioned a nitrogen-vacancy (NV) diamond nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spec-trometer from an uncalibrated state, reaching ∼1 Hz 1HNMR frequency precision; and reconstructed the per-island magnetization reversal of a 2,178-island artificial spin-ice (ASI) array from diffraction-limited magnetome-try, an analysis now incorporated into a forthcoming ex-perimental study. We conclude with a discussion of the impact of agentic work on our research, the role of hu-man researchers (users) in AI pipelines, the importance of developing protocols for collaborative AI use in scien-tific contexts, and the future of AI in physics research (Sec. V).  

> II. PRINCIPLES FOR COLLABORATIVE AGENTIC RESEARCH

We aim to design an LLM-based agent that usefully functions as a collaborating researcher. An experimental physicist brings to a research team both norms govern-ing how they log, verify, and report results [57, 58]; and the judgment to make recurring research decisions with incomplete information, such as which physical model and approximations to adopt, what experimental pro-tocols and techniques to employ, what data to gather and trust, whether a result survives dimensional, order-of-magnitude, limiting-case, and symmetry checks, when an apparatus requires calibration or debugging, and how a proposed approach to these and other challenges might fail [59, 60]. While current LLMs encounter these norms and decision patterns throughout their training data and apply them capably on benchmark problems from physics olympiads [61], they do so far less reliably on open, research-level problems [62], which demand greater judg-ment and resist benchmarking. As LLMs are designed to handle a wide range of tasks and contexts, the burden falls on the user to clearly communicate desired proce-dures, workflows, documentation norms, and outputs for a given project, something that even advances in AI rea-soning skills cannot necessarily address. Therefore, we begin by carefully considering what behavior is desired from a collaborative AI research agent and what work-flows, organizational structures, and tools best support scientific work, with a particular focus on experimental physics at the benchtop scale.  

> A. Scientific Norms

1. Honesty. Report findings honestly, including weak-nesses, caveats, and negative results, and never manipu-late a metric. 

LLMs are known to exploit objectives [46, 63–65], hal-lucinate numbers [40, 46, 66] and citations [67], fabri-cate claims [41], and misjudge quantitative comparisons 3[46, 68]. Therefore, collaborative agentic research work-flows should emphasize the value of accurate results (pos-itive or negative), freeze key metrics so they cannot be exploited, and require that numerical claims be trace-able to executed code rather than directly computed by agents. 

2. Verification. Use structured procedures, unit tests, and independent subagents to validate results before ac-cepting them as correct. 

To further reduce hallucinations and false claims, col-laborative agentic research should include multiple layers of verification that can detect coding, physics, or logic errors, especially as tasks become more complex. Ver-ification should occur after every key step and should consist of programmatic unit tests, when appropriate, as well as specific checks assessing uncertainty, physical plausibility, appropriate dimensions and units, and noise features. Since an agent’s stated reasoning can be biased, and LLMs assess their own reasoning poorly [69–71] in critical situations, verification should be handled by inde-pendent subagents blind to the reasoning the first agent used. Using different LLMs for initial work and verifica-tion further mitigates blind spots [72]. In order to enable further verification by a human researcher, the collabo-rative AI agent should record input parameters and as-sumptions in a standardized format, save all code in an organized structure, and produce relevant visualizations and reports. 

3. Methodical Inquiry and Traceability. Decom-pose a problem into individually checkable steps, and record each step, its inputs, and its source. 

To improve the consistency of agent behavior, creative and reasoning-based steps should be embedded within predefined structures that enforce honesty and verifica-tion (see above), enable scientific practices such as cita-tion and code availability, and build basic infrastructure that domain expertise can iterate on (see Sec. II C). A provenance system should record the source of figures and claims, whether they come from existing literature, user-provided information, direct measurements, model-based simulations, or derived quantities. All code should be well-documented and version-controlled, and provenance records should document the specific version of the code that produced the figure or claim. Derivations should be clearly noted and preserved. Workflows should enforce planning and verification steps; and step-by-step proce-dures should ensure that common domain-specific con-cerns are addressed, including calculating uncertainty, considering geometric and other physical constraints, or comparing the answer to trivial solutions.  

> B. Effective Collaborative Agent Use

4. Communication. Specify the agent’s desired behav-ior explicitly and falsifiably, and include the rationale, stakes, and context for every task and handoff. 

Language used to communicate with research agents must be chosen with care, as it measurably affects agent behavior [73]. General guidance and desired workflow should be contained within scaffolding documents and system prompts, allowing the user to focus on task-specific prompts. Scaffolding documents should state de-sired behavior explicitly, with an emphasis on concrete actions against which the agent can objectively evaluate its compliance. The agent should seek clarifying infor-mation from the user rather than making assumptions, and the system should aid in the creation of scaffolding documents for complex projects, reducing the burden on the user to create them from scratch. 

5. Focus. Keep the agent’s working context small and focused on the task at hand. 

Context is the full set of input text (including system and user prompts, conversation history, and retrieved data) that an agent reads to generate a response. Un-necessary context adds to the token cost and degrades performance: models retrieve mid-context information less reliably [74], irrelevant material lowers accuracy even when the needed information remains present [75], and working loops degrade as prompts grow and orienting instructions fade [76, 77]. The context window should therefore be carefully managed. Scaffolding should be separated into tiers, with a persistent core of essential information accompanied by a larger body of supple-mentary documents ingested only during specific tasks. Longer sessions should make use of subagents or context compaction to maintain the most important information in the working context and avoid dilution with every rea-soning step from a long session. 

6. Autonomy & Safety. Ensure user intervention is efficient and targeted. 

Collaborative agentic frameworks must prioritize both safety and efficiency. They should include mechanisms (gates) for detecting potentially harmful or irreversible actions and flagging them for user intervention or sign-off. For experimental physics, these gates should cover use of computational resources and file management, significant changes to the framework itself (see Adaptability ), safe control of physical experiments, and researcher-defined criteria for data recording and processing, with thresh-olds adjusted based on risk severity. At the same time, closed-loop agentic work is a powerful tool, and early or excessive stops are a common failure mode. There-fore, the framework should allow variations of parame-ters within a safe range, and contain mechanisms to pre-vent early stops. Such mechanisms include embedding loops within a programmatic loop that handles calling new agents, eliminating the need for a single agent to continue working for extended periods. To take optimal advantage of user input, project goals and boundaries should be mutually agreed on by the user and collabo-rative agent before closed-loop sessions begin, giving the user an opportunity to provide targeted feedback and 4corrections in a single session and enabling productive iteration in longer independent runs. 

7. Adaptability. Keep the framework as a living docu-ment that improves through iteration. 

Collaborative agentic frameworks should be flexible and adaptable in response to user interactions, outcomes, new domains and use cases, LLM advances, and other software updates. In order to ensure revisions are gov-erned with the same evidential stringency as results, clear rules and procedures should be created for editing and maintaining the framework to minimize unnecessary growth [78, 79], drift from its design principles, or overly specific changes [80]. Improvements should be easy for the user to initiate, and standardized review of sessions and user feedback should identify areas for improvement. 

8. Resource Management. Use experimental and computational resources deliberately and efficiently, and account for their constraints. 

As collaborative agents are developed for a wide range of users and settings, they do not always properly take into account user-specific resource limitations such as the intended wall time of the campaign, local simulation ca-pacity, cluster compute allocations, or experimental duty cycles and resource constraints. Therefore, the frame-work should include well-defined and tested instructions to ensure experimental and computational resources are used efficiently, including performing shorter tests before launching long runs, monitoring to catch failures early, and assessing the cost versus return when considering longer run times. Agents should be directed to con-sider the required computational and experimental re-sources (including wall time) when comparing protocols; and general procedures, including closed-loop iteration, should be both adaptable and user-regulated in order to accommodate experiments of different time scales and other resource constraints (e.g., peak power usage or con-sumption of finite experimental materials).  

> C. Domain Expertise

9. Physics-Informed Reasoning. Ground every step in the relevant physics, including geometry, units, scales, symmetries, and uncertainty, and judge results on phys-ical plausibility. 

LLMs are fundamentally generalists, and while they excel in a wide range of domains including earning gold medals in physics olympiads [61], they remain far weaker on research-level physics problems [62, 81] and can still return physically incorrect solutions [41]. These limita-tions can be mitigated by incorporating domain-specific information into the framework that guides the collabo-rative AI agent. For example, supplying an LLM with re-trieved, physics-relevant knowledge can improve its per-formance on physics problems [82]. The same holds in agent-driven closed-loop discovery, where domain pri-ors can improve results on real inverse-design problems [33] and physics-constrained search yields compact tur-bulence models that generalize to flow conditions outside the training data [28]. For applications in experimen-tal physics, domain-informed reasoning skills can include checking trivial cases, dimensional analysis, proper scal-ing of results with physical parameters, simulation valida-tion and verification procedures, geometric and physical compatibility tests, uncertainty propagation, and statis-tical tests. 

10. Experimental Reasoning. Employ hardware-in-the-loop protocols for experimental control tasks. 

Experimental tasks, including instrument and mea-surement protocol design, direct hardware operation, and data analysis, rely on specialized expertise and detailed technical information; hence, agents performing these tasks benefit from dedicated procedures and guidance. Unlike computational or analytic tasks where systems may behave deterministically according to known rules, experiments can include unknown behavior that must be observed empirically (e.g., instrument performance, envi-ronmental conditions). An experimental research frame-work should support reasoning about calibration, envi-ronmental drift, measurement time concerns, noise man-agement, experiment characterization, hardware-specific debugging and control protocols, and experimental de-sign practices. Agents often do not have context about what “reasonable” suggestions are for experimental im-provements, so the user should set clear boundaries on feasible changes to experimental setups.   

> III. PHYSKIT: A STRUCTURED ENVIRONMENT FOR COLLABORATIVE AGENTIC RESEARCH A. From Principles to Structure

To transform off-the-shelf LLMs into reliable and col-laborative research assistants that reflect the above prin-ciples, we developed PhysKit, a structured research framework implemented through a collection of “skills” for experimental physics. Here, a skill consists of a Mark-down file delineating in natural language a procedure for an AI agent to follow for a particular class of tasks, op-tionally accompanied by scripts, templates, and reference material. Several widely used LLM harnesses, includ-ing Claude Code and Codex, implement skills as a stan-dard feature [83, 84]. A user can invoke skills directly or they can be automatically triggered by the agent if the user’s prompt matches a skill’s description. While the systematic study of skills remains at an early stage, re-cent preprints report better average task completion and instructional compliance with customized skills [85, 86]. PhysKit directly encodes the principles for collabora-tive agentic research, outlined in Sec. II, into its skills 5and the resulting procedures, focusing on tasks perti-nent to experimental physics research. Besides natural-language instructions, PhysKit also embeds determinis-tic programmatic components within agent-driven work-flows. These components handle mechanically specifi-able operations (including computing predefined met-rics, running executable correctness checks, and record-ing standardized experimental products) while agents re-main responsible for formulating hypotheses, interpret-ing results, and exercising scientific judgment. While no framework can completely eliminate the risk of error, in our experience PhysKit can substantially reduce time spent redirecting agents as well as the effort required to audit and correct their output. In Sec. IV below, we de-scribe six case-study applications of the PhysKit frame-work, drawn from the Walsworth group’s active experi-mental projects. A precise quantitative assessment of the framework’s performance and systematic effects remains for future work. The current version of PhysKit consists of 26 skills, which can be grouped roughly into four categories: 1. Campaign orchestration skills establish the re-search environment, organize its central workflow, and maintain its continuity (see Secs. III B–III C); 2. General skills strengthen scientific assurance and communication, for example, by subjecting results to independent adversarial checks (performed by the verify skill), building a battery of reusable tests for properties including analytic limits, numerical convergence, and conservation laws ( correctness-suite ), and writing scientific reports ( report ); 3. Specialist skills provide procedures for tasks such as analytical derivation ( derive ), numerical debug-ging ( debug ), dimensional analysis ( dimensional-analysis ), and comparison of competing physical explanations ( model-selection ); 4. Development skills handle the improvement and maintenance of the framework itself, including 

campaign-review , which reviews a completed cam-paign and suggests improvements to the framework based on the results; and improve-kit , which guides agents through making updates according to the framework’s design principles. A complete list of PhysKit skills and their functions, along with detailed documentation, is publicly available via GitHub [56]. As the framework is applied to a broader range of tasks, we expect to add new skills and expand existing ones. While PhysKit skills can be invoked directly by the user, they are designed to be interconnected and to com-pose larger research procedures. The verify skill provides a central example. Rather than operating only as a stan-dalone command, it is called by other skills, including de-rive , discovery-loop , experiment-design , model-selection ,

fork-campaign , and report before a result is relied on or reported. Verify in turn draws on other skills: it invokes 

dimensional-analysis as an initial units and bounding check, uses the physical inventory and expected ranges established by frame-problem , and treats the state of the 

correctness-suite as evidence about the reliability of the underlying numerical machinery. PhysKit skills are also designed to create a standard-ized set of documents and organizational structure, to help manage complex projects across multiple sessions. This structure includes an index of all files in the project, a record of engineering discoveries about the project, and a list of key system and measurement parameters. The handoff skill helps agents preserve work and trans-fer knowledge from one session to the next. Finally, since closed-loop research modes involve similar scaffold-ing documents for each campaign, PhysKit includes tem-plates for key files that the agent adapts to the specific problem during setup. The templates help ensure infor-mation is recorded consistently, maintain compatibility with programmatic elements, and guard against omitted steps. Records are also kept in standardized formats, improving the transparency of project documentation to both human and AI readers. PhysKit supports two work modes: closed-loop cam-paigns (Sec. III B), in which the agent iterates semi-autonomously toward a fixed goal with minimal hu-man intervention; and interactive sessions (Sec. III C), in which the researcher collaborates with the agent in real time in an open-ended conversation. Because both modes share the same organizational scaffolding, it is straightforward to switch between them. An extended interactive session can define goals and constraints for a subsequent closed-loop campaign, and such a campaign can be paused for an interactive session for the researcher to probe and verify its findings and decide whether any adjustments in strategy or goals must be made.  

> B. Closed-Loop Research Campaigns

The closed-loop mode is designed for extended, semi-autonomous work toward a well-defined goal. During closed-loop campaigns, agents follow an iterative pro-cess, proposing, testing, verifying, and evolving solutions (Fig. 1), aided by structured scaffolding documents that maintain project goals, rules, and results history. Un-like interactive sessions (Sec. III C), where the presence of the user allows immediate clarification and revision, a campaign must gather comprehensive details about the project’s context and goals to allow it to run for long periods (hours or days) with minimal or no human inter-vention. Work in closed-loop mode can be designed around the optimization of a single reward metric, as in find-ing a quantum pulse sequence with the best fidelity (see Sec. IV D). However, it can also be centered on a well-defined, multifaceted goal, such as finding a physical model for experimental data that satisfies a list of cri-6teria (e.g., low fit residuals, strong explanatory power for systematic effects, and consistency with published re-sults). Finally, it is also possible to perform an open-ended “discovery campaign” that seeks to discover a new scientific product (a computer algorithm or mathemati-cal expression, for example) fulfilling certain predefined criteria that are then rigorously validated (see Ref. [23] for a prior implementation in pure mathematics). 

Problem selection. Not all problems are suitable for a full closed-loop research campaign. Such efforts provide the most value when the parameter space is large and poorly suited to conventional optimization techniques. In the pulse sequence design case study (Sec. IV D), sub-stantial physics-based reasoning is required to narrow down the enormous parameter space of all possible se-quences into a few promising subtypes, which can then be addressed with exhaustive enumeration and/or conven-tional optimization algorithms. However, smaller-scale problems can often be efficiently solved through an in-teractive, user-attended session (Sec. III C). Since setting up the full campaign scaffolding requires a non-negligible upfront investment, a reasonable strategy is to evaluate a lightweight solution first through a single well-framed prompt before deciding whether to scaffold a full closed-loop campaign. PhysKit is designed to keep projects or-ganized in a way that enables realistic switching between work modes as needed. 

Defining the reward metric. One of the most impor-tant elements of a productive long-term closed-loop cam-paign is defining precise reward metrics that correlate strongly with the target validity of the proposed solu-tion. For some problems, this is fairly straightforward, because the metric itself is hard to satisfy by accident. For example, if the metric tests neural network perfor-mance on previously validated data (Sec. IV A), a net-work that scores well is very likely coded correctly and trained on an appropriate dataset. In contrast, other problems have metrics that only correlate weakly with target validity. A common task is to fit a mathemati-cal model to existing data (e.g., Sec. IV B), where the sum of squared fit residuals between the fit and data is the apparent metric. However, scientific judgment is re-quired to decide whether the residuals reflect the statisti-cal noise of the data or a systematic problem with the fit, the model, or the data. These problems often need sec-ondary metrics or guardrails, such as tracking the para-metric dependence of residuals for signs of systematic er-ror or directly measuring fit accuracy on simulated data. Guardrail metrics are also suitable for problems that re-quire multiple simultaneous constraints. One example is our microwave device design case study (Sec. IV C), which has the goal of providing circularly polarized mi-crowaves to a solid-state spin-defect sample, while meet-ing specified targets for experimental performance (peak microwave field strength and polarization purity) within instrument limitations, such as the maximum available microwave input power.

Running the campaign. During each loop iteration of the closed-loop campaign, the agent is instructed to con-sult the campaign scaffolding documents and records; propose and implement modifications, evaluated using the frozen metric; interpret the outcome; and write new records about the result and what was learned. If an experiment appears to have succeeded in improving on the reward metric, it is recorded as the “current best” result together with a label stating how it was verified, and an agent “hook” (a script that fires automatically at a defined point in the agent’s workflow [87]) ensures that the label is accurate and that a refuted result is never promoted. The level of verification, which can in-clude unit tests, self-review, or review by an independent agent, is determined by a user preset. If progress appears to stall, the agent follows PhysKit’s guidance for chang-ing approach. The loop runs continuously until the user intervenes, the agent determines that the campaign goal has been reached, or an optional predefined maximum campaign duration is reached. Reporting. A campaign ends with a final report that states the recommended solution and the decision it supports; documents the campaign history and scope, including runner-up candidates and their relative per-formance; tabulates every physical parameter with its provenance (literature, calculations, measurements) and the in-campaign evidence behind it; includes figures of key results; and carries a bibliography in which every entry has been checked against its real source. The re-port is itself verified by the agent before it reaches the researcher, with important values recomputed from the stored artifacts rather than transcribed. This last step is handled as a special case of the report skill, which ren-ders the document as compiled L ATEX and records input parameters, assumptions, literature records, results, and conclusions in a step-by-step way for easy verification. Because every experiment within a campaign is archived with the exact code, configuration, pinned input data, and environment that produced it, any reported result can be traced, re-executed or reanalyzed, or reused as a starting point for future work.  

> C. Interactive Sessions

In addition to closed-loop research campaigns for struc-tured iteration, PhysKit supports a broader range of day-to-day research tasks through interactive sessions be-tween the human user and collaborative agents, guided by general instructions and skills. Interactive sessions can be used for a variety of single-prompt tasks such as building a graphical user interface (GUI), processing a new dataset, or refining a fit. They can also refine the metrics, constraints, guardrails, and strategies required for a successful closed-loop research campaign. Interac-tive sessions require less setup than campaigns, as the user can provide context and corrections in real time, and are well suited to tasks that can be solved with minimal iteration, such as data visualization and modifications to existing workflows. Working within PhysKit sessions is also easier than working in an unstructured environment, as the framework handles general instructions on process, documentation, and verification, allowing the user to fo-cus on task-specific prompts. The default workflow for interactive sessions is as follows: 1. Clarify. Ensure the agent fully understands the user’s goal and which decisions the work will in-form, states the assumptions it is making, and raises the competing readings of an ambiguous re-quest rather than choosing one without consulting the user. 2. Frame. Collaboratively plan how to approach the problem and how to know whether the answer is right, in the context of what is already known. This includes what is in and out of scope, which physical effects matter and which can be neglected, what the literature and the project’s own records already establish, and what parameter range the answer should fall in. 3. Decompose. Collaboratively break the task into steps that can be checked individually, and hand in-dependent steps to subagents that work in parallel when appropriate. 4. Execute. The agent carries out the steps above, keeping changes attributable, checking between steps where an error would compound, and propa-gating an uncertainty estimate through each quan-tity it derives. 85. Verify. The agent validates results through unit tests and an independent subagent, working from a structured brief that targets specific error classes. 6. Record and Report. The agent records and re-ports to the user what was done and what was found in the interactive session, including failures and the gap between predictions and the outcome, along with the configuration and code needed to reproduce the session. Each step is carried out by one or more skills that bring their own procedures and reference material, and can involve several substeps. For example, framing (step 2) calls the frame-problem skill, which produces an in-ventory of the relevant physics with a stated reason for every effect left out, a verification plan written before any work is done, and a decomposition with checkpoints placed in proportion to risk. Verification (step 5) calls the verify skill, which grades what it finds on a fixed scale rather than returning a binary verdict, so that a result resting on a weak premise is recorded differently from one that is simply wrong. The PhysKit core prompt also includes a list of general core principles that we find to be useful to mitigate some of the most common agent failure modes encountered through testing. These failure modes include a lack of honest and thorough disclosure of the limitations of results; prioritization of task “success” over accuracy; inadequate consideration of physical plau-sibility; weighing a claim’s origin (including user errors) instead of evidence; and a tendency to treat technical creativity as incompatible with rigor.  

> D. Hardware in the Loop

PhysKit supports closed-loop research campaigns in-volving control of laboratory equipment, in which the agent designs, runs, and analyzes experiments with pre-cautions taken to ensure safe operation. During cam-paign setup, the user and agent define the allowed range of every instrument setting, with hardware safety limits set at sustainable operating points rather than absolute maxima. Each proposed experiment then passes through a deterministic gate that rejects any proposal that omits a required setting (rather than filling in a default value) or that specifies a setting outside its allowed range, allow-ing the agent to correct the error before any instrument time is spent. Approved experiments are handed to the laboratory’s existing deterministic (non-agentic) instrument control program through an exchange of files, so that either pro-gram can crash and restart mid-campaign without loss of information, and a full record of each request and its result remains on disk. The campaign loop monitors the experiment so it can intervene if an instrument stops pro-ducing data or fails to meet predetermined criteria. Each completed acquisition passes a data-quality check before it is scored by the campaign’s frozen evaluator, after which the campaign proceeds as described in Sec. III B. The NV-NMR case study described in Sec. IV E was con-ducted as such a campaign.  

> IV. CASE STUDIES

Drawn from our laboratory’s active projects, the six case studies below demonstrate the potential of agentic research to advance objectives in experimental physics. The closed-loop research campaigns outlined below (i) reduced the test error of a published current density reconstruction network by 33%; (ii) produced a photo-dynamic model that predicts experimental data better than published models; (iii) designed a fabrication-ready microwave device for zero-field V − 

> B

magnetometry; (iv) identified quantum control pulse sequences that outper-form published sequences in simulation by more than an order of magnitude; (v) commissioned an NMR spec-trometer to ∼1 Hz precision on live hardware; and (vi) built a fitting pipeline that recovers the magnetization state of each nanomagnet in an artificial spin-ice array. We deliberately select case studies within our own exper-tise in quantum sensing, but the methods themselves are not subfield-specific, and we expect them to generalize to other domains.  

> A. Current density reconstruction

Recovering the two-dimensional (2D) source current density spatial distribution from a measured 2D magnetic field map is a common inverse problem across physics, engineering, and biomedicine. The Biot–Savart law pro-vides a simple forward calculation (from 2D current den-sity distribution to 2D magnetic field pattern), but its inversion by traditional analytic methods amplifies noise without bound [89], severely degrading performance on low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) data and motivating al-ternate approaches such as neural networks. We used a closed-loop agentic workflow in PhysKit to search for improved neural network architectures rela-tive to the previous best result, a hand-optimized con-volutional neural network (CNN) developed for current density inversion (MAGIC-UNet) [88]. The agent was asked to reduce the average root mean square error (RMSE) between the ground truth and prediction on a fixed set of synthetic test data at four different noise levels. Before beginning the optimization, the agent en-sured it could reproduce the paper results [88], which applied MAGIC-UNet to current density reconstruction from two-dimensional images of vector magnetic fields acquired by a quantum diamond microscope. The agent designed the research campaign to use GPU time efficiently on a single 12 GB consumer GPU. A tiered protocol of short feasibility probes, epoch-capped single-seed screening runs, and full-length runs reserved for promising candidates helped ensure efficient use of GPU time; and the agent ultimately spent approximately 110 GPU-hours across 38 logged experiments. > B

The evaluator was frozen before the search; it fit each candidate model to all five PL traces as a set and scored the fit by the sum of squared resid-uals (SSR), with masking of the low-count bins between pulses and normalization such that all traces contribute equally, regardless of brightness (denoted fractional SSR in Fig. 3c). Ref. [101] instead normalizes all five traces together and quotes the SSR against smoothed traces, which suppresses counting noise; we use that convention when comparing with the published fits. The agent could edit only the model specification: namely the number of energy level states, the transition rates between them, the laser-power scaling of each rate, which rates are fixed or free, and the spatial profile of the optical excitation across the flake. The radiative rate ( kr ) and the ms=0 intersystem-crossing rate ( γ0) were fixed to the values used in Ref. [101], and the other fitted rates were checked against direct measurements in the literature for physi-cal consistency. The campaign first refitted both pub-lished models (seven- and nine-level [101]) as baselines; refitted with all five traces normalized together, as in Ref. [101], they reproduce the published fits. Because adding states increases the number of free parameters, a lower SSR alone does not establish a better model. To guard against overfitting and test the model’s pre-dictive power, promising models were refitted five times, once with each laser power withheld, and scored on their predictions of the withheld traces (leave-one-power-out cross-validation, LOPO). The agent tested 101 model structures in 198 logged experiments over four days, accumulating about 55 hours of active fitting time. Every structure was fit from multiple sets of initial conditions and assessed by frac-tional SSR; promising structures were also scored on predictions for withheld laser powers. The best model emerged through four attributable additions to the pub-lished nine-level model: photoconversion out of the ex-cited state as a one-photon step ( ∝ P ) instead of a di-rect two-photon step ( ∝ P 2), together with light-driven recovery from the dark states; a third dark state; a light-driven shelf state attached to the singlet; and a return path from the dark states to the ground state in the dark, which lets the repeated pulse measurement reach a steady state. A final refinement, averaging the PL over the laser spot on a finite flake, improves the fit only slightly. The resulting agent-selected eleven-level model (Fig. 3b) fits the full PL dataset better than both published models. Scored as in Ref. [101], its SSR is 0.83, versus 2.13 for the nine-level and 3.44 for the seven-level model; scoring the unsmoothed traces instead adds about 4.6 to every model from counting noise and leaves the ranking unchanged. These values come from refits with all five PL traces normalized together, as was done for the published fits; the fractional SSR used in the search gives the same ranking (Fig. 3c). The agent-selected model also predicts PL data at withheld laser powers much better than the seven- and nine-level models, with the clearest improvement over the nine-level model at the highest power (21.30 mW). Importantly, the agent did not simply follow the best fractional SSR score. It passed over six models that set a new best score, five because they predicted withheld laser powers worse and one for physical inconsistency, and it identified ten candidates with higher (worse) frac-tional SSR despite having better withheld laser power predictions or physical consistency (Fig. 3c). > C. Microwave device design

Near-zero-field magnetometry with optically probed solid-state spin defects leaves the sample unperturbed and enables applications such as zero- to ultralow-field (ZULF) NMR [110–112]. But at near-zero field, the two electronic spin resonances of an S = 1 defect, ms =0 → ± 1, overlap within the transition linewidth; hence linearly polarized microwaves, which drive both reso-nances, produce no first-order change in the optically de-tected magnetic resonance (ODMR) spectrum [111] (see appendix). In contrast, circularly polarized microwaves preferentially drive one spin resonance and restore the first-order ODMR response in near-zero field [111–114]. However, experimental generation and delivery of circu-larly polarized microwave fields typically require two or-thogonal components driven 90 ◦ out of phase; and for 

V − 

> B

defects, whose spin axis is normal to the hBN flake, both microwave components must lie in the plane of the flake. Sadi et al. [115] reported spin-state-selective driv-ing of V − 

> B

in a few-mT bias field using circularly polar-ized microwaves delivered with a cross-shaped microwave waveguide; but the published description does not con-tain a detailed design, so the device could not simply be reproduced in our laboratory. We therefore set out to explore whether a collaborative agent equipped with an electromagnetic solver can generate a customized, possi-bly improved waveguide design for delivery of circularly polarized microwaves to resonantly drive V − 

> B

spin defects in an hBN sample at ∼3.5 GHz. Using PhysKit, we froze an evaluator that scores the mean amplitude of the in-plane circularly polarized mi-crowave field at the hBN flake per square root of available amplifier power, computed with a finite-element solver (Ansys HFSS) and taken at the worse (lower amplitude) of the two circular polarizations. Four constraints set by our laboratory hardware were enforced as automatic pass/fail checks: conductor and gap widths of at least 100 μm, limited by the in-house circuit-board mill; a foot-print within 50.8 mm, constrained by the sample stage; no metal in the optical window above the hBN flake; and drive from a single 16 W amplifier, which sets the max-imum input microwave power. Two further guardrails were a polarization impurity threshold and impedance match to the 50 Ω feed. The topology, substrate, and all copper dimensions of the waveguide were left free. The closed-loop agentic campaign ran for six days, scoring 418 simulations in about 140 solver-hours, which correspond to 313 distinct designs. In periodic check-ins, we twice corrected the agent’s understanding of physics against the literature; otherwise, the agent operated largely independently, including unattended stretches of up to 27 hours. The final waveguide design is a symmet-ric copper cross (Fig. 4a, b): four identical 100 μm traces in one copper layer meet at the hBN flake site, making the two orthogonal drive axes equally strong by symme-try. In simulation, the agent-optimized waveguide deliv-ers circularly polarized microwaves at ∼3.5 GHz with a mean amplitude of about 7 .5 × 10 −4 T/ √W, yielding a

However, for a spin ensemble in which resonant spin–spin dipolar interactions become appreciable over the interrogation time, refocusing each spin is not enough. Global π/ 2pulses must also be applied to rotate the secular dipo-lar interaction among different toggling frames, allowing both local disorder and spin–spin interactions to average toward zero over each sequence cycle. No general ana-lytic method builds pulse sequences that satisfy this con-dition from first principles, even as average Hamiltonian theory (AHT) [122] supplies some analytic conditions to narrow the search space. Thus, finding better pulse se-quences to extend solid-state spin coherence times and improve quantum sensing performance remains an active area of research. Peng et al. We explored whether a closed-loop agentic work-flow could improve upon the Peng et al. result. The collaborative agentic campaign adopted the same simulation setup as Ref. [123]: a one-dimensional chain of three spins evolving under dipolar coupling J between neighbors and a uniform frequency offset, with time dis-cretized into steps that either idle or apply a single global 

π/ 2 rotation about the ±x or ±y axes. Systematic pulse angle error is included, so imperfections accumulate co-herently and can only be removed by the ordering of the pulses, and not averaged away; and the frozen evaluator contains no noise or ensemble sampling and is exactly de-terministic. We extended the maximum sequence length to 96 steps and normalized the score per step, so pulse sequences of different lengths are compared over equal to-tal evolution time. The figure of merit (frozen evaluator) is Reff = − ln(1 − Feff ), where the fidelity Feff measures how close the state evolution is to the identity operation, so one unit of Reff corresponds to a factor of e (2.72) decrease in infidelity 1 − Feff .The campaign was set up with our PhysKit frame-work; and the agent was instructed to perform an open search for pulse sequences improving Reff over the yxx48 sequence of Ref. Because a single metric is easy to overfit, every promoted candidate was additionally evaluated on a 23-point bat-tery of robustness tests: sweeps of coupling-delay prod-15 uct, offset, and pulse angle, and also finite-pulse-width errors, applied in different combinations; and then the entire procedure was repeated on larger 8-spin, 10-spin, and 12-spin chains. The agent was connected to a local knowledge base of 89 indexed journal articles on rele-vant topics to inform the pulse sequence search. Unlike Ref. The standard methods included exhaustive enumeration over structured subspaces, stochastic local search, and analytic constructions drawn from the literature. This was the largest single gain of the campaign (Fig. 5a). Further prompting across 25 more experiments did not improve on that suc-cess, and the agent reported that the remaining barrier was analytic rather than computational. After an hour, the latter returned Wrev (Reff = 19 .49), which was fed back into the main agentic loop and assessed with the same evaluator. The agent warned, however, that W rev beat B1 in only two conditions across the 23-point robustness battery, sug-gesting that past a certain point, the main Reff metric alone no longer strongly correlated with practically use-ful sequences. The campaign completed with 49 exper-iments over about 16 hours, scoring roughly 10 million candidate sequences. Following Ref. While the W rev sequence has the highest value of Reff for the main evaluation metric (reaching an effective infidelity of 3 .4 × 10 −9, 753 × be-low yxx48, and keeping its lead up to a 12-spin chain), it performed poorly on the random disorder test, dropping below the performance of the yxx48 benchmark at mod-est disorder (Fig. 5b). > E. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiment commissioning

NMR is a central analytical tool in chemistry, biol-ogy, and materials science. NV-NMR replaces a conven-tional pickup coil with NV color centers in diamond to enable NMR spectroscopy and imaging of micron-scale and nanoscale samples [125–129]. Setting up an NV-NMR measurement (locating the resonances, calibrat-ing the pulses, and then optimizing signal acquisition) is currently a detailed, time-consuming task that requires an expert’s focused attention [130]. Thus, we tasked an agent, working within PhysKit, with commissioning an NV-NMR spectrometer from an uncalibrated state, and then optimizing its 1H NMR spectroscopy frequency precision within a 36-hour budget. The NV-NMR spec-trometer operated with a bias magnetic field near 21 mT and a sample of water (volume <1 nanoliter) contain-ing 5 mM 4-hydroxy-TEMPO for signal enhancement via Overhauser dynamic nuclear polarization (DNP) [128]. The agent was given control of the experiment hardware (Sec. III D) and told only essential technical information about the apparatus: the NMR sample’s identity; that the ensemble-NV-diamond sensor is 15 N-enriched; and operational and safety limits on applied microwave and radio-frequency (RF) waveform amplitude, pulse dura-tion, sweep length, and DNP pulse energy. The agent was also given an eight-step commissioning protocol that fixes the order of the following tasks while containing no experimental parameters: (1) broadband ODMR of the NV-diamond sensor; (2) hyperfine-resolved NV ODMR; (3) Rabi calibration for driving the NV ensemble elec-tronic spins; (4) NV-NMR detection-chain validation and optimization, using a test-signal coil to apply an RF sig-nal similar to the expected NMR signal from the sample; (5) setting the DNP frequency on a hyperfine transition of the radical; (6–7) calibration of the NMR π and π/ 2pulses; and (8) NV-NMR spectroscopy on the water sam-ple, optimized under the frozen figure of merit. The agent had to determine the bias magnetic field value, the NV and 1H spin resonance frequencies, the DNP frequency for the radical, pulse lengths and amplitudes, and the pulse sequence timing (echo spacing, number of echoes, acquisition window, and DNP duration) used for system characterization, NV-NMR spectroscopy, and DNP. The campaign’s frozen figure of merit (FOM) rewards time-normalized 1H NMR frequency precision. This FOM does not change when an NV-NMR spectrum acquisition is sliced into more or fewer blocks, does not reward (i.e., down-scores) scatter that happens to fall below the es-timator’s uncertainty, and penalizes measured NV-NMR spectral drift until the error model accounts for it. The ratio s/ ¯σest is the Birge ratio [131]: a value near 1 means the estimator’s error model explains the observed scatter, and a value well above 1 signals unmodeled effects such as drift. We call experiments with a Birge ratio ≤ 1 CRB-limited and those with a Birge ratio > 1 drift-limited (Fig. 6). The campaign ran unattended for 20.8 hours, com-prising 151 experiments (Fig. 6). The agent completed protocol steps 1–7 in about three hours. From broad-band and hyperfine-resolved ODMR it determined a bias field of 20.95 mT and chose an ODMR spectral feature for NMR sensing, on which it calibrated a 116 ns NV π

pulse. With the test-signal coil, the agent showed that the detection chain accurately recovers an injected RF tone ( ≈0.9 MHz) to within 0.04 Hz, at two different sens-ing frequencies and with the tone offset from the sensing frequency; and it measured an initial RF sensitivity of 258 pT/ √Hz, already below the protocol’s 300 pT/ √Hz target, which it improved to 162–177 pT/ √Hz by dou-bling the number of XY16 repetitions in the sensing se-quence. The first NV-NMR 1H signal was detected at

After calibrating the NMR pulses (RF pulses applied to the sample to induce a detectable NMR sig-nal), it ran its first scored experiment at t + 3 .2 h (FOM 0.74). The agent then optimized one parameter at a time, bracketing each change with interleaved controls and replicates. It rechecked its NMR pulse calibration, the DNP pump settings, and the NV PL readout timing. Tuning the readout timing (the delay of the photodetec-tor PL acquisition relative to the readout laser pulse) im-proved the test-signal sensitivity further, to 104 pT/ √Hz, and raised the FOM from 1.19 to 1.87 at t ≈ 9 h. The best acquisition delay, 0.8 μs, agrees with the 0.6–0.9 μs opti-mum we had previously found by hand on this apparatus. Finally, at a fixed echo-train duration of about 0.4 s, the agent lengthened the echo spacing from 6 to 15 ms (and shortened the train from 64 to 26 echoes). Each step lengthened the signal record per echo, which sharpened the estimator’s frequency resolution and lowered ¯ σest , so the FOM that a CRB-limited experiment would reach rose from 1.86 to 2.60. The best experiment, at t + 15 .5 h, reached FOM 2.51: a 1H NMR frequency uncertainty of 0.97 Hz in one 10-minute run on the 891.8 kHz NMR spectral line, or 1.1 ppm fractional precision. Across all 17 ten experiments at these final settings, σarm ranged from 0.97 to 2.1 Hz (median 1.47 Hz). What limited the ultimate NV-NMR frequency preci-sion was system drift, not intrinsic NMR signal or NV sensor properties. Of the 34 scored experiments, 25 were drift-limited, as were nine of the ten at the final settings, with a mean block scatter of 4.5 Hz against a mean es-timator uncertainty of 3.0 Hz. The agent traced the ex-cess scatter to the history of the DNP pump. After 30 minutes or more without pumping, the 1H line was 32– 36 Hz higher, and the NV ODMR lines shifted by up to ∼50 kHz, reflecting both a temperature change and a change of up to 0.7 μT in the bias field at the diamond. The agent spent most of the final five hours on ODMR sweeps testing whether this pump-driven field change ex-plains the scatter; the test was inconclusive. Having found no remaining parameter to adjust, the agent de-clared its search complete at t + 17 h, and we ended the campaign at t + 20 .8 h, with 15 hours of its budget un-used. An independent claim audit (Sec. III B) then checked the campaign’s record. It rescored all 34 experiments from the raw data, reproduced the headline result in a fresh process, and confirmed the 638.1 MHz DNP line against the radical’s known magnetic parameters. It re-jected the agent’s claim of a replicated record: the best experiment was one of ten at identical settings and the only CRB-limited experiment among them, so we quote the median result as well as the best. The audit also found that the agent stopped lengthening the echo spac-ing while the FOM was still improving at the end of the tested range, identifying a direction for potential future performance improvement. The experimentally realized NV-NMR frequency pre-cision ( ≈1 Hz per run requiring ∼10 minutes) is compa-rable to the ∼1 Hz spectral resolution achieved in high-resolution NV-NMR spectroscopy [127, 132]. However, over the ∼20 hours of the campaign, the measured 1HNMR frequency moved by tens of hertz due to system drift, highlighting the opportunity for a future campaign to aid in improving system stability. The result shows that agentic research can be used successfully to com-mission a hardware experiment that pursues a sensitive precision measurement. For comparison, equivalent man-ual commissioning of the same NV-NMR apparatus takes multiple days of automated linear parameter sweeps or 

∼10 hours of an expert’s focused attention. The agent reached a scored NV-NMR measurement 3.2 hours after starting from an uncalibrated state, without supervision; and the same campaign can straightforwardly recommis-sion the system from scratch after a bias field or NMR sample change.  

> F. Artificial spin-ice (ASI) magnetization reconstruction

Artificial spin ice (ASI) is a lithographically patterned, typically two-dimensional array of interacting single-domain nanomagnet islands (Fig. 7a), each behaving as a two-state Ising spin and arranged so that geomet-ric frustration prevents their interactions from all being minimized at once [133, 134]. ASI arrays are designer platforms for studying frustrated magnetism, emergent magnetic monopoles, and neuromorphic computing, all of which require reading out the microstate (the orien-tation of every island) as the array is driven through a magnetization reversal via an applied bias magnetic field, with associated magnetization hysteresis. We tasked a series of agents, working within PhysKit, with recon-structing the per-island magnetization reversal of a pin-wheel ASI [135] consisting of 2,178 nanomagnets from single-component confocal NV magnetic imaging data (Fig. 7b). This problem was challenging for two reasons. First, the problem is ill-posed as the NV/ASI standoff of ∼700 nm and pixel pitch of 500 nm suppress the fine magnetic field features distinguishing individual island states at the nanoscale by a factor of ∼10 4–10 5.Second, the reconstruction involves roughly a dozen imperfectly known instrumental and physical parame-ters and several interdependent subproblems that have to be solved in order to progress to the final solution. Progress on each subproblem depended on the others; for example, errors in the standoff or NV confocal mi-croscope point spread function (PSF) can be partially absorbed by the fit or bias magnetic field gradient sub-traction. Arriving at the final optimization algorithm (Fig. 7c) ultimately required a close, collaborative effort between the human users and a series of agents over ap-proximately a dozen separate campaigns and interactive sessions spanning two months, with extensive discussion between agents and users. To our knowledge, the re-sulting solution provides the first reconstruction of the Ising microstate of an ASI array from diffraction-limited magnetic imaging data; a thorough treatment of these results will be included alongside experimental details in an upcoming manuscript [136]. The forward model used in ASI magnetization recon-struction was established piece by piece during successive campaigns: fitting experimental NV data to determine the NV/ASI standoff, NV confocal microscope PSF, and the sensing NV azimuthal angle in the lab frame; in-cluding per-frame lattice rotation; accounting for field gradients caused by the bias magnet; and modeling the Stoner–Wohlfarth canting of the nanomagnet moments [137], which agents validated independently with Mu-Max3 [138] micromagnetic simulations they designed and ran themselves. > G. Lessons from Case Studies

The above case studies demonstrate that collaborative agentic research is capable of making meaningful contri-butions to multiple projects in our laboratory; and we expect that the principles and techniques discussed here will generalize to many areas of experimental physics. In particular, the results from the presented case stud-ies directly surpass our previously published work in a variety of ways, including performance, fit model agree-ment with experimental data, and time required to reach an optimal solution. Agents frequently found creative solutions to a wide range of problems, handled debug-ging in real time, and designed well-targeted follow-up experiments (both synthetic and with hardware) to test hypotheses. With carefully designed agentic workflows and scaffolding, we could substantially extend the com-plexity and difficulty of problems that LLMs can handle beyond what fits in a single context window. Across all closed-loop campaigns, a critical element, unsurprisingly, is defining the right metric for the problem; a well-chosen metric functions as an effective filter for most errors or hallucinations that the agent makes. For research prob-lems in this field, an effective metric must often include several connected objectives paired with a set of carefully chosen constraints, in order to prevent unintentional re-ward hacking. For especially complex problems, multiple campaigns with distinct objectives and workflows may be required, as seen in our ASI case study (Sec. IV F). The lessons learned from these case studies have been incor-porated into the PhysKit research framework [56].

> V. DISCUSSION

We have presented a principles-based approach to col-laborative agentic research in experimental physics, to-gether with a framework (PhysKit) that codifies these principles into agent-driven loop-based workflows using various skills tailored to experimental work. In six case studies, we demonstrate the applicability of agentic re-search to diverse tasks, including interpreting experimen-tal data, optimizing quantum control protocols, design-ing hardware devices, and controlling an active quan-tum sensing experiment. We now discuss three themes: the broader impact of agentic workflows on experimen-tal physics, their implications for the role of human re-searchers, and a future outlook. 

Impact on experimental physics. Agentic workflows are fundamentally transforming experimental physics research across diverse tasks and problems. Pre-viously, establishing whether a new research direction was viable required days or weeks of coding and testing on laboratory instrumentation, inhibiting rapid explo-ration of promising ideas. The low marginal cost of agen-tic research assistants enables efficient pursuit of lines of inquiry that would previously have been set aside, allow-ing human researchers to explore a wider range of op-portunities and ideas, home in on the best options, and pursue them in greater depth. In our laboratory, im-plementing agentic workflows has resulted in measurably improved device designs, more rigorous data analyses, as well as a wealth of new theoretical and experimental directions that are benefiting our work. However, effec-tively using this capacity requires caution: the ability to quickly test the feasibility of nearly any idea with only a few prompts means that it is possible to waste time and resources on underdeveloped or poorly conceived ideas if the researcher is unfamiliar with the relevant material or fails to dedicate sufficient attention to the project. The long-term impact of agentic research on experi-mental physics remains to be determined. One possibil-ity is that human researchers will be tempted to focus on rapidly producing incremental results, for which agents are well suited, rather than investing effort in fewer, more groundbreaking ideas and challenging experiments. In-deed, early AI adopters appear to be successful in increas-ing their number of publications, but rarely open new re-search directions [139], although these early assessments may not carry over to later adopters or to more deliber-ate practice as the use of AI matures. In the long term, the ability of agents to accelerate useful, albeit incremen-tal, experimental progress and identify subtle patterns in existing data may lay the groundwork that inspires inno-vative discovery. 

Evolving role of the human researcher. Collab-orative agentic research effectively shifts the role of hu-man researchers from doing labor-intensive software and experimental optimization tasks toward generating ideas, devising research strategies, orchestrating agents, assess-ing data, and verifying results. For the six case studies 20 described in Sec. IV, agentic research campaigns were ini-tially based on human researcher specification and often required human redirection or iteration, such as ques-tioning a dubious assumption, recommending a search direction, or trying different models, workflows, or re-search strategies if the agent failed to progress. Ad-ditionally, some campaigns uncovered findings that call into question the specified premise or metric, requiring further investigation and judgment from the human re-searcher. In all our case studies, we found that detailed verification and assessment of agentic results by human researchers is an essential task, generally replacing tech-nical implementation as the limiting step of a research project. Even when work is well documented, verify-ing that the conclusions of agentic research are suitable for peer-reviewed publication demands considerable time and expertise from human researchers. As both direction of agents and verification of their work products require knowledge and intuition, understanding how to train stu-dents to use agentic tools and make such decisions effec-tively will become increasingly important. A prominent strand of recent work centers on fully au-tonomous “AI scientists” designed to operate with min-imal human direction through the entire research pro-cess, from idea generation through paper drafting [46– 48]. There are reasons to be skeptical that this ap-proach is currently appropriate for experimental physics research. First, we find that agents struggle to exhibit scientific “taste”: evaluating which questions are worth exploring in the first place, when to give up on an idea or explore it further, or how to shape results into a com-pelling narrative for a high-quality publication; our ex-perience is consistent with emerging literature that iden-tifies these areas as particular weaknesses [49, 140]. Sec-ond, human verification is important for maintaining an accurate scientific record, particularly in empirical sci-ences where theories can only be tested statistically, and accounting for systematic bias and uncertainty can re-quire significant judgment and expertise. Third, while AI can implement and optimize experimental procedures, as demonstrated in the NMR case study (Sec. IV E), automating the construction of new experiments is cur-rently out of reach. We therefore prefer a workflow where collaborative agents provide technical and analytic sup-port that enhances our ability to make expert-level judg-ments, rather than handing such judgments to AI. Our ASI reconstruction case study (Sec. IV F) is an example of what this process can look like: it took a dozen cam-paigns and many discussions between human researchers and agents to refine the final code, with many key insights arising through human–agent interaction in response to campaign results. 

Future outlook. We expect collaborative agentic research to affect most areas of experimental physics (fundamental and applied), reshaping workflows and the skills required of human researchers. As its use spreads, establishing scientific norms for AI behavior becomes cen-tral, both for human researchers using agentic tools and for developers designing scientific harnesses and post-training refinements. The principles of Sec. II restate norms that predate computing, while the files, hooks, and loops that enforce them track the capabilities and failure modes of current agentic models. We expect these techniques to continue to evolve, e.g., via exploration of file customization for harnesses optimized for the diverse activities of experimental physics. As of this writing, clear standards for documentation, reporting, and verification of AI work in physics (and the natural sciences more broadly) do not exist. Once established, such standards could be enforced more sys-tematically, for example, through journal review pro-cesses, stricter data and agentic model availability re-quirements, and robust community standards [141]. Ad-ditionally, validated benchmarks are needed to quantify agentic performance on realistic research problems in ex-perimental physics, so that different training protocols or frameworks can be compared. For example, exper-imental physicists in precision measurement, rare-event searches, and particle physics regularly blind their anal-yses to protect results from human bias [142–145]; as AI agents become increasingly common in experimental physics research, we should extend the same care to en-sure they strengthen rather than erode the reliability of the scientific record.
