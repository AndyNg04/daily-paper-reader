Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to simulate human behavior in social science research, including decisions, interactions, and social relation-ships. However, in network formation tasks, existing evaluations often focus on aggregate network properties, while networks with plausible structural properties may still fail to reproduce individuals’ social and political environments. Here, we generate LLM-based political networks using personas sampled from the nation-ally representative ANES 2020–2022 Social Media Study and use respondents’ survey-reported partisan exposure as an empirical benchmark for the political environments generated for the corresponding personas. We first characterize the structural properties and political assortativity of the generated networks, and then compare ego-level partisan exposure with the survey. We find that the structure and political assortativity of the generated networks vary across exper-imental configurations. More importantly, when comparing generated partisan exposure with the survey, some models generate stronger separation between Democratic and Republican egos than reported in the survey, with higher same-party and lower cross-party exposure, while others mainly underestimate Republican exposure across ego groups. Moreover, networks with similar polit-ical assortativity can still generate different partisan environments around the same personas, including among networks with plausible structural properties. 

1arXiv:2610.23199v1 [cs.SI] 16 Oct 2026 Finally, generated networks show group-specific errors, under- or overestimating exposure to either Democrats or Republicans for specific social groups. These findings show that evaluating LLM-based social simulations only through aggre-gate outcomes can hide errors in the local environments through which those outcomes emerge, highlighting the importance of validation at both aggregate and individual levels.

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as tools for social simulation because they can generate human behavioral traces conditioned on attributes and beliefs that can be experimentally manipulated [1–3]. Recent work uses LLMs to generate survey responses, simulate individual decisions, model political attitudes, and represent interactions among synthetic or survey-derived personas [3–9]. Yet the validity of these simulations remains an open challenge [7, 10–16]. Simulations of heterogeneous human populations rely largely on priming the model with personas that specify sets of demographic or political characteris-tics [4, 7, 17]. LLMs, however, may reduce multidimensional personas to simplified demographic profiles, flattening heterogeneity and reproducing stereotypes or group-specific biases [12, 18–21]. These distortions may compound when LLMs are used not only to generate individual responses but also collective dynamics [22, 23] or networks of social relationships [24–27], which are shaped by individual character-istics and by patterns of homophily and assortative mixing [28–31]. These concerns become particularly relevant, for example, when LLMs are used to simulate patterns of political polarization in social networks [24, 26], given that people’s political stances are influenced by the ideological composition of their social contacts and political environments [32–39]. Crucially, however, these distortions may go unnoticed because models can pro-duce realistic collective outcomes in aggregate while deviating from realistic human behavioral traces at the level of subgroups or individuals [40]. A synthetic network generated by LLM agents with political personas, for example, may display realistic density, clustering, modularity, assortativity, and political homophily, yet still fail to reproduce the distribution of political contacts observed in real-world data for indi-viduals or specific political subgroups. Existing evaluations of LLM-generated social networks nonetheless focus on coarse structural properties such as density or modu-larity [24, 25, 27, 41]. This limited analytic resolution stems largely from the scarcity of fine-grained empirical data that would allow researchers to assess how well syn-thetic networks reproduce individual nodes’ perspectives on their surrounding social environments. In this paper, we use a persona-based approach to generate LLM-based social networks grounded in real-world survey data from the American National Election Studies (ANES) 2020-2022 Social Media Study [42], which includes political attributes 2and self-reported exposure to Democratic and Republican social contacts, and use respondents’ reported partisan exposure as an empirical benchmark for the political environments generated around the corresponding personas. First, we test whether the resulting networks exhibit plausible high-level structural properties. Second, we assess whether LLM-generated ties reproduce survey-reported patterns of individual-level partisan exposure, comparing simulated ego networks to survey-based measures. Third, we examine whether networks with similar political assortativity generate sim-ilar partisan environments around the same personas, and whether exposure errors vary across demographic and political groups. We find that both the structure and political assortativity of LLM-generated networks vary across models and prompting configurations. More importantly, com-parison with survey-reported partisan exposure reveals differences across models and configurations in both the direction and magnitude of the errors. Specifically, some models generate stronger separation between Democrats and Republicans than reported in the survey, with higher same-party and lower cross-party exposure, while others mainly underestimate Republican exposure across ego groups. More-over, networks with similar political assortativity can still generate different partisan environments around the same personas, including among networks with plausible structural properties. Finally, exposure errors vary across demographic and political groups, and across models. Overall, these results show that LLM-generated political networks differ across models and prompting configurations in both their aggregate properties and the par-tisan environments generated at the individual level. In this context, survey-reported exposure provides an empirical benchmark to evaluate whether the generated net-works reproduce the political environments experienced by respondents beyond what can be captured by network-level measures alone.

2.1 LLM-generated network structure varies across configurations 

To assess how the structure of the generated networks varies and whether these networks reproduce structural properties observed in empirical social networks, we compare reciprocal networks (see Methods section) with those observed across 57 friendship and friendship-like networks from 13 empirical datasets, following previous work [24]. Here, reciprocal networks include a tie between two personas only when both nominate each other as potential friends. To this end, we consider five main network metrics: (i) average degree, which measures the average number of ties per node, (ii) the average clustering coefficient, which captures the tendency toward local clustering, (iii) the proportion of nodes in the largest connected component (LCC), (iv) the average shortest path length, and (v) Louvain modularity, which measures the extent to which the network separates into communities. A generated network is considered structurally plausible when all five measures fall within the corresponding empirical ranges (see Methods). Figure 2a shows how network structure differs across prompting configurations, and in particular based on the number of potential friends included in the prompt. The pairwise configuration (c1) shows the largest variability between models and, for some models, between runs and attribute orderings, generating networks whose struc-tural properties vary even within the same model. Mistral 7B tends to produce nearly complete networks, with density close to 1, whereas Llama-3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B show the highest variation across runs. Networks remain largely connected across configurations, 5although the proportion of nodes in the LCC decreases for some models with larger sets of candidates. On average, these changes move the structural properties closer to the empirical ranges, especially for c25 and c50, meaning that the number of candidates in the prompt strongly affects the structure of the generated networks. Based on these measures, 332 of the 1,000 reciprocal networks (33 .2%) fall within the empirical ranges for all five metrics. The proportion of plausible networks increases from 2% for c1 and 0% for c5 to 20% for c10, 64% for c25, and 80% for c50. In this evaluation, the average degree is the most restrictive criterion, with all generated networks whose average degree falls within the empirical range also falling within the ranges for the other four measures. Differences also emerge across models, with Qwen2.5 7B having the highest proportion of plausible networks (61%), followed by Llama-3.1 8B (40 .5%), Llama-3.1 70B (40%), and Qwen2.5 72B (24 .5%), with none of the Mistral 7B networks meeting the criteria. Figure 2b shows the results on attribute assortativity. Gender assortativity remains close to 0 in most models and list-based configurations, while race and ethnicity, edu-cation, and religion show increasing assortativity as the number of candidates in the prompt increases and the average degree decreases. By contrast, and consistent with previous work [24, 27], assortativity by political vote is considerably higher, though it varies widely across models and prompting configurations. Among the list-based methods, the two Llama models show high political assortativity, which increases with the number of candidates, while Qwen2.5 7B and 72B have lower but still high values. Mistral 7B instead shows lower political assortativity across most configurations, with values decreasing for larger candidate sets. Some models, such as Llama-3.1 8B and Qwen2.5 7B and 72B, are also sensitive to attribute order, showing higher vote assor-tativity when vote is presented first in the persona description (see triangle markers in Fig. 2b). 

2.2 Survey-based evaluation reveals model-specific errors in partisan exposure 

While political assortativity captures the tendency of generated ties to connect polit-ically similar individuals, the measure does not indicate whether these patterns reproduce the partisan environments reported by individuals. We therefore move from network-level sorting to ego-level exposure using the nomination networks (see Meth-ods), which include all outgoing friendship choices made by each persona without requiring that the friendship choice is reciprocated by the alter. This permits us to capture directional differences in friendship choices, including cases in which an ego nominates an alter who does not reciprocate the choice, which may be lost when considering only reciprocal ties. Specifically, we compare exposure to Democrats and Republicans in the generated networks with the corresponding ANES measures in the survey, as described in the Methods section. For each ego, we compute the propor-tions of ties to Republican and Democratic alters and bin these proportions into 5 categories to match the 1–5 Likert scale of the survey question “How many of your friends or family are Democrats/Republicans?”. # 3 Discussion 

We evaluated LLM-generated political networks through their structural properties, political assortativity, and the comparison between ego-level partisan exposure and survey-reported exposure. Our findings show that, even if LLMs can generate networks with plausible structural properties, these properties strongly depend on the model, parameters, and prompting techniques used for the tie formation, and do not follow a clear relationship with model size. When comparing generated partisan exposure with the ANES survey, we find that the partisan environments generated for individ-ual personas can differ from those reported by respondents. Specifically, some models generate stronger separation between Democratic and Republican egos than reported in the ANES survey, with higher same-party and lower cross-party exposure, while others mainly underestimate Republican exposure across ego groups. In this context, 11 increasing model size does not necessarily lead to generated partisan environments that are closer to the survey benchmark, with the larger models considered that generate stronger separation between Democratic and Republican egos. This is consistent with recent work showing that model scale can change the structural and relational patterns generated by LLMs without implying improvements in realism [27]. At the same time, our results show that similar levels of political assortativity do not necessarily imply that the partisan environments experienced by individuals are also reproduced cor-rectly. This is because while political assortativity summarizes the overall tendency of ties to connect politically similar individuals, it does not indicate how exposure is rep-resented across Democratic, Republican, and other egos and alters. As a consequence, similar levels of assortativity can correspond to different patterns of same-party and cross-party exposure, also among plausible reciprocal networks. Moreover, these errors are not distributed uniformly across the population, as specific demographic and polit-ical groups show consistent patterns of under- and overestimation in partisan exposure across models and configurations, extending previous concerns about the represen-tation of demographic personas in LLMs [7, 12, 18, 19, 43]. In this context, errors in individual tie formation can therefore accumulate into group-specific differences in partisan exposure, even when average network properties appear plausible. Therefore, aggregate measures can hide representational errors in who is exposed to whom, in which direction, and for which social groups, even when the generated network appears plausible from a structural perspective. These findings connect to concerns about the empirical validity of LLM-based social simulations, with previous studies that have shown that LLMs may reproduce aggregate distributions or collective outcomes while having different patterns at the individual or subgroup levels. For example, LLM-based populations can reproduce collective cooperation dynamics, while failing to capture individual heterogeneity and conditional behavior [40], while synthetic survey responses can approximate overall population averages, but with lower variation and distorted patterns across demo-graphic groups [11]. Moreover, this micro–macro mismatch has also been shown in multi-agent settings, where interactions among LLM agents can generate emergent collective patterns and population-level biases that differ from those observed at the level of individual agents [22, 23]. Although these studies focus on repeated interac-tions among agents, our results show that the difference between micro and macro outcomes can also emerge in a simpler tie formation setting, where plausible aggregate network properties can hide errors in the partisan environments generated at the ego level. This also supports recent calls for empirical benchmarking of LLM-based social simulations [16]. This distinction is also relevant for political networks, where the composition of individuals’ political environments and the political context can be associated with their political behavior [32, 33, 35, 38, 39]. Our results show that some models mis-represent these political environments. Some overestimate same-party exposure and underestimate cross-party exposure, generating stronger partisan segregation. Other models mainly underestimate exposure to Republicans, producing asymmetric errors. Therefore, errors in micro-level tie formation decisions can alter the political contexts 12 generated around personas, and may affect simulated social and political processes such as influence, persuasion, communication, or polarization. The structural properties of the generated networks also depend on how the tie formation task is implemented, with pairwise and list-based prompts producing differ-ent network structures. This is consistent with recent work showing that prompting architecture and methodological choices can affect the structural and assortative prop-erties of LLM-generated networks [24, 27]. Therefore, the ability of LLMs to generate realistic networks strongly depends on how the tie formation task is operationalized and should not be interpreted as a property of the models. Our work comes with limitations. First, the survey measures from the ANES 2020-2022 Social Media Study are self-reported answers of respondents’ exposure to Democratic and Republican friends or family and do not provide direct observations of complete social networks. However, they provide an empirical measure of respon-dents’ perceived partisan environments that can be used to compare the partisan exposure generated for the corresponding survey-based personas. Second, due to the computational cost of generating all possible ties across multiple models, runs, tem-peratures, prompting methods, and attribute orderings, we use a reduced sample of ANES respondents (i.e., N = 100). Although the sampling procedure preserves the main demographic and political composition of the survey, this step could reduce het-erogeneity in the sample. Third, the personas include a limited set of demographic and political attributes, while friendship formation depends on a wider range of individual characteristics, social contexts, and opportunities for interaction [28–31]. Fourth, our analysis considers a limited set of open-weight instruction-tuned models that can be deployed locally, including quantized versions of the larger models, which can affect research results in ways that are difficult to predict [44]. Therefore, the observed dif-ferences across model families and sizes may not generalize to proprietary models, base models, or other instruction-tuned architectures. Finally, the tie formation task represents friendship as a one-step decision, along with the undirected reciprocal net-works, and does not capture the interactive and dynamic processes through which social relationships emerge, persist, and change over time. Despite these limitations, our findings show that plausible aggregate outcomes in LLM-based social simulations can hide errors at the individual level. In our setting, plausible social network properties and similar levels of political assortativity can hide differences in the partisan environments generated around individuals and across social groups. This macro–micro mismatch highlights the importance of evaluating LLM-based social simulations at both the aggregate and individual levels, especially when micro-level decisions and interactions contribute to the collective outcomes being studied. 

# 4 Materials and Methods 

4.1 Data sources and sampling 

We use data from the American National Election Studies (ANES) 2020-2022 Social Media Study [42], which includes 5,750 respondents in the 2020 pre-election wave and 5,277 in the 2020 post-election wave. Our analysis focuses on respondents with valid 13 post-stratification weights and available information on demographic characteristics, political preferences, and self-reported social exposure. To characterize the personas used in the tie formation tasks we consider age, gender, race and ethnicity, educa-tion, region, religion, and vote choice. The survey also captures the composition of the respondents’ social environments, including the number of friends or family members identified as Democrats or Republicans on a Likert scale from 1 (none of them) to 5 (all of them), which we use as empirical benchmark for ego-level partisan exposure. From the survey, we keep respondents with valid post-stratification weights, valid survey-based partisan exposure measures, and complete information for the demographic and political attributes used to construct the personas. Due to the computational costs of generating ties across all possible pairs of respondents and repeating the procedure across multiple models and prompt configurations, we construct a reduced sample of 100 respondents. To this end, we use weighted stratified sampling based on gender, age group, race and ethnicity, and education. For each joint stratum, we compute its share of the eligible ANES population using the survey post-stratification weights and allocate the target sample proportionally to this weighted share. Respondents are then sampled within each stratum with probability proportional to their post-stratification weight. We additionally compare the resulting sample with the weighted ANES pop-ulation across the stratification variables, region, religion, and 2020 presidential vote. The distributions remain close to the weighted survey population (SI, Table S1). 

4.2 Models 

To perform the tie formation tasks we use open-weight instruction-tuned LLMs that can be run locally on a single NVIDIA A40 GPU. Specifically, we use five models: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-AWQ4, and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct-AWQ4. For each model, we generate ties using two temperature values, 0 .1 and 0 .7. This local setup is important given the computational cost of the task. Generating a network requires evaluating potential ties among all sampled personas, and the procedure is repeated across models, temperatures, prompt formats, chunk sizes, and attribute orderings. For this reason, we use models that can be deployed on local GPU resources, including quantized versions of larger models when needed, such as Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-AWQ4, and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct-AWQ4. 

4.3 Persona construction and tie formation task 

For each sampled respondent, we construct a persona using age, gender, race and ethnicity, education, state of residence, religion, and vote choice in the 2020 U.S. presidential election (see Figure 1a). We use two attribute orderings to test whether the position of political information in the persona description affects tie formation (i.e., vote as first or last attribute in the description). We then ask the model to decide whether the persona would consider another persona, or list of personas, as friends. We use two prompting formats, as shown in Fig. 1b. In the pairwise configuration (c1), the model evaluates one potential alter at a time and returns a binary friendship decision. In the list-based format, the model evaluates multiple potential alters within 14 the same prompt. For the list-based task, we vary the number of candidates included in each prompt using sets of 5, 10, 25, and 50 candidates (c5, c10, c25, and c50). For each candidate set, the prompt presents the ego persona followed by the IDs and attributes of the candidate alters, and asks the model to return only the IDs of the individuals it would befriend. Each potential alter is therefore evaluated once for each ego in every generated network, while self-ties are excluded. For each generated run, we construct two network representations from these friendship decisions. First, we construct a nomination network, where a directed edge from the ego i to alter j is created when the model returns a positive friendship deci-sion. This network therefore includes all ego-to-alter nominations, representing the set of potential friends selected by each persona. However, because each ego makes friend-ship choices independently, the model might select alters who would not reciprocate the choice, producing unreciprocated nominations that reflect an artifact of the friend-ship decision [45]. Second, we construct a reciprocal network, where an undirected tie between two personas is created only when both nominate each other, capturing mutually selected friendships and providing a more direct comparison with empirical friendship networks. In the study, we primarily use the nomination networks to mea-sure ego-level partisan exposure, while reciprocal networks are used for the structural evaluation. Each configuration is repeated across 10 runs, changing the random seed to account for variation in the model outputs. This results in 1 , 000 generated runs across the five models, five prompting methods, two temperatures, two attribute orderings, and ten repetitions. 

4.4 Network measures and structural plausibility 

We evaluate the structural properties of the generated networks using reciprocal friendship ties, creating an undirected tie between two personas when both nominate each other, which allows us to compare the generated networks with empirical friend-ship networks. We characterize the generated networks using five structural measures: average degree, average clustering coefficient, the proportion of nodes in the largest connected component (LCC), average shortest path length, and Louvain modularity. The average clustering coefficient is computed across all nodes. The average shortest-path length is computed on the largest connected component, normalized by its size. Finally, we identify communities using the Louvain algorithm and compute the corresponding modularity. To evaluate the structural plausibility of the generated networks, we compare these measures with a reference set of 57 friendship and friendship-like networks from 13 empirical datasets. These include Baerveldt Dutch Social Behavior [46], Gales-burg [47], Hi-tech [48], High School 2013 [49], Kapferer Tailor Shop [50], Knecht class 12b [51], Lazega Lawyers [52], MSW Students [53], Moreno High School [54], PEAS [55], Prison [56], Sciences Po [57], and Zachary Karate [58] (see SI, Table S6). We use these empirical networks to define reference ranges for each structural mea-sure. When a dataset includes multiple networks, we assign equal total weight to each dataset and divide this weight across the networks that belong to it. We then use the 15 weighted 2 .5th and 97 .5th percentiles of each structural measure to define the empir-ical reference ranges. The resulting empirical ranges are reported in SI, Table S7. A generated network is classified as structurally plausible when all five measures fall within the corresponding ranges. Density is not included in this classification because it depends directly on network size. We also measure assortative mixing by gender, race and ethnicity, education, reli-gion, and vote choice in the 2020 U.S. presidential election by computing the attribute assortativity for each attribute. Positive values indicate that connected personas tend to share the same attribute more frequently than expected from the corresponding mixing pattern, while values around zero indicate limited assortative mixing. 

4.5 Validation of the partisan exposure using the ANES survey 

To evaluate how the LLM-generated networks capture individual-level partisan expo-sure, we compared ego-level metrics from the simulated networks with responses to the ANES 2020-2022 Social Media Study survey question: “How many of your friends or family are Democrats/Republicans?”, which uses a Likert scale ranging from 1 ( None of them ) to 5 ( All of them ). Our primary measure of generated exposure uses the friendship nominations made by each ego. For each ego, we compute the proportion of outgoing ties directed toward Democratic and Republican alters. The denominator includes all outgoing alters with a valid political affiliation, including those classified as neither Democratic nor Repub-lican. For egos with no valid outgoing ties, exposure is set to zero. To compare the generated exposure with the survey, we transform these proportions into five equally spaced categories. Exposure between 0 and 0.2 is assigned to category 1, between 0.2 and 0.4 to category 2, between 0.4 and 0.6 to category 3, between 0.6 and 0.8 to cat-egory 4, and between 0.8 and 1 to category 5. We then compare, for each ego, the generated exposure category with the corresponding survey response.
