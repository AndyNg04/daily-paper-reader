## ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for multi-turn agentic tasks that require interactions with users and environments over extended sequences of actions. A common approach to improving LLM agents is to use more capable ex-pert models to provide training supervision, either by behavior cloning the expert’s trajectories or by using the expert to correct the actions in trajectories generated by the weaker student model. However, expert LLMs can provide richer supervi-sion than actions alone: they can directly generate textual critique that explain why 

specific actions are incorrect. We study whether training on such feedback bene-fits LLM agents and propose Critique-Augmented Agentic Fine-Tuning (CAFT), a framework that elicits textual expert feedback on student-generated trajectories. For each incorrect action, the expert yields both a corrected action and a textual critique explaining the student’s mistake—which we then use as the training target for fine-tuning. Across four student models from the Nemotron 3 and Gemma 4 families on the agentic domains τ 2-Bench Airline+Retail and WebShop, adding critiques to action-level supervision improves performance by 8% on average over action-only distillation. Additionally, CAFT checkpoints can also provide a better warm start for reinforcement learning than expert-trajectory distillation. Our re-sults demonstrate that textual critiques can strengthen supervision for multi-turn LLM agents. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as agents in multiturn tool-using settings that require interacting with users and environments over extended sequences of decisions (Moham-madi et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Winata et al., 2026). Success in these settings requires correctly executing a sequence of interdependent actions. Importantly, agent actions can be incorrect for many different reasons; such as violating a domain policy, issuing an invalid tool call or acting before having collected all the information it needs. Therefore, training reliable multi-turn agents requires supervision that steers them away from this diverse set of failures. When a stronger expert LLM is available, the simplest way to improve a smaller student agent is behavior cloning the expert’s successful trajectories (Chen et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025b). However, expert trajectories primarily demonstrate how the expert succeeds and provide lit-tle direct supervision on the mistakes that a weaker student itself tends to make. DAgger-style (Ross et al., 2011) methods thus instead obtain expert supervision on states the student visits. Recent work has adapted this idea directly to long-horizon LLM agents by relabeling learner-relevant states with expert actions (Li et al., 2026), or by providing dense token-level teacher supervision on sequences generated by the student itself (Agarwal et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2026). While these approaches supervise what action the expert would execute, they do not provide super-vision on why the student’s original action is incorrect. Yet as the expert is itself an LLM, it can 

directly make this explanation explicit by identifying the specific policy, information requirement, or tool-use constraint violated by the student’s action. This capability makes it possible to construct

Does training an LLM agent on the expert’s critique of its incorrect actions im-prove the policy beyond training on the corrected expert action alone? 

To study this question, we introduce Critique-Augmented Agentic Fine-Tuning (CAFT). CAFT first rolls out the student policy to collect the states and mistakes it encounters under its own trajectory distribution. A privileged expert model then reviews these trajectories, identifies incorrect student actions, and provides both a corrected action and a natural-language critique explaining what was wrong with the student’s original action. CAFT then creates a fine-tuning dataset that keeps each er-roneous action in context and trains the student to produce both the critique and the counterfactually corrected action generated by the expert. We evaluate CAFT on two benchmarks: WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) and the Airline and Retail domains of τ 2-Bench (Barres et al., 2026). Across four student models from the Nemotron 3 and Gemma 4 families (Blakeman et al., 2025; Abd et al., 2026), CAFT consistently improves over action-only supervision on student trajectories by 8% on average and by up to 27 % on WebShop with Nemotron 3 Nano 4B. We further find in a single-model case study that CAFT checkpoints provide a stronger initialization for reinforcement learning (RL) than either the base model or standard expert-model distillation. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce Critique-Augmented Agentic Fine-Tuning (CAFT), which trains LLM agents on their own trajectories annotated with expert critiques explaining why a mistake occurred in addition to the corrected actions. • We show that CAFT consistently improves over training on corrected actions alone across four student models and two agentic environments. Furthermore, targeted ablations show that the textual critiques provide a stronger learning signal than the corrected actions alone. • We demonstrate the importance of privileged information in generating high quality cri-tique generation, compare CAFT with expert-trajectory distillation and repeated fine-tuning, and evaluate it as an initialization for RL. 2Preprint

## 2 TRAINING LLM A GENTS ON TEXTUAL CRITIQUES 

To provide agents with explicit supervision on why their actions are incorrect, CAFT fine-tunes a student policy π0 using expert critiques constructed from the student’s own trajectories (refer to Figure 1 for an overview). We first roll out with π0 on a collection of multi-turn agentic tasks, and then use an expert C to evaluate each agent turn. For turns judged incorrect, the expert provides both a natural-language critique and the corrected action it would have taken instead. These annotations are converted into supervised examples used to fine-tune a policy πθ from π0. In our experiments, 

C is an LLM with access to privileged task information, such as reference actions and evaluation criteria, which grounds its judgments in task requirements unavailable to the student. The complete procedure for constructing this training dataset is outlined in Algorithm 1. 

Multi-turn agent setting. To formally represent the multi-turn agentic setting that our method focuses on, we define the agent interaction as a sequential decision making process. Let X denote a collection of task instances and let x ∼ pX denote a sampled task. Given x, the environment E

emits an initial agent-visible observation o1 describing the task. At agent turn t, the policy generates an action based on the previous interaction history 

at ∼ π(· | ht), where ht = ( o1, a 1, . . . , o t),

after which the environment E produces the next observation ot+1 . Here, an action at is the agent output at turn t (e.g., a tool call or a user-facing message), while ot is the information returned to the agent (e.g., a tool call result or a user reply). A rollout therefore produces a trajectory 

τ = ( o1, a 1, o 2, a 2, . . . , o T , a T , o T +1 ).

We denote by pπ, E (τ | x) the trajectory distribution induced by policy π and environment E on task x. Each task is evaluated by a binary correctness grader R(x, τ ) ∈ { 0, 1}, where R(x, τ ) = 1 

indicates successful completion. The agent’s objective is to maximize expected task success, 

J(π) = Ex∼pX Eτ ∼pπ, E (·| x) [R(x, τ )] .

CAFT aims to improve J by constructing supervised training data from trajectories sampled from the base policy π0.

Environments and evaluation. We evaluate on the Airline and Retail domains of τ 2-Bench ( τ 2

AR) (Barres et al., 2026), and WebShop (Yao et al., 2022). τ 2 AR evaluates conversational agents that must interact with a user and external tools while following domain-specific policies, on tasks such as booking a flight or modifying an online order. WebShop evaluates agents that navigate a simulated online store to find and purchase an item satisfying a natural-language request. For τ 2

AR, we use the benchmark’s outcome-based evaluator, and for WebShop, we report the fraction of episodes that purchase an item satisfying all the request’s constraints. For both datasets, we collect 

16 rollouts per task. Full details are in Appendix A. 

Models and data collection. We evaluate four student policies: NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Nano 4B, Gemma 4 E2B, Gemma 4 E4B and Gemma 4 31B (Blakeman et al., 2025; Abd et al., 2026). We use Gemma 4 31B as the expert model for generating turn-level correctness judgments, critiques, and corrected actions. When Gemma 4 31B is also the student, the expert critiques its own trajec-tories, so this setting tests whether a model can improve from self -critique given privileged task information. We partition both environments into training and evaluation task splits. Then, for each student–environment pair we roll out the base policy π0 over the training tasks with 16 rollouts per task and have the expert annotate every agent turn. Complete collection, dataset-construction, and optimization settings are given in Appendix C.

The second is privileged task information such as the gold reference actions, the user scenario and the evaluation criteria used to that the student never sees (Appendix B). To isolate the contribution of each, we construct an ablation on the three smaller student models that independently varies whether the expert receives a detailed instruction or privileged task in-formation. We re-annotate the student trajectories with the same expert under each condition and rerun CAFT. Table 2 shows that privileged information is the dominant factor. Supervised fine-tuning is commonly used to warm start reinforcement learning (RL), placing the policy in a higher-reward region to improve sample efficiency (Ouyang et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2025). Canonically, warm-starting is achieved by distilling trajectories from a stronger model. We thus ask if CAFT yields a better initialization than expert distillation. As an initial investigation, we use Nemotron 3 Nano 4B on τ 2 AR as a case study. We run 300 steps of GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) on the τ 2 AR training split, varying only the initialization: (i) the base model (ii) the expert distillation checkpoint and (iii) the CAFT checkpoint. All three runs share the same GRPO hyperparameters, with full details in Appendix D. As shown in Figure 2, CAFT outperforms both alternatives by around 5% on the held-out tasks. Notably, while the expert distillation warm-start achieves a higher training reward than the base model, it does not transfer— with its validation performance collapsing to the base model. Since both warm starts are trained on the same training tasks that RL later optimizes, we hypothesize that behavior cloning on the expert’s solutions to these tasks instills task specific patterns, which RL then amplifies. This then raises training reward without improving generalization. Meanwhile, CAFT’s supervision instead targets the student’s own mistakes, which appears to transfer better to unseen tasks. Lastly, we note CAFT also makes RL more sample-efficient: the CAFT initialized run matches the base-initialized run’s best held-out success after about 60 of 300 steps. 

## 4 RELATED WORK 

Expert supervision for training LLM agents. A common approach to train LLM agents is to imitate the behavior from the trajectories generated by stronger models (Zeng et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; 2025b). However at inference time, a weaker student may encounter states absent from the expert data. DAgger addresses this problem by obtaining expert labels on states induced by the student policy (Ross et al., 2011). Analogously, on-policy distillation trains on student-generated sequences with dense token-level supervision from a teacher model (Agarwal et al., 2024). Recent work has extended these ideas to long-horizon LLM agents (Li et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2026) and to 

action-level supervision on student-visited states, where a privileged teacher rewrites the student’s erroneous step (Choudhury & Sodhi, 2025; Lyu et al., 2026), or a teacher continues from a student prefix for one (Sun et al., 2026) or a few (Ye et al., 2026) steps. However, all the existing approaches directly drop the student’s mistakes from the training context by replacing them with the teacher’s action. Unlike the previous methods, CAFT shares the goal of supervising the states the student itself visits (our Action Distillation baseline, Section 3.1, instantiates this form of supervision), but keeps the mistake in context so that we can train on the expert’s critique of it. 8Preprint 

Training from natural language critiques. Several works have explored using critiques as super-vision for language models. In single-turn reasoning, Critique Fine-Tuning (CFT) trains models to critique noisy responses rather than imitate correct solutions (Wang et al., 2025). LEMA fine-tunes on an explanation of an incorrect solution followed by its correction (An et al., 2023) and Ye et al. (2025) show how error-correction data can be used for mathematical pretraining. Critique-Guided Distillation (CGD) instead trains the student to refine its own response given a teacher’s critique (Ka-pusuzoglu et al., 2026). Related reinforcement-learning approaches likewise use natural-language critiques to guide policy improvement (Zhang et al., 2026b; Lin et al., 2026). For agents, Early Experience trains a policy to write its own reflection on why an expert action beats an alternative at expert-visited states (Zhang et al., 2026a), and CAST conditions a tool-calling policy on a sepa-rate critic’s verdicts (Saeidi et al., 2026). CAFT builds on these works by asking whether training a multi-turn agent to explain its own mistakes improves its decisions beyond learning the expert’s corrected actions alone. 

Reflection and error recovery in LLM agents. A complementary line of work uses natural lan-guage feedback to enable LLM agents to reflect and self-correct. Reflexion uses verbal feedback stored in an episodic memory to improve subsequent attempts without updating model parameters (Shinn et al., 2023). Critique-Guided Improvement (CGI) trains a separate critic to generate ac-tionable natural-language feedback and an actor to revise its candidate action conditioned on the critic’s generated feedback (Yang et al., 2025). Other work directly trains agents to exhibit reflec-tion or error-recovery behavior. Agent-R constructs revision trajectories by joining erroneous and successful branches discovered through Monte Carlo tree search, then trains agents to recover from mistakes (Yuan et al., 2025). Agentic Critical Training instead rewards an agent for judging which of two candidate actions is better (Liu et al., 2026), and STeP trains on teacher-generated self-reflected trajectories containing both erroneous behavior and its correction (Chen et al., 2025a). CAFT like-wise trains on critiques and corrections, but its correction specifies what the agent should have done in place of the mistake, rather than how to proceed afterward. Additionally, we explicitly isolate the supervision value of the critique itself, asking if it can better improves the agent’s task performance. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

This work introduces CAFT, a critique-augmented SFT method for multiturn LLM agents that fine-tunes the student on textual critiques and corrected actions generated by an expert over the stu-dent’s own trajectories. We find that critiques carry learning signal beyond the corrected action with CAFT-Critique outperforming CAFT-Correction and action-only distillation across all tested settings. Training an agent to explain its mistakes thus improves its ability to perform the correct actions. More broadly, CAFT exploits the fact that expert and student share a natural-language inter-face: critiques are a cheap and rich form of feedback that can be incorporated directly into training. We see them as one more axis along which LLM agents can be improved. Several interesting directions follow from this work. Our Gemma 4 31B results suggest that a model given privileged information can improve from critiques of its own trajectories. Extending this self-critique setting to more models and domains is a natural next step. Because the critique target is natural language, CAFT could also consume feedback from sources other than an expert LLM, most notably direct user feedback at deployment, which suggests online variants that train on corrections as they arrive. Additionally, while our experiments with CAFT+BC show promise in combining critiques with expert demonstrations, identifying the precise conditions under which these mixtures succeed and exploring integrations with other supervision methods remain important areas for future study. Finally, scaling to larger students and broader agentic domains, and developing a theoretical account of why critique targets help, remain open.

## A ENVIRONMENTS , T ASK SPLITS , AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

τ 2-Bench Airline and Retail. τ 2-Bench (Barres et al., 2026) evaluates conversational agents that interact with a simulated user while operating a domain database through tool calls and obeying a written domain policy. We use the Airline and Retail domains, which we report jointly as τ 2 AR. The agent observes the domain policy and tool schema in its system prompt, and interacts with a user simulator. Each task terminates when the agent ends the conversation or the turn budget is exhausted ( 40 agent turns). The benchmark uses a binary outcome grader, which checks the final database state against the task’s gold reference actions and evaluates any required user-facing information with a natural-language judge. In our experiments, both the user simulator and the judge are Gemma 4 31B. We hold out 60 tasks spanning both domains for evaluation and the remaining tasks form the collection pool. The data split follows the train and evaluation split specified in the official code release of τ 2-bench (originally intended for people running RL training experiments). Each evaluation task is rolled out 16 times and we report mean task success. 

WebShop. WebShop (Yao et al., 2022) places the agent in a simulated e-commerce site with a product catalog. Given a natural-language product request, the agent issues search queries, clicks results and option buttons, and finally purchases an item. Following the evaluation protocols of pre-vious papers—cf. the work of Chen et al. (2024), Wu et al. (2024) and Oh et al. We further use the second 100 goals to form the training pool. We run training and evaluation using the text rich observation mode. Each eval-uation goal is rolled out 16 times. E.1 HOW MUCH SHOULD CORRECT TURNS BE SUPERVISED ?Section 2 retains expert-approved turns as ordinary behavior-cloning targets, weighted by a scalar 

λ relative to the corrective turns (we use λ = 0 .1 throughout, originally chosen from a sweep on a small training subset). In this section we evaluate a sweep of λ ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1, 0.5} on the τ 2 AR validation data for Gemma 4 E2B and Nemotron 3 Nano 4B. Table 6 shows that λ = 0 .1 is best for both students. Upweighting correct turns to λ = 0 .5 costs 

7.2% and 5.6% for Gemma 4 E2B and Nemotron 3 Nano 4B, respectively, and nearly removing them ( λ = 0 .01 ) costs 0.8% and 7.3%. We believe that at λ = 0 .5 the objective is dominated by imitating the student’s own executed actions, rather than on the critiques of incorect steps. However, removing the correct turns altogether is even more damaging. Although similarly the actual success rate with intervention drops – suggesting that while CAFT adapted to prevent the errors the expert critique does catch, the errors not caught by the expert become more fatal.
