# ABSTRACT 

Can an AI agent turn high-level user intent into a functioning robot and teach it how to behave? We study this question from two complementary aspects: embodiment and behavior creation. For embodiment creation, we investigate how well current multimodal models capture mechanical requirements, and in-troduce computational metrics for evaluating generated designs before fabrica-tion. For behavior creation, we present RoboForge , an agentic framework that turns task specifications into executable policies through motion generation, pol-icy learning, and execution-grounded revision. Given a robot embodiment and task, the agent generates a reference motion, configures the learning objective, and trains a policy. Crucially, motion remains in the loop: rollout feedback can revise the reference motion, reward, or both. We evaluate a generated embod-iment across multiple MLLMs and behavior creation across four diverse robots against reward-refinement and one-shot motion baselines. Our computational metrics expose recurring mechanical failures in generated designs, while behav-ior experiments show that execution-grounded refinement improves task-level behavior and execution quality. Finally, we fabricate an agent-generated Go-rilla and directly deploy learned behaviors on the physical robot. Project page:

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

“Design is not just what it looks like and feels like; design is how it works.” — Steve Jobs 

Small expressive robots such as Microduck and recent character robots (Grandia et al., 2025; M¨ uller et al., 2026) suggest a future in which people can personalize not only how a robot looks, but also how it moves and behaves. Yet creating such a robot today still demands substantial expertise. A high-level concept must be translated into a mechanical structure, suitable joints and actuators, a valid simulation model, desired motions, and ultimately a control policy capable of executing them. These stages typically require mechanical designers, animators, and control engineers to iterate together. With increasingly capable multimodal foundation models, we ask: can robot creation become an agentic process, where users describe what they want and agents carry out much of the engineering required to make it physically realizable? 

Recent work has begun to automate individual stages of this process. Foundation models have been used to generate robot morphologies and articulated designs (Ringel et al., 2025; Sontakke et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025). In parallel, LLMs can synthesize and iteratively refine rewards for reinforcement learning (Ma et al., 2024b; Lee et al., 2026), while MimicAgent generates reference motions for downstream policy learning (Nayak et al., 2026). Together, these advances suggest that much of the robot-creation pipeline may become amenable to agentic automation. Connecting these pieces, however, reveals a fundamental gap: a robot that looks plausible is not necessarily buildable, and a motion that looks plausible is not necessarily executable .We study this gap from two aspects of robot creation. For embodiment creation , rather than in-troducing another morphology generator, we ask how well current multimodal large language mod-els (MLLMs) understand the mechanical requirements that matter before fabrication. A visually convincing CAD model may still contain disconnected structures, insufficient actuator clearance, self-collisions, or unsuitable contact geometry. We therefore identify a set of mechanically relevant 1arXiv:2610.17881v1 [cs.RO] 13 Oct 2026 requirements and introduce multi-faceted computational metrics to quantify them before fabrica-tion. Using these metrics, we systematically compare different MLLMs, characterize the mechanical knowledge they already possess, and expose recurring failure modes in their generated designs. Im-portantly, we have fabricated an agent-generated robot Gorilla and demonstrate that designs passing these checks can be assembled and operated as physical systems. For behavior creation , physical feasibility is harder to assess from generation alone. A motion may appear natural but remain incompatible with a particular embodiment because of its timing, pose, contact sequence or joint excursion. Such incompatibilities often become apparent only when a pol-icy attempts to execute the motion. This observation motivates RoboForge , an agentic framework for turning desired behaviors into executable robot policies through generation, physical execution, and revision . Given a robot embodiment and a task description, the agent generates a reference mo-tion and a learning objective, trains a policy, and inspects the resulting rollouts. Execution therefore serves as a physical verification signal rather than merely the endpoint of learning. When the policy fails, the agent reasons about whether the failure stems from what behavior is being requested —such as motion timing, target pose, contact sequence, or trajectory—or from how the behavior is being learned —such as reward terms and their relative weights. It then revises the corresponding component and retrains the policy. This closes a motion–policy feedback loop in which learning outcomes expose deficiencies in generated motions, while generated motions continue to provide structured guidance for learning. We evaluate behavior creation across four diverse embodiments, including existing platforms such as Unitree Go2 and Microduck as well as agent-generated robots, and compare RoboForge with representative reward-refinement and one-shot motion-generation approaches. RoboForge leads to policies that are better aligned with the intended task, as evidenced by both user preference studies and complementary quantitative metrics. Besides, we analyze the intermediate failures and revisions throughout the process to understand how execution feedback drives improvement. We further de-ploy the resulting policies on the physical robot we fabricate, demonstrating behaviors that transfer from high-level user intent to real-world execution. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Automated Robot Generation. Automating robot design has progressed from morphology search and modular optimization toward agent-driven generation. Prior methods translate high-level spec-ifications into robot morphologies through language-guided search or generative design (Ma et al., 2024a; Ringel et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025; Qiao et al., 2025). Recent methods further gener-ate articulated robot and object models from text, images, or their combinations (Chen et al., 2026; Sontakke et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025), while morphology–control co-design couples embodiment optimization with downstream control performance (Qiu et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025). 

Policy Learning Guided by Reward and Motion. Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are increasingly used to reduce manual specification in robot learning. One line of work synthesizes reward functions from textual task descriptions (Kwon et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2024b). Subsequent methods refine rewards using human feedback, training statistics, or execution feedback (Hazra et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026). A complementary line uses reference motion to specify desired behavior. Classical motion-imitation and motion-prior methods enable complex locomotion and whole-body control (Peng et al., 2018; 2021; Escontrela et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022), and recent systems scale this paradigm to large motion datasets and expressive humanoid skills (Luo et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2026). More recently, MimicAgent uses MLLMs to generate task-specific reference motions for downstream RL (Nayak et al., 2026), but without policy feedback. Existing approaches typically improve either the learning objective or the reference behavior in iso-lation: reward-generation methods refine how a policy is optimized, while motion-generation meth-ods refine what behavior the policy is asked to reproduce. In contrast, our framework uses policy rollouts to diagnose whether failure originates from the reference motion, the reward specification, or both, and revises the corresponding component accordingly. This creates a closed motion–policy loop in which execution feedback jointly shapes the behavioral target and its optimization guidance. 23 EMBODIMENT CREATION 

Given a reference image and a set of task descriptions, we use GPT-6 OpenAI (2026) to generate a robot embodiment. The reference image specifies the desired coarse morphology, while the task descriptions provide functional cues about the motions the robot should support. We additionally provide CAD assets and specifications for candidate actuators, together with official assembly exam-ples. In practice, we find that such assembly examples are important: they help the model infer how motors, links, fasteners, and supporting structures should be connected, rather than merely placing components in visually plausible configurations. The generated embodiment D contains three con-sistent representations: (1) editable mechanical parts in STEP format for inspection and assembly, (2) an STL representation for fabrication, and (3) a URDF model for simulation and control. 3.1 PRE -F ABRICATION FEASIBILITY METRICS 

Fabricating every MLLM-generated design is prohibitively expensive and provides feedback only after substantial time and material have been invested. We therefore introduce a set of computational checks that evaluate whether a generated embodiment is likely to be manufacturable, assemblable, and capable of supporting the intended behaviors before fabrication. We group the checks into three categories according to the type of evidence they require: 

1) Design and Assembly checks whether parts can be manufactured and fitted together. It verifies valid single-solid CAD parts of positive volume, watertight meshes positioned on the print bed, and matching volumes in source and exported models. It checks whether each part fits the printer’s build volume in its printing orientation. At mating interfaces, it examines mounting holes, tool openings, and support material; worst-case tolerance checks verify that clearance remains under dimensional variation. Assembly checks also examine insertion and tool-access paths, all fastener interfaces and traceable purchased-part specifications, and unwanted intersections in the complete assembly, including fasteners and cable envelopes. 

2) Mechanical and Actuation Feasibility assess mechanical margins under the frozen mate-rial, load, and routing conventions. Local structural screening compares specified sections under recorded loads with an assumed allowable stress; load-path checks cover load-bearing parts and connections under defined load cases. Mass checks reconcile material estimates, link masses, and CAD and simulation totals. Fastener length chains compare selected screws with measured grip and nominal interface depths. Trajectory-limit margins check actual joint angles against existing limits; cable checks examine full-range clearance, bending, and twisting of the fixed routing candidate. Structural outcomes are conditional calculations, not measured strength; routing failures concern this candidate alone. 

3) Motion and Task Execution evaluate whether the complete embodiment can realize the behav-iors specified by the user. For each requested task, we construct a task-relevant reference motion by the agent and evaluate the robot along the corresponding trajectory. Checks include consistency of the simulated joint and inertial model, collision-free motion in the original CAD geometry, track-ing error in simulation, actuator torque and saturation, joint-limit violations, and clearance margins under tracking error and fabrication tolerance. Appendix I.2 provides each check’s criteria and implementation details. 3.2 EVALUATION

# 4 ROBO FORGE 

We propose RoboForge (Figure 2), an agentic framework that learns executable robot behaviors from a given embodiment D and task specification T . RoboForge generates a reference motion M ,configures a task-specific reward r, and trains a policy π, then uses policy execution to refine M , r,or both while keeping D and T fixed. 4.1 REFERENCE MOTION GENERATION AND POLICY LEARNING 

Given the robot model D and task specification T , the agent instantiates the requested behavior through a robot-specific procedural motion specification. Rather than directly predicting joint trajec-tories, the agent specifies a root-pose trajectory B(t), end-effector targets E(t), and explicit trajec-tories qK(t) for selected task-relevant joints K. Together, these components specify the whole-body motion, limb targets, and task-specific joint behavior. Given a robot embodiment and task, the agent generates and validates a reference motion, configures the learning objective, and trains a policy. Execution feedback then refines the motion, reward, or both while keeping the embodiment and task fixed. Using T and M , the agent configures an initial reward r from a DeepMimic(Peng et al., 2018)-style reference-tracking library of composable tracking terms, for example, joint-pose, joint-velocity, and body-motion tracking terms, and trains a policy π through reinforcement learning. Here, M specifies a concrete realization of the behavior, while r provides the optimization guidance for learning it. Together, they form the initial learning guidance, whose effectiveness is subsequently evaluated through policy rollouts. 4.2 EXECUTION -G ROUNDED MOTION AND REWARD REFINEMENT 

A semantically valid and kinematically feasible reference does not necessarily provide effective learning guidance, as valid realizations can differ substantially in posture, timing, support, whole-body coordination, and learnability. We therefore evaluate policies through simulation rollouts for task-semantic compliance and execution quality, including motion quality, support, and balance. We denote the initial motion, reward, and policy by M0 = M , r0 = r, and π0 = π. At refinement round k, Mk and rk are used to train πk. If the trial budget is exhausted, πk is returned without further review; otherwise, its rollout is evaluated against the same criteria. If successful, πk is returned; otherwise, the agent revises Mk, rk, or both to obtain (Mk+1 , r k+1 ).4.3 LEARNABILITY -A WARE EXECUTION DIAGNOSIS AND REVISION 

For each rollout that enters refinement, the agent localizes the failure across four levels: the in-tended task, the reference motion Mk and body trajectory, the policy command qcmd (t), and the realized motion qactual (t) together with contact states. This helps distinguish limitations of the current motion realization from failures introduced during policy learning and execution. Tracking discrepancies are treated as diagnostic evidence rather than the evaluation objective. The agent diagnoses whether the failure stems from Mk, considering motion structure, geometric and joint margins, timing, support transitions, and whole-body coordination, or from the optimiza-tion guidance in rk. Motion-related failures lead to revisions of posture, trajectory, timing, support coordination, or phase structure while preserving task semantics and successful behavior; reward-related failures lead to adding, removing, or reweighting reward terms. # 5 EXPERIMENTS 

This section addresses three central questions: Q1: Does execution-grounded motion-reward re-finement improve policy learning? Q2: What does keeping motion in the loop change? Q3: Can GPT recover the refinement strategy without explicit guidance? Together, these experi-ments evaluate the effectiveness of execution-grounded motion–reward refinement, examine how keeping motion in the loop allows execution feedback to reshape the concrete realization of a task toward more learnable behaviors, and test whether the GPT agent can independently recover and execute the proposed refinement strategy. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Tasks and robots. We evaluate four embodiments: Unitree Go2, MicroDuck, and GPT-generated Ostrich and Gorilla, with the Gorilla additionally fabricated and evaluated on hardware. We use five natural-language tasks for each of the first three robots and four for Gorilla, yielding 19 tasks in total. The tasks span locomotion, stylized behaviors, and whole-body skills with diverse coordination and contact patterns, while admitting multiple realizations consistent with the same task semantics. Robot and task details are provided in Appendix A and C, respectively. 

Baselines. We compare Eureka-style and One-shot Motion with RoboForge . Eureka-style, in-spired by Eureka (Ma et al., 2024b), refines rewards from policy execution without reference mo-tions. One-shot Motion follows a MimicAgent-style approach (Nayak et al., 2026), using the same validated motion-generation pipeline as RoboForge but fixing the reference once policy learning be-gins. RoboForge instead refines the motion, reward, or both from execution feedback, with at most three training trials per task. All methods share the same PPO architecture, simulator configuration, and GPT-6 Astra agent (OpenAI, 2026). Further details are provided in Appendix B and D. 

Metrics. We evaluate final policy behavior using metrics independent of each method’s reference motion and reward formulation, since neither reference-tracking errors nor optimized reward val-ues are directly comparable across methods. Task-level behavior is assessed through a visual user study , where raters score compliance with the natural-language task and execution quality. We also report four reference-independent quantitative metrics. For walking tasks, which require straight-line locomotion without a target speed, Yaw Err. measures directional compliance and Foot-slip 

characterizes contact stability. Across all tasks, Action Variation ( Action Var. ) and Joint Acc. mea-sure smoothness at the command and realized-motion levels, respectively. LEARNING ?Table 2 compares RoboForge with two complementary alternatives. Eureka-style retains reward re-finement in the execution loop but provides no explicit motion guidance; RoboForge improves the overall rating from 2.10 to 3.97, indicating the value of adding structured motion guidance. One-shot Motion provides such guidance but fixes the accepted reference once policy learning begins. Relative to this baseline, RoboForge improves the overall score from 3.60 to 3.97 and achieves the highest rating on three of four embodiments, with only a slight decrease on Ostrich. Since One-shot Motion corresponds to the first policy-training trial of our pipeline, this comparison shows the benefit of keeping the initially generated learning guidance revisable after observing policy execu-tion. Together, the two comparisons highlight complementary roles: motion provides an explicit behavioral realization, while execution-grounded refinement allows that realization and its learning objective to adapt when execution exposes their limitations. The user-study results are supported by the reference-independent quantitative metrics in Table 3, where our method performs best on all four measures. Lower Yaw Err. and Foot-slip indicate more accurate directional control and cleaner support contacts, while lower Action Var. and Joint Acc. indicate smoother commands and realized motion. These results show that the user-rated improvements are accompanied by measurable gains in execution quality. Figure 3 illustrates this process on the Gorilla handstand task. Eureka-style briefly lifts the rear legs but fails to complete the motion, while One-shot Motion rotates the lower legs outward and rapidly loses balance. Our method instead revises the realization by folding the lower legs inward before rotating the body upward, producing a more compact transition that completes the handstand. # 6 REAL -W ORLD DEMONSTRATION 

We validate RoboForge on hardware using the agent-generated 8-DoF Gorilla, which we 3D-print and assemble (Appendices A.3 and A.4). We directly deploy the simulation-trained walking and hand-waving policies: walking requires repeated hand–foot support transfer, while hand wav-ing maintains support through the remaining limbs (Figure 6). These demonstrations extend the behavior-learning results from simulation to physical execution on the fabricated robot. 

# 7 CONCLUSION 

We studied agentic robot creation from two complementary aspects: embodiment and behavior creation. For embodiment creation, our computational metrics expose mechanically relevant failures in MLLM-generated designs before fabrication. For behavior creation, we presented RoboForge ,which learns executable policies through motion generation and execution-grounded refinement. Across four embodiments, RoboForge improves task-level behavior and execution quality, while direct deployment on the fabricated Gorilla demonstrates learned behaviors on hardware. Together, these results highlight the value of physical verification for generated embodiments and adaptive motion guidance for behavior learning. 9AI U SE STATEMENT

Ostrich has 15 actuated degrees of freedom, each driven by a single motor. Its kinematic tree consists of 18 links connected by 17 joints, including 15 revolute joints and two fixed joints. The torso (base link ) forms the root, from which four branches extend to the neck and head, the left leg, the right leg, and the tail. 

Neck and head: six motors. The neck and head form a six-DoF serial chain. Five pitch joints connect the torso to four successive neck links and then to the head mount, followed by a yaw joint connecting the head mount to the head. This branch therefore contains six links beyond the torso: four neck links, one head mount, and one head link. 

Left and right legs: four motors each. Each leg branches from the torso through four actuated joints: hip roll, hip pitch, knee pitch, and ankle pitch. The corresponding link chain consists of the hip-roll link, thigh, shin, ankle, and foot. The foot is rigidly attached to the ankle through a fixed joint and therefore introduces no additional actuated degree of freedom. Together, the two legs account for eight motors. 

Tail: one motor. A single pitch joint connects the torso directly to the tail link. Overall, the 15 motors are distributed as six across the neck and head, four on each leg, and one on the tail. A.3 GORILLA KINEMATIC TREE 

Gorilla has eight actuated degrees of freedom, each driven by a single motor. Its kinematic tree consists of nine links connected by eight revolute joints. The torso ( base ), which also incorporates 13 Figure 8: Fabricated Gorilla embodiment. the head, forms the root of four branches corresponding to the left arm, right arm, left leg, and right leg. 

Left and right arms: two motors each. Each arm contains two pitch joints. A shoulder-pitch joint connects the torso to the upper arm, followed by an elbow-pitch joint connecting the upper arm to the lower arm. The hand is integrated into the lower-arm link and therefore does not introduce a separate wrist joint or actuated degree of freedom. Each arm consequently contains two links and two actuated joints. 

Left and right legs: two motors each. Each leg similarly contains two pitch joints. A hip-pitch joint connects the torso to the upper leg, followed by a knee-pitch joint connecting the upper leg to the lower leg. We fabricated the agent-generated Gorilla from its verified CAD design. D.1 UNGUIDED GPT AND OUR MOTION –RL L OOP 

GPT and RoboForge use the same training budget of up to three trials per task. Each trial runs

GPT. The unguided GPT baseline is provided with the shared actor template, the robot and task specification, and general RL tools for constructing rewards, training policies, and evaluating execu-tion. It is not given a prescribed procedure for reference-motion generation, motion refinement, or learnability-aware diagnosis, nor is it provided with the reference-tracking guidance used by Robo-Forge. Within the same training budget, the agent determines how to construct the learning objective and organize its training process. No motion-based strategy is prohibited: the agent may generate, use, or revise a reference motion if it chooses to do so. This setting examines whether a general-purpose agent independently discovers and executes a motion-based refinement strategy from task execution feedback. 

RoboForge. RoboForge is provided with a DeepMimic-style reference-conditioned RL scaffold and explicit guidance for generating, validating, and iteratively refining the learning guidance. Be-fore policy training, the generated reference motion is checked for kinematic validity and task-semantic consistency. After training, policy execution is evaluated against the requested behavior and used to diagnose deficiencies in posture, timing, support transitions, and whole-body coordina-tion. The resulting feedback can revise the reference motion, the reward, or both, while the robot embodiment and task semantics remain fixed. This comparison therefore tests whether the execution-grounded motion–reward refinement strategy emerges autonomously from a general RL interface or benefits from being explicitly instantiated 18 as part of the agentic learning procedure. The full refinement procedure is described in the main Method section, while Appendix H analyzes the strategies observed in the unguided GPT baseline. D.2 MIMIC AGENT -STYLE ONE -SHOT MOTION 

One-shot Motion. Our One-shot Motion baseline follows the generate–validate–train structure of MimicAgent (Nayak et al., 2026). A reference motion is first generated and validated, and the resulting motion is then used to guide imitation-based reinforcement learning. MimicAgent itself al-lows trajectory generation to iterate in response to task-agnostic validity tests before policy training. Accordingly, “one-shot” refers to the absence of execution-driven motion refinement after policy learning begins, rather than to generating the reference motion only once; the motion itself may be iteratively revised during generation and validation. In our implementation, both the accepted reference motion and the initial reward remain fixed throughout policy training. To provide the one-shot baseline with the same initial learning guidance as RoboForge, we use the same reference-generation procedure, semantic checks, and initial reward. In addition to kine-matic and physical validity, the generated motion is checked against the requested task semantics, style, and required action phases before training. The two methods therefore begin from the same reference and reward; they differ in whether these quantities remain fixed after observing policy execution. Concretely, One-shot Motion is the terminal policy obtained from the first formal trial of RoboForge. The trial uses 4,096 parallel environments and a cumulative budget of at most 3,000 PPO iterations. Once this trial is complete, the reference motion and reward are fixed, and no execution-grounded revision is performed. Ours may instead use subsequent trials to revise the motion, reward, or both according to policy execution feedback. This comparison focuses on the effect of keeping the generated learning guidance open to revision after policy execution. Each refinement trial retrains the policy from scratch without reusing check-points from previous trials, so improvements across rounds cannot be attributed to warm-starting or accumulated policy optimization. One-shot Motion trains once with the accepted reference, whereas RoboForge retrains under the same initialization protocol after each revision. If RoboForge termi-nates after the first trial, the two methods coincide. D.3 EUREKA -STYLE REWARD REFINEMENT 

Eureka-style. Our Eureka-style baseline instantiates the reward-search and reward-reflection procedure of Eureka (Ma et al., 2024b) within our common RL interface. For each robot and task, the agent first constructs the task environment and an evaluator. Before reward search begins, the observation interface, action mapping, reset and termination rules, PPO configuration, and evalua-tion interface are fixed. Reward design is then the component revised throughout the search. The agent may modify reward terms, functional forms, weights, and temperature parameters, but does not use reference motions or framewise tracking objectives. This provides an iterative reward-only counterpart to RoboForge. We run two reward-search rounds with two candidates per round. Each candidate is trained from scratch using 4,096 parallel environments for 2,250 PPO iterations. The two candidates within a round are proposed before either training result is observed. After training, GPT receives the result-ing reward-component statistics and task-evaluation feedback, compares the candidates, and deter-mines how to construct the next round of reward programs. The second round therefore builds on the reward code and execution feedback from the first round, while policy parameters, optimizer state, and observation-normalization statistics are reinitialized for every candidate. Candidate comparison and reward revision are performed by the agent rather than by a hand-crafted selection rule. We adapt Eureka’s reward-refinement procedure to the same agent, simulator, and RL stack used by the other methods. Whereas the original Eureka experiments use GPT-4-0314, pre-existing bench-mark environments and fitness functions, IsaacGym, and substantially broader reward search, our implementation uses GPT-6 Astra, Isaac Lab, RSL-RL, and a two-round search with two candidates per round. The search breadth is chosen to match the formal training allocation used by GPT and RoboForge while preserving Eureka’s core loop of reward generation, policy training, execution feedback, and reward revision. 19 D.4 TRAINING BUDGETS AND FINAL -P OLICY EVALUATION

# E USER STUDY PROTOCOL 

Because the compared methods may construct different reference motions and learning objectives, method-specific quantities such as tracking errors or training rewards are not directly comparable across methods. We therefore evaluate the final learned policies under a common behavioral cri-terion: whether the executed behavior fulfills the natural-language instruction while maintaining satisfactory execution quality. The user study operationalizes this criterion through a shared rating rubric applied identically to all methods. E.1 PARTICIPANT INSTRUCTIONS AND RATING RUBRIC 

Before rating the policies, participants read a common set of instructions defining the evaluation criteria and the five-point rating scale. For each assigned task, participants are shown the natural-language task description together with four policy videos presented in randomized order. Each video is rated independently on an integer scale from 1 to 5; because the study evaluates each policy rather than requiring a ranking among methods, equal ratings are allowed. The rubric asks participants to jointly consider two aspects of the final behavior. First, task-semantic fulfillment measures whether the policy realizes the behavior specified by the instruction, including the requested action, style, and temporal phases when applicable. Because a task may admit multiple valid motion realizations, participants are instructed not to prefer one realization merely for appear-ing closer to a particular motion once the task requirements are satisfied. Second, execution quality 

captures properties of the realized motion, including smoothness, stability, coordination, recovery, and recognizable stepping when relevant. These aspects are combined into a single holistic rating: once task semantics are satisfied, differences in rating should primarily reflect execution quality rather than preference for a particular realization. Table 8 summarizes the five rating anchors. E.2 VIDEO PRESENTATION AND STUDY INTERFACE 

The study contains 76 policy videos, corresponding to four methods (Eureka-style, One-shot motion, GPT and RoboForge) evaluated on each of the 19 tasks. All videos depict executions of the learned policies and are presented at 1280 ×720 resolution and 30 frames per second. E.3 PARTICIPANTS , T ASK ASSIGNMENT , AND RESPONSE FILTERING 

The study includes 57 participants. Participants are assigned subsets of the 19 tasks, so the number of valid raters varies across tasks. Within each task, however, the same participants evaluate all four methods. This paired presentation keeps the rater population fixed within each method comparison and avoids confounding method differences with differences in task-specific rater composition. Incomplete or otherwise excluded questionnaires are omitted from the analysis. The questionnaire system additionally applies a three-second minimum completion-time filter and rejects submissions below this threshold.
