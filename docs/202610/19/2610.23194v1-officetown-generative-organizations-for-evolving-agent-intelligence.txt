## ABSTRACT 

AI agents increasingly use files and software tools to produce professional deliver-ables. Such work draws on materials, decisions, and feedback accumulated across assignments. We introduce OfficeTown, a generative simulation framework for professional organizations in which tasks and their context arise from continuing organizational work. From a natural-language description of an organization’s purpose and activities, OfficeTown initializes an organization whose members co-ordinate work, delegate bounded assignments to assistant agents, and review their deliverables. Artifacts, decisions, and unresolved matters persist and shape subse-quent work, while responsibilities and permissions determine what each member can see and do. We curate work requests, their context, and review feedback into executable resources for evaluation and learning. We construct OfficeTown Bench with 406 tasks and 4,836 evaluation criteria from 23 simulated organiza-tions across eight professional domains, incorporating domain-expert feedback. Frontier-model evaluations reveal substantial remaining headroom. Supervised fine-tuning and on-policy distillation on OfficeTown data substantially improve Qwen3.5-9B on external professional-work benchmarks, including GDPval. Sup-plementary evaluations show that the principal–assistant harness achieves higher scores than the single- and multi-agent baselines tested. These results highlight the value of organizational simulation in generating work-grounded data and en-vironments for the continued development of generalist agents.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models are increasingly expected to produce professional deliverables using files, software tools, and organizational knowledge. Recent benchmarks (Patwardhan et al., 2025) and environment-synthesis methods (Dong et al., 2026) provide valuable resources for evaluating these capabilities, but typically treat tasks as isolated instances. Real professional work is rarely isolated. In organizations, assignments arise from ongoing activity rather than appearing independently. De-liverables are reviewed and revised, decisions create follow-up requests, and materials accumulated in one project become inputs to later work. Tasks therefore emerge from a continuing process in which work products and work context co-evolve. This observation suggests a different perspective. Instead of generating standalone tasks, we may seek to model the system that continuously produces professional work. Such a system would naturally generate new assignments, contextual materials, review feedback, and long-range depen-dencies, offering a scalable source of both evaluation and training data. Professional organizations are precisely such systems. We therefore introduce OfficeTown, a gener-ative simulation framework for professional organizations. ## 2 OFFICE TOWN : A G ENERATIVE ORGANIZATIONAL SIMULATION 

## FRAMEWORK 

OfficeTown initializes an organization with roles, goals, resources, and authority, then simulates ongoing professional work progressing asynchronously (Figure 2). Requests, messages, commit-ments, and unresolved matters activate professionals, who coordinate work and delegate bounded assignments to assistants. They review delivered artifacts, request revisions, and arrange follow-up work. Files, communications, and decisions update persistent, permissioned organizational state throughout this process, shaping subsequent assignments. 2.1 ORGANIZATIONS , R OLES , AND AUTHORITY 

An OfficeTown instance represents a professional organization or team initialized from a natural-language description of its purpose and activities (Figure 2a). The description guides organizational objectives, professional roles, and reporting relationships. Each role defines continuing responsibil-ities and role-specific authority: which matters the professional handles and what they may execute, delegate, approve, or share. Figure 2d shows Juniper Financial Modelling, a twelve-role instance. Each role is represented by a model-driven professional whose profile draws on O*NET and ESCO occupational descriptions. Professionals act as principals responsible for ongoing work across as-signments; assistants perform delegated executions. The organization starts with documents and data, termed the seed. These combine retrieved or adapted public sources with organization-specific synthetic records, preserving source references and the distinction between these origins. The seed provides initial working context that subsequent work can extend or revise. Appendices A.1–A.2 detail construction, role skills, and Juniper’s setup. 2.2 PERSISTENT AND PERMISSIONED ORGANIZATIONAL STATE 

Organizational state includes personal working files, shared materials, communications, commit-ments, and pending decisions. Each professional maintains a persistent workspace (Figure 2e) and structured work records separate from active conversation context, so refreshing a model session does not erase unresolved work. Each activation presents selected work records alongside current requests, prioritizing outstanding commitments and discussions awaiting a response. This state is persistent but not globally observable. Role permissions, ownership, sharing rules, and confidentiality determine each member’s information view; role-specific authority determines what they may do with it. Reporting relationships do not automatically grant file access. Assistants receive writable working files and permission-filtered, read-only shared references within the principal’s authorization, not access to other professionals’ private workspaces. Saving, accepting, and sharing a deliverable are distinct actions. Saving retains files, including drafts and pending revisions; professional review determines acceptance; explicit sharing makes an accepted deliverable available to authorized recipients. Thus, saved work need not be accepted or visible to others. Acceptance, version, classification, and recipient permissions are checked before broader publication. Appendices A.4–A.6 cover workspace checks, sessions, and recovery. 2.3 COORDINATION , D ELEGATION , AND REVIEW 

Work follows a shared simulated calendar, with professionals activated asynchronously by incom-ing requests, messages, meetings, follow-ups, or assistant deliveries (Figure 2b). The simulation controls activation and permitted actions; the professional’s model decides how to respond using current requests, commitments, and accessible materials. When responsibilities intersect, members exchange permitted materials, question assumptions, and seek colleagues’ input. Team leads can meet to clarify objectives, identify missing inputs, and allocate responsibilities; meetings are not required for every assignment. Agreed work is recorded as requests specifying the intended outcome, responsible person, supporting materials, and deadline. Appendix A.3 provides a recorded example. For delegated execution, the principal specifies the objective, available inputs, constraints, and ex-pected deliverable. The assistant works within the authorized workspace and can ask the principal to clarify requirements or consult colleagues. On delivery, the principal inspects the actual artifact and decides whether to accept it, request revisions, or seek further information. Review can change both deliverable requirements and the work that other members need to undertake. These reviews are organizational decisions within the simulated organization, not independent benchmark judgments. Revisions build on submitted artifacts and review comments. Accepted results can become inputs to authorized colleagues’ work, while unresolved questions remain available for later investigation. These updates link assignments through accumulated context, extending work beyond the initial seed. Appendix A.7 illustrates material accumulation over six simulated months. 2.4 FROM ONGOING WORK TO EXECUTABLE TASKS 

An executable task pairs a work request with materials available to its responsible professional when the work was assigned (Figure 2c). This task-time snapshot preserves both temporal and permission boundaries: personal files, authorized shared resources, and existing artifacts needed for revision may be included, but later solutions and evaluator-only information are excluded. Each attempt starts in a fresh workspace initialized from these fixed inputs, where the agent can inspect and edit files, execute commands, and use the provided interfaces. The continuing orga-nization is the source of the assignment; individual evaluation or learning attempts do not require replaying its entire history. Section 3 explains selection, expert-informed refinement, and packaging into benchmark and training resources.

3.1 BENCHMARK DESIGN 

OfficeTown Bench covers eight professional domains selected for economic relevance and digital executability, inspired by GDPval (Patwardhan et al., 2025). It contains 406 tasks and 4,836 evalu-ation criteria from 23 simulated organizations, involving delegating professionals with 139 distinct job titles. The benchmark includes 247 file-based tasks and 159 tasks involving service state. Fig-ure 3 and Table 1 report its domain coverage and input-material composition. Each sample combines a work request, an executable environment, and a task-specific rubric. En-vironments supply the files, tools, and authorized organizational state for the assignment; rubrics assess professional requirements but remain outside the agent’s starting context. Figure 4 illustrates a mechanical-design assignment. Given workpiece geometry, feature tables, de-sign assumptions, and engineering references, the agent must compare two locating-and-clamping approaches in an editable workbook with reproducible calculation code. The rubric assesses me-chanical plausibility, sensitivity to uncertain inputs, and agreement between the workbook and code. 3.2 CONSTRUCTION AND EXPERT REVIEW

Appendix B details construction, annotation instructions, and review coverage. 3.3 TRAINING DATA 

The SFT resource comprises 2,202 complete execution trajectories distilled from DeepSeek-V4-Flash, pairing task inputs with teacher reasoning, tool calls, observations, and final responses. We retain complete histories without truncation or filtering by task-success score. The OPD resource provides 828 task–environment pairs for the subsequent distillation stage. Each supplies a work request and its initial environment without a teacher response history, using a system message and tool interface aligned with SFT. Appendix E describes resource construction; Section 4 reports training and evaluation results.

To test learning value beyond the source setting, we fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B on DeepSeek-V4-Flash demonstrations, then continue with OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024) in OfficeTown environments using Qwen3.8-Flash-Next as teacher. Table 3 compares Base, SFT, and SFT + OPD. External bench-marks use their respective metrics; GDPval and Terminal-Bench exclude search-dependent tasks (Appendix E.3). On 79 shared GDPval tasks, the rubric score rises from 65.75 to 70.30 after SFT and to 73.65 after OPD, gaining 7.90 percentage points over Base. JobBench-Easy (Li et al., 2026b) and APEX-Agents (Vidgen et al., 2026) also improve across the reported stages. Together, the results support the learning value of OfficeTown-generated work beyond its source organizations. The corresponding OfficeTown Bench Weighted Scores are 26.20, 29.20, and 31.33 for Base, SFT, and SFT + OPD, respectively. Appendices E.4–E.5 report training dynamics and retrospective budget curves for these runs. 4.3 HARNESS EVALUATION 

This supplementary study tests whether the principal–assistant design for delegation, clarification, and review is useful beyond the simulated organizations. We compare OfficeTown Harness with Codex, MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024), and CAMEL (Li et al., 2023) using GPT-5.5 xhigh throughout on external tasks (Table 4). OfficeTown has the highest reported point estimate in all five columns, covering initial development and feedback-guided revision (PRDBench DEV/DEBUG; Fu et al., 2026), business rules (GDPevo; Zhou et al., 2026), terminal work (Merrill et al., 2026), and research code (Hua et al., 2025). Token use varies by benchmark. On research code, OfficeTown scores 46.40 versus MetaGPT’s 41.15 with 21.8% fewer reported tokens, while using 3.49 times Codex’s tokens. Terminal results use different budgets, and Codex’s GDPevo reference includes few-shot skills.

OfficeTown connects organizational simulation with executable environment generation. Earlier artifacts, decisions, and unresolved matters shape subsequent work, while roles, ownership, and sharing rules scope the information and authority available for each assignment. This persistent, permissioned process generates tasks, context, and feedback for evaluation and training. 

## 6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

OfficeTown models professional organizations as persistent, permissioned, and evolving work sys-tems. Tasks, contextual materials, and feedback arise from ongoing work, rather than being con-structed as isolated instances. Benchmark evaluations reveal a gap between partial fulfillment and complete professional delivery, while training results demonstrate the learning value of the generated work on external benchmarks. Looking ahead, we will extend these simulations to more professional domains and institutional settings, with richer interactions, longer-running workflows, and collaboration across organizations. We will also investigate how persistent history, scoped access, and review shape task generation 9and learning outcomes. Improving simulation fidelity and efficiency, and validating simulated work against real professional practice, will be important as these systems expand. 

Ethics Statement. OfficeTown uses referenced public sources, adapted materials, and synthetic records; it does not use private customer records. Domain experts contributed feedback on tasks and rubrics, and we report their contributions in aggregate. Occupational taxonomies and gener-ative models can encode social and professional biases, so simulated organizations are examples rather than authoritative representations of occupations, institutions, or demographic groups. Tool-using agents can create privacy, security, and operational risks. In OfficeTown, agents operate within permission-scoped workspaces and synthetic authority; they are not used for real-world financial, le-gal, clinical, employment, or external-release decisions. Releases should preserve source attribution and licensing, remove sensitive operational information, and document intended use and limitations. OfficeTown is the name of this research project, not a Microsoft Office product.

A.4 SESSIONS AND MEMORY 

Work records are stored independently of active conversation context. They retain commitments, ongoing discussions, unresolved decisions, and recent interactions with colleagues (Figure 9(b)). Commitments record what is owed and to whom; discussions identify who should respond next. Communication and work actions update these records through explicit requests and rule-based message cues. Clearing an incoming reminder does not mark the promised deliverable as com-plete. Simulated time is managed separately from wall-clock execution time. When adaptive pacing is enabled, OfficeTown slows or pauses the simulated clock as unfinished work accumulates, then resumes its advance as the backlog decreases. The clock is periodically saved together with the run’s original start and target times. A resumed run continues toward that target rather than beginning another full simulation period. Routine activations follow working hours and meeting schedules; designated interruptions can re-ceive earlier attention. Professionals act asynchronously within limits on concurrent model calls and assistant workers. Work awaiting capacity remains pending. Incoming messages remain in persistent inbox files, with acknowledgments tied to the exact source rows processed. Reading or queuing a message does not acknowledge it as processed. A partially processed batch retains acknowledgments even when rows finish out of order, leaving unprocessed rows available for later delivery. Capacity-deferred delegations retain their original identities and arguments when reconsidered for execution. Workspace recovery follows the checked-update boundary shown in Figure 10(b) and described in Section A.5. The runtime saves the preceding workspace state and journals an update before 19 committing changes. An interruption before commit leaves the persistent workspace unchanged; an interruption during commit can be recovered by restoring the saved files and classification metadata. The recovery is recorded explicitly. This mechanism applies to local workspace changes, not to reversing an email already sent or another external service action. The runtime also monitors its action bridge and model-session service and supports replacing failed services. When the simulation process remains alive, active sessions can be reopened using saved conversation records and retained session metadata. This reconstructs context for subsequent turns rather than resuming an interrupted model generation internally. Transient connection failures re-ceive bounded retries, and unsuccessful attempts remain recorded. Run-level monitoring can stop execution on detected violations or record them while allowing an endurance run to continue. The latter policy leaves operation-level authorization and validation checks in force. At shutdown, the runtime stops new activity, allows a bounded interval for ongoing work to settle, and reconciles assistant results with delivery records. Unfinished requests, failed attempts, and incomplete reviews remain in the saved state; reaching the target time does not mark them as finished. A.7 A S IX -M ONTH OFFICE SIMULATION

To ground tasks and evaluation criteria in professional practice, we engaged 71 domain experts across eight professional domains during benchmark construction. Task-specific expertise require-ments covered relevant specialization and occupational background, the ability to make independent professional judgments, and familiarity with native working materials and applicable standards. The construction-stage annotation records cover 1,051 candidate tasks and 32,274 rubric criteria. Among 1,011 recorded task-level familiarity assessments, 98.9% indicated that reviewers were familiar or very familiar with the assigned work. Experts assessed task realism and feasibility and examined rubric correctness, coverage, and importance, providing 3,842 criterion-level recommendations to edit, remove, merge, or split criteria. The annotations subsequently underwent a separate review pass, combining expert assessment with follow-up quality control and producing refinement feed-back traceable to individual tasks and criteria. The review instructions ask reviewers to assess task realism, objective clarity, input sufficiency, feasibility, deliverable clarity, permission boundaries, answer leakage, and unnecessary restrictions. Rubric review separately examines task alignment, professional correctness, assessability, clarity, redundancy, completeness, importance, evidence gaps, and valid alternative solutions. Reviewers record separate overall decisions for the task and rubric, with reasons and proposed re-visions. For individual criteria, they can retain the standard or recommend the revisions described above, identify missing requirements, and assign the importance levels introduced in Section 3.2. These decisions concern the evaluation instrument, not an agent’s answer. Expertise or evidence limitations are recorded rather than treated as approval. Critical (P0) failures make the work essentially invalid or unusable; Major (P1) failures substantially affect its intended outcome or professional usability. Minor (P2) deficiencies do not substantially un-dermine the core outcome; Optional (P3) criteria describe desirable refinements with limited impact on overall success. Concrete edits remain linked to the reviewed task and criterion identities. ## E TRAINING DATA AND MODEL ADAPTATION 

E.1 TRAINING RESOURCES 

The preparation cohort contains 6,172 cases, including 6,082 with completed text-consistency edit-ing records. Teacher execution covers 3,718 file-based tasks; service-dependent tasks lie outside this configuration rather than count as failed executions. These are preparation counts, not a census of raw Live activity. The SFT resource contains 2,202 complete trajectories distilled from DeepSeek-V4-Flash (Xu et al., 2026a). From 2,317 trajectories in the original SFT partition, we exclude 46 tool-serialization cases, 62 exceeding context capacity and seven parser round-trip cases, leaving 2,202. Trajectories are neither truncated nor filtered by task-success score. The resource is split into 1,982 training and 220 validation demonstrations. Two training and four validation padding rows contain no supervised tokens and are not counted as additional demonstrations. OPD provides 828 task–environment pairs with task requests and original initial environments, with-out teacher response histories. Its public system message, chat template and serialized tool defini-tions align with the SFT input format. The benchmark and training resources are not a single random three-way partition of the intermediate construction pool. 30 E.2 SFT AND ON-P OLICY DISTILLATION
