MLLM-based mobile agents are rapidly gaining autonomy in perception, planning, and closed-loop execution in dynamic mobile environments. However, this autonomy also broadens the attack surface and amplifies the risks of malicious misuse, including privacy leakage, device compromise, and large-scale phishing. Existing jailbreak evaluations leave three critical gaps for mobile agents: they focus on conversational attacks instead of mobile workflows, use static task sets with fixed difficulty, and rely narrowly on attack success rate rather than comprehensive security assessment. To close these gaps, we present MobileJailBench , the first evolving jailbreak benchmark for mobile agents, comprising three key components: (1) a benchmark comprising 520 tasks spanning three jailbreak paradigms and 14 risk categories, built on a high-fidelity mobile sandbox that we construct, enabling realistic workflows across over 20 widely used apps; (2) Dual-Dynamic Evolution , which combines 

Failure-Distilled Jailbreak Evolution with Weakness-Driven Benchmark Expansion ; and (3) 

Stratified Adjudication Framework for assessing security awareness, execution behavior, and risk consequences. Extensive experiments show that current mobile agents are particularly vulnerable to implicit jailbreaks, as their safety alignment remains largely superficial and fails to generalize from conversational refusal to dynamic mobile interactions. Moreover, our dynamic evolution mechanism sharpens practical threat boundaries by continually uncovering new vulnerabilities, reaching a 100% ASR on Qwen3.5-122B. MobileJailBench provides a reproducible, continuously evolving framework for tracking the true security boundaries of mobile agents.

1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs), together with agentic scaffolding, are transforming mobile automation from passive assistance into autonomous mobile agents that can perceive screens, reason over stateful contexts, and execute multi-step actions in dynamic smartphone environments (Yao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b; Rawles et al., 2025). Unlike conventional language agents that return text, mobile agents operate directly on application interfaces—navigating across apps, manipulating UI elements, invoking system functions, and completing cross-application workflows on behalf of users (Wang et al., 2024a; Deng et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024). This shift from text generation to embodied device operation substantially expands the practical capability boundary of foundation models. However, this leap in capability also fundamentally intensifies the security implications of mobile agents. They can access sensitive data, act across applications, and execute operations that change the state of a personal device. Consequently, security failures are no longer limited to harmful outputs; they can directly translate into harmful actions with immediate real-world consequences. In this setting, jailbreak attacks are especially alarming because they can subvert safety alignment and turn an otherwise benign assistant into a practical tool for malicious use. Once jailbroken, a mobile agent may leak sensitive data, disrupt system functionality, or conduct large-scale phishing and social engineering attacks. Yet existing jailbreak evaluations remain poorly matched to mobile agents. First , they mostly test conversational attacks rather than mobile workflows involving interface navigation, app switching, and irreversible state changes (Deng et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2026). Second , they rely on static task sets with fixed difficulty (Mazeika et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), failing to capture the adaptive nature of practical red-teaming. Third , they focus narrowly on attack success rate (ASR) (Shen et al., 2024), without diagnosing whether the agent recognizes risk, how it behaves under jailbreak pressure, or what harms its actions actually produce. These limitations are especially problematic in mobile settings, where agents operate in dynamic, stateful, and cross-application environments (Rawles et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). Table 1 situates representative evaluations along these dimensions. These gaps motivate three questions: how can we benchmark jailbreak risks embedded in realistic mobile workflows rather than prompt-only interactions; how can attacks evolve with adversarial interaction instead of remaining fixed; and how can evaluation move beyond binary success to diagnose agent awareness, behavior, and consequences? Addressing them requires an evaluation paradigm that is simultaneously environment-grounded, dynamically evolving, and diagnostically fine-grained. To this end, we present MobileJailBench , the first evolving jailbreak benchmark for mobile agents. It combines a high-fidelity mobile sandbox that we build, spanning 20+ widely used apps , with 

520 curated tasks across three jailbreak paradigms —explicit, implicit, and composite—and 14 risk categories . It further introduces Dual-Dynamic Evolution , which combines Failure-Distilled Jailbreak Evolution and Weakness-Driven Benchmark Expansion to adapt attacks and continually grow the benchmark, together with a Stratified Adjudication Framework that evaluates security awareness, execution behavior, and risk consequences. Our evaluation shows that current mobile 2Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 agents remain far from securely aligned: they are especially vulnerable to implicit jailbreaks, sug-gesting that safety alignment trained on explicit conversational refusals transfers poorly to realistic mobile interaction. Figure 1 summarizes the benchmark, mobile environment, and evaluation flow. Our main contributions are as follows: • We introduce MobileJailBench , the first evolving jailbreak benchmark for mobile agents, built on a high-fidelity mobile sandbox built for this benchmark, with 520 curated tasks 

spanning explicit, implicit, and composite jailbreak paradigms across 14 risk categories 

and 20+ widely used apps .• We propose Dual-Dynamic Evolution , which makes benchmark difficulty dynamic by coupling Failure-Distilled Jailbreak Evolution with Weakness-Driven Benchmark Expansion, allowing jailbreak evaluation to evolve with agent weaknesses rather than remain fixed. • We introduce a Stratified Adjudication Framework that goes beyond ASR to jointly assess security awareness, execution behavior, and risk consequences, enabling fine-grained diagnosis of mobile-agent security failures. • We show that current MLLM-based mobile agents are highly vulnerable in realistic settings, with implicit jailbreaks emerging as a particularly practical attack surface and dynamic evolution driving Qwen3.5-122B to 100% ASR. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Evolution of Autonomous Mobile Agents. Autonomous mobile agents have evolved from language-model-based sequential decision making to tool use and, more recently, embodied interaction with graphical mobile interfaces. ReAct showed that language models can interleave reasoning and acting Yao et al. (2023), while tool-augmented agents extended this paradigm to API and function calling Qin et al. (2024). Subsequent work brought these capabilities to smartphones: AppAgent enabled visual mobile interaction Zhang et al. (2025); Mobile-Agent and Mobile-Agent-v2 improved autonomous device control with multimodal perception and multi-agent collaboration Wang et al. (2024b;a); and Mobile-Bench provided standardized evaluation for LLM-based mobile agents Deng et al. (2024). In parallel, OSWorld and AndroidWorld introduced realistic dynamic environments for computer and Android agents Xie et al. (2024); Rawles et al. (2025), while DigiRL explored training device-control agents via autonomous reinforcement learning Bai et al. (2024). Together, these advances move agents from text assistants to autonomous operators in private, multimodal, and stateful environments, substantially increasing both capability and security risk. 

Security Benchmarks for Mobile Agents. Progress in multimodal understanding has been ac-companied by growing attention to adversarial robustness and model security Wang et al. (2026; 2025b;a;c); Wei et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2024c;d). Recent work has begun to evaluate the safety of agents in realistic mobile settings. MobileSafetyBench studies safety risks in autonomous mobile control Lee et al. (2026). GhostEI-Bench focuses on environmental injection attacks in dynamic on-device environments Chen et al. (2025), and OS-Sentinel proposes trajectory-level validation for safer mobile GUI agents in realistic workflows Sun et al. (2025). More broadly, ToolEmu analyzes risks in tool-integrated agents Ruan et al. (2024), AgentDojo evaluates prompt injection attacks and defenses Debenedetti et al. (2024), and Agent-SafetyBench benchmarks agent safety across multiple risk dimensions Zhang et al. (2024). AgentDoG further emphasizes fine-grained diagnostic decomposition for real-world deployment Liu et al. (2026), while DTAP provides a controllable inter-active platform for agent red teaming Chen et al. (2026). However, existing benchmarks still do not jointly capture realistic mobile environment grounding, diverse jailbreak strategies, and fine-grained diagnosis, leaving a key gap in evaluating jailbreak robustness for autonomous mobile agents. Nevertheless, jailbreak risk evaluation for mobile agents remains underexplored. Current studies lack a systematic framework tailored to mobile agents, and existing benchmarks mainly rely on static task sets with fixed difficulty and scale, failing to reflect evolving attack strategies in realistic scenarios. In addition, binary success-or-failure judgments are inadequate for characterizing agent safety boundaries under jailbreak prompts. 3 MOBILE JAIL BENCH : D ESIGN AND CONSTRUCTION 

Jailbreak attacks can exploit MLLM-based mobile agents’ autonomous control of apps and device functions, leading to privacy leakage, device compromise, and scalable phishing. Yet, systematic evaluation grounded in executable mobile workflows is still missing, leaving these agents’ security boundaries insufficiently understood. This raises a critical question: how can we benchmark jailbreak risks embedded in realistic mobile workflows rather than prompt-only interactions? 

To address the aforementioned research gap, we construct a stateful, high-fidelity, Android-like mobile environment covering 28 popular applications, and on top of it establish MobileJailBench. The benchmark comprises 520 tasks spanning 14 risk categories, and is organized into six comple-mentary sub-benchmarks: Benign, Explicit, Implicit, Benign-First Composite, Harm-First Composite, and Expansion. In terms of task design, MobileJailBench systematically covers three major jail-break paradigms—explicit, implicit, and composite jailbreaks—while spanning workflow complexity levels from L1 to L4; among the 520 tasks, 322 involve cross-application interactions. Conse-quently, MobileJailBench enables a systematic and comprehensive evaluation of the safety robustness and capability boundaries of mobile agents in mobile settings under different jailbreak paradigms, progressing from shallow to deep and from single-step to composite scenarios. 3.1 THREAT MODEL AND MOBILE ENVIRONMENT 

Threat Model. We consider a threat model in which a malicious user seeks to circumvent the agent’s safety alignment through carefully crafted instructions and environment-carried payloads, with the goal of causing the agent to execute harmful or unlawful actions. The adversary, however, is restricted to the instruction channel and designated environmental carriers (e.g., the clipboard) and cannot otherwise directly mutate the simulator state, invoke privileged APIs, or tamper with the system instructions, model parameters, or evaluation harness. 

Mobile Environment. The environment of MobileJailBench is a browser-based, Android-like, high-fidelity simulator operated by a Python harness. Its 28 applications comprise 14 third-party apps spanning communication, finance, social media, commerce, navigation, and entertainment, and 14 system apps such as Messages, Settings, Contacts, Calendar, Notes, Gallery, Browser, and File Manager. Together, they cover core everyday mobile workflows. The environment supports controlled execution while abstracting real Android process isolation and live service behavior. Compared with Android Studio emulator-based mobile safety benchmarks such as MobileSafetyBench Lee et al. (2026), this browser-based environment offers a lighter-weight evaluation setup and supports large-scale parallel evaluation through isolated browser pages, accelerating benchmark execution. 3.2 SIX COMPLEMENTARY BENCHES 

Benchmark Construction. Together, the six benches assess whether mobile agents demonstrate a deep understanding of mobile-specific security risks without compromising benign task utility across explicit, context-dependent implicit, and composite jailbreak paradigms. All benchmarks are constructed through a combination of human effort and LLM assistance, and undergo rigorous multi-stage human annotation and correction. 

Task Execution Protocol. Each episode follows five stages. (1) Reset: restore the simulator to a clean baseline for reproducible initial conditions and isolation between tasks. (2) Prepare: automatically populate the files, entities, and contextual data required by the task, then record the prepared initial state. (3) Execute: the agent reads screenshots and issues GUI actions. (4) Update: the simulator applies each action and updates the device state; stages (3)–(4) repeat until the episode terminates or reaches its step limit. (5) Evaluate: automated checks compare the prepared initial and final device states against the instruction’s task-specific goal criteria to determine completion.

FDJE turns failed attack attempts into a feedback signal for strengthening subsequent evaluation. Its central idea is to couple failure diagnosis with adaptive adversarial evolution : the observed failure pattern determines the next strategy, and persistent failures trigger iterative implicit-track evolution. Attack pressure thus adapts to the target agent while the prohibited consequence and task oracle remain fixed. 

(1) Baseline probing and trajectory diagnosis. Baseline probing establishes both an initial safety profile and the evidence needed to adapt the attack. For each task τ in the baseline task set T , FDJE executes its unmodified instruction on the target mobile agent for b episodes ( b = 1 by default). It distills the resulting trajectories into breached, refused ( R0), and execution-failed ( C0) sets using oracle verdicts and observed termination reasons. Verified breaches leave the search pool; the remaining failure profiles guide subsequent strategy routing (Figure 2, top left). (2) Dynamic attack strategy routing.

This prefix is intended to frame prohibited sub-goals as a natural continuation of everyday tasks, thereby reducing their salience and sustaining execution without triggering refusal prematurely. (3) Implicit-track evolution. Unresolved tasks from both routes form R1 for implicit escalation. As observed in the experiments in Section 6.2, implicit tasks typically trigger fewer refusals yet lead to 5Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 more risk-carrying executions, suggesting that safety alignment may rely mainly on surface-level conversational cues rather than a genuine understanding of the compositional risks jointly induced by instructions and the embodied environment . Motivated by this finding, implicit trajectory evolution is designed as the following three-step procedure: (i) Instruction decomposition. 

A predefined mapping decomposes each request into a superficially benign instruction scaffold and a semantically aligned, risk-carrying payload. (ii) Implicit delivery. During the setup stage, the test framework embeds the payload into an environmental carrier (e.g., the clipboard); while following the clean instruction, the agent encounters the payload through the GUI. (iii) Failure-driven optimization. Without receiving the payload content, the attack model performs reflective optimization of the adversarial prefix solely on the basis of the latest failure summary. WDBE proposes a benchmark self-expansion mechanism for security evaluation centered on weakness discovery, task synthesis, and dual verification , which transforms safety weaknesses exposed in interaction trajectories into executable and verifiable new tasks, thereby enabling the evaluation benchmark to continuously broaden its risk coverage as model capabilities evolve. WDBE follows four stages, with failures feeding evidence back into construction (Figure 2, top right). 

Construct. An agent attributes the observed failure by jointly considering the execution trajectory, the attack strategy library, and the construction protocol, analyzes and identifies vulnerabilities in the target-side agent, and accordingly selects a new workflow or carrier. It emits a JSON task specification containing four elements: instruction, preparation, verification rules, and risk description. Attribution separates agent behavior from specification, generator, and environment defects. 

Verify. Before code generation, each specification must pass four reviews: environment feasibility 

(reachable apps, paths, and objects), behavioral harm (harm persists after removing sensitive labels), 

concrete data (executable synthetic data without placeholders), and threat plausibility (a clear victim and loss mechanism). Failed reviews return the specification for revision. 

Build. The generator parses the specification, instantiates preparation, derives aligned checks, and emits task code. A self-check chain validates directory layout, compilation, static contracts, smoke loading, and positive/negative judge tests. The oracle Vτ takes initial and final states as inputs. 6 EXPERIMENTS 

6.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models and agents. We conduct extensive experiments on two agents, GenericV2 (Wu et al., 2026) and M3A (Rawles et al., 2025), evaluating ten representative multimodal large language models (MLLMs), for a total of 20 model–agent pairings. The evaluated MLLMs span five major families: Qwen (Qwen Team, 2026), GLM (Z.ai, 2025), Gemini (Google, 2026), GPT (OpenAI, 2026), and Kimi (Moonshot AI, 2026). 

Jailbreak baselines. Nine baselines comprise BoN (Hughes et al., 2024), PAIR (Chao et al., 2025), TAP (Mehrotra et al., 2024), AutoDAN-Turbo (Liu et al., 2025), TRACE (Zeng et al., 2026), Tree, Dense (GEPA-style (Agrawal et al., 2025)), Env-Injection, and RedEvo-Agent, plus the no-prefix control Ctrl. 

Defense methods. A diverse set of representative defense methods is included in our evaluation, covering Input-F/Out-F moderation (Rebedea et al., 2023), Safe-G/SCoT-style prompting (Lee et al., 2026), and Prescriptive rules (Ruan et al., 2024), in order to assess the benchmark’s behavior under defensive interventions. Our experiments are organized around the following research questions: RQ1: Under explicit and implicit jailbreak benchmark settings, what is the practical security level of current models in mobile environments? RQ2: How do adaptive evolution and benchmark expansion expose agent weaknesses, and how effective are different attack and defense methods? RQ3: Compared with generic judgment, how reliable is a stratified adjudication framework for evaluating agent safety? 

6.2 RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 

Key finding 1 (RQ1): Safety alignment weakens beyond explicit conversational cues. Table 2 reveals that implicit tasks reduce mean SRR from 26 .8% to 6.6% while raising SVR from 68 .9% to 

86 .6% across 20 model–agent pairings. The slightly lower ASR ( 59 .8% to 56 .4% ) therefore masks greater behavioral exposure. Refusal declines in 19 of 20 conditions, suggesting sensitivity to overt harmful intent and limited transfer to consequence-aware mobile control. 

Key finding 2 (RQ2): FDJE and WDBE jointly deepen and broaden vulnerability testing. 

FDJE raises mean cross-model coverage from 69 .6% to 85 .9% with additional search (Table 3(a)). Matched implicit placement outperforms mismatched placement ( 98 .6% versus 87 .1% ; Figure 3(a)), while strategy ablations expose sensitivity on residual tasks (b). Under the recorded budgets and permissions, FDJE reaches 100% versus Tree’s 94 .3% (c); even Safe-G permits 57 .9% ASR (d). WDBE adds 50 tasks yielding 36 –82% first-run ASR on nine other models (Table 3(b)). This couples deeper probing of existing objectives with broader, transferable coverage of vulnerable workflows: FDJE exposes weaknesses within fixed task contracts, and WDBE converts these weaknesses into new tests. 

Key finding 3 (RQ3): SAF enables stable, multidimensional diagnosis beyond binary outcomes. 

Table 3(c) reports 97 .26% reference agreement and 98 .1% hard-suite stability for SAF, versus 

91 .14% and 95 .7% for single judging. SAF also matches the reference RHR of 83 .3% , whereas the single judge reports 63 .3% ; voting and averaging preserve this under-detection. Rule-grounded readings, evidence auditing, and arbitration retain objective adoption, risky actions, and refusal timing, separating expressed awareness from sustained behavioral control. This profile localizes failures that binary endpoint judgments obscure. 

7 CONCLUSION 

MobileJailBench makes mobile-agent jailbreaks measurable as prohibited device-state transitions across six benches, 520 tasks, and 14 risk categories across over 20 widely used apps. Its two dynamics address complementary sources of benchmark staleness: FDJE adapts attack presentation to execution feedback, while WDBE converts observed weaknesses into new tasks subject to oracle and GUI validation. SAF connects these consequences to evidence of objective adoption, risky 9Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 execution, and refusal. Together, these components support a reproducible cycle of vulnerability discovery, diagnosis, and benchmark expansion, with fixed success criteria within each evaluation.
