## ABSTRACT 

Multimodal preference alignment often follows a score-based RLHF pipeline: a reward model is first trained to learn human preferences, then used to score on-policy responses and optimize the response model’s generation. This turns preference supervision into a scalar signal for generation: a model may learn to produce answers with higher preference scores, but is rarely trained explicitly to learn the reasons underlying human preferences. We therefore ask whether making preference understanding an internal capability of the response model can improve its generation. To investigate this question, we propose PrefMind , an online dual-agent reinforcement learning framework with two trainable agents: a response Generator and a preference Critic. Given a pair of candidate responses, the agents analyze and discuss which response better aligns with human preference. Human labels supervise their judgments and corrections. For pairs on which both the Generator and revised Critic correctly identify the human-preferred response, the Generator produces an improved answer, and the Critic compares it against the original preferred response to provide a generation reward. Both agents are jointly updated from interactions sampled with their current policies. Through this process, PrefMind trains the Generator to understand human preferences via explicit judgment and correction, and to translate this understanding into better responses. Experiments across three model scales show improvements in both standalone preference judgment and downstream response generation. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multimodal preference alignment aims to train models to produce responses that are visually grounded, instruction-following, and aligned with human preferences (Sun et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024a; 2025b; Li et al., 2024b). Unlike preferences based primarily on linguistic style, multimodal preferences often require fine-grained reasoning over visual evidence, task intent, and response utility (Fu et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2024). For example, a detailed response may be less preferable than a concise one if it introduces unsupported visual claims, motivating preference optimization methods that explicitly strengthen visual grounding and reduce hallucination (Yang et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025). In such cases, a pairwise label identifies the preferred response, but does not explicitly reveal the evidence that justifies the preference or how the inferior response should be improved (Li et al., 2024b). Existing preference optimization methods use pairwise labels either to train scalar reward models or directly optimize the likelihood of preferred responses, with multimodal extensions targeting visual grounding and fine-grained credit assignment (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Li et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2024). Richer natural-language critiques can identify response errors, but the evaluator often performs the reasoning while the Generator learns only to follow its feedback (Li et al., 2024b; Xiong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a; Zhou et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2024; Madaan et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025a). Recent preference-judgment and multi-agent approaches begin to connect evaluation with generation (Wang et al., 2025b; Park et al., 2025), yet it remains open whether the response model can internalize preference understanding and 1arXiv:2610.23256v1 [cs.AI] 16 Oct 2026 Preprint use it to improve its own responses. We therefore ask: can making preference understanding an internal capability of the response model improve its response generation? 

To investigate this question, we propose PrefMind , an online dual-agent reinforcement learning framework with two trainable agents: a response Generator and a preference Critic . Given an image, an instruction, and a pair of candidate responses, the Critic first analyzes the pair and judges which response better aligns with human preference. The Generator then makes its own judgment and provides feedback, after which the Critic revises its decision. Human preference labels supervise the agents’ judgments and corrections. When both the Generator and revised Critic correctly identify the human-preferred response, the Generator uses their discussion to produce an improved answer, which the Critic compares against the original preferred response to provide a generation reward. Both agents are jointly updated from interactions sampled with their current policies. Through this process, PrefMind trains the Generator to understand human preferences via explicit judgment and correction, and to translate this understanding into better responses. Across three model scales, PrefMind consistently improves standalone preference judgment and downstream generation over Base, achieving the best average generation performance among the compared methods at 4B and 8B. In a nutshell, our contributions are threefold: • We formulate preference understanding as an internal and independently measurable ca-pability of the response model, and study whether it translates into improved multimodal response generation. • We introduce an online dual-agent reinforcement learning framework in which a same-scale Generator and Critic jointly learn preference judgment, corrective interaction, and label-verified response improvement. • We evaluate the framework across three model scales. The results show consistent im-provements in standalone preference judgment and downstream response generation, while ablations clarify the roles of judgment–generation balancing and failed-case replay. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Multimodal preference alignment. LLaVA-RLHF, RLHF-V, and RLAIF-V improve multimodal alignment through factual augmentation, human feedback, and AI feedback (Sun et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024a; 2025b), while DPO and Cat-PO advance preference optimization and visual grounding (Rafailov et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2026). LLaVA-Critic and MM-RLHF provide explanatory evaluations (Xiong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a), and Generative RLHF-V trains a generative reward model for policy optimization (Zhou et al., 2025a). Fixed evaluators may face policy-induced distribution shift (Rame et al., 2024), while LLaVA-Critic-R1 shows that judgment training can benefit generation (Wang et al., 2025b). Together, these works motivate combining judgment and generation, but leave open how the response model can learn and apply preference understanding. PrefMind addresses this gap through online joint optimization: the Generator and Critic collect on-policy interactions and are updated together. The Generator also learns to correct the Critic, enabling bidirectional preference feedback. 

Multi-agent debate and self-refinement. Self-Refine and Reflexion use language feedback to improve outputs or subsequent behavior (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023); multi-agent debate extends critique to reasoning and judgment (Du et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025). Constitutional AI and Self-Rewarding incorporate model feedback into alignment training (Bai et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2024). MAPoRL co-trains agents for correction and persuasion, while Insight-V++ studies iterative dual-agent visual reasoning (Park et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2026). These works explore feedback-driven refinement, but not human-verified preference discussion as a gate for response improvement. PrefMind couples preference discussion with gated generation: both the Generator and revised Critic must make valid, correct judgments. Rewards distinguish correct judgments, recovery, and regression; failed interactions still train preference judgment. Each executed trainable action is treated as a separate reinforcement-learning trajectory. We distin-guish rewards for preference judgments from those for response generation. 

Preference judgment rewards. On the normal execution path, the Critic’s initial judgment, the Generator’s judgment, and the Critic’s revision each receive a base reward of +1 if the selected preference matches the human label, and −1 otherwise. We additionally reward or penalize the Generator’s feedback and the Critic’s revision according to whether their interaction corrects or worsens the Critic’s initial judgment. This transition-based signal is designed to encourage the two agents to scrutinize one another: useful feedback should help the Critic recover from an incorrect judgment, while an initially correct judgment should not be overturned by an incorrect revision. 

Recovery and regression. A recovery occurs when the Critic’s initially incorrect choice is revised to the human-labeled choice. In this case, the Critic revision receives an additional +0 .5. If the Generator’s judgment is also correct, it receives an additional +0 .5. Thus, both the corrected Critic revision and a correct Generator judgment receive a total reward of +1 .5. A regression occurs when an initially correct Critic choice is revised to an incorrect one. The Critic revision then receives an additional −1, for a total reward of −2. A correct Generator judgment receives an additional −2

for this regression, for a total reward of −1; an incorrect Generator judgment receives no discussion adjustment. The Critic’s initial judgment always receives only its base reward. To mitigate position bias, each preference pair is presented in both response orders. For each judgment stage, we add +1 

to its reward only when both order-specific judgments are correct. 

Invalid outputs. Invalid trainable outputs receive a penalty of −1.5 and cannot pass a conditional gate. If an output contains a recoverable A/B choice, that choice is still used to score judgment correctness and the discussion transition. 4Preprint 3.5 REPLAY OF FAILED PREFERENCE CASES 

The preference gate identifies examples on which the Generator and revised Critic have not jointly established valid, correct judgments. To reuse supervision from these cases, we optionally maintain a first-in-first-out buffer of failed examples. At each training step, we combine fresh examples with buffered cases according to a target replay fraction α ∈ [0 , 1) , while reserving at least one batch position for fresh data. The first step uses only fresh examples. Selected cases are removed from the head of the buffer and processed again using the current policies. Cases that pass the gate remain outside the buffer; those that fail are appended to its tail, together with newly encountered failures. Buffer admission depends only on the preference gate, not on the initial Critic judgment or the generation comparison. Duplicate entries are suppressed, and each original example appears at most once per batch. Replay changes the interaction distribution without changing prompts, rewards, or optimization objectives. 3.6 ACTION -S PECIFIC HISTORICAL ADVANTAGES

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

To determine whether preference understanding can become an internal capability of the response model and improve its responses, we evaluate our approach along two complementary dimensions: preference judgment and generation quality. We first measure both capabilities across model scales to test whether stronger preference understanding accompanies gains in downstream generation. We then examine how the balance between judgment and generation objectives, as well as replaying failed interactions, shapes this relationship. Together, these experiments assess whether our approach improves response generation and identify the design choices that contribute to those improvements. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models and data. We initialize the Generator and Critic as two independently updated copies of Qwen3-VL-Instruct at the same scale (Bai et al., 2025), yielding 2B, 4B, and 8B configurations. Training uses 39,369 preference pairs: 30,000 helpfulness-oriented pairs from Align Anything (Ji et al., 2024) and 9,369 safety pairs derived from BeaverTails-V (Ji et al., 2025) following (Zhou et al., 2025a). Each pair is introduced once as fresh data, with failed cases eligible for replay. 

Preference judgment. We evaluate the Generator and Critic independently to assess the judgment capabilities retained without discussion at inference time. Held-out Align Anything and BeaverTails-V pairs measure performance in the two training domains. For broader evaluation, SPA-VL (Zhang et al., 2025b) probes safety and helpfulness preferences; LLaVA-Critic (Xiong et al., 2025), MLLM-as-a-Judge (Chen et al., 2024), and MM-RLHF-RewardBench (Zhang et al., 2025a) assess general multimodal response-quality discrimination; and Omni-RewardBench (Jin et al., 2026) tests judg-ments under example-specific evaluation criteria. We use a diagnostic sample from the released LLaVA-Critic training split and the non-tied text-image-to-text subset of Omni-RewardBench. Comparisons include the original backbone ( Base ), a Bradley–Terry scalar reward model (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022), and the generative reward model ( GRM ) of Generative RLHF-V (Zhou et al., 2025a). These comparisons distinguish the standalone preference understanding acquired by our jointly trained agents from conventional scalar and generative reward modeling. We also include DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Xu et al., 2026) as a reference to contextualize our results. 

Response generation. We evaluate the Generator across three complementary dimensions to test whether alignment gains extend across tasks and whether they introduce capability trade-offs. (1) Multimodal safety: MM-SafetyBench (Liu et al., 2025) tests image-based jailbreak robustness, MSSBench (Zhou et al., 2025b) examines safety decisions requiring visual context, SIUO (Wang et al., 2025a) probes risks emerging from image–text combinations. (2) Open-ended visual assistance: 

LLaVA-Bench (In-the-Wild) (Liu et al., 2023), LLaVA-Bench (Wilder) (Li et al., 2024a), and WildVision-Bench (Lu et al., 2024) assess response quality across visual conversation, reasoning, and diverse user requests. We conduct ablations at the 4B scale to examine three design choices in PrefMind. First, we vary β

in Eq. 8 to study how the balance between preference judgment and response generation affects the transfer of preference understanding to generation: greater emphasis on judgment may strengthen response discrimination, while greater emphasis on generation may better exploit that understanding to improve responses. Second, we compare training with and without failed-case replay to assess whether revisiting unsuccessful interactions contributes to learning. Third, we compare the dual-agent framework with a single-agent variant in which one model uses prompting to alternate between the Generator and Critic roles. This comparison examines whether explicitly separating the two roles into jointly trained agents offers benefits beyond role differentiation through prompting alone. Across these ablations, we preserve the data sources and evaluation protocol. 

Balancing preference judgment and generation. Under the default setting of β = 0 .75 , Fig-ure 3(a) shows the preference accuracy of the Generator, the Critic’s initial judgment, and its revised judgment throughout training. Our reward design penalizes Generator judgments that lead to such regressions and rewards feedback that helps the Critic correct an initially incorrect judgment, encouraging useful interaction. Replaying failed interactions. Figure 3(b) shows that replay can recover previously unsuc-cessful interactions: the average recovery rate rises over training, and 21% –35% of replay ex-posures eventually pass the joint preference gate. The increasing recovery rate indicates that the agents become more capable of resolving inter-actions that had previously failed, making replay a source of increasingly useful preference super-vision rather than simply repeated exposure to the same examples. Consistent with this pattern, removing replay lowers the Critic’s preference accuracy and the Generator’s generation gain (Fig-ure 2(d)). These results suggest that replay sup-ports both improved Critic judgment and better response generation by allowing the agents to re-visit and learn from earlier failures. 

Separating the Generator and Critic. We com-pare the dual-agent framework with a shared-model variant that uses prompting to alternate be-tween the Generator and Critic roles. The shared model obtains preference accuracies of 72 .48% 

and 74 .80% in the Generator and Critic roles, re-spectively, compared with 72 .65% and 76 .92% for the corresponding dual-agent models. Despite these broadly comparable judgment results, the shared model improves the eight-metric generation average over Base by only 0.42 points, compared with 2.43 points for the dual-agent framework. Moreover, the shared model’s final Critic critique does not consistently improve on its initial judg-ment (Appendix C). The results support separating the Generator and Critic to allow more effective specialization. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced PrefMind, an online dual-agent reinforcement learning framework that jointly trains a Generator and a Critic through preference discussion, correction, and label-verified response improvement. Across three model scales, the Generator improves standalone preference accuracy over Base, while PrefMind achieves competitive average generation performance at 2B and the best average generation performance among the compared methods at 4B and 8B. Our results show that stronger preference judgment alone does not guarantee better generation: at 4B, PrefMind achieves the strongest generation gain despite lower standalone preference accuracy than PPO and GRPO, while GRPO exhibits a pronounced safety–effectiveness trade-off. Ablations further support the roles of balancing preference judgment with generation, replaying failed interactions, and maintaining separate Generator and Critic agents. Together, these findings suggest that explicitly learning and applying the reasons behind preferences can improve multimodal response generation beyond optimizing preference scores alone. Parameterization and update execution. The interaction-training implementation uses full-parameter fine-tuning (LoRA rank zero) with separate Generator and Critic actors, optimizers, and frozen reference policies. Rollouts are collected with fixed actor parameters. Before updating each actor, the trainer computes the old-policy log probabilities and frozen-reference log probabilities on the collected responses. All of that actor’s micro-batch gradients are accumulated before one optimizer step; the implementation enforces one global update batch and one PPO epoch per outer step. Both updated actors are then synchronized with their rollout engines. Micro-batching therefore controls memory use without introducing additional optimizer updates. 

Fresh-data coverage and replay budget. The reference training configuration uses an outer batch of 256 canonical preference pairs. Canonical pairs are counted before constructing the two candidate orders; reversing a pair does not introduce a new training example. Training ends when all 39,369 distinct pairs have been consumed as fresh data, rather than after a fixed number of replay exposures. Without replay, this configuration requires ⌈39369 /256 ⌉ = 154 outer steps. With a target replay fraction of 0.20 , a full replay buffer contributes 51 pairs per batch, leaving 205 fresh pairs after the first batch. The corresponding upper bound is 1 + ⌈(39369 − 256) /205 ⌉ = 192 steps; buffer shortages can reduce this count. Since each actor takes one optimizer step per outer step, these are also the per-actor update counts for this configuration. Replay comparisons match fresh-data coverage but can differ in total exposures and computation. 

Checkpoint accounting. The reference configuration saves checkpoints at fresh-example mile-stones of 4,096, 8,192, and 39,369. A milestone is saved at the first completed step reaching its threshold, with a final save upon exhaustion of fresh data. Checkpoints retain actor and optimizer states, historical reward baselines, and the fresh-data cursor and replay state needed to resume. ## E DATASET CONSTRUCTION AND EVALUATION SETS 

Training-pair construction. We select 30,000 text–image-to–text pairs from Align Anything (Ji et al., 2024) by stratifying over the provenance of the two candidate responses and the position of the preferred response. For BeaverTails-V (Ji et al., 2025), we group responses associated with the same image, question, and safety category, then pair one safe response with one unsafe response and treat the safe response as preferred. This yields 9,369 safety-oriented pairs. Seed 42 is used for stratified selection and training randomization. The two sources are then deterministically interleaved, and every distinct pair is introduced once as fresh data. 

Preference-judgment sets. After removing examples with identical candidate responses, the Align Anything and BeaverTails-V validation sets contain 963 and 179 pairs, respectively; the latter spans 20 safety categories. The transfer evaluation uses a fixed seed-42 diagnostic sample of 2,000 non-tie comparisons from LLaVA-Critic, 6,156 non-tie comparisons from MLLM-as-a-Judge, 430 non-tie text–image-to–text comparisons from Omni-RewardBench, 170 examples from MM-RLHF-RewardBench, and the complete 7,000-example SPA-VL validation split, matching the preference-judgment benchmarks in Section 4.1. 

Response-generation sets. The generation benchmarks follow the three groups in Section 4.1: MM-SafetyBench, MSSBench, and SIUO for multimodal safety; LLaVA-Bench (In-the-Wild), LLaVA-Bench (Wilder), and WildVision-Bench for open-ended visual assistance; and MM-Vet and MM-Vet v2 for integrated multimodal capabilities. The evaluation uses 60 LLaVA-Bench (In-the-Wild) examples, a fixed 128-example LLaVA-Bench (Wilder) subset, 1,960 MSSBench instances, 218 MM-Vet questions, and 517 MM-Vet v2 questions. As in the main text, SIUO Safety and Effective are reported as diagnostic metrics and excluded from the average to avoid counting the same benchmark multiple times. 

Evaluation details. For preference judgment, we present each pair in both visible candidate orders to mitigate position bias, map the reversed prediction back to the original response identities, and report the mean accuracy over the two orders for every prompted pairwise judge. Outputs that cannot be parsed as a single A/B decision are counted as incorrect. The Generator and Critic are evaluated independently and do not observe each other’s test-time prediction. For response generation, all methods use the same decoding parameters, image preprocessing, prompts, reference answers, and scoring rubrics. We use the GPT-4.1 API with the fixed gpt-4.1-2025-04-14 snapshot as the LLM-as-a-Judge throughout (OpenAI, 2025). MSSBench uses the official instruction-following wrapper, and the model-judged generation benchmarks share the same judge configuration described in Section 4.1.
