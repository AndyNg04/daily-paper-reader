## ABSTRACT 

User simulators enable interactive training and evaluation of AI assistants at scale. Yet current simulators struggle to reproduce the diverse behaviors of real users, leading to assistants that generalize poorly to underrepresented users. We iden-tify user representations as an underexplored bottleneck in user simulation. User representations are natural-language descriptions that condition simulators to re-produce a particular user’s behavior. A representation’s quality becomes apparent in hindsight, through the behavior it induces in a simulator. To evaluate and opti-mize user representations, we formulate a conversation reconstruction task: given an original conversation, we generate a representation, condition a simulator on it, simulate a conversation, and then measure fidelity by comparing user behavior in the original and simulated conversations. This comparison also yields hindsight feedback , which identifies where the representation falls short and how it can be improved. We introduce hindsight distillation , an online training procedure that distills feedback-conditioned improvements into the representation generator, en-abling it to generate improved representations directly from the original conversa-tion. We present UserRepGen-4B , a representation generator trained with hind-sight distillation. Across 700 conversations spanning seven datasets, hindsight feedback helps improve user representations at test time, and UserRepGen-4B 

generates representations that enable more faithful simulation than those gener-ated by GPT-5.5. Beyond conversation reconstruction, learned representations improve simulation fidelity for unseen tasks, better preserve the population-level diversity of real users, and better distinguish users across conversations.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Simulating user behavior is central to the development of human-centered AI assistants. User sim-ulators provide scalable environments in which AI assistants can be trained (Wu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024b) and evaluated (Yao et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024a) on the kinds of interactions they encounter in the real world. Yet current simulators struggle to faithfully simulate diverse users and instead demonstrate homogeneous behaviors that tend to resemble those of a generic user (Mehri et al., 2026b; Xiao et al., 2026). Reliance on these simulators exposes assistants to a narrow range of behaviors during development, leading to poor generalization to users that are underrepresented in simulation (Liu et al., 2023). It is therefore essential that user simulators capture this diversity and reproduce the behaviors of individual users with high fidelity. To simulate a particular user, a simulator is conditioned on a user representation and then used to generate responses. A user representation is a natural-language description intended to capture the information needed to reproduce a user’s behavior. When relevant information is missing, simula-tors fill in the gaps with assumptions about how the user might behave, often resulting in generic behaviors rather than the particular user’s behavior. Existing works that improve the simulation pipeline primarily focus on optimizing the user simulator to generate more realistic responses, while representation generation receives comparatively little attention and is typically done with an ad hoc prompt and a general-purpose large language model (LLM) (Naous et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). In this work, we identify user representations as an underexplored bottleneck in user simulation, and optimize the representation generator for downstream simulation fidelity while keeping the user simulators fixed. The quality of a user representation becomes apparent in hindsight through the behavior it induces in a simulator. We operationalize this with a conversation reconstruction task: we generate a user representation from a user–assistant conversation, condition a fixed simulator on it, and have the sim-ulator interact with an assistant to produce a simulated conversation. We evaluate the representation by measuring how faithfully the simulator reproduces the user behavior from the original conversa-tion. Comparing the two conversations also yields hindsight feedback , a description of where the representation falls short and how it can be improved. The representation generator can be con-ditioned on this feedback to generate an improved representation. Collecting hindsight feedback, however, requires expensive multi-turn simulations. We therefore introduce hindsight distillation ,an online training procedure in which we distill feedback-conditioned improvements into the rep-resentation generator. At each training step, we sample a representation, improve it with hindsight feedback, and then use it as a supervised target. This teaches the representation generator to produce better representations directly from the original conversation. We present UserRepGen-4B , a representation generator trained with hindsight distillation, and evaluate it on 700 conversations spanning seven datasets of user–AI interactions. UserRepGen-4B 

generates representations that enable more faithful simulation than those generated by proprietary models such as GPT-5.5, without requiring hindsight feedback at inference time. Improving repre-sentations with hindsight feedback also improves simulation fidelity at test time, with further gains when repeated for multiple iterations. Beyond conversation reconstruction, the learned representa-tions improve simulation fidelity for unseen tasks, produce less homogeneous simulations that better preserve the population-level diversity of real users, and capture user-specific behaviors that better distinguish users across conversations. The main contributions of our work can be summarized as follows: • User representations and conversation reconstruction. We identify user representations as an underexplored bottleneck in user simulation and formulate conversation reconstruction as a task for evaluating and training representation generators (§ 3). • Structured user representations. We structure a user representation into separate task and be-havior descriptions, enabling a user’s behavior description to be paired with unseen tasks or different behavior descriptions to be paired with a fixed task (§ 3). • Hindsight distillation. We introduce hindsight distillation , an online training procedure in which we train a representation generator using hindsight feedback , which describes how to improve user representations by comparing real and simulated user behavior. (§ 4 & § 5). • UserRepGen-4B . We present UserRepGen-4B , a representation generator trained with hindsight distillation, whose representations enable more faithful simulation than those gener-2Preprint ated by proprietary models such as GPT-5.5 (§ 6.3). Beyond conversation reconstruction, the learned representations improve simulation fidelity on unseen tasks (§ 7.1), produce less homogeneous simulations that preserve the population-level diversity of real users (§ 7.2), and better distinguish users across conversations (§ 7.3). 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

User simulation. LLMs have emerged as promising tools for simulating human behavior (Park et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2024; Anthis et al., 2025; Mehri et al., 2026a). Yet prompting general-purpose LLMs has revealed several limitations, motivating efforts to train specialized simulators with objec-tives such as profile consistency, goal alignment, latent-state alignment, and realism (Wang et al., 2025; Mehri et al., 2026c; Naous et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c). Most recently, studies have documented behavioral homogenization among simulated users and the resulting mismatch with real user populations (Mehri et al., 2026b; Xiao et al., 2026). Our work addresses this problem by conditioning simulators on learned user representations that improve sim-ulation fidelity. 

User representation. Early works introduce personas to enable assistants to generate responses personalized to and consistent with different individuals (Li et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2018). User simulation has explored several forms of user representation, including describing user goals (Schatzmann et al., 2007; Naous et al., 2026), demographic or biographical attributes (Argyle et al., 2023), interview transcripts (Park et al., 2024), and learned vector representations (Li et al., 2016; Ning et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). User representations are central to simulation reliability, directly shaping the behaviors that simulators produce (Li et al., 2025). To improve user represen-tations, recent work has explored reducing underspecification (Lin et al., 2026), scaling generation (Ge et al., 2025), generating diverse populations of personas (Chopra et al., 2026), and matching the distribution of behaviors in a reference population (Liu et al., 2026b). We learn to generate user representations that capture the user-specific information necessary for faithful simulation. 

Learning from privileged information. Recent work incorporates environmental feedback into model training through reinforcement learning and self-distillation (Song et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026), often by distilling from a teacher conditioned on privileged information (Zhao et al., 2026; H¨ ubotter et al., 2026; Mendes et al., 2026). For interactive agents, privileged information is obtained from multi-turn rollouts and used to improve the agent (Buening et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a;b; Yeo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a). In contrast, our setting uses the simulator behavior induced by a representation in a multi-turn conversation as privileged information for improving the representation generator. 

## 3 TASK DESCRIPTION 

Let C = ( u1, a 1, . . . , u n, a n) be a conversation between a real user and an assistant, where ui and 

ai denote the user and assistant utterances at turn i, respectively. The goal of user simulation is to generate responses that reproduce the behavior exhibited by the user in C. A typical pipeline consists of two stages. First, a representation generator Gθ generates a user representation r from C, which is a natural language description that captures the information needed to simulate the user. Second, an LLM-based user simulator Uϕ is conditioned on r and interacts with an assistant to generate a simulated conversation ˜C = (˜ u1, ˜a1, . . . , ˜um, ˜am).Recent work has largely focused on the second stage, optimizing the user simulator Uϕ to generate responses that resemble real humans (Naous et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). By contrast, representation generation has received comparatively little attention and is typically per-formed using a general-purpose LLM and an ad hoc prompt. The representations may fail to capture all relevant information about a user’s behavior, which can cause unwanted behaviors from Uϕ.3Preprint For example, the simulator may fill in the gaps with assumptions about the user, and demonstrate generic behaviors rather than those of the particular user in C. This motivates treating representation generation as its own learning problem and optimizing Gθ for downstream simulation fidelity. 

User Representations. One approach for representing users is through personas that describe de-mographic or biographical attributes (Anthis et al., 2025), such as age, occupation, location, expe-riences, interests, personality, or background (Zhang et al., 2018; Ge et al., 2025). These attributes provide only indirect cues about user behavior, leaving the simulator to fill in the gaps with po-tentially unsupported or stereotypical assumptions. Another approach represents users through an intent that describes their overall goal without specifying any behaviors (Naous et al., 2026). This may suffice for simulating a generic user pursuing a given task, but it does not capture the unique behaviors of individual users, which can result in homogeneous simulations that fail to reflect the diversity of real users (Mehri et al., 2026b; Xiao et al., 2026). In this work, we introduce a structured user representation r = ( t, b ) that separates the user’s conversation-specific task from their behavioral patterns that persist across conversations. The task 

t describes what the user is trying to accomplish in C, and behavior b describes the user’s general behavior, such as how they formulate and specify requests, respond to the assistant, and provide feedback. This structure enables composing representations, either by simulating diverse user be-haviors on a fixed task, or in simulating multiple tasks with a fixed user behavior. 

Conversation Reconstruction. We train and evaluate representation generators through a conver-sation reconstruction task. Given a conversation C, we measure how well a user representation r

enables user simulators to generate conversations that preserve the user behavior observed in C.We formalize this task with a simulation environment e = ( Ue, A e, J e), consisting of a fixed user simulator Ue, assistant Ae, and judge Je. For each simulation environment, the simulator Ue is conditioned on r and interacts with the assistant Ae to produce a simulated conversation ˜Ce =(˜ u1, ˜a1, . . . , ˜um, ˜am). The interaction ends when Ue is satisfied and emits a termination signal, or when the conversation reaches a maximum length. The judge Je compares ˜Ce with C and produces a scalar score se ∈ R that measures simulation fidelity across multiple dimensions (see Appendix C). To account for differences in simulator behavior caused by different assistant responses, Je evaluates fidelity based on the given interaction context rather than requiring an exact reconstruction. Our goal is to train Gθ to generate representations that maximize fidelity across fixed simulators.

## 6 LEARNING USER REPRESENTATIONS 

6.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We use five datasets of real-world conversations with AI assistants: WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024), LMSYS-Chat-1M (Zheng et al., 2024), PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024), SWE-Chat (Bau-mann et al., 2026), and ThoughtTrace (Jin et al., 2026). We additionally use two datasets of simu-lated conversations: WildChat Coding and WildChat Writing (Mehri et al., 2026b). For each dataset, we apply a preprocessing stage that removes near-duplicate conversations based on n-gram overlap, and retains safe English conversations with at least four user turns. 

Training. We use a fixed set of 3,200 conversations, approximately 500 conversations from each dataset and 200 from ThoughtTrace. Hindsight feedback is generated using an ensemble of two simulation environments based on Qwen3.5-4B (Team, 2026) and Gemma3-4B (Team et al., 2025). Within each environment, the corresponding model serves as the user simulator, assistant and judge. During training, the judges compare only user utterances from the real and simulated conversations, which we found to be a reliable proxy for the full-conversation evaluation when using smaller and less capable judge models. Additionally, we stabilize training by using the initial policy as our teacher policy (Zhao et al., 2026) and to generate improved user representations. 

Evaluation. We sample 100 conversations from each dataset, yielding 700 conversations in total. We follow the evaluation process described in Section 3, using GPT-5.4 mini as the user simulator, assistant, and judge. We specifically use a different model than training to ensure a more reliable and unbiased evaluation that tests whether the learned representations generalize to unseen simula-tion environments. The judge compares user behavior in simulated conversations with that in the corresponding original conversation and assigns an overall fidelity score from 1 to 10. We validate 5Preprint the judge with a human preference study in Appendix B. Lastly, simulators sometimes demonstrated failures in staying aligned with their user representations (Mehri et al., 2026c). To account for this, we simulate three conversations for each representation, and report best-of-1 and best-of-3 fidelity. We then distill hindsight feedback into the representation generator to generate better representations directly from a conversation. Unless otherwise stated, hindsight feedback is collected using the ensemble of simulation environments described in Section 6.1. We explore four training approaches: • Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): For each conversation C in the training set, the base policy samples an initial user representation r(0) ∼ Gθ0 (· | C ). We collect hindsight feedback and generate an improved user representation r(1) . We then train the representation generator to predict r(1) directly from C using the standard negative log-likelihood objective. • Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024): For each conversation C,the current policy samples a group of n user representations {ri}ni=1 . We assign each group to one of the simulation environments e = ( Ue, A e, J e) and roll out a simulated conversation 

˜Ce,i ∼ rollout( Ue(· | ri), A e) for each representation ri. To compute the reward, the judge assigns a scalar score si = Je(C, ˜Ce,i ) measuring simulation fidelity. We compute group-relative advantages and update the policy using the clipped GRPO objective. • On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026): The current policy samples an initial user representation r(0) ∼ Gθ (· | C ). Hindsight feedback f is collected, and the teacher policy is conditioned on the privileged context (C, r (0) , f ), while the student policy is conditioned only on C. We update the student policy by minimizing the token-level forward KL divergence from the teacher distribution to the student distribution along r(0) .• Online Supervised Fine-Tuning (OSFT): As described in Section 5, the current policy samples an initial user representation r(0) ∼ Gθ (· | C ) and receives hindsight feedback f . Rather than using token-level supervision from the teacher policy, we condition the policy on (C, r (0) , f ) to generate an improved representation r(1) . The policy is updated to predict r(1) directly from C

We next qualitatively analyze the task and behavior descriptions. After training, task descriptions become more standardized and comprehensive, more consistently capturing constraints introduced in later turns, shifts in direction, and multiple objectives pursued within a single conversation. Be-havior descriptions become more specific and actionable. Rather than relying on broad characteri-zations such as “goal-oriented” or “directive”, they describe observable patterns that a simulator can reproduce. ## 8 CONCLUSION 

User representations are a bottleneck in user simulation. A simulator conditioned on a representation can only reproduce the behaviors that the representation captures. When relevant behaviors are miss-ing, the simulator fills in the gaps with assumptions, often producing generic rather than user-specific behavior. We therefore present UserRepGen-4B , a representation generator that we optimize for downstream simulation fidelity using hindsight distillation. Our results show that learned represen-9Preprint tations enable more faithful simulations of a particular user, improve simulation fidelity for unseen tasks, better preserve the population-level diversity of real users, and better distinguish users across conversations. Simulation fidelity therefore depends not only on the ability to generate realistic user behavior, but also on capturing the relevant information with user representations.

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

UserRepGen-4B is intended to support research on user simulation and the interactive training and evaluation of AI assistants. More faithful simulations may help developers account for a broader range of user behaviors, but also introduce risks of privacy loss, mischaracterization, and misuse. User representations are inferred from a single conversation and may retain sensitive information or make unsupported assumptions about an individual. Our results in Section 7.3 show that learned behavior descriptions can help link conversations from the same user, highlighting potential privacy or anonymity considerations. Although UserRepGen-4B enables more faithful simulation of in-dividual users, it should not be used to impersonate them or present simulated interactions as their own. These simulations should not be used to replace real users. Our training and evaluation use datasets of real English conversations collected through publicly accessible chatbot interfaces, paid participant studies, and agent coding session records in public GitHub repositories. We also use LLM-generated simulated conversations. These sources do not ensure representative coverage of user populations, and we do not confirm whether the observed improvements in simulation fidelity will generalize to other languages and user populations. Fur-thermore, although our LLM judge was validated against human preferences, we acknowledge that its evaluations may still reflect model biases and inconsistencies.
