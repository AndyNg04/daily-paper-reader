Abstract 

Delays in AI responses pose a significant challenge to immersive Virtual Reality experiences. While previous research demonstrates that feedback can reduce the perception of waiting, we lack understanding of which types work best and why. We conducted a within-subjects study with 18 participants to systematically compare three isolated feedback approaches during VR agent interactions: verbal fillers (e.g., “ um... ,” “ hmm... ”), visual indicators (loading spinners), and embodied gestures (thinking animations). Our results revealed that embodied gestures significantly outperformed visual loading indicators across multiple dimensions, making agents feel more lifelike and socially present. Interestingly, explicit visual feedback like loading spinners actually degraded perceived humanness and broke immersion by forcing users to shift from social to technical mental models. These findings suggest that natural, behavior-based feedback maintains the illusion of conversing with a thinking entity, while technical indicators expose the computational substrate. We present empirically-grounded design guidelines for creating more engaging AI-powered VR agents. 

Keywords: Virtual Reality, Large Language Models, Latency, Intelligent Virtual Agents, Embodied Interaction, Social Presence

# 1 Introduction 

The convergence of Virtual Reality (VR) and Large Language Models (LLMs) has ushered in a new era of “Intelligent Virtual Agents” (IVAs) capable of open-ended, free-form conversation. This big shift moves VR from an isolating experience toward a socially responsive environment where interaction mimics real-world dynamics (Elfleet & Chollet, 2024). From educational tutors providing personalized feedback to virtual companions offering emotional support, LLM-powered avatars entails the creation of a seamless “social presence” of being with another human being in a shared space. However, one major flaw of LLM powered avatars threatens to break the illusion of presence: inference latency . Modern LLMs, particularly those with sufficient reasoning capabilities to maintain a coherent persona and context (e.g., GPT-4, Gemini Pro), often require several seconds to process complex user input and generate a contextually appropriate response. In traditional text-based interfaces, such as messaging apps, users are accustomed to asynchronous communication; visual artifacts like “typing bubbles” effectively bridge the temporal gap, signaling that a response is forthcoming. However, in face-to-face VR interactions, time perception is significantly compressed. The expectation of immediacy in spoken conversation is deeply ingrained in human psychology. A silence of 2–4 seconds—common in cloud-based LLM interactions—is perceived not merely as a delay, but as a “break in presence.” In human conversation, gaps longer than 200ms are often interpreted as hesitation, confusion, cognitive loading, or even social disengagement (Clark, 1996). When a VR avatar freezes or stares blankly for seconds at a time, the immersion collapses, and the user is starkly reminded that they are interacting with a software process rather than a social entity. Prior research in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) has proposed various strategies to mitigate this perceived latency. Many systems use “fillers” like “ Hmm... ” or “ Let me see... ” to show they are thinking. While these work well for smart speakers, they can feel annoying and distracting in immersive settings like Virtual Reality. The experience can feel unnatural, if the filler does not match the emotional tone of the conversation. Alternatively, visual indicators (e.g., LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 4loading spinners, progress bars) borrow from 2D web and mobile design patterns to provide more transparency. While these explicitly inform the user of the system’s operational status, they risk breaking the diegesis of the virtual world—inserting a floating 2D User Interface (UI) element into a 3D narrative space, forcing the user to toggle between “social” and “technical” mental models. Recent work by Elfleet and Chollet (2024) demonstrated that a “multimodal” approach combining verbal fillers, visual cues, and gestures significantly improved perceived response time compared to a no feedback of silence. While this establishes that some feedback is indeed better than none, it obscures the specific cognitive contributions of each modality. Does the user feel better because the avatar used a filler, or because it shifted its weight? Did the visual spinner actually hurt immersion while the gesture helped it, or did they act synergistically? Understanding these component-level effects is crucial for designing interfaces that are not just quicker, but more socially credible. To address this gap, we present a rigorous component-level analysis of latency feedback mechanisms in VR. We conducted a within-subjects study ( N = 18 ) where participants interacted with an LLM-powered avatar under three isolated feedback conditions: (1) Verbal Fillers 

(auditory only), (2) Visual System Status (explicit UI), and (3) Embodied Gestures (non-verbal behavioral cues), alongside a no feedback condition for control. Our results reveal a fascinating divergence between “operational transparency” and “social immersion”. This work offers three primary contributions to the field of social Virtual Reality: 1. Empirical Decomposition: We provide a component-level analysis isolating latency feedback modalities for LLM based interactions in VR. Contrasting with prior multimodal studies, we demonstrate that embodied non-verbal behavior is the primary driver of perceived animacy and immersion, independently outperforming verbal and visual strategies. 2. The Cost of Transparency: We demonstrate that explicit system-status feedback can backfire in social VR contexts. Contrary to established usability principles that prioritize LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 5“Visibility of System Status,” we show that technical indicators (like loading spinners) break social immersion by reminding users they are interacting with a computational system. This suggests a fundamental trade-off in immersive agent design: transparency for trust versus coherence for presence. 3. Design Framework: We provide a simple “cheat sheet” for creators. Instead of using computer-like progress bars, we show how to use “Embodied Wait States”—having the agent act like a human who is thinking—to make wait times feel shorter and more natural. 

# 2 Related Work 

# 2.1 Understanding Presence and User Experience in Immersive Environments 

When users interact with virtual characters, the quality of that experience depends fundamentally on how immersed they feel—a concept researchers call “presence,” defined as the psychological experience of “being there” in a virtual world. Presence is not purely a technical property; instead, it emerges from how well the virtual environment’s features align with users’ expectations and intentions (Triberti et al., 2025). In social VR contexts where a user interacts with an avatar, presence hinges on whether the avatar’s behavior feels coherent and meaningful. Early foundational research by Slater and Steed introduced the concept of “breaks in presence” (BIPs)—moments when a user’s perception suddenly shifts from the virtual world back to the physical world—and showed that more frequent BIPs correlate with lower presence ratings (Slater & Steed, 2000). Caldas et al. (2024) recently demonstrated that when individual presence cues (such as embodiment, social presence, or narrative realism) are degraded, users’ engagement and emotional responses decline measurably. This suggests that maintaining a cohesive virtual experience requires attention to every element that users perceive. For virtual experiences, interface design plays a critical role. Research in diegetic design LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 6has identified two distinct UI approaches: diegetic elements, which are integrated into the virtual world’s narrative and logic, versus non-diegetic elements, which exist outside the world and openly signal the underlying technical system (Gottsacker et al., 2021; Saling et al., 2021). Saling et al. (2021) compared these approaches in a VR game and found a clear preference for diegetic UIs, with trends toward higher presence ratings. Gottsacker et al. (2021) extended this finding to cross-reality interruptions, showing that fully and partially diegetic representations of social events yield higher place illusion and plausibility, lower interaction overhead, and fewer reported drops in presence compared to non-diegetic overlays or system notifications. This diegetic preference is not merely aesthetic—it reflects a fundamental principle: when interruptions or status information are rendered as in-world social events rather than as floating UI panels, users remain psychologically embedded in the virtual environment for longer. Conversely, a 2D loading spinner floating above an avatar functions as a non-diegetic HUD, which foregrounds the computational substrate and constitutes a designed break in presence. The diegetic design literature thus predicts that such cues can harm immersion even if they improve technical transparency, a prediction directly supported by the findings in this paper. 

# 2.2 Latency, Responsiveness, and Perceived Waiting in Human-Computer Interaction 

The passage of time in interactive systems is not merely a technical metric—it is a core element of user experience. Research in both desktop and mobile contexts has established that even small delays degrade satisfaction, with delays exceeding 2 seconds widely perceived as unacceptable in many interactive contexts. Notably, mobile users exhibit different tolerance thresholds: Arapakis et al. (2021) found that mobile web search users are approximately four times more tolerant of response latency than their desktop counterparts, although this tolerance collapses rapidly when delays exceed 7–10 seconds. This context dependency is important: mobile users may expect longer processing times due to network variability, yet they still form rapid judgments about system responsiveness. LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 7One strategy for managing perceived delay is to provide active feedback during waiting periods. Research on visual feedback shows that dynamic progress indicators and animated loading bars can reduce perceived waiting time compared to static indicators or silence (Yu, 2023). However, the benefits of such feedback are not universal. A recent study on the effect of visual feedback types on mobile app wait interfaces found that cartoon-style feedback led to shorter perceived waiting times than generic spinners, suggesting that the aesthetic and narrative quality of feedback matters (Chen & Li, 2020). The key insight is that feedback serves a communicative function: it reassures users that the system is working and gives them permission to wait. In spoken dialogue and face-to-face interaction, the problem of latency becomes more acute. Clark established that in human conversation, gaps longer than 200 milliseconds are already interpreted as hesitation, confusion, or social disengagement (Clark, 1996). This sensitivity is deeply rooted in human turn-taking norms. Conversational turn transitions in spoken English occur at an average gap of roughly 239 milliseconds (Jacoby et al., 2024), a remarkably tight temporal window that reflects the collaborative nature of dialogue. When a speaker pauses to think or retrieve information, listeners do not sit in silence—instead, speakers deploy a variety of signaling behaviors to indicate that they are still holding the conversational floor and will soon respond. Backchannels (brief utterances like “uh huh” or head nods), fillers (non-lexical utterances like “ um ” or “ let me see ”), and non-verbal behaviors (gaze shifts, hand movements) all serve this floor-holding function (Knudsen et al., 2020; Vinciarelli et al., 2015). Knudsen et al. (2020) corpus analysis of Dutch and German conversation found that approximately 16–19% of conversational turns consist of backchannels, and utterance-initial fillers are likewise ubiquitous. These minimal signals are easy to plan and produce, which is why they emerge so naturally and frequently in dialogue—they buy the speaker time for planning while maintaining engagement with the listener. # 2.3 Natural Feedback Mechanisms in Conversational Agents 

As conversational agents increasingly move into immersive and embodied contexts—such as virtual avatars in educational or social VR applications—the question of how to signal latency without breaking immersion becomes pressing. Prior research in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) offers foundational insights. Wigdor et al. (2016) conducted a Wizard-of-Oz study with a physical robot (Robovie) and found that simple verbal fillers (e.g., “ Let me see ”) significantly improved the robot’s perceived aliveness compared to silent delay, suggesting that auditory acknowledgment can mitigate the negative impact of latency. As conversational technology moved to screen-based agents, Boukaram et al. (2021) conducted a large-scale study ( N = 360 ) comparing verbal fillers against visual spinners in web-based dialogue agents. Their results revealed a subtle trade-off: contextualized fillers improved perceived speed but did not necessarily increase likeability relative to visual indicators. Kum and Lee extended this work by contrasting verbal fillers with gestural fillers (e.g., chin stroking) in studies of digital human avatars, and found that gestural fillers were more effective at reducing perceived latency, suggesting that embodiment—the presence of an animated body—plays a crucial role in maintaining conversational floor (Kum & Lee, 2022). Maslych et al. (2025) distinguished between “Natural” feedback (combinations of gestures and voice) and “Artificial” feedback (system-like signals), and found that the Natural condition mitigated long LLM delays (up to 6.5 seconds) more effectively, supporting the premise that socially meaningful behavior is more tolerant of delay than explicit system indicators. Both verbal fillers and gestures are grounded in human communication norms. From the perspective of grounding theory (Clark & Brennan), successful communication requires that conversational partners coordinate both content and process (Clark, 1996; Resnick et al., 1991). This coordination is not achieved through explicit status messages; rather, it emerges through multimodal signals—voice, gaze, gesture, and timing—that constitute evidence of continued attention and engagement. Kendrick et al. (2023) recently demonstrated that in human face-to-face conversation, turn-taking coordination is fundamentally multimodal: gaze direction and manual gestures jointly regulate turn transitions, with averted gaze and unfinished gestures LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 9serving as powerful signals that the speaker intends to retain the floor. Building on this, Andrist et al. (2013) showed that virtual agents exhibiting well-timed gaze aversion during conversational pauses are perceived as thinking, elicit more user disclosure, and are better able to hold the conversational floor compared to agents that stare continuously or avert gaze at inappropriate moments. These findings establish that silence combined with embodied thinking behavior—gaze aversion, micro-movements, posture shifts—already constitutes a recognizable floor-holding signal in human expectations. In this light, both verbal fillers and gestures can be understood as natural feedback: they leverage innate human communication patterns to signal continued cognitive engagement. Visual system status indicators (such as loading spinners or progress bars) represent a fundamentally different category. These elements explicitly reveal the computational status of the underlying system—they are non-diegetic cues that place the agent’s processing into the foreground of user attention. While such transparency is a cornerstone of usability heuristics, in immersive social environments this transparency comes at a cost. 

# 2.4 Embodied Conversational Agents: Perception, Anthropomorphism, and Responsiveness 

An embodied conversational agent (ECA) is a lifelike virtual human capable of producing speech, hand gestures, and facial expressions. The success of an ECA is not determined solely by dialogue quality or knowledge depth; instead, users’ perception of the agent as a believable social entity—what researchers call anthropomorphism and social presence—heavily influences the interaction. Anthropomorphism refers to the human tendency to attribute human-like qualities (emotions, intentions, beliefs) to non-human entities (Damiano & Dumouchel, 2018), while social presence refers to the sense of “being with” another agent and the degree to which that agent feels like a sentient being (Caldas et al., 2024; Wong et al., 2026). Visual appearance is a significant driver of anthropomorphism. Thaler et al. (2020) compared autonomous ECAs to human-driven avatars (face-tracked representations) and found LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 10 that increased visual humanness correlates with increased perceived humanness, though interestingly it also correlates with increased perceived eeriness (the uncanny valley effect). Nowak and Biocca (2003) foundational work showed that people respond socially to both human and computer-controlled virtual entities, and that the presence of a visual representation increases telepresence and social presence compared to no image at all. However, higher anthropomorphism does not automatically translate to better interaction. They also found a nuanced result: participants interacting with less-anthropomorphic avatars reported more copresence and social presence than those interacting with highly anthropomorphic images, suggesting that overly realistic avatars set up higher expectations that go unmet, leading to reduced presence when the agent’s behavior does not match its appearance. In conversational VR agents equipped with LLM capabilities, both familiarity (whether the agent resembles a known person) and knowledgeability (domain expertise) influence user perceptions of the agent as credible, trustworthy, and worth future engagement (Yang et al., 2025). These studies establish that users actively evaluate agents along multiple dimensions—not just on what they say, but on whether they respond appropriately, attend to the user’s needs, and demonstrate coherent intentionality.

# 3.3 Ethical Considerations 

This study was conducted in accordance with ethical research principles. All participants provided informed consent via digital waiver forms prior to participation. Participants were informed that their involvement would require wearing a VR headset and interacting with a 3D avatar in a virtual environment, engaging in conversations across multiple experimental conditions, completing questionnaires and surveys, and participating in a brief debriefing session. The total time commitment was approximately 30 minutes in a single session. Data collection included automatically tracked data (response times and session logs with timestamps and conversation logs) and self-reported data (demographic information including age, gender, and VR experience level, as well as questionnaire responses regarding perceived latency, immersion, and user experience). To protect participant privacy, personal information was kept separate from research data. All data were stored securely on an encrypted, password-protected database with access limited to the research team. Participants were informed of potential risks including temporary symptoms such as motion sickness, nausea, dizziness, disorientation, eye strain, fatigue, headaches, and physical discomfort from wearing the VR headset. Participants were given the right to take breaks at any time or discontinue participation without penalty if they experienced discomfort. All participants confirmed their understanding that participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw at any time, that data would be kept confidential and used only for research purposes, and that they should inform the researcher immediately if they experienced any discomfort. 

# 3.4 Materials 

The study employed a Meta Quest 2 VR headset connected to a PC via Oculus Air Link. The virtual environment consisted of a cafe setting with a ReadyPlayerMe avatar (“Alex”) capable of full-body inverse kinematics, idle animations, and speaking animations. The system utilized Google Gemini 2.5 Flash API for natural language processing, Google Cloud Speech-to-Text for LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 13 transcription, and Google Cloud Text-to-Speech for audio synthesis. Four feedback conditions were implemented: 1. No Feedback: The agent remained in a standard idle state with no audio or visual indication during processing. 2. Visual Indicator: A 2D loading spinner appeared above the agent’s head 0.3s after the user finished speaking. 3. Verbal Fillers: The agent uttered a randomized, context-agnostic non-lexical filler (e.g., “Hmm ,” “ Let me see ,” “ Uh ”) selected at random from a predefined set of 24 pre-recorded utterances (see Supplementary Materials, Appendix C for the complete inventory) approximately 0.5s after the user’s speech ended. These fillers were not dynamically generated based on conversational content or emotional tone, representing a methodological choice to isolate the effect of verbal acknowledgment without introducing additional processing latency that we discuss in Section 5 (Limitations). 4. Gestures: The agent performed one of two “thinking” animations—tapping fingers on the table or folding his arms and looking around. 

# 3.5 Procedure 

We employed a within-subjects design to evaluate the impact of different feedback modalities on user experience during VR-based human-agent interactions. The experimental flow was designed to minimize learning effects while ensuring participants were fully accustomed to the VR environment before data collection began. The process followed a linear progression through three main phases: 1. Onboarding: Participants first provided informed consent and completed a demographic survey. To mitigate the “novelty effect” of VR, they underwent a two-minute trial session. LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 14 During this time, they practiced using the VR controllers and navigated the environment to become comfortable with the spatial layout and the agent’s presence (Figure 2A). 2. Experimental Trials: Participants completed four interaction sessions (no feedback, Verbal, Visual, Gesture) in a randomized order. Each session lasted until the participant successfully extracted 4 unique facts (approx. 5–8 minutes). 3. Data Collection: In each session, participants were tasked with extracting four unique “Life Facts” from the agent. These facts were randomly assigned and varied between sessions to ensure no overlap. Immediately following each session, participants completed a post-session survey, which included five questions chosen from Godspeed Questionnaire, six questions from Likert scale based custom questionnaire about each feedback modality and finally a open ended feedback question.

# 5 Discussion 

Our findings challenge a fundamental assumption in HCI design: that transparency always improves user experience. When developers show users “what the system is doing,” they often assume this helps. In VR, we found the opposite. While prior work advocates for transparency (letting users know the computer is processing), our results show that in immersive social environments, transparency can paradoxically degrade the user experience. This tension drives three main insights. 

# 5.1 The Diegetic Gap: Why Transparency Breaks Immersion in VR 

The visual spinner condition showed a statistically significant drop in perceived humanness (Anthropomorphism, p = 0 .002 ) and lifelikeness (Animacy, p = 0 .007 ). This finding directly contradicts a cornerstone of interaction design: Nielsen’s heuristic of “Visibility of System Status,” which recommends that systems always keep users informed about what is happening. In a traditional interface—a web page, a mobile app, a command-line tool—a loading spinner is genuinely helpful. It reduces uncertainty. Users want to know: “Is the system working or did I break something?” In VR, the same design pattern produces a radically different effect. When a spinner hovers above an avatar’s head in a 3D caf´ e, it violates what film theorists call the “diegesis”—the story world. A 2D floating icon tears users out of the social narrative and forces them to switch mental modes: from “I’m talking to a person” to “I’m monitoring a process.” This toggle is so jarring that users report the agent feels less human afterward, even though they now have better information about system status. # 5.2 Gestures as Social Keep-Alive Signals 

Embodied gestures succeed because latency in human-AI interaction is fundamentally a social event , not a technical delay. Humans don’t freeze while thinking—we shift weight, avert gaze, tap fingers. These conversational “floor-holding” signals indicate engagement and request patience. By replicating these cues, avatars reframe “the LLM is computing” into “the agent is thinking,” transforming latency from system failure into character expression. This aligns with grounding theory: mutual understanding emerges through non-verbal signals (backchannels, gaze, gestures) rather than explicit status messages (Andrist et al., 2013; Resnick et al., 1991). Participants recognized this: “I didn’t mind waiting when he looked like he was thinking; it felt polite to wait.” Embodied behavior doesn’t distract from latency—it reinterprets it as natural social interaction. 

# 5.3 Implications for AR/VR and Conversational AI 

These findings extend to any conversational interface. For mobile AR and partially-virtual agents, avoid explicit system UI (spinners, progress bars) for delays under 3–4 seconds. Use behavioral cues (head tilts, gaze shifts, gestures) that maintain the social frame. For voice assistants, verbal fillers must be contextually aware, reflecting the user’s emotional tone rather than using randomized utterances. The core principle: behavioral congruence trumps technical transparency . Socially appropriate responses (gesture, gaze, tone) maintain engagement despite latency. LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 24 

# 5.4 Design Framework: Embodied Wait States 

For developers building social VR, AR, and mobile experiences, we propose the Embodied Wait State framework as an alternative to traditional “loading states”: 1. Hide the System, Reveal the Character. For conversational delays under 3–4 seconds, avoid 2D UI overlays (spinners, progress bars). The cost to perceived humanness outweighs the benefit of technical transparency. Instead, use behavioral cues. 2. Embody the Wait. Implement procedural “thinking behaviors”: shifting weight, gazing upward thoughtfully, tapping fingers, or adjusting posture. These should trigger immediately upon detecting user silence and continue throughout the processing period. 3. Silence + Gesture > Random Fillers. If verbal fillers cannot be emotionally contextual, omit them. A silent, thoughtful pause with appropriate gesture is more credible than a misaligned vocal filler. 4. Test for Diegetic Consistency. Before deploying any feedback mechanism, ask: “Does this feel like part of the character’s world, or like a system overlay?” If it feels like the latter, redesign it. This framework is immediately implementable. Most VR/AR engines (Unity, Unreal) already support procedural animation and state machines; the design shift is conceptual, not technical. 

# 6 Limitations and Future Work 

Our study faced several constraints that frame our findings and point toward future research directions: • Network-Dependent Latency Variance: We relied on a cloud-based LLM (Gemini 2.5 Flash) via API, which introduced response time fluctuations ranging from 1.5s to 4.0s due LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 25 to network conditions. While our session randomization successfully distributed this variance evenly across conditions (Friedman test: χ2(3) = 1 .13 , p = 0 .769 ), preventing systematic bias, this variability may have weakened the precision of causal claims about feedback effectiveness. Future work should use on-device models (e.g., edge-deployed LLMs) to achieve millisecond-level latency control, enabling more precise testing of perceptual thresholds. • Context-Agnostic Verbal Fillers: To isolate the effect of verbal acknowledgment without introducing confounding processing delays, we implemented verbal fillers as randomized utterances from a fixed list (e.g., “ Hmm ,” “ Let me see ,” “ Uh ”). This meant fillers could not adapt to the emotional tone or semantic content of the user’s prior utterance. Due to a logging error, the specific filler played on each turn was not recorded, so effects of individual fillers could not be analyzed. Participants explicitly noted this limitation, reporting that fillers sometimes felt “jittery” or tonally mismatched. Future implementations should explore lightweight, low-latency sentiment classifiers or edge-optimized language models to generate contextually appropriate fillers in real-time (e.g., empathetic acknowledgments for serious topics, enthusiastic responses for casual exchanges). • Sample Homogeneity and VR Novelty: Our participant sample consisted predominantly of university students ( Mage = 21 .3 years), with 61.1% reporting minimal or no prior VR experience. While we found that feedback preferences were consistent across VR experience levels (gestures outperformed visual cues in both novice and experienced groups), the lack of age and cultural diversity limits generalizability. Older adults or cross-cultural populations might exhibit different tolerance thresholds for silence, or different interpretations of gestural cues. Additionally, the novelty effect of VR for our sample may have elevated baseline engagement, potentially masking subtler differences between conditions. • Animation Fidelity: The gestural animations were procedurally generated using standard LATENCY FEEDBACK IN VR AGENTS 26 inverse kinematics. Higher-fidelity motion capture data or real-time eye-tracking (allowing the agent to react to the user’s gaze direction) might yield even stronger effects for embodied feedback conditions. 

# 7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we decomposed the black box of multimodal latency feedback to evaluate its individual components’ impact on user immersion in VR. Explicit visual indicators, while functional, distract users and significantly reduce the sense that the agent is human-like. In contrast, subtle, embodied gestures successfully mask latency while enhancing the agent’s perceived animacy. As VR agents become ubiquitous in our lives, handling their latency and processing time will be just as critical as generating the words themselves. Our results advocate for a paradigm shift from technical wait states to Embodied Wait States —where the agent remains socially present, even when cognitively absent. By treating latency as a moment for character performance rather than a system loading error, designers can preserve the magic of immersion.
