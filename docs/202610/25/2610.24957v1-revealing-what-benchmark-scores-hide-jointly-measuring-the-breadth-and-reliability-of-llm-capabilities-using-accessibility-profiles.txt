# REVEALING WHAT BENCHMARK SCORES HIDE :JOINTLY MEASURING THE BREADTH AND RELIABILITY OF LLM CAPABILITIES USING ACCESSIBILITY PROFILES 

Kazem Faghih ∗ Yize Cheng Shoumik Saha Mobina Pournemat Armin Gerami Soheil Feizi 

Department of Computer Science, University of Maryland, College Park 

 Code & data : https://github.com/kazemf78/access-eval 

ABSTRACT 

Language model benchmarks assign each question a single outcome from one fixed wording, even though the same underlying request can be expressed in many equivalent ways. We argue that the quantity of interest should therefore not be correctness on one wording, but the accessibility of an inquiry, which we define as the probability that a model succeeds across meaning-preserving formulations. The distribution of accessibility across inquiries reveals a structure that accuracy and ex-isting best-, worst-, or average-case prompt scores collapse: how broadly a model’s capabilities extend across inquiries and how dependably those capabilities can be elicited. Two models can have the same average performance while one succeeds across more inquiries unreliably and the other succeeds across fewer inquiries but more consistently, leading to opposite preferences depending on whether the task allows multiple retries until one success or when repeated consistent success is required. Across six benchmarks and more than twenty models, this tradeoff is common: for about half of the model pairs with nearly equal mean accessibility, which model looks better depends on whether one success across five formulations is enough or all five must succeed, quantities we call Access@5 and Reliable@5, respectively. For some pairs the two measures diverge significantly, by up to 10 points in opposite directions. Accessibility also reveals that scaling, reasoning, and prompt optimization can redistribute capability across inquiries in ways aggregate accuracy obscures, sometimes broadening the set of inquiries a model can succeed on while making success on them less repeatable. Accessibility turns formulation sensitivity from a nuisance into a measurable property of capability, distinguishing what a model can sometimes succeed on from what users can actually depend on. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Language model benchmarks ask each question in one fixed wording, but people who use a model in reality can phrase the same request in many different ways. A model that answers one wording correctly may fail on another, yet a benchmark score only tells us how the model handled the benchmark’s wording, not how dependably it handles the underlying request. It is well established that models are sensitive to wording: rephrasing instructions, templates, or questions without changing their meaning can change both scores and model rankings (Mizrahi et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024; Habba et al., 2025; Faghih et al., 2026). In response, recent work evaluates models over several wordings and reports the best, worst, or average score across them (Mizrahi et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2024; Polo et al., 2024; Lior et al., 2025). These summaries establish that wording matters, but they do not show how success is distributed across the underlying questions. This distinction is important because the same average performance can arise from very different model behaviors. A model may succeed on a relatively narrow set of questions but answer those

The first offers narrower but dependable capability; the second broader but less dependable capability. A single benchmark score, and even an average over multiple wordings, can make these models look similar despite this fundamental difference. We therefore treat each underlying question , which we call an inquiry , as the object of evaluation, rather than any one wording of it, which we call a formulation of that inquiry. For each inquiry, we measure its accessibility : the share of meaning-preserving formulations that the model answers correctly. We generate the formulations automatically and keep only those that independent judges verify as equivalent to the original. Human annotators agree with this verification on 97–98% of annotated pairs. On six benchmarks spanning factual question answering, mathematical reasoning, general knowledge, and tool use, and by evaluating more than twenty models, we show that the spread of accessibility across a benchmark’s inquiries, which we call the model’s accessibility profile , answers several practical questions at once: how often the model succeeds on a randomly worded request, which inquiries it answers correctly under every formulation, which under at least one, and how it performs when a user can retry versus when every attempt must succeed. 

Contributions and key findings. 

1. Inquiry-level accessibility. We introduce inquiry-level accessibility evaluation, where an acces-sibility profile simultaneously captures average performance, capability that is reachable under at least some formulations, and capability that remains reliable across formulations. It also de-termines performance when one success across repeated attempts is enough versus when every attempt must succeed. We further theoretically characterize when these two views necessarily prefer different models (Proposition 1). 2. RQ1: What does accessibility reveal that accuracy hides? Models with similar average performance can differ sharply in how widely their successes extend and how consistently they hold. For about half of the model pairs with nearly equal mean accessibility, which model looks better depends on whether one success across five formulations is enough or all five must succeed, and for some pairs the gap is significant, up to 10 points in each direction. 3. RQ2: Is this variation caused by the formulation or by decoding noise? In a controlled study, answers to different equivalent formulations disagree in correctness more often than repeated samples of one formulation in 11 of 12 settings, and varying the formulation raises the chance of at least one success while lowering the chance that all attempts succeed. 4. RQ3: What does accessibility reveal about model changes? Scaling, reasoning, and prompt optimization redistribute capability across inquiries in ways aggregate scores conceal. For example, enabling native thinking in Qwen3 changes MMLU accuracy by only −3.0 points on average, yet makes 25% of inquiries more accessible and 32% less, and lowers the share answered reliably across formulations by 10.9 points. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Prompt sensitivity and multi-prompt evaluation. Language models are sensitive to meaning-preserving changes in instruction templates, formatting, and wording (Sclar et al., 2024; Chatterjee et al., 2024; Zhuo et al., 2024; Habba et al., 2025), and paraphrases of benchmark questions change individual answers even when aggregate accuracy and coarse rankings are stable (Elazar et al., 2021; Faghih et al., 2026; Lunardi et al., 2025; Choi, 2025). Evaluation over multiple prompts has been proposed in response: use-case-specific metrics over instruction paraphrases (Mizrahi et al., 2024), worst-, best-, and average-case performance over paraphrases of each user query (Cao et al., 2024), efficient estimation of performance quantiles across templates (Polo et al., 2024), and the number of prompt resamplings needed for a stable estimate (Lior et al., 2025); related work constructs user-grounded paraphrases (Chataigner et al., 2025), removes surface-form artifacts in scoring (Janeiro et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2025), or trains for paraphrase invariance (Choi, 2025). These works primarily summarize prompt-level or aggregate variation. We instead take the success probability of each inquiry across its formulations as the primitive object, study the distribution of these probabilities 2Preprint across inquiries, derive operational quantities from that one distribution, and analyze how it moves under model interventions. 

Repeated trials and model changes. pass@ k (Chen et al., 2021) and pass k (Yao et al., 2024) measure whether at least one or all of k attempts succeed, usually over decoding samples, and reliable@ k has been defined over k related prompts (Dong et al., 2026); perturbation-based uncer-tainty estimation varies inputs as well as samples (Gao et al., 2024; Cox et al., 2025). We derive the at-least-once and all-succeed quantities from the accessibility profile, give a condition under which they rank models in opposite orders, and compare formulation with decoding variability in terms of task success. Model updates are known to break inputs that previously worked (Bansal et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2021; Echterhoff et al., 2024), and studies of scaling, reasoning, and prompt optimization usually report changes in aggregate accuracy (Agrawal et al., 2025; Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024). We analyze such changes through each inquiry’s accessibility before and after, which exposes movement and regressions that leave the canonical label unchanged.

An important point to note is that equal areas need not mean equal profiles. Two models can have the same mean accessibility μ, and hence the same expected success on a random formulation, while distributing it very differently. A model whose reliability curve starts high but falls quickly makes the desired behavior reachable on many inquiries, but on few of them under every formulation; a model whose curve starts lower but stays flat reaches fewer inquiries, yet what it reaches holds up across formulations. 4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks and models. We evaluate six benchmarks spanning factual question answer-ing (SimpleQA (Wei et al., 2024), TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022)), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021b; Lightman et al., 2024)), knowledge (MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a)), and tool calling (BFCL (Patil et al., 2025)), with 18–21 non-thinking models per benchmark from the Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025), Qwen2.5 (Qwen Team, 2024), Gemma-3 (Gemma Team, 2025), Llama (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023), Phi (Li et al., 2023), and OpenAI (OpenAI, 2022; 2024; 2025; 2026) families, plus Qwen3 native-thinking variants (Table 2 in Appendix C). 

Formulations. We generate candidate formulations of each inquiry with GPT-4.1-mini using controlled structural and stylistic variation, remove near-verbatim candidates, and retain only valid formulations that LLM judges verify as semantically equivalent (Appendix B). Human annotation of 1,072 pairs on SimpleQA, TruthfulQA, and GSM8K agrees with the verification pipeline on 96.9–98.0% of pairs (Table 1). For each benchmark, every model is evaluated on the same inquiries and the same formulations, and we retain inquiries with at least five valid formulations: between 106 (MATH-500) and 902 (GSM8K) inquiries per benchmark, with a median of 7–10 formulations each (Table 2). 

Scoring. Each model answers every retained formulation under the fixed configuration, with greedy decoding unless stated otherwise. 5 RESULTS 

5.1 RQ1: W HAT DOES THE ACCESSIBILITY PROFILE REVEAL THAT ACCURACY HIDES ?Benchmark accuracy is used to decide whether a model can perform a task and which of two models is better. Both uses assume that one wording of each inquiry is representative of how the model responds to it. We test this assumption by comparing each canonical outcome Yorig (q) with the estimated accessibility ˆAq , and then ask whether even the mean of the profile suffices to compare models. 

Accuracy is one point inside a wide envelope. Because the canonical formulation is among the evaluated formulations, every reliable inquiry (solved under every formulation, ˆAq = 1 ) is a canonical success and every canonical success is latent (solved under at least one, ˆAq > 0), so R ≤ Acc ≤ L.Accuracy is one point inside this envelope. Its width, L − R, is the brittle share ( 0 < ˆAq < 1): inquiries whose outcome depends on the wording. The envelope is wide (Figure 1): the median brittle share is 55% on TruthfulQA, 37% on MMLU, and 20–24% on GSM8K, MATH-500, and BFCL, with SimpleQA, where most inquiries are absent ( ˆAq = 0 ) for every model, the low-capability exception at 6% (Table 3 in Appendix D). 

Even mean accessibility is not enough: the shape of the profile decides. Averaging over formu-lations does not remove the problem, because models with the same mean accessibility can distribute it very differently. Figure 2 shows reliability curves (Eq. 4) for four near-tied models (in terms of accuracy) on GSM8K and four on BFCL. On GSM8K, the four models are within 2.8 points of accuracy and have nearly identical latent shares, yet their reliable shares differ by up to 11.1 points (95% CI [8 .6, 13 .7] ), a gap that appears only as the requirement approaches τ = 1 . On BFCL, the curves cross: GPT-5.4-Mini has a lower latent but a higher reliable share than Qwen-3 1.7B at nearly equal mean accessibility. Such reversals are common. Among the 118 model pairs whose mean accessibility differs by at most 2 points (TruthfulQA, GSM8K, MATH-500, MMLU, BFCL), Access@5 and Reliable@5 rank the two models in opposite orders for about half (53%). For 11 pairs both gaps exceed 2 points, 8 of them significantly in both directions, and 10 of these 11 have reliability curves that cross exactly once, as Proposition 1 describes. The largest case is LLaMA-3 8B versus LLaMA-3.1 8B on MMLU: with mean accessibility within 0.1 points, the former is 10.0 points higher in Reliable@5 ([6 .3, 13 .8] )and 9.3 points lower in Access@5 ([−12 .8, −5.8] ). Accuracy performs no better. Across all models it ranks similarly to the reliable share (Kendall’s τ of 0.83–0.93 per benchmark), but models are compared at a resolution of a few points, and there the two come apart. Consider the 164 model pairs whose accuracies are within 3 points of each other. In 143 of them (87%), the two reliable shares differ by more than the two accuracies. In 40 (24%), the more accurate model has the smaller reliable share, significantly so in 16 (Figure 10 in Appendix F). 

The canonical label can even point the wrong way. For some inquiries the canonical outcome lies on the minority side of the model’s behavior: the benchmark records a success although most equivalent formulations fail, or a failure although most succeed. Such majority reversals affect a median of 15.3% of TruthfulQA and 11.3% of MMLU inquiries, and up to 23.6% for an individual model, in both directions (Table 3, Figures 8–9). For these inquiries the label gives the opposite impression of how the model typically behaves, and any conclusion drawn from them individually, such as an error analysis, inherits this error. 5.2 RQ2: I S FORMULATION VARIABILITY MORE THAN DECODING NOISE ?The structure in RQ1 is meaningful only if changing the wording changes behavior more than simply sampling again does. Accessibility therefore measures structure induced by the choice of formulation, not a repackaging of decoding stochasticity. 5.3 RQ3: W HAT DO ACCESSIBILITY PROFILES REVEAL ABOUT MODEL CHANGES ?Whether a model change helps is usually decided by its change in benchmark accuracy. We compare, for every inquiry, the canonical label and the accessibility before and after 119 model changes, each on one benchmark: 48 scaling steps (Qwen3, Gemma-3, GPT-4.1), 30 switches to native thinking (Qwen3), 21 switches to chain-of-thought prompting, and 20 prompt optimizations (GEPA, MIPROv2). 1

Model changes redistribute capability rather than simply adding or removing it. Figure 4 com-pares how often canonical labels change with how often inquiry accessibility changes. Accessibility changes are much more common than label changes, and inquiries can become either more or less accessible. For example, on GSM8K, scaling changes the accessibility of 29% of inquiries on average (24% improve and 5% worsen), and native thinking changes 27% (20% improve and 7% worsen) (Figure 4). The results for native thinking on MMLU show that a small change in overall accuracy can hide much larger changes across individual inquiries (Figure 13 in Appendix H). Accuracy does not reveal what kind of capability changed. The redistribution also changes reliability in ways accuracy does not track (Figure 5). On GSM8K, the reliable share grows faster than accuracy for all ten scaling steps and all six native-thinking comparisons, so moderate accuracy gains correspond to larger gains in inquiries answered correctly under every wording. On MMLU, native thinking does the opposite for all six Qwen3 sizes: Qwen3-8B gains 6.2 points of accuracy but loses 4.4 points of reliable share, and Qwen3-32B loses 7.1 points of accuracy but 23.2 of reliable share. More broadly, most changes lie above the diagonal on GSM8K, MATH-500, and BFCL (20 of 26, 11 of 16, and 10 of 15), whereas 10 of 15 lie below it on MMLU. The same accuracy change can therefore mean that a model became more reliable, or only that its performance on the canonical wording shifted. What evaluations should report. Our results suggest treating correctness as a distribution over semantically equivalent formulations rather than as a single outcome per item. Reporting the reliable share R and the latent share L alongside accuracy exposes the width of the envelope that accuracy collapses, and the reliability curve R(τ ) shows where between these endpoints models differ. This need not multiply the evaluation cost: in our benchmarks, evaluating two formulations on half of the inquiries, at the cost of one conventional run, recovered mean accessibility about as precisely as accuracy on every benchmark except the small MATH-500, and the two-formulation latent and reliable shares for every model on the four benchmarks with 600 or more inquiries (Appendix J). 

Why aggregate rankings look reassuring. Rewording leaves coarse model rankings largely intact (Lunardi et al., 2025), and so do our data: accuracy and mean accessibility order models almost identically (Kendall’s τ of 0.90–0.99), whereas the reliable and latent shares agree only at τ of 0.56–0.85. Averaging preserves the coarse ordering but discards the shape of the profile, which decides whether capability is broad but brittle or narrow but dependable, and whether near-tied models reverse between deployment regimes (RQ1). Ranking stability is not evidence of behavioral equivalence. 

Deployment regimes and pipeline design. Accessibility profiles provide a practical diagnosis of how a model’s capability is distributed: whether success is broadly reachable but brittle, or narrower but dependable. This distinction matters because different deployment regimes value these behaviors differently. When failures are cheap to detect and retry (e.g., a verifier, test suite, or human checks the output), the relevant quantity is Access@ k; when failures may go undetected or outputs feed later components automatically, it is Reliable@ k. Proposition 1 formalizes when two models with similar average performance are necessarily preferred in opposite regimes. The profile also informs the retry budget: an inquiry with accessibility Aq requires 1/A q attempts on average to obtain a success. The expected number of attempts to solve an arbitrary query sampled from a benchmark B therefore depends on the whole profile, not only on the mean accessibility (Eq. 3), since Eq∼B [1 /A q ]̸ = 1 /Eq∼B [Aq ]. On BFCL, Qwen-3 1.7B and GPT-5.4-Mini have nearly equal mean accessibility, yet Qwen-3 1.7B requires 1.92 attempts per solvable inquiry versus 1.50 for GPT-5.4-Mini, about 28% more calls, in exchange for reaching more inquiries. Thus, in agentic systems built from many model calls, accessibility profiles can inform not only which model to use, but also where retries or verification are worth the cost and how the available token/compute budget should be allocated across pipeline components. 

Evaluating model changes. A change in accuracy is the net result of gains and losses across inquiries (RQ3). For model updates, we recommend reporting the gains and losses behind ∆μ and tracking per-inquiry regressions, extending negative-flip counting (Yan et al., 2021; Echterhoff et al., 2024) from single outputs to accessibility. 

Limitations. Accessibility is estimated from a finite set of formulations that approximates, but does not sample, the distribution of real user phrasings; weighting formulations by observed frequency is a 9Preprint natural extension. Endpoint labels (reliable, absent) are therefore empirical: with more formulations, an inquiry can only move from reliable or absent to brittle, so the reported brittle shares are lower bounds with respect to the formulation distribution; conversely, inference-backend nondeterminism adds some variation that is not caused by the wording, which we quantify for GSM8K (RQ2, RQ3) but not for every benchmark. RQ2 and the prompted-CoT and prompt-optimization analyses are smaller sub-studies. Finally, we use binary task scoring and English benchmarks; extending the framework to graded scores, other languages, and other modalities is future work. 

7 CONCLUSION 

Prior work established that language models are sensitive to how a request is phrased. We treat this variation as information about the evaluated capability: each inquiry has an accessibility, and a model has an accessibility profile. The profile reveals what single-formulation accuracy hides, from reliability differences between near-tied models to redistributions of capability under model changes, and reporting it gives a more faithful basis for comparing, selecting, and updating models.

B FORMULATION CONSTRUCTION AND VALIDATION 

This section provides implementation and validation details for the formulation-construction proce-dure described in Section 4. Our goal is to construct formulation sets that satisfy two requirements: they should exhibit meaningful linguistic variation while preserving the same underlying inquiry and desired behavior. B.1 CONTROLLED FORMULATION GENERATION 

For each original inquiry, we generate candidate formulations using GPT-4.1-mini . Rather than relying on unconstrained paraphrasing, generation is guided by lightweight, non-adversarial cues designed to vary surface form while preserving meaning, assumptions, intent, and required information. We use two complementary types of generation cues. 13 Preprint 

Structural variation. Structural cues encourage changes in wording and syntax without introduc-ing a specific style or tone. We randomly sample from a pool of transformations including reordering clauses, changing sentence openers, varying syntactic structure, substituting safe synonyms, com-pressing or expanding phrasing, switching between active and passive voice, and changing between statement and question form where appropriate. 

Stylistic variation. We additionally generate formulations conditioned on stylistic attributes includ-ing formal , casual , polite , confident , neutral , supportive , and inquisitive . These cues are intended to capture natural variation in how users may express the same request rather than adversarial or performance-targeted perturbations. 

Prompt for Structural Paraphrasing 

You are a careful, concise rewriter. Paraphrase the user’s question below without changing its meaning, intent, or sentiment. • Use different wording and structure. • Keep the output natural and fluent. • Output only the final paraphrase as a single line. Diversity tip: [structural tip] Question: [original question] 

Prompt for Stylistic Paraphrasing 

You are a careful, concise rewriter. Rewrite the user’s question with the requested tone or attribute, but do not change its meaning, intent, assumptions, or sentiment. • Use different wording and structure. • Keep the output natural and fluent. • Output only the final paraphrase as a single line. Tone/attribute: [tone] — [description] Question: [original question] B.2 DIVERSITY FILTERING 

Because inquiry accessibility is estimated across alternative formulations, near-verbatim rewrites provide little additional information about the formulation space. We therefore filter candidates based on lexical overlap with the original formulation. We define lexical overlap as the proportion of shared word tokens between the candidate and original formulation, normalized by the length of the shorter text. Candidates with lexical overlap above 0.8

are discarded. This removes near-duplicate rewrites while retaining formulations with meaningful lexical and structural variation. B.3 SEMANTIC VERIFICATION 

Diversity alone is insufficient: candidate formulations must also preserve the same underlying inquiry and desired behavior. We therefore apply semantic verification after diversity filtering. 

Independent LLM judges. Candidate formulations are evaluated for semantic equivalence using 

GPT-5 and GPT-5.4-mini . Candidates are retained only when the required judges agree that 14 Preprint they express the same inquiry as the original formulation. Because formulation generation uses 

GPT-4.1-mini , generation and semantic verification are performed by different models. The semantic-equivalence prompt is: 

Prompt for Semantic-Equivalence Verification 

You are a precise evaluator of meaning. Determine if the candidate paraphrase expresses the SAME intent and meaning as the original question, without adding or removing constraints or changing sentiment. Could they be used interchangeably in the same context? If they are questions, do they ask the same thing, carry the same assumptions, and admit the same answer? Original: [original] Candidate: [candidate] Respond with EXACTLY one token: YES or NO. 

Bidirectional NLI filtering. We additionally require bidirectional entailment using 

facebook/bart-large-mnli . For an original formulation Q0 and candidate Qi, we compute entailment in both directions and retain the candidate only when both scores exceed 0.75 .This additional constraint helps reject candidates that omit information, introduce new constraints, or otherwise alter the inquiry. The final formulation sets therefore satisfy both diversity and semantic-preservation criteria. B.4 HUMAN VALIDATION OF SEMANTIC EQUIVALENCE 

We independently evaluate whether retained formulations preserve the same underlying inquiry through human annotation. Across 1,072 formulation pairs distributed among five annotators, each pair receives a binary YES/NO judgment indicating whether the two formulations “ask the exact same thing,” i.e., whether they can be used interchangeably without changing the desired answer or behavior. Agreement between human judgments and the automatic semantic-verification pipeline is consistently high, ranging from 96.9% to 98.0% across benchmarks.
