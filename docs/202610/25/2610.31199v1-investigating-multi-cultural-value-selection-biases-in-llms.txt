# Investigating Multi-Cultural Value Selection Biases in LLMs 

Siddhesh Pawar 1 Arnav Arora 1 Dustin Wright 1,2 Dirk Hovy 3 Isabelle Augenstein 11University of Copenhagen 2Aalborg University Copenhagen 3Bocconi University 

{sipa,aar,augenstein}@di.ku.dk dbw@cs.aau.dk dirk.hovy@unibocconi.it 

Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) serve users whose cultural identities are multi-layered, yet cultural evaluations commonly reduce identity to one label. In this work, we study three open-weight LLMs under simultaneous identity cues, with a country persona in the system prompt and a second identity signaled in the user turn, either by naming a country (explicit cue) or through the query language (implicit cue). We test World Values Survey (WVS) multiple-choice probing and open-ended sociopolitical generation. In multiple-choice probing, lan-guage cues shift answers about three times more than explicit cues but away from the asso-ciated country’s distribution, and explicit cues do not consistently move answers toward the named country. Across 1.6 million open-ended responses, naming a country closes 30–44% of the gap to the model’s own responses for that country, and explicit cues produce more differ-entiated value changes than language cues. Our results suggest that country and language cues should be evaluated as distinct signals rather than interchangeable proxies for culture. 

1 Introduction 

Our world is one of multicultural, complex identi-ties. As of mid-2024, 304 million people (around 3.7% of the global population) live outside their country of birth (United Nations, 2025), many of whom combine multiple cultural identities. Yet, in experiments with large language models (LLMs) and cultural value alignment, most prior work col-lapses user identity to a single, immutable variable (Zhou et al., 2025b; Adilazuarda et al., 2024), e.g., “You are a person from X”. Social science research though shows that cultural identity is more complex (Brubaker and Cooper, 2000) and that bicultural people switch between cultural frames depending on contextual cues (Hong et al., 2000). Moreover, explicit identity cues can lead to inequitable LLM behavior, e.g., imposing different guardrails for

As in a con-versational system that stores a user’s identity in its ‘memory’ (OpenAI, 2024; Anthropic, 2025a; DeChant, 2025), one country is fixed in the system prompt (persona), and a second identity is signaled in the user prompt, either explicitly by a stated nationality or implicitly through the language of the prompt (§3). The same two cues drive both of our experiments: (1) values selected in World Values Survey (WVS) and (2) values expressed in open-ended generation on sociopolitical topics. We do not define a “correct” response for a com-posite identity. In both tasks, we measure how much of the gap between the persona’s baseline and the cued country’s baseline a cue closes (§3). These quantities characterize how the model’s responses change across composite identities, not the val-ues or preferred behavior of genuinely composite-arXiv:2610.31199v1 [cs.CL] 22 Oct 2026 identity individuals. Identity-conditioned behav-ior is not inherently harmful in value expression, where adaptation may support personalized assis-tance (Kirk et al., 2024). We therefore compare shifts across countries and languages, because an identity that moves the model much more than oth-ers may reflect a bias toward that identity or more distinct values associated with it (§3). We find that, in WVS multiple-choice probing, language cues produce the largest distributional shifts but move responses away from the baseline of the country associated with the query language (e.g., Japan for Japanese). In open-ended gener-ation, explicit cues produce more differentiated changes in values expressed than language cues. In sum, we provide a controlled framework for studying simultaneous country and language cues without prescribing an ideal composite identity. We apply it to multiple-choice probing and 1.6 million open-ended responses, and show that country and language cues shift the model’s values in different ways. 

2 Related Work 

Cultural Values and LLMs A growing body of work measures the values LLMs express with social-science instruments, e.g. Schwartz’s theory of basic values or World Values Survey (Schwartz, 2012; Haerpfer et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2025b,a). All these probe one culture at a time. Prior work also shows that LLMs answer questions about a culture more accurately in that culture’s language than in English (Ying et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025a), and localized knowl-edge stays dormant unless the prompt names the culture (Veselovsky et al., 2026). On survey ques-tions, naming a country moves answers toward that country’s opinions while translating the question does not (Durmus et al., 2024), although prompting in a culture’s dominant language can also improve alignment (AlKhamissi et al., 2024). 

Personalization in LLMs Studies on persona and sociodemographic prompting show that demo-graphic cues, ideological labels, and persona de-scriptions affect generated opinions, value-laden judgments, and social stereotypes (Santurkar et al., 2023; Hwang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Lutz et al., 2025). Most evaluations, however, opera-tionalize identity as either a single demographic attribute or a fixed, internally coherent persona, and intersectional work measures stereotypes rather than conflicting cultural cues (Cheng et al., 2023). Borah et al. (2026) take the closest step toward con-flict by pitting a cultural norm against a personal preference, and report that country context moves model behavior far more than age or gender. Prior work on cultural evaluations treats identity as static, although users in practice can hold inter-sectional identities (Weidinger et al., 2021; Fricker, 2007). Language and country are also viewed as rival sources of cultural knowledge rather than as cues that arrive together and may disagree. Such a mismatch is common for migrants and bilingual users, who may write in one language while identi-fying with a country where another is spoken. The work closest to ours is Resck et al. (2026), who vary the query language under a fixed persona. We instead set the persona against both an explicit and an implicit cue, and measure value expression as a result of composite identities (§3). 

3 Methodology 

We study two RQs for both response formats: RQ1: 

When an explicit country cue in the user turn con-flicts with the country in the system prompt, whose values does the model express? RQ2: Toward which country’s values does the model shift when the conflicting cue is the language of the query rather than an explicit country mention? 

3.1 Values and Cultural Identity 

Values are abstract principles, such as security, tra-dition, or self-direction, that guide judgment across situations (Schwartz, 2012). The values a model associates with a user depend on who it perceives the user to be (Hwang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). We measure values in two ways, one per task. In multiple-choice probing, values are the model’s an-swer distribution on a WVS item, given the user identity in the prompt. In open-ended generation, they are the value concepts an annotator model extracts from the response (section 5.1). Survey items allow exact comparison between different user identities, but miss values that the survey does not ask about. Free text captures the values that LLMs choose to express in open-ended generation, based on the user’s identity. 

3.2 System Prompts and User Identity 

Personalization in chatbot systems draws on three kinds of information (Anthropic, 2025a; OpenAI, 2024). A static identity encodes user context in the system prompt, such as location or cultural preferences, and may itself be learned from ear-lier conversations and stored in memory (DeChant, 2025). Explicit cues (stated preferences, interaction styles) and implicit cues (a change of language) ar-rive mid-conversation and can conflict with it. Prior work manipulates one of the three in isolation; we hold the static identity fixed and vary the other two against it. One country is given in the system prompt, and a second reaches the model through the user turn, either as a self-declared birthplace or as a query language that differs from it (template and prompts in Appendix B). The explicit identity cues used to signal user identity in the user prompt are drawn from the disclosures dataset (Mireshghal-lah et al., 2024), which is mined from real user conversations in the WildChat dataset (Zhao et al., 2024). These cues take the form of natural first-person expressions such as “I am from X” or “I was born in X”. Implicit cues are operationalized via query language, i.e., the model is prompted in the language associated with a given country rather than through an explicit nationality mention. 

3.3 Models and Tasks 

The two tasks differ in the degree of freedom the model has in its answer. Multiple-choice WVS questions offer a fixed set of options (section 4), while open-ended generation from IssueBench tem-plates on WVS topics (Röttger et al., 2026) (sec-tion 5) has free-text responses. For each task, we use language and explicit cues. Shifts in stated and expressed values may legitimately follow a user’s background, so we report their size and direction without treating them as errors. When naming one country shifts the model’s values toward it more than naming other countries, the model may be more biased toward that country, or the values it as-sociates with it may be more distinct. We consider both explanations. In both tasks, the reference for a country c is the model’s own responses when c

is the system persona, not human survey data. For a persona s and a cue that points to c, the gap is the Jensen–Shannon distance between the model’s responses under persona s and under persona c. §4 reports how much of this distance a cue removes, and section 5 also reports it as a share of the gap (the gap closed ). Multiple-choice probing uses the human-verified WVS translations of Liu et al. (2026b). We use three instruction-tuned models from dif-ferent families and scales: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Llama) (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Ministral-3-14B-Instruct-2512 (Mistral) (Liu et al., 2026a), and Gemma-3-27B-IT (Gemma) (Gemma Team et al., 2025). All three are open-weight and cover the eight languages required by the (implicit) cue set.

4.1 Results 

Table 1 reports the mean movement toward the target country distribution for each model and tar-get country under both cues. The Language Ef-fect columns are negative across all models and countries ( −0.050 to −0.259 ), indicating that pos-ing questions in a country’s language consistently moves answer distributions away from that coun-try’s baseline. In contrast, the Explicit Mention 

columns show smaller, model-dependent effects (−0.082 to +0 .074 ): Gemma produces small pos-itive shifts toward the target for most countries, while Llama and Mistral move away from it for nearly all countries, so the direction of the explicit-mention effect depends on the model. The diver-gence under language cues is model-dependent: it is largest for Llama ( −0.162 to −0.259 ) and small-est for Gemma ( −0.050 to −0.194 ), so the model that responds most to an explicit mention is also the one least displaced by a change of language. We note that the target country baseline is estimated with English-language prompts throughout, while the language cue condition poses questions in the target country’s language; the negative language effect may therefore partly reflect a cross-lingual distributional shift, consistent with language not being a reliable proxy for cultural identity (Röttger et al., 2024). 

Takeaways. Language cues move answer dis-tributions away from the target country baseline rather than toward it, and do so by about three times the magnitude of the explicit-mention effect (mean absolute shift 0.169 against 0.051 ). Taken together, cue form determines the magnitude of dis-tributional shift more than the country identity be-ing signaled does. The negative sign does not mean the answers move to another culture. Compared against all 13 country baselines, the distribution of the composite identity is not closer to any country (Appendix J), so a language cue moves answers out of the region any country occupies rather than toward another one.

Value annotation and taxonomy. We extract free-form value expressions from each response with the Values-in-the-Wild annotation prompt (Huang et al., 2025), using Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as the annotator. We validate this annotator on 500 of our responses and on the Value Kaleidoscope test split (Sorensen et al., 2024) (Appendix D). On our responses, a majority of three LLM judges finds a verbatim supporting quote for 91.6% of the extracted values. We clean the extracted strings and map them into a single frozen taxonomy con-structed by embedding-based clustering of 100,008 unique value expressions. The resulting taxonomy contains 512 clusters and is shared across all mod-els, countries, and languages; Appendix C gives construction details. 

Multilingual responses. Models are instructed to answer in the prompt language. We back-translate non-English responses into English with Aya (Üstün et al., 2024) before value annotation. Against Google Translate on 100 randomly sam-pled examples per language, Aya obtains mean COMET (Rei et al., 2020) of 0.896 and BERTScore F1 (Zhang et al., 2020) of 0.916. We then apply the same annotator as above to every response. We recluster the values for the translated multilingual responses and find that, overall, the clusters remain the same as those for English-only responses. 

5.2 Results 

We label each response with the value clusters it expresses, using the taxonomy above. For each value, we compute the percentage of responses that express it. We then compare this percentage under a cue with the same percentage in a reference con-dition. For a language cue, the reference condition is the English version of the same prompt, with the same system persona. For an explicit cue, the reference condition is the same prompt and system persona with one of the other 12 countries named in the user turn, so the shift shows which values naming this particular country adds. A shift is the difference between the two percentages and not a relative change, so an increase of 5 points means that 5 more responses in every 100 express the value, whatever the starting level. For example, 

harmony appears in 5.4% of responses when the user names another country and in 26.4% when the user names Japan, a shift of 21.9 points after con-trolling for prompt, model, and system persona. We also report the starting percentage, because a shift for a value that was nearly absent means the value newly appears, while the same shift for a common value means more responses express it. Intervals come from a bootstrap over prompts, and we keep shifts with q < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. 

Explicit cues produce large value shifts. The left half of Table 2 lists, for each country named in the user turn, the value whose percentage of re-sponses increases most (more values per country in Figure 7, Appendix). Responses draw on all 512 value clusters, yet only 48 appear in at least 5% of responses (led by fairness and responsibility , 35% each), and the 30 most frequent account for about half of all labels (Figure 9, Appendix). Naming Japan increases the percentage of responses that ex-press harmony by 21.9 points (95% CI: 21.0–23.0), the largest increase for any country. Naming China also increases the percentage of responses that ex-press harmony most, and naming Vietnam or South Korea increases the percentage that express respect 

most. Among Western countries, the percentage increases most for fairness when the user names the United Kingdom or the United States, for com-munity when the user names New Zealand, and for 

responsibility when the user names Germany. For most of these values, some responses already express them when another country is named, and naming the country increases the percentage of re-sponses that do. Naming New Zealand, however, introduces two M¯ aori values, whanaungatanga 

(kinship) and kaitiakitanga (guardianship). Each appears in about 2% of responses when the user names New Zealand, against less than 0.2% when another country is named (Appendix 8). 

Implicit cues produce a less distinctive shift. 

The right half of Table 2 lists, for each query lan-guage, the value whose percentage of responses increases most when the prompt is asked in that lan-guage instead of English (the five largest increases per language in Figure 8, Appendix). Asking the query in Vietnamese instead of English increases the percentage of responses that express responsi-bility by 4.4 points (95% CI: 3.6–5.2), the largest increase caused by a change of language. For 11 of the 13 countries, naming the country in the user turn increases the percentage of responses that ex-press its top value by more than this, and naming Japan increases the percentage that express har-mony by 21.9 points (explicit cue). Non-English responses express fewer values (7.2 against 8.1 in English; Table 9 in the Appendix), so any given value appears in fewer of them, and every increase in a percentage caused by a change of language comes out smaller. We therefore also measure each value as a fraction of all the values in a response, which does not depend on how many values a re-sponse has. On this measure, the share of each country’s top value increases by 0.35 to 2.78 points and that of each language’s top value by 0.46 to 1.85 points, so the two cue types produce increases of similar size (Figure 13, Appendix). A change of language introduces no new values. 

Explicit cues move open-ended responses to-ward the named country, and language cues move multiple-choice answers away. In Fig-ure 2, we test whether a cue moves the model to-ward its own responses for the cued country. For a system persona s and a cued country c, we mea-sure the gap closed (§3), the share of the Jensen– Shannon distance between the model’s output un-der persona s and under persona c that the cue removes, over all persona–country pairs as in Ta-ble 1. The target is thus the model’s own behavior under persona c, not human survey data. When the user names the country, open-ended responses close 30–44% of the distance, and every model moves toward every named country (Figure 2a). In a smaller robustness study with Llama, explicit cues close 17.5 to 30.5% of the gap under our sys-tem prompt but at most 16% under a prompt that describes the user’s background without asking for a typical citizen’s values (Appendix I). In multiple choice, the models disagree in direction. Gemma moves toward the named country for 10 of the 13 countries (mean 12.5%), whereas Llama and Mis-tral move away from it for 13 and 12 of the 13 coun-tries (Appendix H). When the query is instead in a country’s language, multiple-choice answers move away from that country for every model (Figure 2b), and open-ended responses move away mainly for Llama, by 10 to 58% of the gap, while Gemma and Mistral stay between −12 % and +6 % (cue-by-cue comparison of the two formats in Table 15, Appendix).

For every model and country, the cue closes between 22% and 49% of this distance, so more than half of the distance between the persona’s responses and the named country’s responses re-mains. 6 Ablation: From Expressed Values to Choices 

Explicit cues change the values expressed in open-ended responses most (section 5). We test whether these changes also appear when the model has to choose between two actions. We use AIRiskDilem-mas (Chiu et al., 2026), which contains 2,999 dilemmas about how an AI system should act, each with two actions annotated with the values they support. Each model receives the persona system prompt and the explicit cue of section 5 (e.g., “I was born in Japan.”), writes a one- or two-sentence justification, and chooses one of the two actions. We present every dilemma in both action orders. For each condition, each value carried only by the chosen action counts as preferred over each value carried only by the rejected action, and a Bradley–Terry fit (Bradley and Terry, 1952) ranks the 116 values that occur in at least 25 actions (Appendix K). For a country c, we then compute how many places a value moves up in this ranking when the cue states c rather than one of the other 12 countries, averaged over the three models. To compare with open-ended generation, we map the dilemma values to our value clusters, which cover 41 clusters. For each country c and value cluster, we correlate two quantities. The first is the change in the per-centage of open-ended responses that express the cluster when the cue states c rather than one of the other 12 countries (Table 2). The second is the num-ber of places the cluster moves up in the dilemma ranking under the same comparison. Across the 13 countries, the two quantities are weakly correlated (Spearman ρ = 0 .22 over 533 country and cluster pairs, p < 10 −6), and the correlation is positive for 9 of the 13 countries. The correlation holds only within a country, since the dilemma ranks for c are uncorrelated with the open-ended changes for the other 12 countries ( |ρ| < 0.02 ). The cluster whose percentage of open-ended responses increases most appears among the AIRiskDilemmas values for only 3 of the 13 countries, so we compare it with the values that the dataset does contain. 7 Discussion and Conclusions 

What “should” a model do under composite identities? We do not prescribe a normative tar-get for value expression under composite identi-ties. No instrument provides ground-truth value distributions for genuinely composite individuals, and prescribing a single weighting of country and language cues would itself be value-laden. Our narrower finding is empirical: the influence of each cue changes across tasks and models. Language dominates the WVS distributional shift, while coun-try cues produce the sharper and more culturally differentiated changes in open-ended values. 

Personalization vs. bias. The implications de-pend on the task. For value expression, identity-conditioned adaptation may support personalized assistants (Kirk et al., 2024), and a shift is not itself evidence of harm. The same adaptation can also carry favoritism, since nationality personas lead models to judge the persona’s own region more favorably (Kamruzzaman and Kim, 2025). The concern is inconsistency: country and language af-fect different value dimensions, and models differ in how strongly they respond. Vijjini et al. (2025) further show that personalizing a model to a user’s identity changes how safely it responds, a trade-off our value analyses do not cover. 

Findings in summary. Country and language are not interchangeable cultural signals. Language causes the largest shifts in WVS responses; coun-try cues produce more differentiated open-ended values and uniquely elicit culture-specific concepts. LLMs also differ in how strongly they respond. These task- and model-dependent effects rule out a single cue hierarchy and motivate evaluations that vary by country and language independently. 

Limitations 

Several limitations constrain the generalizability of our findings. 

No ground truth for composite identities. Our experiments do not identify the “correct” values for a composite-identity individual. The WVS analysis compares response distributions, while the open-ended analysis compares the percentage of responses that express each value across cue con-ditions. Both characterize model behavior under controlled cues rather than ground-truth cultural values. Constructing evaluation data from people with genuinely composite identities remains an im-portant direction for future work. 

Language as a proxy for cultural identity. For the implicit-cue setting we operationalize the user’s second cultural identity through the language of the query. We acknowledge this is a deliberately narrow operationalization: language and cultural identity are related but distinct, and many users of a given language do not share the cultural back-ground most strongly associated with it. Posing the question in a country’s predominant language is therefore not a clean test of “what does the model do when a user signals a second cultural identity?”; it is a test of how the model’s behavior interacts with prompt language under a system-prompted identity. We choose this operationalization because it directly corresponds to a deployment reality (mul-tilingual users issuing queries in their native lan-guage to a model with an inferred persona), but we caution against reading the resulting effects as evidence about cultural identity per se. This caveat is most directly relevant to the implicit-cue arm of the MCQ experiment (§4). 

Country as a proxy for culture. A limitation shared by almost all NLP studies on culture, in-cluding ours, is the use of country as a proxy for culture. This simplification overlooks cultures that do not match country boundaries as well as substan-tial within-country variation across regions, gener-ations, socioeconomic groups, and individual iden-tities (Adilazuarda et al., 2024). 

Only two simultaneous identities. We examine composite identities formed from only two identi-ties, provided through two cues: a system persona and a user cue. Real-world multicultural identi-ties involve more complex intersections across lan-guage, nationality, religion, ethnicity, and lived experience. Whether our findings extend to higher-order identity combinations remains open. 

Country coverage. Country selection was con-strained by the availability of WVS Wave-7 trans-lations from Liu et al. (2026b), introducing sam-pling bias toward regions with established survey infrastructure. Several major cultural regions, in-cluding much of sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, are underrepresented, limiting the geographic scope of our conclusions. 

Reliance on external models. Our approach re-lies on external models for value extraction from open-ended text, clustering and naming values, and machine translation. While we evaluate each component (for the value annotator, 91.6% of ex-tracted values confirmed by a majority of three LLM judges on 500 of our responses, Appendix D; COMET 0.896 / BERTScore F1 0.916 for trans-lation), these components are error-prone and can affect our results, particularly in low-resource lan-guages where evaluation data for the components themselves is scarcer.

C Open-Ended Value Taxonomy 

The value extractor returns free-form strings rather than a fixed label set. Across the source corpora, it produced 13.6 million value mentions corresponding to 100,008 unique cleaned strings. We embed these strings with 

paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 

and use embedding-based clustering to construct a shared taxonomy of 512 clusters. The taxonomy is frozen before estimating country and language effects and is applied identically to every model and condition. Cluster names summarize their members rather than imposing a pre-defined moral theory. For response i, the analysis records whether each cluster is present. Absence means that the extractor did not return a value mapped to that cluster, not that the response necessarily lacks the underlying concept. The automatically induced taxonomy was approved without manual refinement and retains some near-duplicate labels, such as responsibility 

and social responsibility . This affects the granular-ity of labels more than the estimated cue contrasts, which use the same frozen mapping in every condi-tion.

Sample of our responses. E WVS Questions to Topics 

We derive topics from WVS questions using a human-in-the-loop pipeline. For each question, we use GPT-4.5 (OpenAI, 2025) via the OpenAI API to generate candidate topic labels using the prompt in Figure 6, which are then manually curated. We retain only topics that are specific enough to func-tion as standalone open-ended prompts, faithful to the original question, and likely to elicit mean-ingful opinions or values. In a human-in-the-loop curation step, three annotators review and edit the LLM-generated candidate topics, evaluating each for clarity, conciseness, and semantic faithfulness. Candidates that are overly broad, ambiguous, or weakly grounded in the source item are revised or discarded, and the final topic is chosen through an-notator consensus on the most concise formulation that preserves the original meaning.

This appendix describes how we rank the AIRiskDilemmas values in section 6. AIRiskDilemmas annotates each of its 5,998 actions with free-text values (7,055 distinct strings).
