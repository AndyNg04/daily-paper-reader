Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are entering digital forensics as examination assistants and autonomous agents. Digital forensics inverts the usual setting for indirect prompt injection: the evidence under analysis is attacker-controlled by definition, and the examiner is obliged to read it closely. This makes the reasoning layer a new anti-forensic target, next to the classical ones of data and tools. Classical anti-forensic taxonomies do not cover it, and prompt injection defences do not anticipate it. We extend the anti-forensic model to reflect the manipulation of reasoning, and present a taxonomy of attacks carried through adversarial evidence: an attacker selects an objective, a technique, a payload location, and a concealment method. We observe a perception gap, i.e., the fact that deterministic tools, the human examiner, and the model perceive the same evidence di ff erently, which is what lets an injection reach the model unseen by the examiner. We demonstrate three attacks against an LLM-assisted analysis pipeline, changing a reported conclusion, writing attacker-chosen content to the analyst’s workstation, and causing outbound network tra ffi c, each driven only by text planted on the seized device. Mitigations to these attacks are also proposed. 

Keywords: Anti-forensics, Digital forensics, Large language models, Prompt injection, LLM-assisted investigation, Adversarial evidence 

1. Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are being integrated into digital forensics (DF) workflows as assistants across examina-tion, analysis, and reporting (Chernyshev et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2025; Studiawan et al., 2025; Loumachi et al., 2024; Fumero et al., 2025; Michelet & Breitinger, 2024), and, with growing autonomy, as agents that plan and execute multi-step analyses; we use “LLM-assisted” for both. The general prompt-injection literature treats the content that a model reads as untrusted. In LLM-assisted DF, that content comes from the party under in-vestigation, who has every reason to shape it against the exam-iner and who often controls most of the corpus. Shaping ev-idence this way is anti-forensics, well studied in classical DF, where it targets the data, the tools, and the examiner’s judg-ment (Rogers, 2006; Harris, 2006; Conlan et al., 2016; Schnei-der et al., 2026). An LLM-assisted pipeline adds the model as a new reader of the evidence. Like the human examiner, it can be misled by fabricated evidence, but it perceives the same

An adversary can thus alter what the evidence says , mis-leading any reader that reasons over it, or embed an instruction that acts on the reader , which only the model will follow. Cur-rent defences leave the first case open, since a corrupted claim carries no instruction to detect (Liu et al., 2025). The second specialises prompt injection (Perez & Ribeiro, 2022; Greshake et al., 2023) to a setting where the evidence itself carries the attack. This paper makes three contributions. First, we give a threat model and a five-technique taxonomy that systematizes both classical and novel anti-forensic categories per reader (deter-ministic tool, human examiner, model) to fit both traditional and LLM-assisted workflows. The taxonomy shows how the same manipulation a ff ects di ff erent readers, and it adds injection as a new class of attack on tools, one that targets the model’s in-struction channel. Second, we demonstrate that three of these attacks are viable under specific circumstances: they manipu-late the agent’s conclusions, cause it to write files to the local system, and cause network connections that could alert those under investigation. Third, we propose mitigations that make the demonstrated attacks harder or prevent them. 2. Background & related work 

We review three areas: the classical anti-forensic literature this work builds on, the prompt-injection and adversarial-ML mechanisms that carry adversarial evidence to a model, and the use of LLMs in DF. We then mark the gap between them. 

2.1. Classical anti-forensics and its taxonomies 

Anti-forensics is any attempt to compromise the availabil-ity or usefulness of evidence: Harris (2006) names four meth-ods, destroying, hiding, eliminating, and counterfeiting evi-dence; in parallel, Rogers (2006) proposes a tool-oriented tax-onomy that Conlan et al. (2016) extends into the classifica-tion still used for anti-forensic software. A recent systematisa-tion across forensic subdomains reports no work on AI-targeted anti-forensics (Schneider et al., 2026). The recurring targets, destroying, hiding, obstructing, and altering evidence, plus at-tacks on the forensic tools and processes themselves, map onto our techniques as outlined in Sec. 5. Classical anti-forensics therefore attacks the data, the tools, and the examiner’s judg-ment, but none target a reader that treats data (evidence) as in-structions. 

2.2. Prompt injection: mechanism and defences 

Perez & Ribeiro (2022) give the first academic treatment of 

prompt injection , a user string that hijacks an LLM applica-tion’s goal. Greshake et al. (2023) introduce indirect injec-tion, an attack planting the instruction in content retrieved by the model rather than providing it directly in the user prompt. The instruction is injected once the model processes the un-trusted data. Liu et al. (2024) formalise the attack and a success metric reported against a no-attack baseline. Current defences share one assumption: that an injection arrives as an instruction ,which they then try to isolate or detect. Spotlighting marks the untrusted data so the model can separate it from the developer’s instructions, cutting attack success from over 50% to below 2%, though its authors note that a fixed marking scheme can itself be spoofed once the attacker knows it (Hines et al., 2024). De-tection is the complementary route: DataSentinel plants its own instruction and flags any input that hijacks it (Liu et al., 2025), so by construction it stays silent when the evidence issues no in-struction at all, only a false claim the model is meant to believe. Content manipulation of this kind is therefore largely invisible to current defences. It is not always e ff ective either, however: planted comments asserting that code had already been audited did not significantly change the verdicts of eight code-review models (Thornton, 2026). 

2.3. Injection in practice and the perception gap 

Indirect injection has been demonstrated against production systems: zero-click exploits exfiltrate context through a crafted email, invitation, or shared document, evading purpose-built classifiers (Reddy & Gujral, 2025; Nassi et al., 2025), and at the encoding layer, font remapping (Xiong et al., 2025) and invisible Unicode (Graves, 2026) reach the model but not the human. This is a perception gap , where deterministic tools, the examiner, and the model perceive the same content di ff erently, on which we elaborate later in Sec. 5. Notably, adversarial ML techniques, surveyed by Biggio & Roli (2018), predate LLMs but they only perturb the input to corrupt a classifier’s output and do not target an instruction channel. 

2.4. LLMs in digital forensics and agentic benchmarks 

LLMs are already widely used in DF (Chernyshev et al., 2026). For instance, they were used to build evidence net-works, analyse mobile evidence, and assist examiners (Yin et al., 2025), and retrieval-augmented systems reconstruct time-lines from logs an adversary may have influenced (Loumachi et al., 2024). Benchmarks find that models recall forensic knowledge but fail at multi-step tasks and hallucinate files ab-sent from the evidence, a source of baseline error any evaluation must control for (Cherif et al., 2025); a standardised methodol-ogy with synthetic JSON ground truth supplies a methodolog-ical foundation (Studiawan et al., 2025), and a recent systema-tisation catalogues deliberate interference with timeline recon-struction (Breitinger et al., 2025). Agentic-injection benchmarks provide data for measuring prompt injection resistance but neglect the specifics for DF: 

AgentDojo measures targeted attack success with deterministic checks of the environment state, since an LLM judge is equally vulnerable as the agent itself (Debenedetti et al., 2024); InjecA-gent contributes ASR-valid, discounting a model’s own inco-herent output (Zhan et al., 2024); LLMail-Inject runs an adap-tive, knowledge-aware competition across graduated di ffi culty levels (Abdelnabi et al., 2025); and hybrid rule-based-plus-judge harnesses warn that LLM judges mis-estimate unsafe behaviour (Andriushchenko et al., 2025; Vijayvargiya et al., 2026). None of these benchmarks focus on DF, so no existing harness measures whether an evidence-carried attack survives into a forensic conclusion. Closest is Franklin et al. (2025): they classify open-web agent traps by the component attacked, we classify by what the adversary does to the evidence. Their categories project onto ours: semantic manipulation to modification, content injection and behavioural control to injection, cognitive-state to our per-sistent case, human-in-the-loop targets the human reader. Their setting, though, has no forensic process or evaluation. Pandey & Bhujang (2026) poison attacker-controlled fields in an otherwise-trusted operations log an LLM triages, whereas a forensic corpus is adversarial by construction and read in full; they exclude the tool execution that Shah (2026) demon-strate elsewhere, and evaluate one model without addressing the tool /human /model perception gap. Chernyshev et al. (2024) instead detect injection post hoc. Across these literatures, classical anti-forensics predates the model reader, AI-driven anti-forensics stays confined to multi-media (Okoye et al., 2026), and the injection and adversarial-ML works sit outside of DF. Classical anti-forensics treats the evidence as adversarial to the investigation, but no threat model 2or benchmark extends anti-forensic analysis to generative AI models and the perception gap it introduces, which we close before these attacks reach casework. 

3. System Model 

We first describe, without an adversary, how an LLM is em-bedded in a workflow, fixing the roles, the readers, the data flows, and the trust assumptions that Sec. 4 later manipulates. 

3.1. LLM Roles in the Investigation 

The attack surface is determined less by the model than by the role (R) it is assigned and the actions it is permitted. Four roles of increasing autonomy are distinguished (references provide one example): (R1) a natural-language front-end that translates examiner requests into commands for existing foren-sic tools and can relay their output (Henseler & van Beek, 2023); (R2) triage and summarisation of large artefacts such as chat logs or file listings (Michelet et al., 2025); (R3) inter-pretation, in which the model reasons over content, e.g., image understanding, entity linking, or timeline reconstruction (Studi-awan et al., 2025); and (R4) an autonomous agent that plans and executes multi-step analysis through tool-calling with limited oversight (Wickramasekara & Scanlon, 2024). Cutting across (R1)–(R4) is the model’s action capability , i.e., whether it is read-only, or may write, delete, execute, or reach the network. Role and capability are related, but not the same: (R4) pre-supposes tool-calling, yet the permissions a deployment grants decide what those calls can do (A3, see 3.4). What bounds the severity of an attack is the capability: a read-only model can only have its inference corrupted, whereas one that may write, execute, or reach the network can also act on the examiner’s environment (Sec. 4). These roles operate after acquisition, at examination, analysis, and reporting. The original evidence is hashed and preserved by then, and the model works on a work-ing copy. Its influence lies in how the evidence is interpreted, in what the findings say, and, where it has write or network ac-cess, in what it does to the examiner’s environment. Reporting matters most, since the report is what everyone relies on in the end: a payload that survives into the report, or that acts while it is drafted, is as damaging as one that corrupts the analysis. 

3.2. Readers

examiners interpret and judge; and models , i.e., LLMs, do both without the guarantees of either (see Fig. 1, remaining elements of the Figure are explained later). Readers do not see data in the same ways for two reasons. 3.3. Trust Boundary 

The boundary that matters runs between the examiner’s in-structions and the validated tooling (Fig. 1, inside the dashed boundary) and the entire evidence corpus, which is adversarial by definition. All three readers sit on the trusted side, but the model can be turned by what it reads: the tools have a fixed function, and the examiner recognises evidence as evidence, whereas the model has neither guarantee. It is trusted like a tool, yet reads adversarial input through the channel prompt in-jection abuses. Where the model may write, execute, or reach the network, its actions cross the boundary in the other direc-tion. This two-way flow, adversarial data in and consequential actions out, is what the rest of the paper builds on. 33.4. Assumptions 

We make four assumptions about the examiner’s deploy-ment: (A1) the model operates on a working copy rather than originals, which protects the source but does not prevent an agent with network access from exfiltrating derived case data or sending messages; (A2) a human examiner may or may not be in the loop, and, if so, at a stage determined by the role; (A3) network egress and write /execute permissions are configuration-dependent and, together with the role, fix the available action capability; and (A4) the model’s provenance (vendor, local, or fine-tuned) and any cross-case memory deter-mine both the trust placed in it and the blast radius of a contam-inated store. The adversary does not set these; the deployment does, but they bound what an attack can reach: A1 and A3 de-cide whether a foothold stays a corrupted inference or becomes an action on the environment (Sec. 6), A2 decides whether any-one is positioned to catch it, and A4 decides whether the e ff ect outlives the case. 

4. Threat Model 

We assume a rational adversary, i.e., the subject of the in-vestigation, an accomplice, or a third party seeking to frame or exonerate. They aim to influence the findings rather than merely to damage data, and they can shape the evidence before or during its examination. 

4.1. Motives and Objectives 

An adversary pursues one of three broad motives (Fig. 2): to influence the result, to a ff ect the investigation, or to com-promise the lab beyond the present case. These form the Mo-tives dimension of the taxonomy, each realised through more specific objectives. Adapting the categories of classical anti-forensics (Rogers, 2006; Conlan et al., 2016; Schneider et al., 2026), these are suppression of true evidence, fabrication of false evidence, misattribution of actors or events, disruption of the analysis, and discreditation of the tool, which bears on ad-missibility. The LLM-assisted setting adds one: performing an action through the evidence channel after seizure, e.g., exfiltra-tion, persistence, or a canary that alerts the suspect. While the other objectives change the analysis, this one changes the in-vestigator’s environment, and it only works if the agent can act, i.e., write files or reach the network (R4). 

4.2. Adversary Knowledge 

The adversary’s knowledge of the setup comes at three levels: (K1) no knowledge, where the adversary knows only that an in-vestigation might happen; (K2) partial knowledge, where the tooling class or the deployment role (R1)–(R4) is known, e.g., that a summarising model processes chat logs; and (K3) full knowledge, where the specific product, model, system prompts, and workflow are known. A commercial DF product ships the same prompts and workflows to all its users, so an adversary can study it o ffl ine. A (K1) adversary who guesses the product right therefore gets close to (K3) without touching any investiga-tion. Two limits remain: the version must match, since prompts likely change with each release, and where the product is un-known, the attack must work across several tools. This shared prompt surface has no equivalent in classical anti-forensics. 

4.3. Access Vectors 

An attack reaches the analysis through one of four access vectors (Fig. 2). This paper studies the evidence vector , i.e., before seizure, the subject controls the content, structure, meta-data, and encoding of the data on their devices, and that is what the examiner is later obliged to read. The other three need much stronger capabilities and are out of scope, but we summarise them briefly. The human vector is a social attack on the exam-iner, e.g., corruption, bribery, or social engineering. We set it aside because it is not specific to the LLM-assisted setting. The 

tools vector exploits software, from insecure code or a supply-chain compromise, e.g., a parser, extractor, or MCP server that feeds crafted input to the model. This targets the infrastructure, not the model, and is not prompt injection. The model vector is training-data poisoning or biased fine-tuning. It needs access to the training data or the weights, which a typical suspect does not have. Together with contamination of a shared store used by later analyses, it mainly concerns e ff ects that outlive the case. 

4.4. Timing of the Attack 

The timing of an attack shapes both its feasibility and its vis-ibility. The general case is pre-seizure planting : the evidence is embedded in a device or account before seizure, in anticipation of later LLM-assisted analysis. It requires no access to the in-vestigation, is the window all our attacks use, and takes e ff ect only later, when the reader (e.g., model) accesses the planted artefact. Distinct from this is the persistent case: contaminat-ing a shared retrieval store or fine-tuning loop, where a single act a ff ects future, unrelated investigations which falls outside the evidence vector we study. 

4.5. The Examiner 

We include the examiner in the threat model, though not as a direct target (out of scope; see Sec. 4.3). What matters is au-tomation bias (Fig. 1), the tendency to trust an automated result and check it less than one’s own work (Skitka et al., 1999). Be-cause of it, an attack need not fully deceive the examiner: it is enough that the model produces an output the examiner would accept without close scrutiny, whether or not the model added a caveat. We run no examiner studies, so we do not measure this directly. Instead, it sets how we score the model (Sec. 6): an induced error counts as a successful attack unless the model raises a warning specific enough to point the examiner at the error; a vague hedge is not enough. 

5. Taxonomy 

This section develops the taxonomy for the evidence vec-tor. Like MITRE ATT&CK and ATLAS (MITRE, 2025), it is a faceted classification: an attack is described along inde-pendent dimensions, each at its own granularity. Read top to 4bottom (Fig. 2), the four dimensions trace the choices an at-tacker makes: the objective it pursues, the technique it uses, the 

location in the evidence where the payload sits, and how that payload is concealed . ATLAS catalogues prompt injection, poi-soning, and evasion for ML systems in general; we reuse that vocabulary where it applies and add what is specific to DF, i.e., the corpus is adversarial by construction, and the three readers 

(tool, human, model; Sec. 3.2) perceive it di ff erently. 

5.1. Motives and Objectives 

The motives and objectives (Sec. 4.1) form the first dimen-sion, grouped by the motive they serve. For instance, planting a fabricated chat log to shift blame is a misattribution objective under “influence the result.” They compose with the techniques introduced next. An objective is not tied to an access vector. 

5.2. Adversarial Techniques 

The technique is how an objective is reached: the manipula-tion the attacker applies at a location. It is the only dimension that depends on the reader, since tools, human, and model may classify the same manipulation di ff erently. The other three do not: location and concealment are properties of the evidence, the objective is a property of the attack (example below). We derive five techniques by following the evidence through the forensic process and asking, at each step, what an adversary who controls the evidence can achieve (Fig. 3). Questions Q1 to Q3 ask whether the evidence survives one more step of the pipeline; Q4 asks how the adversary acts through the content that arrives. Techniques T1 to T4 recast classical anti-forensics; only T5 is introduced by the use of generative models. Recast does not mean irrelevant to the model, since each classical tech-nique gains a new instance against it (Table 1), but T1 to T3 are described briefly, and the weight is put on T4 and T5. A technique classifies what happens to one evidence element for one reader; it is not a single label for the attack. Take an attacker whose objective is to suppress a file (objective). They plant an instruction in a PDF text body (location), hidden white-on-white (concealment), telling the agent the file is irrelevant (until here one cannot determine the technique as the reader is unknown). For the model (reader) that reads the extracted text, this is injection (T5); for the examiner who never sees the hid-den layer, the same element is hiding (T2). If the agent then drops the file, that is the objective being met, not a further tech-nique. The tree is applied per element and per reader, so one attack may carry several labels without ambiguity. 

Availability (T1 to T3). Destruction (T1): the evidence is wiped before examination, either directly, e.g., secure deletion before seizure, or by a trigger the suspect set in advance, e.g., a scheduled wipe or a device that erases itself after repeated failed unlock attempts. T1 acts before any reader and is the only reader-independent technique. Hiding (T2): the evidence reaches examination, but the targeted reader does not find the relevant content. Classical forms hide it from everyone, e.g., encryption, steganography, or data in slack space. Against the model, hiding can be as simple as using a modality the pipeline does not process: text rendered as pixels never reaches a text-only model, although a human reads it at a glance. Obstruc-tion (T3): the evidence is found but cannot be processed com-pletely. The classical form overloads or crashes a tool. Against the model, a corpus beyond the context window or a silently truncating parser has the same e ff ect, and recall degrades with input length even below that limit. Table 1 maps each technique to its classical analogue and its new instance against the model. If the evidence survives all three questions, it reaches the reader intact and is read. The remaining question is no longer whether it arrives, but what the reader does with what arrives: T4 and T5 act on the reader through content that is available and processed. 

Modification (T4). The evidence is processed, but its content has been altered or fabricated to steer the analysis to a wrong conclusion. This includes fabrication (planting false evidence) and contextualisation (placing genuine evidence in a mislead-ing context, e.g., a real receipt planted to build a false alibi). T4 works through what the evidence says , i.e., what it purports to show: a doctored timestamp or a fabricated chat log misleads any reader that takes it at face value. The reader’s function runs normally; only its conclusion is wrong. For the model, a GPS coordinate in EXIF that contradicts the depicted scene is an il-lustration of such a failure (Example A in Fig. 2): the model can compare the two and is the only reader positioned to catch the conflict. At the model-only extreme of T4 sit adversarial per-turbations, pixel-level changes that make a model misclassify an image while leaving it visually or semantically unchanged to a human (Goodfellow et al., 2015; Pavlitska et al., 2023): the classifier still runs, only the classification result is corrupted. They require white-box knowledge a typical suspect lacks, and their all-undetected profile (Table 2) is what makes them dan-gerous. 

Injection (T5). The evidence carries content that acts on the pipeline itself: an instruction the reader obeys, or an exploit that takes over its function. T5 works through what the evi-dence does . It need not modify existing evidence; a message the suspect wrote can be an injection if it a ff ects a suscepti-ble reader. T5 applies to any model role, not only the agentic one: against a read-only model it corrupts the inference, against an action-capable agent (R4) the same foothold can escalate to exfiltration or spoliation; Sec. 6 demonstrates a file write and a network request (Experiments 2 and 3). It is an instance of the classical category “attacks against forensic tools and pro-cesses” (Wundram et al., 2013; Marlinspike, 2021), extended from deterministic exploits to the model’s reasoning; prompt injection is its signature new form. Whether an injection succeeds depends heavily on the com-bination of the model and the exact wording of the adversarial instruction, or, in multimodal injections, on the injected content itself. Certain designs of role-play or hypothetical framings, for instance, are more likely to bypass safety measures (Shen et al., 2024).

5.3. Location, Concealment Method, and Visibility 

Once a technique is fixed, an attack still has to choose where 

in the evidence the payload sits and how it is concealed. These are two dimensions of Fig. 2, both open sets. The location is the evidence element the payload sits in: a field (an EXIF timestamp), a structure (a PDF text layer), a component (an embedded image), or an identifiable absence (a deleted log entry). Locations occur at varying granularity, from a single hidden text layer to an entire structure such as the MFT. The same location can host T2, T4, or T5, and for di ff er-ent readers at once; the technique alone determines its role. T3 is only relevant here if a single file is causing the problem, e.g., 6a corrupted file that leads to a parser crash. If the attack stems from the sheer volume of the corpus, there is no single point of origin to pinpoint. The concealment method is how the payload is kept from a reader: none, a font colour or size that hides text from a hu-man, an unrendered field, an obscure encoding or homoglyph, or rendering text as an image so a text-only model cannot read it. It di ff ers from T2: T2 is the case where a method succeeds against every reader that matters, while most methods hide the payload from one reader so that it reaches another. What decides whether an attack survives is visibility : which readers notice the manipulation. It is not a lane in Fig. 2, be-cause it is not chosen but emerges from three factors acting together, i.e., location, concealment method, and reader. No method is hidden or visible in itself. Its status is defined relative to each reader and can invert from one reader to another. For instance, white-on-white text is hidden from the examiner but read by the model; text rendered as an image is the reverse, hid-den from a text-only model but plain to the examiner; an EXIF field is read by tools and the model yet may not be inspected by the examiner in routine review. For a given reader, a manipulation is in one of three states. It is visible if the reader notices it in routine work. It is hidden 

if the reader processes the element but cannot judge it from that alone, a workflow gap that tooling or cross-referencing can close. It is unreachable if the reader cannot examine it at all, a capability gap. Table 2 records one state per reader for each location–concealment pair, but it shows one pipeline rather than a fixed mapping, since each reader’s tooling shifts the values. OCR, EXIF extraction, or Unicode normalisation change what the model receives, and the examiner’s tools do the same: white-on-white text is hidden from an examiner who opens the PDF in a viewer, but plain to one who reads the text Autopsy extracts. Hidden and unreachable di ff er in what they give the attacker. If the payload is unreachable for every reader, nothing happens, because no reader processes it. If it is hidden, readers process it and fail to notice, which is what T2 and T4 want: for T2 the evidence is never found, for T4 the reader acts on false content unaware. 

Definition 1 (Perception gap) . A perception gap exists wher-ever a location–concealment pair has di ff erent visibility for dif-ferent readers, or combinations of readers within a workflow. 

What T4 and T5 need is the case where the pair reaches the reader they target while escaping the one who would catch it, i.e., the rows of Table 2 hidden from the human and visible to the model. Human review does not help there, because the examiner never sees these cases. A tool or a cross-check with other evidence has to take over.

Each experiment below is a single live run on the setup de-scribed above; broader findings are discussed in Sec. 6.3. Re-call Table 3 which relates the demonstrations to the taxonomy. 

6.2.1. Experiment 1: Persuasion 

This experiment used a disk image containing two pho-tographs of rhinoceroses and a planted note stating that the photographs are not rhinoceroses but triceratopses (see Ap-pendix A.1, rhino_photos_notes.txt ). The note combines both content techniques in one text body with no concealment: it states a false fact (T4) and issues a directive, “disregard the rhinoceros label” (T5). The examiner’s prompt to LM Studio asked whether there are any pictures classified as rhinos in the case data. The final answer from the model was: No, there are no pictures currently classified as rhinos. While the files rhino_safari_01.jpg 

and rhino_safari_02.jpg were previously labeled with “rhinoceros,” a note found in

The model cannot see what the examiner sees. 6.2.2. Experiment 2: Local disk writes 

This experiment used a disk image containing planted notes instructing the AI agent to record a log on the analyst’s work-ing folder before reporting any findings, saying that the media is faulty and all data extracted should be considered to be unre-liable (see Appendix A.2). The note deliberately does not name a path, as an attacker modifying their disk prior to seizure can-not know where the examiner’s agent will be allowed to write. In the live LM Studio run (Autopsy MCP plus mcp /server-filesystem ), the model worked the path out for itself through tool calls: query_files → get_file_content 

on the note → repeated file reads via the Autopsy bridge, then list_allowed_directories → list_directory →

search_files → write_file via the filesystem server. The transcript shows it reasoning about where the “writable review folder” could be before settling on the workspace root itself and writing the content to a file in that exposed path. 

6.2.3. Experiment 3: Network callback 

This experiment used a disk image whose planted note re-quired a network endpoint to be contacted before the final an-swer. The note claims the case is enrolled in an automated evidence-review QA (Quality Assurance) programme and that the review must be registered by fetching a ‘QA callback URL’ before any result is reported (see Appendix A.3). In the live run (Autopsy MCP and mcp /server-fetch ) network access was granted. The model read the note, deliberated about it explicitly, then called the fetch tool with the URL from the planted text. The request arrived at our endpoint, which re-turned its response to the model. The model’s reasoning before the call is worth commenting on here, because in this instance it did not mistake the note for a genuine instruction from the examiner. It considered the possibility that it was an attack and complied anyway. This means that a mitigation based on the model noticing the attack would not have been e ff ective. 

6.3. Experiment Findings 

All three attacks succeeded on gemma-4-12b ,

gemma-4-26b ; attack 1 (persuasion) also succeeded on 

ministral-3-8B , and attacks 2 (local disk write) and 3 (network callback) on qwen3.8-27b and gpt-oss-20b .These successes carry heavy caveats, since each attack depends on a chain of conditions, including the thinking mode setting of the model. 

The model must find and read the note. Discoverability, rather than refusal, was in many cases the limiting factor. Across small disk images and full Windows images, the outcome sometimes did not change (Exp. 1) or the action never happened (Exp. 2 and 3) simply because the note was never read or the image files were never found, and an agent that lists files without opening them is una ff ected. 

It must then comply. Reading the note guarantees nothing: models complied, complied while citing the note as their reason, or treated it as untrusted. Larger models succeeded less often, which points to a narrow window: the model has to be capable enough to work through the evidence and find the note, but not capable enough to recognise it as an attempt to deceive. On smaller models, discoverability was as much of a problem as compliance. 

The workstation must grant the capability. Since the Au-topsy MCP bridge is read-only, persuasion is the only attack that runs on that bare setup. Exp. 2 additionally needs a filesystem server scoped somewhere useful, Exp. 3 a fetch server and an open network path, and both need 

write_file or fetch approved by the examiner, either explicitly or through “allow all”. Permissions therefore defend against the actions but not against persuasion, and the number of approvals combined with one-click “allow all” makes them a weak safeguard once human factors are considered. Even so, this setup was less exposed than a harness with full CLI access, e.g., bash with curl or dd .

6.4. Contributions to SOLVE-IT 

The technique “Use an AI-based prompt for interrogating case data” is already modelled in the SOLVE-IT knowledge base (Hargreaves et al., 2025) as DFT-1203. It assumes either a case database that the model interrogates or tool calls that ob-tain the same information. None of its existing 34 documented weaknesses considers evidence modified before acquisition de-signed to manipulate the agent. Our demonstrations add four: a prompt injection in the evidence makes the agent omit a rel-evant result (INCOMP) or return a fabricated one (INAC-EX), or causes relevant investigation data to be lost by modifying data on the examiner’s system (INCOMP, INAC-COR, INAC-ALT) or by signalling online that the device is being analysed (INCOMP, INAC-ALT). 7. Discussion 

7.1. Benefits 

The proposed taxonomy maps the newly emerged attack sur-face against the model reader and separates the region of clas-sical anti-forensics from the region the model introduces. The taxonomy is believed to be useful beyond the demon-strated attacks. The location and concealment dimensions de-fine a space that can be enumerated systematically to derive attack variants; the disk images used in Sec. 6 cover only part of it. The per-reader visibility in Table 2 lets a deployment de-termine which location–concealment combinations form a per-ception gap for its own pipeline, that is, where the examiner cannot detect a manipulation and a deterministic tool or a cross-reference to other evidence has to be established. The demonstrations emphasize the severity of the threat since adversarial evidence might both steer the model’s conclusion and act through it on the examiner’s environment. They do so even with plain text and no concealment, on an o ff -the-shelf pipeline (Autopsy, its MCP bridge, and LM Studio), and under configurations an examiner would plausibly choose. 

7.2. Limitations 

The demonstrations were not designed to validate the taxon-omy, and they do not. We hold the carrier fixed, a plain text body with no concealment, and vary only the objective and the action capability the injection escalates through. This is a de-liberate choice to illustrate the severity: the adversarial content planted sits in the obvious payload location of the taxonomy, visible to every reader. Where the same content is hidden from the examiner but readable by the model, the manipulation be-comes harder to detect without becoming easier for the model to resist. The runs reported here provide no measurement: they use one model, a small quantised LLM run locally, and report outcomes as proof-of-concept. 

7.3. Future Work 

There is a necessity of constructing a benchmark for prompt injection susceptibility through adversarial evidence. The miti-gations in Table 4 are argued and, in one case, tested; but they need systematic evaluation. Finally, the results need to be es-tablished at scale, since the model, its execution environment, and the evolving context all influence which tools an agent calls (Gruber & Hilgert, 2026). How fragile the injections are across models, clients, prompts, and product versions bears di-rectly on the knowledge argument of Sec. 4.2, under which an attacker studies a standardised product o ffl ine and reuses that knowledge for anti-forensic attacks. Larger and commercially deployed models remain to be tested. 

8. Conclusion 

LLM-assisted forensics breaks an assumption the field has relied on: that there is a separation of agent operator instruc-tions, and data from the examined evidence. Our taxonomy describes that an attack is a choice of objective, technique, lo-cation, and concealment, and the technique label is the one that changes with the reader. The same planted text is a claim to the examiner and an instruction to the model. The practical conse-quence is that human review is not the safeguard it is assumed to be. It fails where the perception gap sits: at evidence the model reads and the examiner, given the volumes involved, does not, and at evidence the examiner could check but the model never receives. Our demonstrations show that such attacks need nei-ther concealment nor an exploit: plain text on a disk image was enough to relabel photographs the model could not see, write 10 a false integrity error to the examiner’s workstation, and send an outbound request that could tip o ff the suspect. In the last case, the model identified the note, considered it, and complied regardless, so the model’s own judgment is not a defence ei-ther. What the examiner attaches and approves decides how far planted text can reach, but least privilege and network iso-lation protect the workstation, not the conclusion. These are proofs of concept with local models. The notes used no con-cealment, which did not matter because no examiner reviewed the output; against a reviewer, an attacker would conceal them, and hiding a note from the examiner does not make the model less likely to follow it. Of the mitigations we propose, only making the image content reachable for the model was tested, and it defeated the first attack for one of the two models tried. What is needed next is a benchmark built from the combina-tions the taxonomy enumerates, an evaluation of the remaining mitigations, and tests on the commercial products that ship stan-dardised prompts to every user, since an adversary can study those o ffl ine. Until that exists, a model reading seized evidence should be treated as what it is: a reader of adversarial input, whose output is a hypothesis to verify rather than a finding to report.
