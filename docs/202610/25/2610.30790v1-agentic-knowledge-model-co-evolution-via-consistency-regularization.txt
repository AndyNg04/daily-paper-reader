## ABSTRACT 

LLM agents can evolve by learning from accumulated experience. Existing ap-proaches extract reusable external knowledge, such as skills and memories, from experience to guide subsequent task solving, with some further internalizing this knowledge through model training. However, different ways of analyzing and transforming the same experience can yield knowledge with substantially different utility, even when the resulting guidance appears reasonable. Inspired by consis-tency regularization, we identify consistent utility gains across different ways of analyzing the same experience, rather than relying on any single way of analyzing it. We introduce SPLICE, a framework for consistency-regularized knowledge-model co-evolution. It constructs evolution views by perturbing how the same experience is analyzed, producing different knowledge candidates. Knowledge updates are then guided by common gain, which accounts for utility gains and their consistency across views. During model evolution, self-distillation internalizes the evolved knowledge alongside reinforcement learning, using knowledge associated with consistent utility degradation as a contrastive reference. The two processes interleave, with model updates driving further knowledge evolution. Experiments across diverse domains show that SPLICE outperforms knowledge evolution and model training baselines by up to 7.1 and 4.6 points in average accuracy, while its evolved knowledge reduces output tokens per correct solution by up to 44% and transfers across model sizes and families. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large Language Model (LLM) agents accumulate experience whenever they reason, use tools, and interact with the environment. For this experience to support continual improvement, an agent

This motivates knowledge evolution, through which agents extract and revise external knowledge, such as skills and memories, from their experience (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2026b; Ouyang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). Such knowledge can also be internalized into model parameters through policy training (Lu et al., 2026a;b; He et al., 2026c). The updated model then generates new experience for further knowledge evolution, allowing knowledge and the model to co-evolve (Xia et al., 2026; He et al., 2026b). External knowledge thus serves as a medium for agent evolution , connecting accumulated experience with subsequent task solving and model learning. However, knowledge derived from the same experience can differ substantially depending on which evidence is selected and how it is interpreted. For example, analyzing a purchase of the wrong size can yield guidance to include size requirements in queries or verify the selected size before purchase. On a product page that lists the requested size but defaults to another option, the first rule leaves the incorrect selection unchanged, while the second corrects it. Consequently, different ways of analyzing and transforming the same experience can yield knowledge with substantially different utility. Recent studies similarly find that even seemingly reasonable criteria for analyzing and transforming experience can yield less useful knowledge (Huang et al., 2026), and that plausible knowledge can even underperform using no knowledge at all (Li et al., 2026a; Dong et al., 2026). This raises a central question: how can experience be effectively transformed into generalizable knowledge to support agentic evolution? 

To tackle this problem, we draw inspiration from consistency regularization (Figure 1). This approach encourages consistent predictions for the same input under random parameter perturbations (Ni et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2021). This reduces prediction sensitivity to such perturbations and can improve generalization. Similarly, we view the transformation of experience into knowledge as a function parameterized by textual instructions, whose perturbations define different evolution views. Here, consistency concerns utility: when different views produce distinct knowledge yet yield consistent positive gains on the same tasks, this suggests that the benefits are not specific to a single evolution view. Rather than relying on any single analysis, we therefore use utility consistency across evolution views to guide knowledge evolution toward more generalizable knowledge. We introduce SPLICE, a framework for consistency-regularized knowledge-model co-evolution. During knowledge evolution, it perturbs how the same experience is analyzed and transformed, creating evolution views that each produce a knowledge candidate. Each candidate is evaluated through task replay, and a common gain is calculated by combining utility gain with cross-view consistency. A knowledge optimizer then updates the current knowledge using the candidates and replay trajectories from tasks with the highest common gains. During model evolution, SPLICE combines GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with self-distillation to internalize the evolved knowledge. The same replay also reveals guidance associated with consistent utility degradation, from which we construct noisy knowledge as a contrastive reference to highlight useful guidance in the evolved knowledge. A token-level gate controls its contribution to retain shared useful guidance. The two processes are interleaved, with the updated model generating new experience for further co-evolution. We evaluate SPLICE on four benchmarks covering math, coding, office work, and web shopping at two model scales. Across these tasks, SPLICE consistently outperforms knowledge evolution and model training baselines, improving average accuracy over the strongest baseline by 3.0 points for Qwen3.5-4B and 4.6 points for Qwen3.5-9B. Notably, its evolved knowledge makes task solving more efficient, requiring up to 44% fewer output tokens per correct solution than the strongest baseline, and delivers larger gains when transferred to other model sizes and families.

We introduce SPLICE, a framework for consistency-regularized knowledge-model co-evolution, illustrated in Figure 2. Knowledge evolution (§ 3.1) uses utility consistency across evolution views to guide updates to external knowledge. Model evolution (§ 3.2) internalizes the evolved knowledge through GRPO and gated contrastive self-distillation. Subsequent rounds repeat this process with new experience under the current model and knowledge. 3.2 KNOWLEDGE -G UIDED MODEL EVOLUTION 

A natural way to internalize the evolved knowledge K is self-distillation (Zhao et al., 2026a), using the model conditioned on K as a teacher. Our knowledge evolution process can also reveal patterns associated with consistent utility degradation. Prior work shows that learning from negative samples can provide useful supervision by discouraging incorrect responses (Zhu et al., 2026). Motivated by these findings, we construct noisy knowledge from these degradation patterns and use it as a contrastive reference to complement the guidance from K during self-distillation. 4Preprint 

Contrastive knowledge guidance. To construct this reference, we follow the task-selection proce-dure in § 3.1, replacing ∆ij with −∆ij when computing common gain. This selects tasks with utility degradation consistently supported across evolution views. For the selected tasks, the knowledge optimizer G similarly extracts and consolidates guidance associated with utility degradation into noisy knowledge K− for contrastive self-distillation. We construct K− anew whenever model evolution starts training with K, rather than evolving K− across knowledge evolution rounds.

We therefore weight self-distillation by the observed task-level benefit of K and gate the contribution of K− at the token level. Knowledge-model co-evolution in SPLICE. Co-evolution jointly updates the agent’s external knowledge K and model parameters θ. Knowledge evolution proposes updates to K through Eq. 5, and model evolution uses the adopted knowledge to update θ through Eq. 7. The updated model generates new trajectories and feedback that provide experience for subsequent knowledge evolution. Since knowledge utility depends on the task model, proposed knowledge is evaluated with respect to the current model as training proceeds. We detail the co-evolution procedures in Appendix B.

Knowledge evolution. With the task model fixed, SPLICE (Know. Evo) matches or outperforms all knowledge evolution baselines on every benchmark. It improves Avg. over the strongest baseline, SkillOpt, by 6.6 and 7.1 points with Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B, with the largest gains on WebShop (13.9 and 11.1 points), where tasks require long-horizon interaction with the environment. 

Knowledge-model co-evolution. SPLICE (Co-Evo) achieves the highest Avg. among methods that update model parameters, surpassing the strongest baseline, ReSkill, by 3.0 and 4.6 points with Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B. Notably, skill-augmented baselines do not always improve over GRPO, suggesting that evolved skills with inconsistent utility can interfere with policy learning on complex tasks. SPLICE mitigates this issue by internalizing only knowledge selected by utility consistency across evolution views. Together, SPLICE couples consistency-regularized knowledge evolution with model evolution, allowing the agent to benefit from useful external guidance while continually improving its own policy. 4.3 ANALYSIS

## 5 RELATED WORK 

Knowledge evolution for LLM agents. LLM agents improve without parameter updates by turning experience into external knowledge, from textual reflections (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024) to structured memories, playbooks, and skills that are continually updated (Ouyang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Alzubi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a). As such knowledge accumulates, recent studies find that it does not reliably improve task performance (Li et al., 2026a; Huang et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026) and that seemingly correct experience can mislead future tasks (Xiong et al., 2026). Some methods therefore retain only updates with positive estimated gain (Tang et al., 2026), evaluating each candidate on its own. Rather than relying on any single analysis, SPLICE uses utility consistency across evolution views to guide knowledge evolution. 

Knowledge internalization and co-evolution. Knowledge can also be internalized into model parameters through reinforcement learning and self-distillation (Lu et al., 2026a;b; He et al., 2026c; Lin et al., 2026), or evolved together with the policy (Xia et al., 2026; He et al., 2026b; Wu et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026). Meanwhile, on-policy self-distillation, in which the model conditioned on privileged context serves as its own teacher (Zhao et al., 2026a), has been refined with token-level modulation (Li et al., 2026b) and contrastive references (Pan et al., 2026). SPLICE constructs its contrastive reference from knowledge that consistently degrades utility during knowledge evolution, and gates it at the token level to retain guidance shared with the evolved knowledge. 

Consistency regularization. Consistency regularization improves generalization by encouraging consistent predictions for the same input under perturbations. It was first widely adopted in semi-supervised learning (Bachman et al., 2014; Sajjadi et al., 2016; Tarvainen & Valpola, 2017; Xie et al., 2020) and has since been extended to supervised training and fine-tuning of large models (Wu et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2024). Inspired by this idea, SPLICE applies consistency to the utility of evolved knowledge. Additional related work is discussed in Appendix C. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduce SPLICE, a framework for consistency-regularized knowledge-model co-evolution. SPLICE updates knowledge based on utility gains that are consistent across evolution views and inter-nalizes the evolved knowledge through reinforcement learning and gated contrastive self-distillation. Across four benchmarks and two model scales, SPLICE outperforms knowledge evolution and model training baselines, while its evolved knowledge enables more efficient task solving and transfers across model sizes and families. Overall, our results suggest that utility gains consistent across differ-ent ways of analyzing experience, rather than those observed under any single analysis, provide an effective signal for turning experience into generalizable knowledge that, when internalized through model evolution, supports long-term agentic evolution. 9Preprint

## B KNOWLEDGE -M ODEL CO-E VOLUTION 

B.1 DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS 

Knowledge evolution and model evolution depend on each other. The utility of knowledge depends on the model that uses it: guidance that helps the current model can become redundant or even harmful after the model is trained. Model evolution, in turn, internalizes knowledge through self-distillation from the model conditioned on it, which requires the knowledge to remain fixed. Co-evolution therefore has two requirements: • (R1) knowledge is evaluated with the model that uses it; • (R2) K and K− remain fixed while the model internalizes them. A further consideration is efficiency. Knowledge evolution typically requires multiple rounds before it produces stable knowledge with clear utility gains. If model evolution waits for these rounds, training stalls; if it uses knowledge too early, it internalizes weak guidance. The two procedures below handle this trade-off differently. The sequential procedure waits and satisfies both requirements exactly, whereas the asynchronous procedure relaxes R1 during knowledge evolution so that training uses knowledge as soon as it proves useful. B.2 SEQUENTIAL CO-E VOLUTION 

The sequential procedure (Algorithm 1) alternates the two processes. Knowledge evolution runs with the model fixed, so every candidate is replayed and validated with the model that will use it. It continues until M consecutive updates are rejected, indicating that knowledge evolution no longer improves the knowledge under the current model. Model evolution then performs T updates with K

and K− fixed. The rejected rounds share the same model and knowledge, and thus provide a common baseline for constructing K− from consistent utility degradation (§ 3.2). Afterwards, knowledge evolution continues from the current K with experience collected by the updated model. This procedure satisfies R1 and R2 exactly, but each process waits for the other: training is idle during the multiple rounds of knowledge evolution, and knowledge evolution is idle during training.

B.3 ASYNCHRONOUS CO-E VOLUTION 

The asynchronous procedure (Algorithm 2) runs knowledge evolution and model evolution at the same time. Knowledge evolution keeps improving its own copy of knowledge, Kevo , using experience generated during training, while model evolution trains with K, the most recent Kevo that improves 17 Preprint held-out validation performance with the current model. Before training starts, knowledge evolution runs several rounds with the initial model to obtain initial knowledge. 

Consistent references for comparison. Training experience is generated under whichever knowl-edge the current stage uses. Knowledge evolution therefore takes trajectories generated under the same knowledge K′ and measures replay gains relative to K′. Updates supported by common gain are merged into the latest Kevo , which can differ from K′; knowledge evolution therefore accumulates improvements instead of restarting with each batch. 

Adopting knowledge for training. Model evolution proceeds in training stages. At the start of each stage, the latest Kevo is compared with no knowledge on the held-out validation set using the current model. It is adopted as K only if it improves validation performance; otherwise, the stage trains with GRPO alone. Each stage that trains with K first constructs K− from the replay results of recent knowledge evolution rounds by selecting tasks with consistent utility degradation using −∆ij

## C MORE RELATED WORK 

Knowledge evolution for LLM agents. LLM agents can improve without parameter updates by turning experience into external knowledge. Early methods reflect on failures and store textual lessons: Reflexion keeps verbal self-reflections for subsequent attempts (Shinn et al., 2023), ExpeL extracts insights from successful and failed trajectories across tasks (Zhao et al., 2024), CLIN maintains a textual memory updated across trials (Majumder et al., 2023), and Agent-Pro reflects at the level of the agent’s policy (Zhang et al., 2024). Another line accumulates procedural knowledge, such as executable skill libraries (Wang et al., 2023), workflows induced from past trajectories (Wang et al., 2024), and skills that are automatically discovered and refined (Alzubi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026), including learnable memory operations (Zhang et al., 2026a). Other methods maintain structured memories or contexts that evolve during task solving, including agentic memory organization (Xu et al., 2026), adaptive test-time memory (Suzgun et al., 2026), reasoning strategies distilled from successes and failures (Ouyang et al., 2026), and evolving playbooks (Zhang et al., 2026b). Training-Free GRPO further distills experiential knowledge from group-relative comparisons of rollouts (Cai et al., 2025). Related studies examine the reliability of skills and agentic reasoning: model-generated skills do not reliably improve performance (Li et al., 2026a; Huang et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026), unchecked accumulation degrades skill retrieval (Zhang et al., 2026c), and agents can fail to use experience and external evidence reliably (Xiong et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026b). Some methods therefore retain only updates with positive estimated gain (Tang et al., 2026). SPLICE focuses on which evidence should drive a knowledge update, using utility consistency across multiple evolution views of the same experience. 

Knowledge internalization and co-evolution. Internalizing knowledge into model parameters is closely related to context distillation, which trains a model without a context to match its predictions with the context (Snell et al., 2022), and to on-policy distillation, which supervises a student on its own generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024). For LLM agents, recent work internalizes skills into the policy through reinforcement learning and self-distillation (Lu et al., 2026a; He et al., 2026c), progressively reduces reliance on skills provided in context (Lu et al., 2026b), or attributes success to skills by contrasting rollouts with and without them (Lin et al., 2026). Other work evolves knowledge together with the policy: skill banks are updated during online reinforcement learning (Xia et al., 2026), skill creation is coupled with policy optimization (He et al., 2026b), experience is distilled into principles that guide further policy learning (Wu et al., 2025), and a single policy learns to select, use, and distill skills (Shi et al., 2026). On-policy self-distillation, in which the model conditioned on privileged context serves as its own teacher, has become a dense training signal for LLMs (Zhao et al., 2026a). Since privileged context can distort the teacher’s token distribution, recent variants modulate token-level guidance by teacher–student disagreement (Li et al., 2026b), reverse the distillation direction (Shen et al., 2026), or contrast signals under correct and wrong hints (Pan et al., 2026). Negative signals have likewise been shown to provide useful supervision in reinforcement learning for LLMs (Zhu et al., 2026). SPLICE derives its negative reference from knowledge evolution itself, using knowledge associated with consistent utility degradation, and combines it with task-level weighting and a token-level gate during internalization. 

Consistency regularization. Consistency regularization encourages a model to make consistent predictions for the same input under perturbations. In semi-supervised learning, early methods enforce agreement under stochastic transformations, dropout, and ensembling (Bachman et al., 2014; Sajjadi et al., 2016; Laine & Aila, 2016), and later methods improve the targets with weight-averaged teachers (Tarvainen & Valpola, 2017) or strong data augmentation (Xie et al., 2020). The idea has been extended to supervised training and fine-tuning through consistency between dropout sub-models (Wu et al., 2021) or under parameter perturbations (Ni et al., 2024). These methods assume that the correct output is invariant to the perturbation and enforce consistency through a training loss. In knowledge evolution, this assumption does not hold at the level of content, since different evolution views are expected to produce different knowledge. SPLICE therefore requires consistency only in the utility gains of the resulting knowledge and uses it as a criterion for selecting evidence. 20 Preprint
