Abstract— Learning
loco-manipulation
policies
requires
demonstrations that capture coordinated base and arm mo-
tion on the target embodiment. Robot-free interfaces allow
demonstrations to be collected independently of the target
robot, increasing flexibility but without directly capturing
platform-specific commands, proprioception, and body-mounted
observations. On-robot teleoperation allows to capture these
signals directly, but requires an interface capable of commanding
the complete system concurrently. This paper presents LocoPilot,
a single-operator bilateral teleoperation and data-collection
framework implemented on a 12-DoF Unitree Go2 quadruped
equipped with a 6-DoF AgileX Piper L manipulator, an Intel
RealSense D435 wrist camera, and an Intel RealSense D405
head camera. A second 6-DoF Piper L arm, used as the leader
and two handheld controllers provide simultaneous control
of the base, body posture, manipulator, and gripper. The
follower arm interaction forces are estimated without a dedicated
force/torque sensor and rendered to the operator through a
passivity-constrained haptic channel. A ROS 2 pipeline records
synchronized multimodal observations together with a unified
11D action shared by teleoperation and policy deployment.
Compared to prior work, LocoPilot uniquely unifies robot-in-
the-loop legged data collection, force feedback without motion-
capture instrumentation, and the same action interface at
deployment. We evaluate the interface across multiple loco-
manipulation tasks and validate the complete collection-to-
deployment pipeline by fine-tuning MolmoAct2 [1] on pick-
and-place demonstrations and deploying on the physical robot
without action retargeting or additional inverse kinematics.
I. INTRODUCTION
Robots capable of both locomotion and manipulation ex-
tend physical interaction beyond the workspace of fixed-base
manipulators. Arm-equipped quadruped robots are particularly
suited to unstructured environments, where they combine
legged mobility with physical interaction capabilities. Prior
work has explored legged loco-manipulation through model-
based whole-body control [2], or learned coordination of lo-
comotion and manipulation [3], [4]. More recently, generalist
Visual Language Action (VLA) models have gained increasing
attention for learning manipulation behaviors from multimodal
demonstrations, leveraging large-scale visual and language
representations [5]–[8]. For loco-manipulation, however, such
demonstrations must capture both base and arm actions on
the target embodiment. Ideally, the collection interface should
therefore minimize the collection–deployment gap while
preserving direct operator authority over all task-relevant
commands.
Existing systems trade off these requirements in different
ways:
Teleoperation systems place a human directly in the
control loop of the physical robot, combining the operator task
understanding with the constraints of the target embodiment.

XR-based systems provide more general cross-embodiment

Immersive interfaces additionally stream the robot’s own
onboard imagery to the operator [12], allowing real-time
inspection of the visual inputs that will directly condition the
downstream policy. For arm-equipped quadrupeds, previous
systems have used wearable motion capture, joysticks, haptic
devices, or hand gestures [15]–[18]. Collecting directly on
the target robot reduces observation and action mismatch, but
simultaneously commanding all task-relevant DoFs becomes
increasingly difficult for a single operator.
Robot-free interfaces make the opposite trade-off. The
Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) [19] records demon-
strations using a sensorized handheld gripper, allowing the
operator to demonstrate manipulation without the kinematic
constraints of the target robot. This improves collection
flexibility, but omits embodiment-specific signals such as base
commands, proprioception, and body-mounted observations.
Deployment therefore requires an additional mapping from
demonstrated end-effector motion to the target embodiment,
as in UMI on Legs [20].
This work aims to combine the collection-deployment
consistency of on-robot teleoperation with the flexibility of
learning-based interfaces for arm-equipped quadrupeds. As
illustrated in Fig. 1, both the human operator and the learned
policy act through the same loco-manipulation interface, while
the resulting demonstrations are collected directly on the
target platform. Achieving this requires addressing two main
challenges.
The first challenge is enabling a single operator to
command the entire loco-manipulation action concurrently.
Reaching and grasping may require the robot to translate,
rotate, or change body posture while the arm is moving, so
sequential base and manipulator control produces segmented
demonstrations rather than the smooth continuous coordina-
tion desired from the final policy. Existing systems address
this through multiple operators, wearable instrumentation,
or partial autonomy, introducing additional coordination or
removing task decisions from the demonstrator.
To retain these decisions under single-operator control,
we propose a bimanual interface design where a handheld
controller is mounted on the end-effector handle of the leader
arm. The dominant hand articulates the manipulator while
operating the mounted controller, and the non-dominant hand
provides the remaining base commands through a second
controller. Together, the two interfaces provide concurrent
control of planar base velocity, yaw rate, body pitch, arm
configuration, and gripper aperture. Interaction forces at
the follower are additionally estimated online and rendered
through the leader arm, providing contact feedback without
a dedicated force/torque sensor.

B. Software Architecture
On the software side, the framework is composed of inter-
connected, distributed modules running on the quadruped’s
onboard computer and an operator workstation, as shown
in Fig. 3. In particular, the quadruped’s onboard computer
runs: the reinforcement-learning base policy of [22], which
converts base-velocity and body-pitch references into joint
position targets for a low-level PD controller; the MUSE state
estimator [23], which provides the state estimates used as
input by the policy; the arm control stack; and the camera
nodes, which publish the two RGB-D streams.
As a direct consequence of unifying the action and obser-
vation interfaces, the same software modules run on the robot
both for data collection and for autonomous deployment. What
changes between the two modes is the operator side. During
demonstration collection the workstation runs: the leader arm
control stack; the teleoperation module described in Sec. III-A
that couples leader and follower and renders contact back
to the operator; and the recording module that assembles
episodes from the observation, state, and command streams.
During autonomous deployment a single policy-inference
node replaces all three, receiving the same observations and
emitting the same commands the teleoperation loop previously
produced. The switch between the operator and the policy
therefore falls at one interface, and everything after it is
unaffected by which of the two is in control.
Having defined explicit software interfaces that decouple
the teleoperation and autonomous deployment stack from the
underlying robot hardware, each hardware-dependent subsys-
tem can be replaced independently, provided that it preserves
the corresponding input-output contract. In particular, the
locomotion subsystem, including the base, leg controller,
reinforcement-learning policy, and state estimator, can be
replaced by any implementation that accepts commanded
planar velocity and absolute body pitch and exposes the leg
states and base-velocity estimate required by the higher-level
stack. Likewise, the manipulation subsystem can be replaced
by any arm and controller implementation that supports joint-
space impedance control and exposes joint position, velocity,
and torque feedback. All components above these interfaces,
including the teleoperation logic, action representation, and
data-collection pipeline, remain unchanged.
This interface-based organization therefore reduces the
engineering effort required to port the framework to a different
platform and makes the overall architecture modular at both
the software and hardware levels. The present implementation
uses ROS 2 Jazzy with Cyclone DDS. Section III details the
central elements of the software stack, i.e., the teleoperation
and data collection modules.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
A. Arm-to-arm Teleoperation
We evaluate the leader–follower teleoperation interface
of Sec. III-A on a tabletop pick-and-place task containing
both free-space motion and sustained object interaction.
The experiment verifies that the two channels required for
demonstration collection operate simultaneously: the follower
tracks the leader motion, while contact with the environment
produces an interaction estimate suitable for haptic rendering.
To obtain a ground-truth reference for the force channel,
the manipulated object is a known payload of 1.5 kg, so
that during transport the expected vertical component of the
estimated interaction force is known.
The position channel is evaluated through the Cartesian
tracking error e between the leader and follower end-effector
positions, while the force channel is characterized by the
norm of the estimated interaction force F. Figure 4 reports
both over a representative episode, in which the robot first
moves in free space, then grasps and transports the payload,
and finally releases it. The weight of the payload is drawn
as a reference line, since it is the force the estimate should
report while the object is held.
We compare the estimate against the value expected in
each phase: zero while the robot moves in free space, and
the weight of the payload while it is carried. Grasping and
releasing take a short time, during which the load builds up
and vanishes, so the second (1 s) before and after each of the
two transitions is excluded from the comparison.
In free motion the estimated force has an RMSE of 1.27 N,
which is the noise floor of the estimate and therefore the
smallest interaction the operator can perceive. While the
payload is held, the RMSE against its known weight is
2.25 N, or 15.3 % of the reference value. Tracking error
follows the same structure: 8.02 mm in free motion against
60.14 mm during interaction, as expected when environmental
constraints oppose the configuration commanded by the
leader. Larger tracking errors occur in the same interval
with a mean of 24.55 mm, as expected when environmental
constraints oppose the configuration commanded by the
leader. The two signals remain complementary, with the
position error quantifying the deviation between commanded
and executed motion and the force estimate exposing the
interaction responsible for it.
B. VLA Deployment
We evaluate the collected demonstrations by fine-tuning
a vision-language-action model and deploying it on the
robot. The purpose of the experiment is to verify that the
recorded episodes are directly usable for policy learning,
and that the coordinated base and arm motion they contain
is reproduced by the learned policy. The episodes exported
by the recorder are used to train the policy, which then
drives the robot through the interface the operator employed,
with no intermediate processing stage between collection and
deployment. We instantiate the command interface of Sec. III-
B with MolmoAct2 [1], a vision-language-action model
that predicts action chunks from camera observations and a
language instruction. The choice is motivated by openness: its
authors release the training data, the training recipe, and the
pretrained checkpoints, so the fine-tuning procedure reported
here contains no proprietary component and can be repeated
in full. In its original paper [1] the model is demonstrated
only on fixed-base manipulators, so fine-tuning it here also
accounts for instructing a new physical embodiment which
includes commanding a mobile base.
The dataset collected with the framework covers two tasks,
Grasp and Push. Grasp motion requires the operator to drive
the quadruped toward a cube, close the gripper on it, and
place it on a colored target region. In Push, the operator
uses the end-effector to push the cube toward the target
without lifting it, starting from a nearby initial position. In
both tasks the target usually lies outside the workspace of the
arm at the stance in which the episode starts, so the recorded
actions contain coordinated base and arm motion rather than
manipulation alone. Either a red or a white cube are used
for the collection, and textual instructions are generated from
templates over the object and target descriptions, which varies
the language input across episodes.
The collection comprises 289 demonstrations, 259 of
grasping and 30 of pushing, of which 258 are used for fine-
tuning and 31 are held out for validation.

Deployment. On the robot the policy replaces the operator
without any change below the interface: it receives the two
camera streams, the proprioceptive state, and a language
instruction, and writes the same command that the teleop-
eration layer produced during collection, at the same rate.
Example instructions include “Move the red cube to the blue
square” and “Pick up the red cube and place it in the blue
square.” Figure 5 shows a successful rollout, in which the
model drives the base toward the cube, pitches the robot’s
body to extend the reach of the arm, closes the gripper, and
carries the cube to the target region, coordinating base and
arm within one continuous motion. The trials are assessed
qualitatively, with respect to the ability to solve the task from
different starting positions. The policy learns the pick-and-
place motion in the simplest scenarios, specifically when
both the cube and the target are visible and no complex
reorientation is required. Complications arise when the target
region leaves the camera’s field of view, since the policy must
then reorient and navigate based solely on surrounding visual
cues, and it sometimes walks and moves the cube in the
wrong direction. Another challenging scenario occurs when
the base stops too early or too far from the target region,
so that the cube is released slightly away from it. In these
situations the smooth transition between motions leads the
policy to perform corrections, nudging the cube toward the
target or re-approaching it for a second grasp until the task
is completed.
V. LIMITATIONS AND APPLICABILITY
The residual of (3) is computed from a model of the arm
alone. The floating base is treated as inertial, so whenever it
accelerates the resulting inertial effects appear at the joints
and the observer attributes them to the environment. A second
error comes from the identified inertial and friction parameters,
whose residual mismatch, dominated by the static friction
of the joint drives, biases the estimate as a function of the
configuration. Neither contribution can be removed entirely,
and together they set a free-motion floor, which is the smallest
interaction the operator can perceive. Compensating the base
acceleration explicitly, from the base state already estimated
on board, would reduce the noise in the force channel, and
we leave it to future work.
These errors affect what the operator perceives but not the
stability of the interface. The passivity layer of Sec. III-A.3
accounts for measured power flow, so an inaccurate residual
costs force feedback accuracy rather than safety. The force
channel is designed as an extension of the position channel,
such that disabling it does not change the recorded action
nor the collection pipeline.
We found that the force channel is most useful when the
environment constrains the end-effector motion in ways not
visible to onboard cameras, and when the resulting interaction
is sustained rather than impulsive. Opening a door or rotating
a valve constrains the end effector along a path that the
operator has to follow, and pushing or pulling a load requires
regulating a force over the whole motion. In these cases the
operator perceives the constraint directly instead of inferring


it from the position error. Tasks dominated by free-space
motion, or in which contact is brief and light with respect to
the floor reported above, gain correspondingly less from the
force channel.
The evaluation covers a single hardware configuration.
The autonomous policy is trained on two task families and
assessed qualitatively over a limited number of trials, and
a quantitative study of the success rate against the dataset
size, or of the contribution of contact feedback to the quality
of the collected demonstrations, would require a controlled
comparison that we leave to future work.
VI. CONCLUSIONS
We presented a teleoperation and data-collection framework
for an arm-equipped quadruped in which a single operator
commands base velocity, body pitch, arm configuration, and
gripper through one 11-dimensional action, and demonstra-
tions are recorded in the same action the learned policy later
writes. Holding it fixed across the two regimes removes the
conversion stage that usually separates a recorded demonstra-
tion from a deployable policy: the exported episodes are used
for fine-tuning as they are, and the resulting policy is executed
by the controllers already in place. We additionally showed
a successful fine-tuning of MolmoAct2 on pick-and-place
data collected with the framework, obtaining a policy that
coordinates base and arm motion on the robot. Beyond that,
the framework simplifies the collection of new data and the
addition of new tasks through the same recording pipeline,
making extensions along this axis straightforward.
