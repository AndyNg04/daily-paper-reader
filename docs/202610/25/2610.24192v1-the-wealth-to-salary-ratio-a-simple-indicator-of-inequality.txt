In previous studies, using agent-based simulations of a model of a capitalist econ-omy proposed by Ian Wright, we showed that inequality in both wealth and income distributions is largely governed by a single key parameter: the ratio between aver-age wealth per capita and the mean salary, denoted by R. This parameter captures the relative importance of accumulated assets compared with labor income. In the model, larger values of R are systematically associated with higher levels of in-equality, as measured by the Gini coefficient. In the present work, we investigate whether this relationship is also observed in real-world economies. Using empirical data from the World Inequality Database (WID) and the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), we construct empirical estimates of R and compare them with standard measures of income and wealth inequality. We find a positive relation between R and inequality across several empirical settings, with the strongest relationships observed for mature and advanced economies. These re-sults provide empirical support for the role of the wealth-to-salary ratio as a simple macroscopic indicator associated with economic inequality, while also highlighting the limitations imposed by differences in data definitions and measurement across countries and periods.

> I. INTRODUCTION

Economic activity can be interpreted through the lens of complex systems, in which collective outcomes emerge from interactions among heterogeneous agents [1–3]. Within econophysics, this perspective has proved particularly useful for understanding empirical patterns in income and wealth distributions [2, 4]. Agent-based models (ABMs), particularly those based on wealth-exchange mechanisms [5, 6], have been widely used to investigate how inequality emerges and evolves under different market rules, regulatory policies [7–9] and interaction structures [10]. Among them, the Social Architecture of Capitalism (SA) model introduced by Ian Wright [11] provides a simple description of interactions between firms and workers, and reproduces important stylized features of wealth distributions, including a two-regime structure [12, 13]. In recent works [14, 15], we systematically explored the parameter space of the SA model and identified a quantity that largely controls the separation between the two wealth regimes. Denoted by R, this quantity is defined as the ratio of average wealth per capita, w, to the mean wage, p. Within the model, the overall shape of the wealth distribution—including the emergence of a gap between classes, increasing concentration of wealth, and the disappearance of an intermediate stratum at high values of R—is largely determined by this ratio, with only minor sensitivity to system size. In particular, inequality, as measured by the Gini coefficient [16], increases monotonically with R. These results remain robust under several realistic modifications of the original model [15]. These findings raise a natural empirical question: does the relationship between R and inequality identified in the model also appear in real economies? The present work addresses this question using observational data. The causal mechanism connecting R to inequality was established through controlled computational experiments in the SA model [15]. We therefore do not attempt to establish causality from observational data alone. Instead, our objective is more limited: to determine whether empirical patterns in real economies are consistent with the relationship predicted by the model. To this end, we construct empirical estimates of R, using measures of average employee compensation and net personal wealth, drawing on data from the World Inequality Database (WID) [17] and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [18]. In Section II, we describe the data sources, the definitions adopted, and the procedures used to make the comparisons as consistent as possible. The empirical analysis is developed in 3Sec: III, where we also connect R to the wealth-to-income ratio, β, widely studied in the literature on wealth accumulation and inequality, particularly in the work of Piketty and coauthors [19]. Conclusions and final remarks are presented in Sec. IV  

> II. DATA AND METHODOLOGY

Both databases used in our empirical analysis, the World Inequality Database (WID) [17] and the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [18], provide internationally comparable measures of income, wealth, labor compensation, and inequality, but they differ in definitions, source data, and methodological procedures. We use the WID data primarily for the analyses based on long time series and distri-butional measures, covering both groups of countries and individual countries. The OECD data are used for a complementary analysis. Although WID incorporates data from OECD and other official statistical sources, differences in definitions and methodological procedures may result in different estimates for some variables. Using both databases therefore allows us to examine whether the observed relationship between R and inequality is robust to differences in data sources and methodology.  

> A. WID methodology

We first construct a balanced panel dataset, in which each country is observed in every year from 1995 to 2019. The period begins in 1995 and ends in 2019 in order to avoid the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic. The countries included in the analysis, listed in Table I, are those classified by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) as advanced economies—or as industrialized economies, in the terminology used at the time—in its 1995 [20] report, with the exception of Iceland, which was excluded due to lack of data. Inequality is measured by the Gini coefficient of pre-tax national income and the Gini index of net personal wealth. To estimate R, we approximate p by average employee com-pensation and w by net personal wealth, both expressed per adult individual and in local currency. It is worth-noting that, according to the OECD definition, wealth refers to the “ownership of economic capital” as a dimension of people’s material well-being. Average annual wages 6per full-time equivalent dependent employee are obtained by dividing the national-accounts-based total wage bill by the average number of employees in the total economy and converting this quantity into full-time equivalent units using the ratio of average usual weekly hours per full-time employee to those of all employees. The Gini coefficient is based on the comparison of cumulative proportions of the population against cumulative proportions of the income they receive.    

> III. EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS OF RUSING REAL DATA

In this Section we examine whether the positive relationship between R and inequality identified in the SA model is also observed in empirical data. We proceed from an aggregate to more disaggregated analyses. (i) First, in Section III A, we analyze advanced economies in groups, using WID and OECD data. (ii) Before proceeding to individual analysis, we briefly discuss, in Section III B, the con-nection between R and the wealth-to-income ratio, β, widely used in the literature following Piketty. This comparison helps clarify the relation between the two indicators and provides a broader context for the empirical analysis. (iii) Finally, we examine, in Section III C, individual countries using longer WID time series. This progression allows us to assess the robustness of the relationship across different aggregation levels, data sources, and historical settings. To assess the strength and direction of the association between different quantities, we compute the Spearman rank correlation coefficient, ρs, a nonparametric measure of mono-tonic dependence. Throughout the text, statistical analyses are evaluated at a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05 and for Spearman correlations, statistical significance is assessed using a permutation test. 7

> B. Comparison of Rwith the wealth-to-income ratio β

To place our indicator in the context of the existing literature, we compare R with the wealth-to-income ratio. In Piketty’s terminology [24], the latter is denoted by β and measures the ratio of aggregate wealth to national income. Although β is closely related to R, the relationship between inequality and the wealth-to-wage ratio considered here has not, to our knowledge, been specifically investigated. In a study by Piketty and Zucman [19], eight advanced economies are analyzed, and the authors concluded that in all these countries there has been a gradual increase in the wealth-income ratio in recent decades. According to the authors, this increase is not necessarily problematic in itself, but it raises important questions concerning taxation and regulation, since high wealth-to-income ratios imply that wealth inequality is likely to play a central role in the overall structure of inequality in the twenty-first century. Madsen, on the other hand, focuses on the last eight centuries of British history and engages in critical dialogue with Piketty’s work, but also concludes that the data suggest that variations in income inequality are accompanied by variations in β, i.e., the time series for income inequality follows a pattern very similar to that of β. The author further suggests that the wealth-to-income ratio may serve as a proxy for inequality [25]. Given the documented relationship between inequality and β, it is important to clarify 10 how β and R are related. The two ratios differ in their denominators: β uses national income, whereas R uses mean wages. Their numerators are also expressed differently, with 

β using national wealth and R using per capita wealth. However, this distinction is purely a matter of normalization, since the ratio of per capita wealth to per capita income is identical to the ratio of national wealth to national income. While an increase in national income does not necessarily translate into a proportional increase in mean wages, the two quantities are structurally related because labor compensation is a component of national income. Thus, changes in national income are expected to be associated with changes in average wages [26]. Consequently, β and R may exhibit a similar qualitative long-run behavior insofar as average wages tend to increase with national income. The two ratios need not, however, evolve proportionally. Their relative dynamics depend, in particular, on the labor share, 

i.e. , the fraction of national income paid to workers as compensation. To compare β and R, we first specify how each quantity is estimated from the empirical data. We use average compensation per capita for the total population, c, reported by WID, as a proxy for the average wage received by workers, p, in our model [27]. We also obtain national income, Y , and total net personal wealth, W , from WID. Denoting the labor share of national income by s and the total population by N , we write, as a first approximation,

This indicates that the concurrent increase in R and the Gini coefficient, whether for wealth or income inequality, 14 is not confined to periods of crisis. Rather, the results suggest that this pattern is more characteristic of non-crisis periods. Piketty argues [24] that the tendency toward increasing inequality under capitalism was temporarily reversed between the two world wars and during the subsequent decades, a period marked by the expansion of the welfare state and the implementation of fiscal policies such as progressive taxation. According to this interpretation, the 1980s marked a return to policies associated with market deregulation and a renewed increase in inequality [24]. The rise in inequality since the 1980s is broadly observed across the countries in our dataset and is accompanied by an increase in R over the same period. Because China’s transition from a centrally planned economy to a market-oriented sys-tem began only after the economic reforms initiated by Deng Xiaoping in 1978, consistent measures of income and wealth inequality are available only from the early 1980s onward. The reforms gradually introduced market mechanisms, private entrepreneurship, and foreign investment while maintaining significant state control over strategic sectors. Consequently, the Chinese observations in our dataset start in 1980, corresponding to the onset of the country’s transition toward a socialist market economy and the emergence of meaningful variation in both income and wealth inequality. While France and the UK are especially valuable because of their long historical series of economic data, and the USA is of particular historical interest given its emergence as a leading capitalist power in the postwar period, the inclusion of China is especially informative because it provides a long-term record of inequality dynamics during a rapid transition from a predominantly egalitarian planned economy to a market-oriented system. The Chinese case therefore offers a valuable opportunity to examine how the relationship between income Gini index and the wealth-to-wage ratio ( R) evolves during a period of profound structural and institutional transformation. Despite some quantitative differences across countries, these empirical results suggest that the positive association between R and inequality predicted by the SA model is also apparent in the long-term evolution of several individual countries whose economies are characterized by stable mass production of goods for market exchange. 15  

> IV. FINAL REMARKS AND CONCLUSIONS

The results suggest that the association between R and inequality predicted by the SA model is particularly evident in advanced, long-established economies. In this group, the association with income inequality is statistically significant. This finding motivates a dis-cussion of how the model’s assumptions relate to the institutional characteristics of the economies analyzed. According to Erik Olin Wright’s conception of capitalism, describing an economy as cap-italist means that capitalist relations are dominant within its economic ecosystem, without necessarily being the only relations present [29]. Since the SA model represents an ideal-ized capitalist system, we may hypothesize that its predictions are more readily observed in economies where these relations are firmly institutionalized. China provides a comple-mentary case for examining this hypothesis: its transition toward a more market-oriented economy involves structural and institutional changes that may affect both R and inequality. The relationship between these quantities should therefore be interpreted in the context of this transformation, rather than assuming that the model applies equally across all peri-ods. However, our analysis does not isolate the effects of institutional differences, so this interpretation remains a hypothesis for further investigation. Another relevant assumption is that the SA model describes a closed economy. Applying it to individual countries therefore requires an approximation, given their integration through international flows of trade, capital, and income. Ellen Meiksins Wood emphasizes that national capitalist economies are interconnected within a global system governed by general capitalist laws [30]. This perspective motivates extending the analysis beyond national boundaries as future research. In this sense, it would be interesting to test whether the relationship between R and inequality predicted by the SA model also emerges when the world economy is treated as a single closed system. At this level, cross-border asset and liability positions would be internal to the system, removing the external-sector issue by construction. Such an analysis would nevertheless require reliable and sufficiently consistent data on the global distributions of wealth and labor compensation. The empirical analysis also has measurement limitations. Definitions of income, wealth, and labor compensation differ across countries and statistical institutions, and methodologies 16 may change over time. Constructing a common indicator such as R therefore involves approximations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Our findings establish empirical associations rather than causal relationships. We assess whether the association predicted by the model is supported by observed wealth and income Gini coefficients; identifying its causal mechanisms would require further investigation. The results nevertheless support the usefulness of the “Social Architecture of Capitalism” frame-work, and of agent-based models more generally, for formulating testable hypotheses about inequality within capitalist economies. The broader motivation for this investigation echoes a classical observation by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations: a society’s prosperity cannot be separated from the living conditions of the majority or from the share of production received by those whose labor sustains it [31].
