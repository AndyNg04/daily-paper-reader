# Abstract 

Although automatic detection of cognitive impairment from conversational speech is a promising way, it becomes less ac-curate, as large real-world datasets often contain unreliable di-agnostic labels due to self-referral and limited clinical verifi-cation, and here we investigated whether neuropsychologically calibrated diagnostic relabeling improves it. We analysed 138 hours of conversational speech from 720 participants, recali-brating unreliable diagnostic labels using attention, executive function, and memory performance via external clinical thresh-olds, Gaussian, and normative z-score calibration. Transformer-based classifiers were trained using both original and recali-brated labels and evaluated internally on 150 clinically verified participants and externally on Pitt, ADReSS and PROCESS-2 datasets. Memory- and MoCA-based z-score calibration pro-duced the greatest improvements. Using our internal dataset, 

F1 increased from 0.70 to 0.83 (+13% ) and from 0.54 to 

0.66 (+12% ) for binary and multiclass classification, respec-tively. For external validation, the increases are +4% , +5% 

and +9% on Pitt, ADReSS and PROCESS-2 corpus. Neu-ropsychologically calibrated relabeling substantially improves speech-based cognitive assessment, highlighting label quality, rather than model architecture, as a critical bottleneck in clini-cal AI for dementia screening. 

Index Terms : dementia, MCI, attention, memory, execu-tive function, neuropsychology, cognitive decline, pathological speech, Transformer, LLM 

# 1. Introduction 

Cognitive and memory difficulties can arise from a range of factors, including fatigue, stress, and illness, and tend to be-come more pronounced with age. When these deficits persist over several months, they may indicate mild cognitive impair-ment (MCI) [1], a condition characterised by deficits in mem-ory, learning, reasoning, attention, language, and motivation. Individuals with MCI often describe experiencing a reduced capacity for clear thinking [2]. Approximately half of those diagnosed with MCI eventually progress to dementia, a pro-gressive neurodegenerative condition, most commonly resulting from Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [3]. Other forms of dementia, including vascular and frontotemporal dementia, frequently dis-rupt behaviour, language, and cognitive functions such as per-ception and executive reasoning. To date, no curative therapies exist for these conditions, only those that slow the rate of de-cline [4–6]. Early identification of dementia provides significant bene-fits, including the opportunity for timely interventions that may delay progression to advanced stages [7]. However, diagno-sis is often challenging, requiring comprehensive evaluations by neurological specialists, longitudinal cognitive assessments maintained by healthcare providers [8], and complex, expen-sive, and time-consuming clinical procedures such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), standardised cognitive testing, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis [9]. These traditional approaches are impractical for large-scale screening of cognitive decline [10] and contribute to underdiagnosis, leaving many individ-uals without timely treatment. Consequently, there is a grow-ing need for remote and intelligent technologies capable of sup-porting healthcare services in delivering accurate and timely as-sessments [11], including approaches that leverage speech, be-haviour, and other non-invasive data sources for scalable early detection. For this purpose, we have previously designed CognoMem-ory (formerly CognoSpeak [12]) to accelerate the identification of early cognitive decline by engaging the participant in a con-versation, which places demands on multiple cognitive domains including memory, language and attention, and subsequently carefully analysing the answers, as described in [12, 13]. This remote, large-scale data collection, either at patients’ homes, at clinics or in community centres, allows us to capture real-world conversational speech, and a similar automatic system will save time and financial resources for health service providers across the world, such as the NHS in the UK. Although the Cog-noMemory system is primarily used to collect data from pri-mary or secondary care memory clinics in the UK (clinical source), it also recruits nationally through volunteer websites and social media (non-clinical source), as it is open to the pub-lic. The diagnostic labels of the participants from non-clinical sources is provided by the participants themselves and is there-fore not verified by a clinician involved in data collection. This introduces a significant source of label uncertainty for down-stream machine learning tasks, as the provided annotations may lack clinical accuracy. Participants may rely on outdated histor-ical diagnoses (such as an individual previously diagnosed with MCI who has since progressed to dementia), or they may in-advertently provide an inaccurate self-report due to a lack of arXiv:2610.28288v1 [cs.SD] 20 Oct 2026 awareness in terms of their own cognitive status. Given the limitations of collecting data through online databases and to address uncertainty in unverified diagnostic annotations, we ex-plore ways to improve diagnostic label accuracy by adopting a neuropsychologically calibrated label reassignment strategy in which diagnostic categories are inferred from objective cogni-tive performance measures and subsequently validated against clinically established ground-truth diagnoses. This study proposes a clinically grounded, threshold-based neuropsychological relabelling framework that replaces self-reported diagnostic labels with neuropsychologically stratified categories derived from measured performance. Specifically, we develop and evaluate relabelling approaches using three complementary thresholds: (i) clinical reference thresholds from an external study (FLAME) [14], (ii) Gaussian-based de-cision boundaries estimated from trusted clinical subsets of CognoMemory, and (iii) normative deviation thresholds based on healthy-control z-scores. The reassigned labels are subse-quently validated against internal (clinical ground-truth diag-noses from CognoMemory) and three external datasets (Pitt [15], ADReSS [16]), and PROCESS-2 [17] through extensive speech-based classification experiments. Our results demon-strate that improving diagnostic label quality produces larger performance gains than modifying model architecture, high-lighting data curation as a primary bottleneck in automatic cog-nitive decline detection. The novelty of this work lies in sys-tematically investigating whether cognitive performance can be used to improve diagnostic supervision for speech-based cogni-tive assessment. Thus, we pose the following research ques-tions. First, how do the neuropsychological profiles of par-ticipants recruited through CognoMemory compare with those reported in the FLAME cohort, the only closely comparable reference dataset available? Second, whether relabelling par-ticipants with unconfirmed diagnoses using objective cognitive scores improves automatic speech-based classification perfor-mance and whether any observed improvements are statistically significant. Third, whether incorporating MoCA scores along-side neuropsychological measures further enhances classifica-tion accuracy. Finally, which one among three complementary relabelling strategies, FLAME-derived thresholds, Gaussian-calibrated thresholds, and normative z-score thresholds, pro-vides the most reliable supervisory signal; and how do the rel-ative importance of attention, executive function, and memory domains for estimating clinically meaningful diagnostic bound-aries assessed. The remainder of the paper answers these questions. Sec-tion 3 describes participant recruitment and dataset construc-tion, demonstrating strong agreement between our cohort and the FLAME dataset, particularly in memory performance, sup-porting cross-dataset generalisability and external validity of the proposed cognitive representation. The same section fur-ther shows that, while small domain-dependent differences ap-pear in attention and executive function across clinical and non-clinical sources, memory scores remain stable, suggesting ro-bustness to data-source heterogeneity, answering the first re-search question. Section 5 evaluates the impact of neuropsycho-logical relabelling on downstream classification, and answers the rest of the research questions. Across both binary and mul-ticlass diagnostic tasks, models using the memory domain and z-score based relabelling consistently achieve the highest F1-scores. Improvements over the original labels are statistically significant in binary classification (up to +0.13 F1), and they are the most pronounced in the more challenging three-class setting (up to +0.12), reflecting increased diagnostic relabelling accu-racy for the cognitively impaired cohort (dementia and MCI) for the internal validation dataset. The same relabelling strat-egy has also performed the best among others for three ex-ternal datasets: Pitt, ADReSS and PROCESS-2, each with an improvement of 4%, 5% and 9%. Adding MoCA into the di-agnostic relabelling strategy also increases performance up to 8 and 12% for binary and multiclass classification, highlight-ing the importance of MoCA. Finally, Section 6 provides in-terpretability analysis demonstrating that neuropsychologically informed labels reveal distinct semantic retrieval patterns in par-ticipant speech. TF-IDF analyses link improved classification performance to interpretable linguistic biomarkers of cognitive decline, supporting the clinical relevance of the proposed re-labelling framework. Taken together, this study establishes a principled methodology for transforming weakly labelled cog-nitive datasets into clinically meaningful resources, thereby ad-vancing scalable, speech-based dementia screening and remote cognitive monitoring. 

# 2. Previous work 

2.1. Neuropsychological Score–Based Diagnostic Stratifica-tion 

Obtaining reliable diagnostic labels remains a fundamental challenge in computational studies of cognitive impairment. Clinical diagnosis of dementia and MCI requires expert inter-pretation of neuroimaging, biomarkers, informant report, collat-eral history, and cognitive assessments [18], making large-scale clinically verified datasets difficult to obtain. Consequently, many neuropsychological and epidemiological studies opera-tionalise diagnosis using cognitive screening scores rather than clinically confirmed diagnostic labels. Traditional neuropsychology defines impairment relative to normative populations using standardised deviation thresholds, typically classifying MCI at approximately 1–1.5 standard de-viations below healthy performance and dementia at more se-vere deviations [19, 20]. Large cohort studies such as the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) employ neuropsychologically anchored cut-offs derived from clinically characterised reference populations to stabilise diagnostic cate-gorisation [21]. Data-driven calibration of diagnostic thresholds has previ-ously been explored for improving cognitive impairment char-acterisation under uncertain clinical supervision [22]. Gaussian and distribution-based modelling approaches enable diagnostic boundaries to be estimated directly from population statistics when expert annotations are limited or heterogeneous [23, 24]. Such approaches additionally treat cognitive decline as a contin-uous spectrum rather than strictly discrete categories, improving robustness to inter-cohort variability and intermediate disease states such as MCI [25, 26]. 

2.2. Speech-Based Detection of Cognitive Impairment 

A person’s speech and language provide sensitive markers of neurodegeneration, motivating extensive research into auto-mated dementia detection from speech recordings. Early studies using the DementiaBank Pitt Corpus showed that lexical diver-sity, pause structure, and syntactic complexity correlate strongly with cognitive decline [15]. Subsequent work demonstrated that acoustic and linguistic features extracted from spontaneous speech can distinguish dementia from healthy ageing with high accuracy [27–29]. Im-portantly, several studies report that speech-derived biomarkers reflect underlying cognitive domains such as memory retrieval and executive control rather than merely capturing superficial linguistic differences [7]. This shift toward cognitively inter-pretable modelling highlights the importance of aligning ma-chine learning pipelines with neuropsychological theory. 

2.3. Learning Under Noisy or Weak Clinical Labels 

Several studies have addressed the problem of unreliable su-pervision in medical machine learning, where real-world clin-ical datasets frequently contain incomplete, weak, or inconsis-tent annotations [34, 35]. Such limitations have motivated the development of weak-supervision and label-refinement frame-works. Methods such as probabilistic label modelling and data programming demonstrate that combining imperfect sig-nals can produce higher-quality supervisory labels than raw an-notations alone [36]. Similarly, learning with noisy labels has been shown to benefit substantially from label correction or re-labelling strategies guided by domain knowledge [37, 38]. 

2.4. Position of the Present Work 

Prior dementia-detection studies typically either rely exclu-sively on clinically confirmed labels from limited-size cohorts or train directly on noisy diagnostic annotations without sys-tematic refinement. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to explicitly reconstruct diagnostic labels using neu-ropsychologically stratified thresholds and subsequently vali-date them against independent ground-truth clinical diagnoses. Unlike prior speech-based dementia studies that rely directly on inherited diagnostic annotations, we reconstruct labels by com-bining clinically anchored diagnostic stratification strategies across attention, executive function, and memory domains for speech-based cognitive decline modelling. Rather than treat-ing unconfirmed labels as fixed supervision, we derive new di-agnostic boundaries from normative z-scores of domain-level neuropsychological performance, enabling improved automatic detection of cognitive decline. 

# 3. Data 

3.1. CognoMemory data collection along with neuropsy-chological stratification 

CognoMemory is an online, innovative AI tool which en-gages with the participant by asking 14 memory-probing, clin-ically effective questions, including motivation, quantifying memory, concern assessments, long- and short-term memory tests, semantic & phonemic fluency tests, cognitive functioning, cookie-theft and complex picture description tasks in a fixed order, to automatically analyse the possible signs of cognitive decline. The questions and the data collection procedure have been largely explained in our previous studies [12, 13]. For this study, we implemented an online platform 1 to collect neuropsychological performance across three domains, such as attention accuracy, executive function and memory. Par-ticipants are asked to complete 8 different tasks which corre-spond to three neuropsychological domains, as described in Ta-ble 1 of the FLAME study [14]. Each scores are summed up for each category. 

3.2. CognoMemory diagnosis labels and data description 

To date, more than 2000 participants have been recruited through the CognoMemory platform, of whom 720 completed the extended neuropsychological performance assessment used in this study. The distribution of diagnostic labels is sum-marised in Table 1. The largest proportion of participants consisted of healthy controls (HC), while the dataset addition-ally included 25 participants with dementia and 90 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Participants were recruited from both clinical and non-clinical sources across the United King-dom. Clinical recruitment pathways included primary and sec-ondary care services, whereas non-clinical recruitment involved sources such as Join Dementia Research, Great Minds, self-referrals, social media, word of mouth, and unknown com-munity pathways. In this study, multilingual participants, pre-dominantly representing ethnic minority communities including South Asian, Chinese, and African populations in the UK, were excluded because our recent work demonstrated that current AI

systems are not yet sufficiently reliable for detecting cognitive decline in multilingual speech [39, 40]. Among the dementia cohort, 6 participants originated from clinically confirmed sources and 19 from non-clinical recruit-ment pathways. Similarly, the MCI cohort consisted of 21 clin-ically recruited and 69 non-clinically recruited participants. In contrast, the HC cohort was predominantly community-based, consisting of 9 clinically recruited and 456 non-clinically re-cruited participants. Overall, approximately 80% of the cog-nitively impaired participants originated from clinically super-vised recruitment streams, whereas the HC group was largely derived from community recruitment. The dementia cohort had a mean age of 71.76 (8.93) years, whereas the MCI and HC groups were younger, with mean ages of 69.16 (7.61) and 65.95 (8.28) years, respectively. Female participants were more prevalent than male participants in all groups except MCI, com-prising approximately 60% of the total cohort. A total of 138 hours of speech audio spanning all 14 cog-nitively effective questions was collected from the 720 par-ticipants, including 140 participants with unconfirmed diag-noses [13], whose data were subsequently incorporated into the proposed neuropsychological relabelling framework for clas-sifier training, explained in Section 4.2. Automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems, including Whisper-medium [41], Wav2Vec 2.0 [42], and NVIDIA NeMo (NVIDIA Neural Mod-ules 1.21.0) [43] were employed to generate transcripts from recorded speech, producing approximately 900.21, 946.56, and 908.05 thousand words, respectively. A handcrafted independent test set of 150 participants was additionally prepared. Unlike the training cohort, all partici-pants in the test set were recruited exclusively through clinical pathways and therefore possessed clinically confirmed ground-truth diagnostic labels. The test set size was selected to rep-resent approximately 20% of the training cohort size (720 par-ticipants), corresponding to a standard 5-fold cross-validation evaluation scheme commonly used in machine learning stud-ies [44–46]. The test set consisted of 23 dementia, 50 MCI, and 77 HC participants, with balanced male and female represen-tation. Similar demographic trends were observed to those in the training cohort, with dementia participants being older on average than MCI and HC participants, although the overall test cohort was slightly older than the training cohort. In total, 870 participants were included across training and testing phases. A subset of participants additionally had Mon-treal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores available, which were incorporated into the clinically informed sorting strategies illustrated in Fig. 1. Because the overall cohort size remained limited, strict demographic and diagnostic balancing across age, gender, and cognitive groups was not feasible without substan-tially reducing the number of available participants; this limita-tion is discussed further in Section 6.1.

4.2. Label Reassignment Strategies 

Because diagnostic annotations originating from non-clinical recruitment sources were not independently clinically verified, they could not be assumed to represent reliable ground-truth su-pervision. Training directly on such labels risks propagating la-bel noise into downstream models, a well-recognised limitation in real-world clinical machine learning [37]. To address this issue, we adopted a neuropsychologically calibrated label-reassignment framework in which diagnostic categories were inferred from objective neuropsychological-performance measures rather than relying solely on the orig-inal annotations. The resulting labels function as clinically grounded proxy annotations, conceptually aligned with weak-supervision approaches that improve supervisory quality using imperfect or partially reliable signals [36]. Improved down-stream performance when evaluated on clinically confirmed test labels was interpreted as evidence of closer approximation to latent clinical ground truth, consistent with prior work showing that label refinement improves generalisation under noisy-label conditions [38]. Rather than learning diagnostic boundaries purely from data, the proposed framework transfers small amounts of clin-ically validated knowledge into large-scale automatically la-belled datasets through neuropsychologically anchored thresh-olding. Diagnostic cut-offs ( T1 and T2) were derived us-ing three complementary strategies: (1) clinically established thresholds from the FLAME cohort ( FLAME ), (2) Gaussian midpoint calibration estimated from a clinically verified sub-set of CognoMemory ( Cogno G ), and (3) normative deviation thresholds defined using z-score distributions relative to HC variability within CognoMemory ( Cogno Z ). Together, these strategies balance clinical priors with data-driven estimation while preserving interpretability. Two label-selection procedures were evaluated. In the first, labels were assigned solely from neuropsychological domain scores. In the second, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores were incorporated as stabilising clinical priors when available. Participants with MoCA ≤ 17 were labelled as De-mentia, scores between 18 –25 as MCI, and scores > 25 as HC, consistent with established screening conventions.

Across neuropsychological domains, these approaches produced distinct decision boundaries. For example, memory-domain dementia thresholds ranged from 67.30 (Cogno G) to 76.75 (Cogno Z), while attention-domain thresholds shifted toward higher performance ranges (86.18–86.25), reflecting domain-specific separability. The original annotations exhibit a strong imbalance toward the MCI category, whereas all strategies systematically redistribute participants toward health-ier classifications, producing class proportions more consis-tent with the external test cohort. Across methods, memory-based reassignment yields the most stable distributions, while attention- and executive-based thresholds introduce larger vari-ability depending on the underlying calibration strategy. Incor-porating MoCA priors consistently stabilises class allocation, reducing extreme shifts in diagnostic proportions and improv-ing alignment across domains. Notably, Z-score thresholding produces the most conservative impairment estimates, assign-ing fewer dementia labels and expanding the healthy-control group, whereas Gaussian midpoint approaches preserve inter-mediate MCI representation. Overall, these patterns indicate that clinically anchored relabeling primarily acts as a label-denoising mechanism, correcting potential over-assignment of impairment present in the original dataset while maintaining co-herent diagnostic structure across neuropsychological domains.

Across labelling approaches, memory-derived thresholds consistently outperformed attention and executive function vari-ants, supporting the hypothesis that memory impairment repre-sents the most robust speech-derived marker of cognitive de-cline. 5.2. Multiclass classification 

Three-class classification constitutes a substantially more chal-lenging setting due to the inclusion of the intermediate MCI category. Nevertheless, neuropsychologically guided labelling again improved performance relative to the original diagnostic labels (Fig. 6(a)). Using the Cogno Z MEM strategy, the highest F1-score have been achieved using the ‘ALL’ task, with a performance increase of +8%, 0.60 to 0.68. The largest task-specific gains were observed for the Memory task (0.54 → 0.66) and the PDT task (0.53 → 0.62), indicating improved separation between de-mentia and MCI participants. While improvements were smaller than in the binary set-ting, they remained consistent across nearly all tasks, suggest-ing that clinically anchored thresholding produces more reliable multiclass diagnostic boundaries. ASR and language-model effects were again secondary to labeling strategy. Whisper transcriptions yielded the most sta-ble performance, while RoBERTa and DistilBERT classifiers showed comparable behaviour across tasks. These findings in-dicate that performance gains primarily arise from improved training labels rather than architectural differences. Confusion-matrix analysis (Figs. 6(d) and 6(e)) demon-strates improved discrimination between dementia and MCI classes, supporting the clinical validity of neuropsychologically informed labelling. 

5.3. Improvement due to MoCA 

Incorporating MoCA-derived clinical priors produced consis-tent performance improvements across all labelling strategies (Figs. 5b and 6b). Paired statistical testing demonstrated that models trained with MoCA-informed labels achieved signifi-cantly higher F1-scores than models trained without MoCA in-formation ( t = 15 .65 , p = 2 .77 × 10 −7, paired t-test). Across tasks, MoCA integration increased performance by an average ∆F1 of 0.094, corresponding to an approximate 9– 10% relative improvement. The effect size was extremely large (Cohen’s d = 5 .22 ), indicating that the improvement is not task-specific but systematic across experimental conditions. These results suggest that global cognitive screening scores act as clinically meaningful priors that stabilise automatically generated diagnostic labels, particularly when distinguishing in-termediate cognitive states such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Due to the lack of MoCA scores for all diagnostic classes, using MoCA alone to relabel the diagnosis has not been adopted. The selection strategy ‘without MoCA’ was only adapted to internal validation, as it didn’t produce better results. 

5.4. External validation 

To evaluate the generalisability of the proposed neuropsycho-logical relabelling strategies, models trained using reassigned labels were additionally evaluated on three independent external datasets: the Pitt Corpus [15], ADReSS [16], and PROCESS-2 [17]. Both Pitt and ADReSS corpus differ substantially from CognoMemory in participant demographics, recording condi-tions, task design, and transcription pipelines, providing a rigor-ous assessment of cross-cohort robustness. They contained only two classes (cases and controls), thus they have been used only for binary classification (Figure 5(a)). As these two cohorts con-tained only picture description tasks (PDT), only Q12 of Cog-noMemory was used for training (acronymed as Pitt PDT and ADR PDT). PROCESS-2 is compiled using the CognoMemory plat-form, and there was no subject overlap between 720 train set (ta-ble 1) and 400 test set from PROCESS-2. Only Q10 , Q11 , and 

Q12 of CognoMemory was used for training. This diagnostic-rich data contains three classes, thus it has been used for both binary and multiclass classification in Figure 5(a) and 6(a) (acronymed as PRO SFT, PRO PFT and PRO PDT). Across all external cohorts, memory-based relabelling strategies consistently achieved the strongest perfor-mance. On the Pitt dataset, the best-performing model was Cogno Z MEM , improving binary classification per-formance from F1 = 0.82 to 0.86 (∆F1 = 0.04 ), while FLAME-based attention and executive-function relabelling substantially reduced performance. Similar trends were observed on the ADReSS dataset, where Cogno Z MEM 

achieved the largest improvement, increasing performance from F1 = 0 .81 to 0.86 (∆F1 = 0 .05 ). The strongest evidence of external validity was observed on the PROCESS-2 benchmark. In the binary classifica-tion setting, all Cogno-derived strategies outperformed the original labels, with Cogno Z MEM producing the largest gain, increasing performance from F1 = 0.77 to 0.85 

(∆F1 = 0.093 ). Similar improvements were observed for Cogno G MEM ( ∆F1 = 0.063 ), whereas FLAME-based attention and executive-function relabelling reduced perfor-mance substantially. In the multiclass setting, memory-based relabelling again produced the largest improvements, with 

Cogno Z MEM increasing performance from F1 = 0 .51 to 

0.60 (∆F1 = 0 .090 ), followed by Cogno G MEM ( ∆F1 =0.057 ). In contrast, FLAME-derived attention and executive-function strategies consistently degraded multiclass perfor-mance. A consistent pattern emerged across all external datasets. Relabelling strategies derived from memory-domain neuropsy-chological performance yielded the largest and most stable im-provements, whereas attention- and executive-function-based strategies produced smaller gains and FLAME-derived atten-tion/executive thresholds frequently reduced performance. No-tably, the normative z-score approach (Cogno Z) demonstrated the strongest cross-cohort transferability, achieving the highest performance improvements on Pitt, ADReSS, and PROCESS-2. These findings indicate that neuropsychological label refine-ment improves not only internal classification performance but also generalisation to independent datasets collected under sub-stantially different conditions, supporting the hypothesis that the reassigned labels more closely approximate latent clinical ground truth than the original diagnostic annotations.

# 6. Discussion 

Across both binary and multiclass classification settings, neu-ropsychologically informed label generation substantially im-proved speech-based detection of cognitive impairment relative to original clinical diagnostic labels. Memory-derived calibra-tion strategies consistently produced the strongest gains, in-creasing overall performance from F1 = 0 .72 to 0.83 in the binary task and from 0.60 to 0.68 in the more challenging mul-ticlass setting, for internal validation test set. Improvements of 4%, 5% and 9% are also observed for the same memory-based calibration while tested on external Pitt, ADReSS and PROCESS-2 data sets. Improvements were observed across nearly all speech tasks and modelling configurations, indicat-ing that performance gains arose primarily from enhanced la-bel quality rather than changes in model architecture or tran-scription system. Confusion-matrix analyses further demon-strated reduced diagnostic ambiguity and improved sensitivity to impaired participants, particularly for cognitively demanding memory elicitation tasks, supporting memory impairment as the most reliable speech-derived marker of cognitive decline. Incorporating global cognitive screening information via MoCA-derived priors produced additional systematic benefits, yielding an average ∼9–10% relative improvement in F1-score with extremely large effect sizes across experiments. These findings suggest that clinically anchored thresholding stabilises automatically generated labels and improves separation be-tween dementia, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and healthy ageing. Notably, gains persisted in the multiclass setting, where intermediate cognitive states are typically difficult to distin-guish, indicating that label refinement rather than model com-plexity represents a key bottleneck in speech-based cognitive assessment. Collectively, the results demonstrate that neuropsy-chologically grounded relabeling, particularly memory-based strategies augmented by clinical priors, provides a robust path-way toward more reliable and clinically interpretable automated cognitive screening. To investigate qualitative linguistic differences underlying classification performance, we performed a TF-IDF analysis of semantic fluency responses using the Cogno Z MEM diagnos-tic labels (Fig. 7). For each diagnostic group, TF-IDF values were normalised relative to the most distinctive word within that group, allowing comparison of relative lexical specificity rather than absolute frequency. Distinct semantic patterns emerged across cognitive states. Participants classified as healthy controls produced a wider range of large or less prototypical animals (e.g., hippopotamus ,

rhinoceros , and whale ), suggesting preserved semantic search breadth and access to lower-frequency lexical items. In con-trast, dementia participants showed higher distinctiveness for more common or visually salient animals such as cat , dog , and 

bird , consistent with restricted semantic retrieval and reliance on highly accessible category exemplars [50–52]. The MCI group exhibited an intermediate pattern, characterised by mid-frequency animals such as squirrel , rabbit , and bear , reflecting partial preservation of semantic diversity [50]. Although percentage values were not explicitly displayed in the figure, normalisation revealed that the most distinctive words within each group reached 100% relative TF-IDF impor-tance, with subsequent words gradually decreasing in distinc-tiveness. This gradient indicates that cognitive decline is associ-ated not only with reduced lexical quantity but also with altered semantic organisation and category exploration strategies. These findings support the hypothesis that neuropsycholog-ically informed labelling captures meaningful linguistic struc-ture rather than merely improving classifier optimisation, pro-viding interpretability linking speech-derived biomarkers to un-derlying cognitive decline processes. Importantly, these semantic differences emerge without manual linguistic annotation, demonstrating that neuropsycho-logically calibrated labelling enables automated discovery of clinically interpretable language biomarkers. 

6.1. Limitations 

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, although neuropsychological-performance-based relabelling improved agreement with clin-ically verified diagnoses, the reassigned labels remain indirect proxies for formal clinical diagnosis and cannot replace com-prehensive neurological assessment. Second, neuropsychological thresholds were derived from available cohort distributions and may reflect demographic, ed-ucational, cultural, or recruitment biases inherent to the source population. Although the proposed framework generalised across three independent external datasets (Pitt, ADReSS, and PROCESS-2), further validation across more diverse interna-tional cohorts will be required to establish broader population-level applicability. Third, the training cohort remained diagnostically im-balanced, with substantially fewer dementia participants than healthy controls. While the observed performance improve-ments were replicated across external datasets with differ-ing class distributions, residual imbalance may still influence learned decision boundaries and performance estimates. Larger clinically verified cohorts with more balanced diagnostic repre-sentation would help further improve robustness. Fourth, dementia participants were older on average than MCI and healthy-control participants, introducing the possi-bility of age-related linguistic and acoustic confounding. The consistent improvements observed across external datasets with different demographic characteristics suggest that age alone is unlikely to explain the reported gains; however, future work should explicitly model age, education, and other demographic factors to disentangle disease-related and demographic effects. Fifth, multilingual participants were excluded because cur-rent ASR and language models remain insufficiently reliable for multilingual cognitive assessment. Sixth, speech-derived biomarkers are influenced by factors unrelated to neurodegeneration, including recording conditions, microphone characteristics, speaker variability, and emotional state, which may introduce residual noise despite improved la-belling strategies. Finally, although multiple ASR and language models were evaluated, the study did not exhaustively explore all possible ar-chitectures. Nevertheless, the consistent gains observed across model configurations and external datasets suggest that label quality exerts a larger influence on performance than model se-lection alone. The framework was additionally validated ret-rospectively, and prospective deployment studies will be re-quired to determine whether neuropsychologically anchored relabelling improves real-world screening accuracy, clinical workflow integration, and longitudinal disease monitoring. 

# 7. Conclusion 

This study introduced a diagnostic label reassignment frame-work based on stratified neuropsychological performance for improving automatic detection of cognitive decline from real-world conversational speech collected through the CognoMem-ory platform. Rather than treating diagnostic annotations as fixed supervision, we reconstructed diagnostic labels using ob-jective neuropsychological-performance measures across mem-ory, attention, and executive function domains, enabling robust model training under uncertain or weakly supervised clinical annotations. Cross-dataset comparison between CognoMemory and the FLAME cohort demonstrated broadly consistent neuropsycho-logical trends despite observable scale differences. While atten-tion and executive function scores showed greater inter-dataset variability, memory performance remained strongly aligned across cohorts, supporting the external validity and generalis-ability of neuropsychologically grounded representations. Within the CognoMemory dataset, comparisons between clinical and non-clinical recruitment sources revealed domain-dependent differences. Attention and executive function measures exhibited occasional shifts attributable to acquisi-tion context, whereas memory performance remained stable across sources. This robustness indicates that memory-derived neuropsychological signals are less sensitive to data prove-nance and, therefore, particularly suitable for large-scale semi-automatic dataset curation. Across both binary and multiclass classification tasks, neuropsychologically informed relabeling consistently im-proved performance relative to original diagnostic annotations. Memory-based strategies achieved the highest gains, with the Cogno Z MEM approach emerging as the strongest overall per-former. Binary classification performance increased by 13% to an F1-score of 0.83, while multiclass performance reached 0.66 with a 12% increase despite the increased difficulty of separat-ing intermediate cognitive states such as MCI. Improvements of 4%, 5% and 9% are also observed for the same memory-based calibration while tested on external Pitt, ADReSS and PROCESS-2 data sets. Notably, incorporating thresholds de-rived from a relatively small number of clinically validated ob-servations substantially improved performance across experi-ments, demonstrating that weakly supervised learning benefits strongly from neuropsychological performance-based decision boundaries. Beyond quantitative improvements, TF-IDF analysis re-vealed systematic semantic differences across diagnostic groups. Dementia participants relied predominantly on highly prototypical category exemplars, whereas healthy controls ex-hibited broader semantic exploration. These findings indicate that neuropsychologically informed labelling uncovers clini-cally interpretable linguistic biomarkers rather than merely im-proving classifier optimisation. Overall, the results demonstrate that accuracy of diagnos-tic labels represents a critical bottleneck in speech-based cog-nitive assessment. By integrating stratified neuropsychological performance with weak-supervision machine learning, the pro-posed framework enables scalable, clinically interpretable, and remotely deployable cognitive screening using conversational speech. Future work will investigate adaptive threshold learning, multimodal biomarker fusion, cross-cohort validation, and strategies to address remaining dataset imbalances, while incor-porating additional cognitive screening instruments, including the Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale (RUDAS), Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE)-III, and Multi-cultural Cognitive Examination (MCE), alongside MoCA to further strengthen neuropsychologically grounded diagnostic calibration and clinical translation.
