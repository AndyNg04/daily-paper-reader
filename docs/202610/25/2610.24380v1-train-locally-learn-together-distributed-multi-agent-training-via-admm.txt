# ABSTRACT 

Multi-agent systems (MAS) can outperform individual language-model agents by combining special-ized roles, diverse reasoning processes, and collaborative decision-making. However, collaborative MAS training is typically centralized, requiring a common trainer to aggregate role-local trajectories and learning signals and maintain model and optimization states. This motivates a distributed multi-agent learning paradigm in which agents improve system-level capabilities through role-local learning and decentralized communication, paving the way toward scalable, resilient, and self-organizing MAS. To this end, we introduce, to the best of our knowledge, the first optimization-grounded framework for distributed MAS training based on consensus alternating direction method of multi-pliers (ADMM). We formulate collaborative MAS learning as a consensus-constrained finite-sum optimization problem, naturally lending itself to distributed optimization via ADMM. The proposed approach alternates role-local learning with inter-agent consensus using distributed communication protocols such as ring all-reduce, enabling joint optimization while keeping each agent’s data and training state local. We evaluate our framework on distributed safety alignment and general reasoning tasks. Under compromised-agent settings, ADMM can reduce HarmBench attack success rate (ASR) by up to 26.4%, while achieving reasoning gains comparable to centralized training. These results show that distributed MAS training retains the benefits of centralized joint optimization without requiring a centralized trainer. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly organized into multi-agent systems (MAS), in which agents assume specialized roles and communicate in natural language or latent messages to solve complex tasks collaboratively (Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2026). By coordinating complementary roles, diverse reasoning processes, and iterative refinement, MAS can achieve stronger system-level performance than individual agents. Recent work has therefore moved beyond prompting and inference-time collaboration toward directly training participating agents through fine-tuning or reinforcement learning to improve their collective behavior (Motwani et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). This shift from inference-time collaboration to learning raises a fundamental question of how interacting agents should be trained as a system. Most existing approaches realize collaborative MAS learning through centralized training , in which a single training process jointly manages multi-agent trajectories, learning signals, parameters, and optimization states (Motwani et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). While effective for coordinating learning across agents, this paradigm assumes that their training information and optimization states can be collected and managed by a common trainer (see Fig. 1(a) ). Such an assumption becomes increasingly restrictive as MAS scale or span different devices, organizations, and trust domains. Training agents independently avoids the need for a common trainer, but gives up the system-level coordination that collaborative MAS learning seeks to achieve. Motivated by this tradeoff, we develop a princi-pled approach to distributed MAS training, where each agent is trained on its own node with locally maintained training information and optimization states, while training is coordinated across agents without a centralized trainer. Conventional dis-tributed learning typically distributes a model-training process across workers or clients, often according to where computation or data is located (Duchi et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2018; McMahan et al., 2017; Yuan et al., 2022; Douillard et al., 2024). In our setting, the partition instead follows the functional structure of the MAS: even when all roles share the same underlying model, each node carries out the local learning associated with one agent role, based on the trajectories and learning signals arising from that role. The resulting local updates must be coordinated across nodes so that the MAS is improved as a whole. The challenge is therefore to distribute collaborative MAS training across role-local learning processes without los-ing the coordination provided by centralized joint training. This motivates our central question: 

(Q) How can we collaboratively train an MAS using a principled distributed optimization framework while retaining the performance of centralized training? 

To answer (Q) , we formulate distributed MAS training as a consensus optimization problem over local copies of the shared model parameters. Each agent performs role-local learning on its own node using the trajectories and learning signals associated with its functional role, while consensus coordinates these local updates across agents. Building on this formulation, we develop an alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM )framework (Boyd et al., 2011; Parikh and Boyd, 2014) as the core optimization mechanism for distributed MAS training (see Fig. 1(b) ). ADMM naturally separates training into two complementary steps: role-local optimization , where each node updates the shared model using the trajectories and learning signals associated with its role, and consensus coordination , where nodes reconcile their local model copies while keeping trajectories and optimization states local. This decomposition allows each agent to train independently between communication rounds while still enforcing system-wide agreement through the ADMM consensus updates. We realize the consensus step either exactly with ring all-reduce or approximately with decentralized gossip (Patarasuk and Yuan, 2009; Boyd et al., 2006). In this way, ADMM provides a principled bridge between local agent learning and global MAS coordination, enabling collaborative training without centralizing agents’ trajectories, data, or optimization states. Prior works have explored distributed or decentralized MAS through inference-time coordination (Yang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Mao and Mirhoseini, 2026), as well as agent-specific policy learning and distributed rollout generation (Zhang et al., 2026a; Fu et al., 2026). In contrast, we distribute the optimization of a shared MAS model across role-local training nodes and coordinate its local copies through ADMM-based consensus. We evaluate our approach on reasoning and safety alignment against frozen MAS and centralized joint training. Across both settings, distributed training closely approaches centralized performance (see Fig. 1(c) ). Component ablations further show neither local-only training nor periodic parameter averaging is sufficient to match the full ADMM procedure. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 

① We formulate the collaborative training of a shared MAS model as a finite-sum distributed optimization problem over role-local objectives induced by agent roles, and recast it into an ADMM-compatible consensus formulation. 

② We develop an ADMM-based framework that alternates role-local learning with inter-agent consensus, supports flexible communication protocols, and keeps each agent’s trajectories and training states local. 2Train Locally, Learn Together: Distributed Multi-Agent Training via ADMM A P REPRINT 

③ Across reasoning and safety-alignment tasks, distributed training closely approaches centralized training perfor-mance, including safety settings where one agent is compromised. We further study distributed MAS training across communication protocols and system scales, while ablations show the importance of consensus and dual updates. 

# 2 Related Works 

Multi-agent training, communication, and collaboration. LLM-based MAS organize multiple agents into comple-mentary roles and coordinate their interactions toward a shared output (Li et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2026). One line of work treats the underlying LLMs as fixed and improves collaboration through agent composition, roles, prompts, and communication structures (Chen et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a,b,c; Yao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). More recent work directly trains agents for collaboration, building on multi-agent RL for credit assignment (Kuba et al., 2022) and optimizing role-specific behavior toward system-level objectives (Liao et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025; Motwani et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Mo et al., 2025; Dang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026). However, these approaches largely assume centralized training, leaving open how agents can learn collaboratively while keeping data and training states local. 

Distributed optimization. Distributed optimization enables multiple learners to optimize locally while coordinating through consensus (Boyd et al., 2011; Shi et al., 2014; Ouyang et al., 2013). Such coordination can be realized through collective communication such as ring all-reduce (Patarasuk and Yuan, 2009) or decentralized gossip (Xiao and Boyd, 2004; Boyd et al., 2006). These principles have been increasingly adopted for distributed training of LLMs, where model updates are coordinated across geographically or computationally separated workers (Yuan et al., 2022; Ryabinin et al., 2023; Douillard et al., 2024; Jaghouar et al., 2024; Borzunov et al., 2023), as well as for parameter-efficient LLM adaptation (Sun et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a). 

Communication and coordination in multi-agent systems. A broad line of work studies how LLM-based agents exchange information and aggregate decisions, including multi-agent debate (Du et al., 2024; Chan et al., 2024), voting and consensus-based aggregation (Kaesberg et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2026), and latent-space collaboration (Zou et al., 2026). Another line focuses on the communication structure itself, ranging from sparse debate topologies (Li et al., 2024) to learned or adaptive communication graphs (Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a). Related work further explores decentralized MAS through agent discovery and communication (Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a) and decentralized coordination (Yang et al., 2025). Beyond inference-time coordination, recent work has explored decentralized MAS training through separate policy trainers (Zhang et al., 2026a) and distributed training infrastructure (Fu et al., 2026). In contrast, we formulate MAS training as consensus optimization, allowing each agent to optimize locally while coordinating model updates through ADMM consensus and keeping trajectories and training states local. 

Multi-agent safety and alignment. Multi-agent interaction introduces additional safety risks: compromised agents can induce harmful behavior through role-play or persona injection (Tian et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a), while jailbreaks and errors can propagate across agents (Lee et al., 2025; Gu et al., 2024; Ju et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025; Cemri et al., 2026), with their impact shaped by communication topology and compromised positions (Yu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b). Existing defenses largely rely on guard models or agents (Inan et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2024; Hu and Hooi, 2026), while other work evaluates safety at the agent and system levels (Andriushchenko et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024b; Naik et al., 2026). Parameter-level alignment has primarily focused on individual models (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Loye et al., 2026), while recent joint agent training remains centralized (Zhang et al., 2026b). We use multi-agent safety alignment as an evaluation setting for distributed MAS training under both aligned and compromised-agent settings. 

# 3 Distributed MAS Training: Motivation and Formulation 

This section motivates and formalizes distributed MAS training. We first show, through a safety study, that composing individually aligned agents into an MAS can amplify unsafe behavior, so alignment must be treated as a system-level learning problem. We then explain why conventional centralized training is ill-suited to this setting and formulate distributed MAS training, in which each agent learns from a role-local objective. These local objectives remain coupled through inter-agent messages, which we identify as the key challenge of interaction-induced coupling.

These observations reveal a new training demand for MAS : safety alignment should be treated as a system-level learning problem that accounts for agents’ functional roles, communication, and collective behavior under composition. More broadly, effective MAS adaptation requires coordinating role-local learning toward a shared system-level objective rather than optimizing agents independently. 

Problem statement: Distributed MAS training. The system-wide training demand identified above requires coor-dinated learning across agents. However, conventional MAS training typically realizes this coordination through a centralized process that collects agents’ trajectories, computes their learning signals, and maintains their parameters (Park et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025; Mo et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Such a design incurs increasing communication, memory, and computational overhead as the MAS scales. It also creates a single point of failure and assumes a central authority can access all role-local information, which may not hold when agents are distributed across devices, organizations, or trust domains. This motivates distributed MAS training , where agents learn from local data, maintain their own training states, and coordinate toward a shared system-level objective. Specifically, consider an MAS of N agents with distinct functional roles interacting over a directed communication graph, where edges carry inter-agent messages during each rollout. Following common practice, agents share the same LLM backbone but use role-specific system prompts (Du et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023). Given a query, each agent conditions on its prompt, the query, and upstream messages to generate its message, with the final message forming the system output. For distributed training, agent i maintains a local copy θi of the shared trainable adapter, implemented as a LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2021). Let fi denote the role-local training objective at agent i. Although agents optimize local copies of the same adapter, their objectives differ because they are constructed from role-specific prompts, received messages, and learning signals arising from the joint MAS rollout. Effect of approximate gossip consensus on ADMM. The consensus averaging in (10) can be approximated by gossip, where neighboring agents repeatedly exchange and average their local variables. This yields a communication– performance trade-off: more gossip rounds im-prove information mixing but increase communi-cation traffic. As shown in Fig. 5 , increasing the number of gossip rounds ( τ ) from 1 to 2 reduces the ADMM primal residual from 2.34 to 0.80 and HarmBench ASR from 8.18% to 4.84% . Further increasing τ to 16 yields little additional change in either residual or ASR, while communication continues to grow. Thus, τ = 2 captures most of the benefit of gossip in this setting at substan-tially lower communication cost. Compared with approximate gossip, ring all-reduce computes the exact consensus average in (10). Since our MAS contains relatively few training agents, we use ring all-reduce by default to obtain exact consen-sus with predictable communication, while retaining gossip as an alternative.

# 6 Conclusion 

We study distributed MAS training, where agents optimize role-local objectives without a centralized trainer. We formulate MAS learning as consensus-constrained distributed optimization and solve it with ADMM, alternating local updates with inter-agent consensus while keeping trajectories and training states local. Across safety alignment and general reasoning, distributed training closely matches centralized training, including compromised-agent settings, and ablations highlight the importance of consensus and dual updates, supporting scalable decentralized MAS training. 

Limitations and future work. Our experiments focus on representative sequential and hierarchical MAS built from a common LLM backbone with shared adapter parameterization, which keeps the model family and parameterization consistent across agents; broader evaluation across larger systems, diverse model families and task domains, and richer communication topologies is needed to establish generality. An important direction is to characterize how communication topology affects convergence, efficiency, and downstream performance, relating these behaviors to graph-theoretic properties such as connectivity, centrality, community structure, and spectral characteristics, potentially with edges weighted by the heterogeneity or interaction strength between roles. More complex MAS may further require hypergraph-based formulations, where dependencies capture computations that jointly involve multiple roles. # C Experimental Details 

C.1 System Prompts 

In the sequential chain, the Solver is asked to solve the question completely, to number its steps and state what each step establishes so that any single step can be checked in isolation, and to commit to a specific answer rather than hedge. The Verifier is shown the candidate solution and told that its only job is to find errors, checking in order whether the candidate answers the question actually asked, whether the method is the right one, whether each algebraic step is correct, whether units, signs and rounding are right, and whether the answer is in the demanded form. The Corrector 

receives the candidate together with the verifier report and repairs the identified errors. The Decider sees the prior agents’ work and issues the final answer. C.2 Backbone and Adapter 

The aligned backbone is Qwen/Qwen3-8B , run in thinking mode with a fixed generation budget. The compromised agent uses mlabonne/Qwen3-8B-abliterated , an abliterated variant with reduced refusal behavior (Arditi et al., 2024). It shares the tokenizer and architecture of the aligned backbone and can therefore replace any role without architectural changes. Each agent maintains a local trainable parameter copy θi, implemented as a rank-16 LoRA adapter with scaling α = 16 , applied to the seven projections ( q, k, v, o, gate , up , and down ) in all 36 layers. Writing the corresponding local update to a base weight as ∆Wi = BiA, we initialize the down-projection A identically across agents and keep it frozen, while training only Bi.

C.3 Details of training Training data. Harmful requests. We construct the harmful training set from AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023) and the standard and contextual HarmBench validation splits (Mazeika et al., 2024), assigning all retained prompts a refusal target. We remove prompts overlapping with HarmBench or XSTest evaluation data, and use only HarmBench validation examples that are disjoint from the test splits used for evaluation. 

Benign requests. A slice of the MMLU-Pro test split (Wang et al., 2024b), kept with their native QA answer type and scored as ordinary questions. They are the over-refusal / stay-helpful control: without them every retained fine-tuning target is a refusal and the trained system collapses into refusing everything. 

Safety. Each agent is then fit on its message with the ordinary deployment prompt as input, so what is learned is the behaviour itself rather than the collection prompt, and nothing at test time depends on the hardened prompt. We draw up to eight trajectories per query, at temperature 0.8 and top -p 0.95 , with a per-agent budget of 4096 tokens. 

General reasoning. Collection is on-policy and repeats every outer round. Queries are selected by excluding every anchor already used in an earlier round, so successive rounds never overlap; each round pre-screens a window of candidates by sampling each twice and dropping those answered correctly both times, then collects a handful of trajectories for each survivor and keeps only the groups whose outcomes are mixed. Sampling uses the same temperature and top -p with an 8192 -token per-agent budget. 

Optimization. Table A2 lists the optimizer settings. Each ADMM round deploys the current consensus adapter, collects (or reuses) a batch of rollouts, and runs L local gradient steps in the x-step of (7). 

Data filtering. Each role is trained using the system-level outcome reward of the full rollout (Wan et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). For reasoning, R is answer correctness, and we retain queries whose sampled rollouts contain both successful and unsuccessful outcomes. For safety, harmful queries receive R = 1 when the final response is judged harmless, while benign queries receive R = 1 when answered correctly; refusals on benign queries are therefore penalized. We further include a role’s message in the safety loss only when the rollout succeeds and the message itself is harmless for harmful queries or non-refusal for benign queries, preventing unsafe intermediate messages from being reinforced by a safe final response. All roles remain trainable, including the compromised role. 18 Train Locally, Learn Together: Distributed Multi-Agent Training via ADMM A P REPRINT 

For both tasks, we balance training examples across roles, de-duplicate repeated targets, and discard generations with incomplete reasoning blocks. By default, the loss is applied to the full message, including its reasoning content. 

C.4 Details of evaluation 

The five safety benchmarks use different scoring criteria, so their absolute values are not directly comparable. Harm-Bench uses its Llama-2-13B classifier (Mazeika et al., 2024) to judge whether the final response is harmful , whereas StrongReject, JailbreakBench, and toxic OR-Bench measure whether the system complies rather than refuses. The HarmBench Llama-2-13B classifier is the primary harmfulness judge throughout, pinned to one revision. A Granite guardian classifier was run as a sensitivity check and is not used for any reported number: it flags terse refusals as harmful and shifts the comparison by roughly five points, in a direction that depends on how verbose an arm is. Where a benchmark ships its own notion of compliance we keep it, so that our numbers stay comparable to how that benchmark is normally reported, and we never compare an absolute value across benchmarks. Evaluation runs in thinking mode at temperature 0.6 and top -p 0.95 with a fixed seed. Two fixed profiles are used and never mixed: safety evaluations allow 4096 tokens per agent and capability evaluations allow 8192 .For general reasoning, we evaluate each benchmark on a fixed subset sampled once and reused across all methods, ensuring that every bar in Fig. 4 uses the same questions. Each subset contains up to 256 examples. Safety benchmarks use their standard evaluation splits.
