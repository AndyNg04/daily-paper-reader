# ABSTRACT 

How does post-training change cooperation during repeated interaction? Within each of seven released model lineages, we compare pretrained (Base), supervised fine-tuned (SFT), and subsequent preference- or reward-optimized (Aligned) check-points across seven social dilemmas. Copies of each checkpoint play one another for 200 rounds, with visible history limited to the last two or forty rounds. The memory curse refers to lower cooperation when agents can see more past rounds. Our central finding is that post-training amplifies this decline. Averaged across the six binary-action games, expanding visible history reduces cooperation at every SFT and Aligned checkpoint. At each post-trained stage, these declines exceed Base’s in six of seven lineages. Most of this average amplification is already present at SFT. With long history, both post-trained stages also cooperate less than Base on average in each binary-action game. In the symmetric binary games, their fitted cooperation rates fall more steeply on average than Base’s as per-round cooperation costs rise, benefits fall, or noncooperation becomes more rewarding. The difference in cooperation rates between Aligned and SFT also changes from one-shot decisions to repeated play. When given the same past records, their choices depend on whether the instructions describe a final round or possible future interaction. Post-training evaluation should therefore go beyond isolated choices to assess whether cooperation is sustained in repeated interaction and how it depends on payoffs and visible history. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Post-training adapts models to follow instructions and satisfy evaluated preferences (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023). Repeated interaction adds a challenge: sustaining cooperation despite incentives to deviate (Piatti et al., 2024). In a public-goods game (PGG), for example, players keep their endowments or contribute them to a pool. The pool is multiplied by a factor greater than one and shared equally among all players, including noncontributors. In our PGG, mutual contribution maximizes group payoff and gives everyone more than mutual retention. Yet withholding one’s contribution while others contribute earns that player even more this round, so mutual contribution is not a one-round Nash equilibrium. Repetition changes the trade-off: the immediate gain from defection can be outweighed by losing future mutual cooperation (Fudenberg & Maskin, 1986). We ask: how does cooperation differ across training stages, and how do these differences change when agents can see more of their interaction history? 

Past interaction matters because agents can use it to decide whether to keep cooperating, withdraw, or try to restore cooperation. A longer record gives them more evidence about their partners; its effect on cooperation depends on how that evidence changes their choices. Whose cooperation is measured? Within each run, we average players and then rounds. We then average runs and payoff configurations. Thus Trust counts each of its three players equally: the investor has weight 1/3 and the two trustees together 2/3. The separate shared-record tests score the queried player’s action. 3One-shot and repeated decisions. One-shot prompts omit history and future-round statements. Repeated actions are simultaneous. Following Tewolde et al. (2026) and Liu et al. (2026), prompts describe probabilistic continuation and a long-run payoff objective. Our prompts state a 99% probability of another round; recording uses a fixed 200 rounds without announcing this endpoint. The history limit (HL) caps visible completed rounds at two (HL2) or forty (HL40); early in play, agents see all available rounds up to that cap. Each configuration uses five trajectory seeds or 20 one-shot draws. Section 5.2 defines the separate final-round and continuing probes. 

Response formats. In the main one-shot and repeated-game evaluation, the model gives a rationale and then selects a legal action label. The shared-record tests use game-native JSON actions; the PGG shown-versus-hidden test also requests a rationale before the action. Sampling uses temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8, and top-k 20. 

What we measure. The cooperation rate is the fraction of decisions taking the game’s designated cooperative action. We report rates as percentages and changes as percentage points (pp): a fall from 70% to 60% is a change of −10 pp. The six binary-action games, including Trust, form our 

cooperation panel ; overall cooperation and history summaries weight these games equally. TD instead measures the chosen claim, normalized from zero for the lowest legal claim to one for the highest. Tables and figures multiply this value by 100 and report it separately. 

How to read the summaries. Each lineage contributes one estimate to an overall mean. We first average runs and payoff settings within that lineage; summaries of the six binary games then weight those games equally. To compare stages, we compute their difference within each lineage before averaging the seven differences. Tables report the mean and, where shown, the SD across these seven estimates. The SD describes how much the models differ; a count such as 6/7 says how many lineages show the stated direction. Intervals give a range for the mean, based on either variation across lineages or repeated sampling with the same models, as specified in each table. 

Complementary stage comparisons. Self-play measures the group behavior each checkpoint produces under common game rules and history limits. Every player uses the same checkpoint, so changing stages replaces both the focal player and its partners and changes the histories generated through interaction. The shared-record tests in Section 5.2 hold the presented past records fixed to compare checkpoints on common inputs.

Does showing past records have a different effect under final-round and continuing instructions? We give SFT and Aligned fresh PGG decisions at (n, r ) = (3 , 1.4) , (3 , 2.2) , (5 , 1.8) , (5 , 4.2) under four combinations: records shown or hidden, each with F INAL or C ONTINUE . Records come from separately collected SFT and Aligned games at these settings. For each lineage and payoff, we sample eight records from each source stage in each time bin: rounds 1–20, 21–50, 51–100, and 101–200. # 6 RELATED WORK 

Cooperation and memory in language agents. Prior studies examine how language agents respond to social objectives, partners, and repeated interaction (Phelps & Russell, 2025; Fontana et al., 2025; Akata et al., 2025; Piatti et al., 2024). A fuller comparison appears in Appendix A. This literature evaluates both cooperation-sustaining mechanisms, such as repetition, reputation, mediation, and contracting in CoopEval (Tewolde et al., 2026), and history effects, including the memory curse (Liu et al., 2026). CoopShift asks whether the cooperation change from showing more history differs across Base, SFT, and Aligned checkpoints within a released lineage. We complement this comparison with probes that give SFT and Aligned the same past records and with the payoffs earned during self-play. These measure, respectively, choices under common historical inputs and outcomes generated by interacting groups. 

Post-training and human behavior. Instruction and preference optimization train models to produce desired responses (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023). We ask how released checkpoints behave when their decisions affect other players. Human experiments provide comparisons for specific responses to costs, benefits, and past interaction. 

# 7 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Differences in cooperation between checkpoints depend on both costs and benefits and the history agents can see. Most of the average memory-curse amplification appears between Base and SFT, making demonstration training central to understanding it. 

Interpreting Base behavior. Base follows the same procedure of giving a rationale and then choosing an action. Its small average history effect combines different responses: InternLM2 Base and Nemotron’s Qwen3 Base decline by about 7.6 and 6.0 pp. Some Base checkpoints therefore respond to history. 

Conclusion. Post-training amplifies the memory curse in our six-binary-game panel. At HL40, both post-trained stages cooperate less than Base on average in each binary game. In the symmetric binary games, their fitted cooperation rates also fall more than Base’s, on average, as per-round cooperation costs rise, benefits fall, or noncooperation becomes more rewarding. Instruction tuning and learning from human feedback improve evaluated instruction following and preferences (Ouyang et al., 2022); DPO optimizes preferences without the same explicit reward-model-and-RL pipeline (Rafailov et al., 2023). Generalization comparisons of SFT and RL (Chu et al., 2025), and the distinction between high-probability reasoning success and solutions recoverable by sampling (Yue et al., 2025), emphasize defining the evaluation target. We compare released stages on their own generated cooperation, payoff responses, and history responses across several preference-and reward-optimization objectives. Economic-game evaluations already demonstrate sensitivity to objectives, prompts, partners and repetition (Phelps & Russell, 2025; Fontana et al., 2025; Akata et al., 2025). SOTOPIA evaluates interactive goals and relationships (Zhou et al., 2024); GovSim adds resource sustainability over time (Piatti et al., 2024), unlike our stationary CPR payoff. Social training studies investigate behavior cloning, self-reinforcement, reward design, and incentive-supported coordination (Wang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2025). Liu et al. (2026) introduce the memory curse as lower cooperation with more visible interaction history and study historical content, reasoning, and interventions. We ask how this decline changes along a training sequence. Our comparisons KS/B and KA/B measure whether longer histories reduce cooperation more after post-training than at Base; KA/S compares SFT with its later Aligned checkpoint. We build on part of that experimental framework with newly collected game data. Studies of reasoning in public goods and norm enforcement further motivate examining both the rationale and the selected action (Piedrahita et al., 2025; Li & Shirado, 2025). Human conditional-contribution schedules (Fischbacher et al., 2001), separately elicited preferences and beliefs (Fischbacher & Gächter, 2010), and distributional/reciprocity choices (Charness & Rabin, 2002) motivate separating an opening action from its subsequent feedback. A.3 GAME -SPECIFIC PAYOFF RESPONSES AND HUMAN COMPARISONS 

Human public-goods studies separately manipulate MPCR/group size in ten-period play (Isaac & Walker, 1988) and private/external returns in ten no-feedback one-shot decisions (Goeree et al., 2002); a single-decision study reports no statistically detected MPCR response (Struwe et al., 2024). These designs motivate identifying both the economic margin and the interaction protocol. Sequential dilemmas vary a second mover’s private deviation gain and harm to a cooperative first mover (Baader et al., 2026). Their positive mutual-cooperation reference supports relative-gain measures; our response statistic instead uses the tested span of each game’s raw parameter. Human TD claims fall with larger reward/penalty sizes under random rematching (Capra et al., 1999); related LLM signatures are studied by Roberts et al. (2025). Human Trust experiments elicit investment and returns in a sequential two-person design (Berg et al., 1995); ours elicits one investor’s and two trustees’ simultaneous contingent intentions. Behavioral-test similarity (Mei et al., 2024) and prediction of observed human choices (Shapira et al., 2026; Binz et al., 2026) are different evaluation targets; the latter also includes one-shot/first-round settings favoring aligned models. Our comparisons concern specific payoff and history responses under each study’s stated protocol. 13 A.4 CONTINUATION , PRESENTATION , AND SHARED -INPUT COMPARISONS 

Repeated-game theory distinguishes equilibrium support from equilibrium selection (Fudenberg & Maskin, 1986). Human repeated-PD evidence studies this distinction empirically (Dal Bó & Fréchette, 2011). Public-goods studies use different finite or probabilistic stopping procedures and report different cooperation patterns (Lugovskyy et al., 2017; Anderson et al., 2024). They motivate specifying both what agents are told about future rounds and how collection actually stops. Our canonical data record 200 rounds under continuing instructions; the shared-record tests vary a current decision’s instructions at fixed presented history and payoff. Externality-framing experiments change how a decision is presented while keeping its potential payoffs fixed (Andreoni, 1995). Our PGG comparison shows or hides past records, which also changes prompt length. In the final-round condition, contributing still changes the player’s current payoff by r/n − 1, regardless of the records shown. These tests ask how a model responds to the information it receives, alongside our measurements of cooperation during actual interaction.
