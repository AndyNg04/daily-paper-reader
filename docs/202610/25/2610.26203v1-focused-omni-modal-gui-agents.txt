Abstract 

Graphical User Interface (GUI) agents increasingly act over streaming omni-modal observations spanning screenshots, audio, video, interaction history, and device states. However, exposing this complete context uniformly at every step ignores that relevance is transient and sparse: the signal determining one action can be-come clutter at the next. Because executed actions feed back into the context, these errors compound into Sequential Distraction, driving the agent into irreversible trajectory drift. We formulate the underlying requirement as Focused Perception: a representation that is step-adaptive, action-sufficient, and non-intrusive. We realize this in FocusGUI , a lightweight adapter that dynamically prioritizes multimodal channels against a step-specific decision reference. Instead of uniformly processing all inputs, it compresses the most relevant evidence into a compact latent and sub-tly injects it into the frozen decoder’s embeddings, guiding the model’s attention without altering its native parameter space. FocusGUI trains from action supervi-sion alone, requiring neither modality-relevance labels nor backbone fine-tuning. 1 Introduction 

Graphical User Interface (GUI) agents are shifting from parsing static screenshots to acting on dynamic, streaming observations across multiple modalities (Henry et al., 2026; Jang et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2026). In realistic computing environments, the evidence needed for the next action need not be confined to the current visual frame. An unexpected notification chime might demand an immediate context switch, a specific transition in a live video can dictate when to click, and spoken instructions must be continuously mapped to visual changes on the screen (ByteDance Seed, 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025). Contemporary omni-modal models can ingest screenshots, video streams, audio, interaction history, and device states within a single network (Xu et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2026). This has naturally led to a default paradigm: exposing the complete multimodal observation to the model at every interaction step and leaving it to implicitly determine what matters while generating the action. Leaving this selection entirely to the decoder assumes that it can reliably rediscover what matters from the complete observation at every interaction step. Our analysis reveals why this is difficult (Fig. 1). When individual modalities are removed during multi-step interaction, the dominant modality switches across nearly half of adjacent steps; even within a single step, only a small fraction of the observation may support the decision, while the rest can become distracting. Relevance is therefore fundamentally a property of the step , not a static property of the observation. Yet the decoder is forced to rediscover this relevance at every step, without any explicit signal of what has changed. In a closed-loop agent, such misreads do not end with the current prediction: the executed action changes the environment and feeds back into the observation and history of the next step, allowing errors to compound over time. We refer to this compounding failure as Sequential Distraction .The most intuitive remedy is to perform subtraction on the inputs. This is exactly what existing methods attempt: cropping parts of the current screen to retain only candidate regions of interest (Lu et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026b; Cheng et al., 2024). However, this strategy of giving less fundamentally misaligns with the nature of streaming interaction in two ways. First, the decisive signal could emerge from audio, video, or device status at any moment, channels entirely outside the scope of spatial cropping. Second, even when the signal resides on the screen, the crudely cropped background often contains the essential contextual evidence needed to ground accurate coordinates and text parameters. The problem is not that the decoder is given too much, but that it lacks guidance on how to prioritize it. A proper focus mechanism should change how the decoder reads the observation, rather than altering what it is given .We realize this principle in FocusGUI (Fig. 2). Since the decoder can already read the full observation and what it lacks is a signal of what matters at the current step, FocusGUI leaves both the backbone and its input untouched and supplies that signal alongside them. At every step, it exposes the observation as separable perceptual channels, scores each against a reference derived from the current observation as a whole, and compresses the prioritized channels into a compact focus latent. The latent enters as a zero-initialized residual at the boundary where the decoder begins to generate the action. This residual reallocates attention over the entire observation without altering it, guiding both the choice of action and the grounding of its arguments. Training uses next-action supervision alone, with no labels indicating which modality matters. On average across four omni-modal backbones, FocusGUI raises Type Match from 31.0% to 64.2% and Exact Match from 11.2% to 33.5% over the frozen backbone, and reaches 1.9 × the Type Match of the strongest enhancement method. The gains grow with interaction depth, as Sequential Distraction predicts: from the first step to the fourth, the frozen Qwen2.5-Omni loses half of its Type Match while FocusGUI retains 86.8%. > Compact Focus and Residual Alignment

The prioritized representation ct determines how different evidence sources contribute to the auxiliary pathway, but still lies in the full shared channel space. We therefore compress it into a lower-dimensional focus representa-tion: zt = Ps(ct) ∈ Rdz , dz < d. This projection constrains the capacity of the auxiliary conditioning signal , rather than the information accessible to the backbone. Because the complete observation remains available through the original multimodal pathway, zt need not independently encode every visual detail, coordinate, or text argument required for execution. Instead, it provides a compact summary of what should receive greater emphasis for the current action. To make this focus representation directly usable by the frozen decoder, we align it with the decoder’s native embedding space at the action boundary. Let

The additive form is equally important: it preserves the original embeddings and uses the focus representation only as an additional conditioning signal. Importantly, Jt consists of existing prompt positions: FocusGUI neither appends textual hints nor removes any multimodal input.

3 Experiments 

3.1 Experimental Setup 

Dataset and Metrics. We evaluate on OmniGUI (Henry et al., 2026), a benchmark of omni-modal smartphone GUI interactions across 29 applications; we use 708 episodes with 2,572 action steps. Each step pairs a screenshot with synchronous audio, video, and action history, so the decisive signal can come from any of the four streams. We report Type Match (TM), Exact Match (EM), and Goal Progress (GP). TM checks whether the predicted action primitive matches the ground truth; EM further requires predicted coordinates to fall within the target element’s bounding box and generated text to match the reference. GP is the fraction of correctly executed steps per episode, averaged across episodes. 5Focused Omni-Modal GUI Agents  

> Models.

We evaluate four representative open-source omni-modal backbones: Qwen2.5-Omni (Xu et al., 2025), VITA-1.5 (Fu et al., 2025), MiniCPM-o 4.5 (Cui et al., 2026), and Baichuan-Omni-1.5 (Li et al., 2025b). The four backbones support textual, visual, auditory, and temporal inputs but differ in architecture and multimodal representation, which lets the comparison probe the generality of FocusGUI across backbone families. For each backbone, the base model stays frozen, and training updates only the lightweight FocusGUI head and action predictors, using the same episode-disjoint split across all four models.  

> Baselines.

We compare against four methods, each applied to all four backbones without modifying their weights. DRS-GUI (Liu et al., 2026b), OmniParser (Lu et al., 2024), and RegionFocus (Luo et al., 2025) refine the visual input at inference by searching over candidate regions, parsing the screenshot into structured elements, and zooming into attended regions. GUI-Actor (Wu et al., 2025a) instead learns an action head over the backbone’s visual tokens, trained on the same annotations and split as FocusGUI with the backbone frozen. All methods receive the same observation and decoding procedure, differing only in how that observation is organized before the action is generated. The trained head separates two explanations for any gain: trainable parameters on this data, which GUI-Actor also has, and step-adaptive organization, which FocusGUI adds.  

> Implementation Details.

Evidence channels are projected into a shared 128-dimensional space and weighted by the full-modal query with softmax temperature τ = 1.0; the weighted representation is compressed to a 64-dimensional action-sufficient embedding and expanded into K = 4 zero-initialized residual tokens added to the last four prompt embeddings before the action boundary, introducing 1.15M–1.45M trainable parameters depending on the backbone. With the encoder, decoder, and LM head frozen, we train only these modules for three epochs with AdamW under the teacher-forced action-token negative log-likelihood, selecting the learning rate 2 × 10 −4 and weight decay 10 −3. At inference, the frozen decoder generates actions autoregressively with greedy decoding. Additional implementation details are provided in the Appendix D. 

3.2 Main Results  

> Consistent gains across backbones.

Table 1 evaluates four omni-modal backbones under identical frozen weights. Averaged across them, FocusGUI adds 33.2 TM, 22.3 EM, and 6.6 GP points over direct inference and 30.1, 23.6, and 8.0 points over the strongest baseline at each backbone. It holds the best TM and the best EM in all 24 backbone-category cells.  

> Why screen refinement backfires.

The baselines fail in a way that is diagnostic rather than incidental. DRS-GUI, OmniParser, and RegionFocus are near-neutral on TM ( +0.9 points on average) yet lower EM by 28% and GP by 35% in relative terms, in 12 of 12 cases for each metric. Narrowing a screenshot makes the action primitive more salient, which is what TM rewards, while discarding the surrounding context that fixes coordinates and text, which is what EM requires. None of the three reaches audio, video, or device state, so a decision resting on those channels stays unrecoverable however the screen is processed. This is what our framing predicts: once relevance is a property of a step rather than of a region, refining the region cannot restore it and may cost more than it returns.  

> Where the gains come from.

The structure of the gains indicates what the representation supplies. Per-category TM gains run from 37.3 points on Temporal, where the decisive signal persists across frames and a single screenshot serves worst, to 27.8 on Instant, where the decision is reactive and largely present on the current screen; EM follows the same order. Across backbones, the overall TM spread narrows from 35.6 points to 13.0, indicating that choosing the correct primitive becomes substantially less backbone-dependent under a better-organized observation. EM variation instead reflects backbone-specific argument-generation capacity. At episode level, improvements in per-step prediction translate into Goal Progress when they are sustained across the trajectory. 3.3 Ablation Study 

All four components contribute, mostly to Goal Progress. Table 2 removes each compo-nent from the full model. Overall Type Match falls by 0.51, 1.20, 4.27, and 5.39 points when action-sufficient projection, residual alignment, step-adaptive prioritization, and channel decomposition are removed, but GP falls by 1.38, 5.38, 5.93, and 5.29 points on a base roughly a quarter as large. In relative terms, every component is worth four to five times more to episode progress than to single-step action type: at most 7.5% of TM against up to 33.9% of GP. Their disproportionately larger effect on GP shows that the components primarily keep a trajectory from unravelling, which is what Sequential Distraction predicts. 

GP reveals contributions beyond TM. Decomposing the observation into channels without prioritizing them raises Type Match by 1.12 and Exact Match by 2.78 points over a single inte-grated channel. Prioritization converts this richer channel access into episode-level stability by deciding which channel to trust. Residual alignment shows the same metric asymmetry more sharply: replacing the additive residual with prompt-embedding substitution reduces EM by 5.30 and GP by 5.38 points, compared with a 1.20-point TM change. Preserving the original prompt embeddings therefore matters most for grounding the selected action primitive into correct arguments. 

3.4 Performance Across Interaction Depth 

Sequential Distraction predicts that interference accumulates. A method that suppresses it should separate from the backbone as a trajectory lengthens, whereas a uniformly stronger predictor would hold a constant margin. We bin every evaluation step by its index within the episode and report per-step accuracy with 90% episode-clustered bootstrap intervals (Fig. 3). Qwen2.5-Omni falls from 64.79 TM at the first step to 32.79 beyond the third, retaining half of its initial accuracy. FocusGUI retains 86.8% over the same range, and its exact-match accuracy rises rather than falls, from 38.73 to 46.72. 4 Related Work 

4.1 GUI Agents in Omni-Modal Environments 

GUI agents interact with digital environments by repeatedly translating user intentions and interface observations into executable actions. Research across mobile, web, and desktop platforms has established this perception–action loop and progressively extended it from isolated grounding decisions to long-horizon interaction (Rawles et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b). Most agents nevertheless organize their observation around the current screenshot, with previous actions or screens retained as auxiliary context. Temporal GUI benchmarks broaden this view by requiring agents to understand dynamic visual content (Jang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b; Chen et al., 2025), while OmniGUI further places each action within an interleaved observation of screen, video, audio, and interaction history (Henry et al., 2026). These developments shift GUI interaction from processing a sequence of interfaces to acting over an evolving omni-modal state. In parallel, omni-modal foundation models increasingly support unified or streaming perception of text, vision, and audio (Xu et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2026). Broader evaluations of real-time assistants similarly emphasize online processing and continuity as multimodal events unfold (Lu et al., 2026). Such capabilities determine what a model can receive; how an agent should organize that evidence for the next GUI action remains open. The closest observation-side approach augments computer-use agents with gated keyframes, audio transcription, and persistent visual narration (Li & Shi, 2026). FocusGUI addresses the complementary problem inside an omni-modal agent: given the complete observation, it learns which evidence should shape the current action and how to deliver that evidence to a frozen decoder. 

4.2 Perception and Grounding for GUI Agents 

Perception is central to GUI agents because an instruction must ultimately be connected to an actionable interface element. One line of work strengthens this connection through GUI-specific representation learning and large-scale grounding data (Cheng et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Gou et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b); another exposes explicit interface structure to general-purpose models through screen parsing (Lu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026a; Fu et al., 2026). Recent visual agents also reduce redundant screen tokens while jointly learning grounding and action generation (Lin et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026c). Together, these 9Focused Omni-Modal GUI Agents 

methods improve how an agent understands the current interface and determines where an action should be executed. A complementary direction makes perception selective. Rather than processing the full screenshot uniformly, visual search and test-time refinement narrow the observation to promising regions (Li et al., 2025a; Luo et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026d); active perception methods further decide when and how to crop, zoom, shift, or expand the visual field (Chen et al., 2026a; Liu et al., 2026b). These approaches demonstrate the value of suppressing irrelevant interface content, but their primary control axis remains spatial: they determine which part of the current screen deserves closer inspection (Zhang et al., 2026a). Omni-modal interaction introduces an additional axis, since relevance also changes across information sources and action steps. FocusGUI extends focused perception from spatial localization to step-dependent representation construction over the complete observation, complementing existing GUI grounding mechanisms. 

5 Conclusion 

We identify Sequential Distraction as a primary challenge in multi-step omni-modal GUI interaction, where unfiltered observations can lead to compounding errors due to transient and sparse relevance. To address this, we formulate the concept of Focused Perception and implement it through FocusGUI, a lightweight adapter that dynamically prioritizes perceptual channels, compresses them into an action-sufficient latent, and delivers the result via a zero-initialized residual at the action boundary. This method relies solely on action annotations and leaves the underlying backbone completely frozen. Evaluations across four backbones demonstrate that FocusGUI substantially improves Type Match over existing enhancement methods and maintains high accuracy at deeper interaction steps where historical context typically degrades. While our current prioritization depends on the present observation, future work could extend this mechanism to anticipate channel relevance, which may help mitigate trajectory drift earlier in the interaction sequence.

B Discussion and Limitations 

FocusGUI is designed as a lightweight mechanism for improving how frozen omni-modal GUI agents organize step-dependent evidence, and it is complementary to advances in perception, grounding, and planning. Our current formulation computes channel priorities from the observation available at each interaction step, which keeps the method simple and model-agnostic; extending this mechanism to incorporate anticipatory signals about which evidence may become important in subsequent steps is a natural direction for future work. Our experiments span four omni-modal backbones and five interaction categories; extending focused perception to broader computer-use settings is a natural next step.

C.3 Prioritization, not capacity. 

FocusGUI adds trainable parameters to a frozen backbone, so part of its gain could in prin-ciple come from that budget alone. Table 5 holds the budget fixed to within 79 parameters and replaces the decision reference with a fixed concatenation layout, leaving the channels available but no longer scored per step. D.1 Prompt and action space. 

All four backbones and all five methods share the system prompt below, so the task descrip-tion, the action vocabulary, and the output format stay fixed while only the organization of the observation differs. D.2 Training target and inference. 

During training, the ground-truth action is serialized as a compact JSON string and ap-pended only as the assistant target, e.g., {"action_type":0,"coordinate":[421,736]} . Loss is computed exclusively over assistant action tokens; all system and user tokens are masked. During inference, the target is absent and the original frozen LM head autoregressively generates the action using greedy decoding with beam size one. The raw backbone and FocusGUI receive exactly the same semantic prompt, multimodal observations, action history, action schema, and decoding configuration. FocusGUI does not append textual instructions or modality hints. Its selected evidence is projected into continuous latent residuals at the assistant action boundary before generation by the original decoder and LM head. 

D.3 Step template. 

Each step is presented with the template below. The multimodal blocks appear in a fixed order, with the history screenshot first when one exists, then the temporal and acoustic observations, and the execution screen last, so the screen the action applies to is always the most recent visual input. The goal and the step index follow, then the actions already executed in this episode, rendered as text rather than as structured state. The observation elements therefore reach the backbone exactly as they would without FocusGUI, and the channels of Sec. 2 are read from this same input rather than replacing any part of it. The closing line ends the prompt and fixes the action boundary bt at which the residual is applied.

F Case Study 

To demonstrate the comprehensiveness and robustness of FocusGUI across diverse inter-action paradigms, we present two distinct case studies encompassing both cross-modal temporal reasoning and multi-step semantic localization. Real-world GUI operation requires agents to seamlessly transition between different cognitive modes: some tasks demand patient monitoring of streaming sensory inputs (e.g., waiting for an auditory cue), while others require precise, sequential spatial navigation through dense interfaces. The following examples illustrate how FocusGUI successfully navigates these contrasting extremes by dynamically prioritizing relevant perceptual channels. Figure 11 highlights a scenario where the decisive signal is entirely auditory and temporal, requiring the agent to ignore static visual saliency until a specific semantic condition is met. Conversely, Figure 12 showcases a highly spatial and semantic task, where the agent must execute a precise sequence of UI interactions (opening filters, selecting specific nested options) based on a composite text instruction. Together, these cases confirm that FocusGUI’s step-adaptive prioritization effectively generalizes across fundamentally different types of GUI challenges, preventing Sequential Distraction in both streaming and structurally complex environments. 21 Focused Omni-Modal GUI Agents
