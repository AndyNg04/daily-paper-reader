ABSTRACT 

Beyond basic tool use and coding skills, effectively incorporating visual references into agentic generation is equally essential for applying AI agents to real-world productivity tasks. We study this crucial capability through webpage development, a practical and readily evaluable setting in which existing benchmarks remain largely idealized: they typically focus on reconstructing visual targets or gener-

Specifically, we introduce RefCraft, a benchmark comprising 300 realistic multimodal webpage-development tasks that combine natural-language instructions with screenshots from one to five reference webpages. Agents must selectively interpret and utilize these references while ren-dering, visually inspecting, and iteratively refining their outputs. We evaluate the resulting open-ended artifacts using independently verifiable keypoints and com-plementary programmatic and VLM judges. Across nine frontier (M)LLMs, the strongest system achieves an overall score of only 52.12 out of 100, revealing that visual-reference-conditioned agentic generation remains a substantial challenge for current models in practical webpage-development settings. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

> “Creativity is just connecting things.”
> — Steve Jobs

Recently, as coding and co-working assistants, (M)LLMs have transformed numerous fields and substantially improved productivity (OpenAI, 2026b; Anthropic, 2026a; Qwen Team, 2026b; Kimi et al., 2026). This success has further fueled growing enthusiasm among researchers and practitioners for extending large models to an even broader range of practical tasks, as exemplified by GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI, 2026d) and Claude Fable 5.1 (Anthropic, 2026b). When we scrutinize some common productivity tasks, we find that visual references play an important role: designers may first study successful examples before developing new proposals and then continuously refine their designs through visual inspection, while CAD engineers examine existing parts to design new ones that fit and function properly. This observation highlights that, for both human experts and AI agents, basic tool use and coding skills alone do not encompass the full spectrum of productive capabilities—effectively 

incorporating visual reference and inspection into agentic generation is equally essential .To evaluate this important yet subtle capability, we ground it in a focused and readily verifiable testbed: webpage development, which is ubiquitous in everyday digital life and produces highly visual, easily assessable outputs. In fact, a growing body of benchmarks has investigated general webpage-related tasks, but most either use natural-language instructions without visual references (Lu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025) or focus solely on reconstruction tasks, such as reconstructing webpages from screenshots (Yun et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026) or videos (Chen et al., 2026). More recent studies have broadened the evaluation setting by incorporating interactive operations (Xiao et al., 2025a) or introducing webpage editing and repair tasks (Lei et al., 2026). Nevertheless, we argue that these prior efforts remain academically idealized and differ considerably from common real-world development workflows . In practice, frontend developers and users often begin with a rough design idea and browse existing websites for inspiration. For instance, a user may adopt the appealing layout of a reference website while redesigning its fonts, content, and other elements for a new purpose. Fully reconstructing an existing website is a far less common or representative task. To bridge this gap and, more importantly, evaluate whether AI agents can effectively conduct such a visual-reference-conditioned agentic generation process, we introduce RefCraft. RefCraft comprises 300 diverse and realistic multimodal queries spanning a broad range of web development scenarios. Each query combines a natural-language user instruction with screenshots from one to five reference webpages, and models solve the task in an inspect-and-iterate setting where they can render and refine intermediate outputs. As shown in Fig. 1, RefCraft varies substantially in domains, reference composition, and the intended degree of reference utilization—from closely following a reference to selectively adapting its overall style or specific local elements. Evaluating such tasks requires moving beyond conventional target-based evaluation: a query may admit multiple valid solutions, and visual similarity alone cannot determine whether the references have been used appropriately. We therefore decompose each complex and subtle query into a verified set of key points, turning open-ended requirements into independently assessable criteria. Moreover, webpages are both visual and interactive artifacts, encompassing subjective perceptual qualities as well as objective executable behaviors. We thus adopt a dual-track evaluation protocol that uses programmatic judges for deterministic requirements and a VLM judge for perceptual and semantic ones. Together, these designs enable reliable evaluation of whether a generated webpage fulfills the user’s intended use of visual references, rather than merely resembling them. 2REF CRAFT : Preprint Under Review We benchmark 9 frontier (M)LLMs on RefCraft under a unified harness. Despite access to visual references and an inspect-and-iterate workflow, the best-performing system, GPT-6 Astra (Ope-nAI, 2026d), achieves an overall score of only 52.12, leaving substantial room for improvement. Component-level results reveal that models often satisfy many individual requirements but fail to realize an essential reference relationship or core behavior. To assess the reliability of RefCraft, we compare its model ranking with both our expert human study and the human-preference leader-board of Arena.AI 1, finding strong agreement with both. These findings indicate that current agents can reproduce salient visual attributes but still struggle to translate instruction-dependent reference relationships into coherent designs—precisely the capability that matters to human users. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 VISUAL -R EFERENCE -C ONDITIONED AGENTIC GENERATION 

In recent years, we have witnessed rapid advances in AI agents. From early frameworks such as Re-Act (Yao et al., 2022) to coding agents such as SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024) and OpenHands (Wang et al., 2025a), substantial effort has been devoted to reasoning, code generation, long-horizon plan-ning, and tool use (Schick et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026). In comparison, vision has received less attention in agentic generation and is typically used only as a perceptual interface for interpreting image or video observations (Xie et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024). Meanwhile, visual inputs serve as important conditioning signals in generative models, particularly diffusion-based models for image and video generation (Seedance et al., 2026; Bai et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b). Yet methods ranging from DreamBooth (Ruiz et al., 2023) and ControlNet (Zhang et al., 2023) to IP-Adapter (Ye et al., 2023) and the OmniGen series (Xiao et al., 2025c; Wu et al., 2025) often assign predefined, coarse-grained roles to visual references—for example, preserv-ing subject identity, transferring overall style, or imposing structural constraints. Consequently, both prominent research directions leave a challenging yet practical setting underexplored: visual-reference-conditioned agentic generation, where an agent must interpret subtle user instructions and selectively incorporate heterogeneous visual references throughout the agentic generation process. 2.2 WEB DEVELOPMENT BENCHMARKS 

Existing web development benchmarks broadly follow two task formulations. Text-driven bench-marks, such as WebGen-Bench (Lu et al., 2026) and FrontendBench (Zhu et al., 2025), ask models to construct webpages or applications from natural-language specifications (Tran et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025). Visual-target benchmarks instead formulate generation as reconstruction, including screenshot-to-code tasks such as Design2Code (Si et al., 2025) and Web2Code (Yun et al., 2024), video- or interaction-conditioned generation (Chen et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2025a), and sketch-to-code generation (Jain et al., 2019). More recent benchmarks, including DesignBench (Xiao et al., 2025b), ArtifactsBench (Zhang et al., 2025), and WebCompass (Lei et al., 2026), extend beyond one-shot generation to interactive behavior, editing, repair, and multi-turn refinement (Yang et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Saxena et al., 2026). Evaluation for these tasks has evolved accordingly, moving from visual-similarity measures based on CLIP features (Radford et al., 2021), layout blocks, colors, and positions (Wang et al., 2026; Si et al., 2025) toward MLLM- or agent-as-a-judge evaluation over rendered webpages (Zhang et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2026; Zhuge et al., 2024). Nevertheless, existing benchmarks typically treat visual input as a target to reconstruct or an artifact to edit. They rarely capture the common workflow in which users consult one or more webpages as partial design references, which is precisely the setting studied in our work.

3.1 TASK FORMULATION 

Each task t in RefCraft consists of a natural-language instruction qt and a set of visual references 

Rt = {Ri}Nt

i=1 , where 1 ≤ Nt ≤ 5 and each Ri contains screenshots sampled from a reference webpage. Together with the evaluation keypoints Kt, they define the task τt = ( qt, Rt, Kt). Model m

executes the task and produces hm,t = Agent m(qt, Rt), a self-contained HTML webpage, without access to Kt. During generation, the agent may render its intermediate implementation, visually inspect the result, and iteratively revise the webpage. Unlike screenshot reconstruction, the objective is not to reproduce any reference exactly. Depending on the instruction, the agent may need to closely follow one reference, combine properties from multiple references, adapt selected design elements, or deliberately avoid particular characteristics. The task therefore evaluates whether an agent can interpret and appropriately utilize visual references within an inspect-and-iterate workflow. 3.2 REFERENCE POOL CURATION 

Reference webpages are important because they determine what information an agent can draw upon and therefore form the foundation of RefCraft. We curate the reference webpage pool according to three principles: diversity , covering a broad range of topics and design styles; visual richness ,providing sufficiently salient properties for users to reference and evaluators to verify; and realism ,reflecting designs that may plausibly appear in practical development workflows. Following these principles, we combine three sources: (1) openly licensed websites and webpage templates; (2) con-ventionally styled webpages synthesized by multiple models to increase diversity; and (3) deliberately synthesized webpages that deviate from conventional modern styles and common model-generated patterns. The rationale is that synthesized pages may inherit common model priors, allowing an agent to produce superficially similar designs based on its prior knowledge without consulting the refer-ences. To mitigate this shortcut, the third group incorporates less conventional visual characteristics, such as unusual color palettes, uncommon font combinations, and hard-edged shadows. 4REF CRAFT : Preprint Under Review We represent each reference using images rather than source code or a live URL, preventing implemen-tation details from leaking to the evaluated agent and better reflecting practical scenarios. Because a single static screenshot cannot expose dynamic properties such as hover effects or interactive state changes, we use Playwright 2 to capture a sequence of visual frames along scripted interaction traces involving scrolling, hovering, clicking, typing, and key presses. To support subsequent task construction, we also profile the observable information in each reference along six dimensions: 

layout , palette , typography , components , interaction , and overall visual character . Each dimension is annotated as rich , weak , or none , together with a concise description of the observed evidence. This profile prevents later queries from referring to properties that are absent or visually ambiguous. 3.3 CONTROLLABLE QUERY SYNTHESIS 

Directly prompting an LLM to generate user queries leads to substantial mode collapse: the resulting instructions are often similarly phrased and repeatedly emphasize only a small subset of the available reference information. We instead adopt a specification-first approach: we first determine the intended reference usage and task composition and then verbalize them as a natural user request. 

Reference-use specification. As shown in Fig. 2(b), let D denote the six visual dimensions above. We represent the reference-use specification for task t as Zt = {(d, o t,d , ρ t,d ) : d ∈ D} . For each dimension, ot,d ∈ { copy , adapt , invert , specify , ignore } specifies the requirement type, while ρt,d 

identifies the assigned reference, with ρt,d = ∅ for specify and ignore . The five types are copy ,which closely follows the reference; adapt , which preserves only selected characteristics; invert ,which deliberately avoids the referenced characteristic; specify , which follows an explicit textual requirement rather than a reference; and ignore , which leaves the dimension unconstrained. We retain a sampled reference-dependent requirement only when the assigned reference contains sufficiently clear and discriminable evidence for the corresponding dimension. 

Compositional query design. We construct a design matrix whose axes describe the user persona, target content, form and tone of the request, selected references, the reference-use specification Zt,and other relevant factors. Values are sampled across these axes and subsequently filtered to remove contradictory, implausible, or otherwise pathological combinations. An LLM then converts each valid combination into a fluent natural-language instruction. This procedure preserves linguistic naturalness while maintaining systematic control over task diversity. Additional generation details and examples are provided in Appendix B.2. 3.4 VERIFIABLE KEYPOINT CONSTRUCTION 

A query in RefCraft may admit many valid implementations, and its requirements can be fine-grained, subtle, and distributed across both language and visual references. Consequently, evaluating against a single canonical webpage or a holistic similarity score would be inappropriate. Inspired by rubric-based evaluation (Yu et al., 2026; Hashemi et al., 2024; Pathak et al., 2025), we instead decompose each query into a set of non-overlapping keypoints, each capturing one independently assessable requirement. 4 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Given the benchmark instances constructed above, our evaluation protocol standardizes how models interact with the task, converts their unconstrained HTML outputs into structured evidence, and scores them against the corresponding keypoints. 4.1 UNIFIED AGENTIC ENVIRONMENT 

We evaluate each model as a coding agent, reflecting the increasingly common use of agentic harnesses in practical development workflows . To ensure comparability, all models operate through the same open-source coding harness, opencode 3, with identical tools, workspace structure, and output requirements. For each task, the agent receives the user instruction, visual references, and an environment brief. It is given a dedicated working directory and may render, screenshot, and inspect intermediate implementations before revising them. The final result must be stored as a single self-contained HTML file, ensuring a uniform artifact format across models. We restrict access that could reveal benchmark internals or ground-truth evaluation criteria. Each working directory is isolated from the benchmark repository, and attempts to access the repository or evaluation keypoints are denied. Network access remains available to preserve a realistic coding environment, but searching for or downloading the original source of a reference webpage invalidates the run. 4.2 EVIDENCE COLLECTION 

Generated HTML files vary substantially in structure and may exhibit visual and interactive behaviors that cannot be reliably evaluated from source code alone. Therefore, for each artifact hm,t , we collect structured evidence Em,t = ( Vm,t , Mm,t ), where Vm,t contains captured views and Mm,t 

contains browser measurements. Specifically, for each benchmark instance, we derive a task-specific capture script specifying a sequence of actions, such as scrolling, hovering, clicking, and typing. The script is designed to expose the page states required to evaluate as many relevant keypoints as possible. We load the generated HTML in Playwright, execute the actions sequentially, and capture screenshots of the resulting states. In parallel, we apply a fixed suite of browser-based measurements to assess properties such as rendering integrity, responsive behavior, robustness to abnormal content, and whether interactive controls produce genuine state changes. Together, the screenshots and measurements provide downstream judges with targeted evidence rather than requiring them to infer page behavior from raw HTML, which would be poorly aligned with practical usage. 4.3 DUAL -T RACK JUDGING AND SCORE AGGREGATION 

Our evaluation first produces keypoint-level scores from Em,t using complementary programmatic and VLM judges. The programmatic judge compares browser measurements against deterministic keypoints and returns a binary verdict. For keypoints requiring semantic or perceptual judgment, the VLM judge receives the keypoint, relevant screenshots, and supporting measurements, and assigns a rating on a four-level scale from 0 to 3. 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We evaluate nine frontier models—GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI, 2026d), Claude Fable 5 (Anthropic, 2026a), Qwen3.8-Max (Qwen Team, 2026b), Grok 4.6 (xAI, 2026), Kimi K3 (Kimi et al., 2026), GLM-5.3 (Z.ai, 2026), DeepSeek-V4-Pro (Xu et al., 2026), Seed2.1 Pro (ByteDance Seed Team, 2026b), and Gemini 3.1 Pro (Google DeepMind, 2026)—as coding agents on RefCraft. All models are evaluated using the same OpenCode v1.18.25 harness, with high reasoning effort and a maximum of 100 turns. Keypoints are generated by Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026f) and verified by GPT-5.6 Sol (OpenAI, 2026c). We use Gemini 3.1 Pro as the VLM judge. We report the overall score S

together with its three constituent metrics. Further details are provided in Appendix B. 5.2 MAIN RESULTS

Visual-reference-conditioned generation remains far from solved. 6 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we introduced RefCraft, a benchmark for studying reference-based webpage develop-ment and, more broadly, visual-reference-conditioned agentic generation in frontier models. RefCraft comprises 300 realistic web-development tasks in an inspect-and-iterate setting. We decompose each query into independently verifiable keypoints and evaluate the generated artifacts using comple-mentary programmatic and VLM judges. Our evaluation of nine frontier (M)LLMs shows that this capability remains far from solved: the strongest system achieves an overall score of only 52.12. We hope that RefCraft will provide a valuable testbed for this crucial yet underexplored capability and stimulate further progress in visual-reference-conditioned agentic generation.

B ADDITIONAL IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

§ 3 and § 4 describe the design of RefCraft and its evaluation protocol. Here, we explain how these designs are implemented. We follow the same progression as the main text: reference webpages are first converted into visual evidence, which supports the construction of user requests and their evaluation keypoints. Once these task components are fixed, an agent uses the request and reference images to develop a webpage, while a separate evaluator assesses the submitted artifact against the hidden keypoints. The details below apply to the RefCraft benchmark and clarify how information passes between these stages. B.1 REFERENCE COLLECTION AND VISUAL EVIDENCE 

We begin by preparing a reusable collection of webpages from which task-specific references can be assembled. Following the three-source design in § 3.2, this collection contains 744 pages: 444 openly licensed template-derived pages, 200 conventionally styled synthetic pages, and 100 atypical synthetic pages. These counts describe the available source pool rather than the benchmark instances.

Synthetic reference generation. The synthetic portion complements these existing templates by extending the range of designs available for task construction. From webpages to task evidence. The stored webpages are construction assets, but the references shown to agents consist only of images. To produce these visual inputs, Playwright renders each page at a 1920 × 1080 viewport and captures scripted scrolling and interaction states, yielding 5,522 frames across the pool. For conventionally styled synthetic pages, we retain their original page-specific demonstration scripts. For template-derived and atypical pages, we generate scripts from the measured page height and available hover targets (identified through known selectors or DOM probing), interleaving stepwise scrolling, hovers, and short pauses. We retain the capture-action script and a time-stamped frame manifest for each page so that the resulting images remain associated with the actions and source that produced them. When references are assembled into a task, frame order and source-page boundaries are preserved. The agent can thus distinguish views of the same page from views of different references without receiving their implementations. Before a request can be written, we also need to establish which reference properties these images actually expose. Gemini 3.1 Pro (Google DeepMind, 2026) profiles the frames along the six visual dimensions in § 3.2, providing a description of the visible evidence and a rich , weak , or none label for each dimension. These profiles connect reference collection to query synthesis: they restrict the assignment of reference-dependent requirements to properties supported by the supplied images, rather than by information available only in the underlying webpage. B.3 KEYPOINT CONSTRUCTION AND QUALITY ASSURANCE 

Keypoint construction must preserve the reference relationships established during query synthesis while also covering the content and functionality expressed in the completed request. We handle these two parts separately. Reference assignments and explicit textual constraints are converted programmatically into fixed items from the structured specification, retaining their intended sources and requirement types. Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026f) then reads the user request and adds 21 REF CRAFT : Preprint Under Review keypoints for content, structure, navigation, and functionality without rewriting the fixed items. This division keeps the specified reference relationships intact while allowing the keypoints to cover requirements that emerge in the natural-language request. To make the added keypoints assessable, the construction prompt (Fig. 12) links each criterion to both the user request and the evidence needed to evaluate it. Each item must first cite the supporting request text and clarify what it checks: the existence of an element, the correctness of its implementation, or the precision of its details. Distinguishing these levels prevents the mere presence of an element from being treated as sufficient evidence that it has been implemented as instructed. The resulting criterion must then be answerable from the evidence available during evaluation, ruling out questions requiring source-code inspection or unavailable timing measurements. For interaction-dependent requirements, this also means identifying both the control and the action needed to reach the relevant state, so that the subsequent browser-capture process can expose the behavior being assessed. The generated keypoints are then reviewed by GPT-5.6 Sol (OpenAI, 2026c) with high reasoning effort and the prompt shown in Fig. 13. The review separates completeness from item validity: a global pass first proposes missing requirements, after which a local pass examines individual items and returns keep , modify , or remove , together with a reason. Both passes use the reference-use specification to check whether the criteria preserve the intended task. In particular, an ignored dimension must not introduce a requirement, an adaptation must not become an exact-match demand, and an inversion must not reward copying. These checks address errors that could otherwise make a correct implementation appear unsuccessful. Generation and model review use a configured maximum output of 128,000 tokens. Finally, the expert review described in § 3 revises the keypoints before they are used for evaluation. Fig. 11 presents the instruction template for expert keypoint review. 

Final task set. The final benchmark contains 300 tasks, each pairing a user request and ordered reference screenshots with a verified set of keypoints. Together, these tasks use 534 distinct web-pages in 972 reference assignments and expose 3,491 reference frames. Stable task identifiers and input hashes preserve the correspondence between each request, its references, and its evaluation specification when these components are passed to the execution and judging stages. B.4 STANDARDIZED AGENTIC EXECUTION 

With the task components fixed, execution exposes only the information intended for the evaluated agent: the user request, ordered reference images, and an environment brief. The verified keypoints remain hidden and are used later by the evaluator. All released runs use OpenCode 1.18.25 with the same 100-turn cap, implementing the shared coding-agent setting described in § 4.1. The environment brief is matched to the language of the request and explains both the required deliverable and the available development workflow. It asks the agent to “write the finished page to 

index.html ,” requires a single HTML file, and states that “grading renders the page offline.” It also describes the inspection loop explicitly as “draft → screenshot → compare → fix → screenshot again.” To make this loop executable, the workspace provides shell and file-editing tools together with a Playwright-based shot.py helper. The helper renders the current HTML at widths of 1920, 768, and 390 pixels and returns image paths along with page height, console errors, and attempted external requests. These outputs allow the agent to inspect its rendered implementation and identify problems before revising the page. Network access remains available during development, but it does not relax the offline submission requirement. Dependencies must be inlined in the final page so that its evaluation does not rely on external resources. Likewise, the availability of development tools does not grant access to benchmark data, hidden criteria, or original reference implementations. Such access is denied and audited. At completion, we retain one official artifact per model–task pair, together with a run record containing the enforced limits, stop reason, transport attempts, token usage, and artifact hashes. The agent’s inspection tools support development, but the evidence used for scoring is collected separately from the submitted index.html . This distinction ensures that the evaluator examines the final webpage through its own capture and measurement procedure rather than relying on the views the agent happened to inspect. The hidden keypoints guide this procedure by identifying the states and properties that need to be exposed. The resulting evidence is then passed to the programmatic and VLM judging tracks described in § 4.3. 

Capture and measurement settings. Playwright/Chromium first renders the artifact at 1920 ×1080 ,with a target of five to ten distinct primary frames. Since requirements can concern states that are not visible on initial load, a deterministic action planner uses the keypoints to plan at most eight states to expose. For each item, it tries up to four candidate controls and allows 900 ms for a state transition. These settings govern how the browser attempts to obtain the interaction evidence specified during keypoint construction. The evaluator also measures the page at widths of 1280, 768, and 390 pixels to assess behavior beyond the primary desktop view. Additional stress fixtures test robustness when content differs from the initial presentation: eligible collections are replaced with zero or 300 rows, an 80-character unbroken string is injected, and mixed CJK/emoji content is supplied. The resulting measurements complement the ordinary rendered views by exposing behavior under narrow layouts and abnormal content. If a measurement is unavailable or inapplicable, that status is recorded explicitly. The absence of a usable measurement is not treated as evidence that the corresponding test succeeded.

Requirements that need semantic or perceptual interpretation are instead assigned to Gemini 3.1 pro, configured with high thinking level and a maximum output of 65,535 tokens. This judge receives visual and measured evidence rather than the submitted HTML, reference source code, or solver identity. Its task is to assess what the webpage demonstrates against the stated requirement, rather than infer success from implementation claims. The perceptual judge processes batches of 5 keypoints, with a separate assessment for each item. Each batch contains the user request, the supporting basis for each item, the reference assignments, and normalized browser facts. To keep these facts and images interpretable, the images follow a fixed order: reference frames, up to ten main artifact views, up to two views per narrow viewport, and up to two stress-fixture views.
