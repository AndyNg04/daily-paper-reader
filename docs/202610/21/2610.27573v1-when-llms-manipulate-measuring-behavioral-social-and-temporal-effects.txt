# Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) can influence user trust and decision-making through subtle linguistic cues, yet the behavioral effects of manipulative outputs remain poorly quantified. We introduce a behavioral computing framework for measuring behavioral vulnerability , defined as users’ susceptibility to manipulative AI responses. Building on DarkPatterns-LLM Asif et al. [2025], we generate controlled manipulative stimuli using Manipulation Gradient Descent (MGD), targeting biases associated with authority, urgency, and social proof. We analyze participant responses using the Multi-Stakeholder Impact Assessment Network (MSIAN) to model cross-user influence and the Temporal Harm Propagation Model (THPM) to characterize the persistence of behavioral effects over time. In a study involving 120 participants, manipulative framing produced significant changes in trust calibration and compliance ( p < 0.01 ), with effects also propagating across social contexts. These findings highlight the need to extend LLM safety evaluation beyond model outputs to measurable behavioral consequences and provide a computational framework for quantifying individual, social, and temporal risks arising from manipulative AI interactions. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed to support decision-relevant tasks in do-mains such as healthcare documentation, patient monitoring, and clinical activity recognition Hamd-hana et al. [2024], Syulistyo et al. [2024], Cleland et al. [2024]. While generative AI systems continue to expand in capability and application Khan et al. [2025], they also introduce behavioral risks related to persuasion, trust, and user autonomy Bommasani et al. [2021], Weidinger et al. [2021]. Unlike traditional interfaces, LLMs can adapt linguistic framing through cues such as authority, urgency, and social proof, potentially exploiting cognitive biases to influence user behavior. Such mechanisms resemble dark patterns , traditionally studied in digital interface design. Prior work in human computer interaction (HCI) and persuasive technology has shown that design choices can influence user decisions by leveraging cognitive biases, through dark-pattern taxonomies such as nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, and forced action Gray et al. [2018], as well as mechanisms such as social proof and scarcity Mathur et al. [2019]. Recent AI safety research has also developed methods for mitigating unsafe model behavior, including fine-tuning procedures that preserve safety alignment Asif and Amiri [2026], targeted harm-mitigation frameworks Campbell et al. [2026], and analyses of the parameter-level fragility of safety mechanisms Karamat and Garcia [2026]. However, such approaches primarily operate at the level of model outputs and internal safety mechanisms, rather than measuring the downstream behavioral effects of manipulative content on

users. Consequently, limited understanding remains of how manipulative AI language affects user trust and compliance, whether these effects persist over time, and how they propagate through social interactions. This gap motivates a shift from content-level detection toward behavior-level safety evaluation. To address this gap, we introduce a behavioral computing framework for quantifying behavioral vulnerability , defined as the degree to which users’ judgments, trust, and compliance are altered by manipulative AI responses. Building on the DarkPatterns-LLM benchmark Asif et al. [2025], our framework integrates four components: Manipulation Gradient Descent (MGD) for generating controlled manipulative stimuli, the Multi-Stakeholder Impact Assessment Network (MSIAN) for modeling cross-user influence, the Temporal Harm Propagation Model (THPM) for characterizing behavioral persistence and recovery, and Dynamic Contextual Robustness Assessment (DCRA) 

for evaluating sensitivity to contextual reframing. We evaluate the framework in a controlled study involving 120 participants exposed to manipulative and neutral LLM responses across authority, emotional urgency, social proof, and related framing con-ditions. We measure behavioral outcomes, including compliance, trust calibration, response latency, and post-interaction assessments. The results show that manipulative framing significantly increases behavioral vulnerability, with authority-based cues producing the strongest effects. Manipulation-induced behavioral changes also propagate across social contexts and persist beyond the initial interaction. Our contributions are threefold: (1) we extend LLM safety evaluation from manipulative outputs to measurable human behavioral outcomes; (2) we introduce a multi-layer framework for modeling individual, social, temporal, and contextual dimensions of manipulation; and (3) we define behavioral safety metrics, including the Behavioral Vulnerability Index (BVI) , Cross-Influence Coefficient (CIC) , Temporal Recovery Rate (TRR) , and Contextual Vulnerability Score (CVS) , to quantify behavioral susceptibility, social propagation, temporal recovery, and contextual sensitivity. 

# 2 Related Work 

Behavioral Computing. Activity and Behavior Computing (ABC) focuses on the quantitative modeling of human behavior using signals such as motion, context, and social interaction. Prior work has primarily examined physical and cognitive states, including activity recognition, affect, and health-related behavior, through sensor-driven observations and machine learning Plötz et al. [2011], Ahmad et al. [2026]. More recent research extends this paradigm toward deep sequence models that capture richer temporal context in human activity recognition Hiremath and Plötz [2021]. However, these approaches generally treat AI systems as observers or facilitators rather than behavioral influencers. As LLMs increasingly function as conversational agents, their linguistic outputs can serve as behavioral stimuli that influence user judgments and decisions. Our work extends ABC from behavior recognition toward behavior modulation by modeling how AI-generated language affects downstream human behavior. 

Persuasive Technology and Cognitive Bias. Persuasive technology examines how computational systems shape attitudes and behaviors Fogg [2003], often through cognitive mechanisms such as authority, social proof, scarcity, and urgency Cialdini [2001], which persuasive-systems design frameworks have formalized into actionable design principles Oinas-Kukkonen and Harjumaa [2008, 2009]. Prior HCI research has shown that interface framing and perceived expertise can influence user trust and compliance Rahman and Adaji [2025]. These effects are particularly relevant to conversational systems, where linguistic framing, tone, and contextual continuity can introduce persuasive cues without altering the underlying semantic content. The distinction between persuasion and manipulation therefore depends not only on the information presented but also on transparency, user autonomy, and the mechanisms through which behavior is influenced. Our framework examines these mechanisms by measuring behavioral responses to manipulative linguistic framing. 

Manipulation and Dark Patterns in AI. Research on dark patterns has documented how digital systems exploit cognitive biases to steer users toward undesirable outcomes Gray et al. [2018], Mathur et al. [2019], Fogg [2003]. Related AI safety research has begun to characterize analogous manipulative behaviors in generative systems. In particular, DarkPatterns-LLM Asif et al. [2025] introduced a taxonomy and computational benchmark covering mechanisms such as authority bias, 2Figure 1: Overview of the proposed multi-layer behavioral analysis framework. However, existing evaluations primarily focus on manipulation in model outputs, with comparatively limited attention to its measurable effects on user trust, compliance, behavioral persistence, and social influence. We address this gap by treating manipulative AI communication as a behavioral perturbation and extending content-level manipulation detection toward behavior-level risk quantification .

# 3 Methodology 

Our objective is to quantify how manipulative AI outputs influence human behavior across individual, social, temporal, and contextual dimensions. Building on the four-layer architecture of DarkPatterns-LLM Asif et al. [2025], we integrate computational manipulation discovery with behavioral data acquisition and modeling. # 4 Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluated the proposed framework through a controlled human-AI interaction study involving 120 participants. All participants provided informed consent, and the study followed institutionally approved ethical procedures. Additional details on the experimental protocol and participants are provided in Appendix A. 

4.1 Participants and Experimental Conditions 

A total of 120 participants ( N = 120 ) were recruited through academic mailing lists and professional online communities. All participants had prior experience with conversational AI systems but were unaware of the study’s manipulation objectives. Each participant completed twelve interaction tasks involving manipulative and neutral LLM re-sponses. The experimental conditions included four manipulation categories: Authority Framing (A) , invoking expert or institutional authority; Emotional Urgency (E) , introducing scarcity, fear, or time pressure; Social Proof (S) , conveying consensus or majority endorsement; and Prosocial Altruism (P) , appealing to moral or communal benefit. A Baseline Control (C) condition used semantically comparable responses without manipulative framing. Stimuli were generated using MGD at three manipulation-intensity levels: low ( Mc < 0.33 ), medium (0.33 ≤ Mc < 0.66 ), and high ( Mc ≥ 0.66 ). Experimental conditions and intensity levels were counterbalanced across participants using a Latin-square design to minimize order effects. 

4.2 Behavioral Data Acquisition 

Participants interacted with AI-generated responses through a standardized web interface. For each interaction, we recorded decision outcomes, response latency, confidence ratings, emotional states derived from post-interaction reflections, and changes in self-reported trust in AI. These measures capture behavioral responses related to compliance, deliberation, affect, and trust. 

4.3 Behavioral Graph Construction 

To evaluate cross-participant influence, participants were subsequently exposed to anonymized excerpts of prior participants’ decision narratives and asked to review and respond to them. These exposures defined a directed behavioral graph G = ( V, E ), where an edge eji represents exposure of participant ui to participant uj ’s response. The resulting graph served as input to MSIAN for modeling secondary behavioral influence and collective susceptibility. 5Figure 2: BVI across manipulation types and intensity levels. Bars represent mean BVI under low, medium, and high manipulation intensities. 

4.4 Evaluation and Statistical Analysis 

Behavioral responses were evaluated using the metrics introduced in Section 3: BVI, CIC, TRR, and CVS. All metrics were normalized to [0 , 1] to facilitate comparison across participants and experimental conditions. Statistical significance was assessed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), with Greenhouse Geisser correction applied where appropriate, followed by Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons. Effect sizes were reported using partial η2. Pearson correlation and regression analyses were used to examine associations between manipulation intensity, experimental conditions, and behavioral outcomes. Predictive performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, with 

p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Further experimental and statistical details are provided in Appendix A.

5.3 Temporal Persistence of Manipulation Effects 

Temporal persistence was evaluated immediately ( T0), after 24 hours ( T1), and after one week ( T2)using THPM. BPI decreased over time, indicating gradual recovery from manipulation-induced behavioral effects. Authority framing exhibited the slowest recovery, with TRR = 0 .42 and BPI = 0 .61 at T2. Emotional framing followed with TRR = 0 .48 and BPI = 0 .57 , compared with TRR = 0 .68 and BPI = 0 .32 for the control condition. Repeated exposure to three or more manipulative interactions was associated with an average 28% reduction in recovery rate, suggesting a compounding effect of repeated manipulation. Complete TRR and BPI results are reported in Appendix B. 

5.4 Contextual Robustness and Framing Sensitivity 

Behavioral consistency across contextual variants was quantified using CVS. The overall mean CVS was 0.63 , indicating moderate robustness to reframing. However, CVS varied significantly across manipulation conditions ( F (3 , 354) = 11 .62 , p < 0.001 ). Authority and urgency framing produced greater behavioral variance across contextually reframed tasks, indicating reduced stability under changes in linguistic framing. These findings support the DCRA analysis, suggesting that behavioral susceptibility depends not only on semantic content but also on how that content is presented. 

5.5 Integrated Framework Performance 

Cross-layer analysis revealed strong associations between computational manipulation signals and observed behavioral outcomes. MGD-derived manipulation intensity was strongly correlated with BVI ( r = 0 .90 ), while BVI was also strongly associated with temporal persistence measured by BPI (r = 0 .80 ). As shown in Figure 3, greater manipulation intensity was associated with higher immediate behavioral susceptibility, while participants with higher BVI also exhibited greater behavioral persistence over time. Across all experimental tasks, the integrated four-layer framework achieved an average behavioral-outcome prediction accuracy of 86.4% under 10-fold cross-validation. 7Overall, these results indicate that manipulative linguistic cues are associated with intensity-dependent behavioral effects that can propagate through social interactions, persist over time, and vary under contextual reframing. 

# 6 Discussion and Implications 

Our findings show that behavioral vulnerability to manipulative LLM outputs is context-dependent and extends beyond immediate individual responses. Variations in authority, urgency, social proof, and related framing produced measurable differences in compliance, trust calibration, and behavioral persistence, even when task semantics were comparable. These findings suggest that behavioral vulnerability is not necessarily a fixed user characteristic but a dynamic response shaped by linguistic framing and interaction context. 

6.1 Behavioral Interpretation and Context Dependence 

Authority and emotional framing produced the strongest behavioral effects, consistent with the role of perceived expertise and urgency in shaping human decision-making. The positive association between manipulation intensity Mc and BVI further suggests that stronger manipulative framing is associated with greater behavioral susceptibility. The temporal results show that manipulation-induced effects can persist beyond the initial interaction. Lower TRR values under authority and emotional framing, together with behavioral persistence at the one-week follow-up, suggest that susceptibility is not necessarily transient. Repeated exposure was also associated with lower recovery rates, indicating that manipulative interactions may have cu-mulative behavioral effects. These findings motivate safety evaluations that consider both immediate responses and longer-term behavioral outcomes. Contextual reframing introduced additional variation in susceptibility, indicating that semantically comparable content can elicit different behavioral responses depending on its presentation. This highlights a limitation of single-context safety evaluations and motivates assessing LLM interactions across diverse persuasive and contextual framings. 

6.2 Social Propagation and System-Level Risk 

MSIAN analysis suggests that manipulation effects may extend beyond direct human-AI interactions through exposure to other users’ responses. Participants exhibiting greater immediate vulnerability also showed stronger downstream influence, as reflected by the positive association between BVI and CIC. This indicates that behavioral effects originating from individual interactions may propagate through socially connected environments. Such propagation is particularly relevant to settings where AI-mediated content is shared or discussed, including online communities and collaborative platforms. Behavioral safety evaluations should therefore consider not only isolated user model interactions but also potential secondary influence across groups. 

6.3 Design and Governance Implications 

Our results motivate extending LLM safety evaluation beyond model outputs to include measurable downstream behavioral outcomes. A response may appear acceptable under conventional content-level evaluation while still influencing user trust, compliance, or decision-making through subtle linguistic framing. Several design directions follow from these findings. Behavioral safety mechanisms could identify potentially manipulative cues, such as excessive authority or urgency, while adaptive interfaces could reduce persuasive intensity when signs of over-trust or uncritical compliance are observed. More broadly, metrics such as BVI, TRR, CIC, and CVS could complement existing model-level safety evaluations by capturing individual, temporal, social, and contextual dimensions of manipulation risk. These findings also have implications for AI governance. Existing transparency and risk-assessment frameworks could incorporate behavioral influence alongside concerns such as model reliability and harmful content. In particular, the observed persistence and social propagation of manipulation 8effects highlight the importance of considering downstream behavioral consequences when assessing risks associated with large-scale deployment. 

6.4 Limitations and Future Work 

This study has several limitations. First, although the participant sample spans multiple demographic and disciplinary backgrounds, it does not fully capture the cross-cultural variability that may influence susceptibility to authority, social proof, or emotional framing. Second, the controlled text-based setting limits ecological validity compared with real-world LLM interactions involving extended conversations, user-driven contexts, and multimodal signals. Although the controlled design helps isolate the effects of linguistic manipulation, it excludes additional cues such as speech prosody, visual authority signals, and richer social interactions. Furthermore, behavioral persistence was evaluated over a one-week period, leaving longer-term effects unexplored. Future work will extend the framework to cross-cultural and multimodal settings, incorporate behav-ioral and physiological signals such as gaze and electrodermal activity, and investigate mitigation strategies for reducing manipulation-induced behavioral effects. The use of behavioral and contextual representations in MGD, MSIAN, THPM, and DCRA may also support extensions to additional modalities and longer temporal horizons. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We introduced a multi-layer behavioral framework for evaluating how manipulative LLM outputs influence human trust, compliance, and decision-making across individual, social, temporal, and contextual dimensions. In a controlled study involving 120 participants, manipulative framing produced significant behavioral effects, with authority-based cues yielding the highest vulnerability and slowest recovery. MSIAN analysis identified potential propagation of these effects through user interactions, while THPM and DCRA characterized their temporal persistence and sensitivity to contextual reframing. Together, these findings demonstrate that manipulative model outputs are associated with measurable behavioral consequences beyond the characteristics of generated content alone. Our findings motivate extending LLM safety evaluation from content-level assessment toward 

behavior-level auditing. BVI, CIC, TRR, and CVS provide complementary measures of immediate susceptibility, social propagation, recovery, and contextual sensitivity. Future work will investigate cross-cultural variability, multimodal behavioral signals, longer-term interactions, and mitigation strategies for reducing manipulation-induced behavioral effects. Incorporating behavioral outcomes into foundation-model evaluation may help support user autonomy as LLMs increasingly mediate human decision-making.

A.1 Participant Demographics and Recruitment 

A total of 120 participants ( N = 120 ) were recruited through academic mailing lists and professional online communities. Participants ranged in age from 19 to 54 years (mean = 28 .6, SD = 6 .3). The sample comprised 52% female, 47% male, and 1% non-binary participants. Educational backgrounds included computer science (34%), social sciences (29%), healthcare (21%), and other disciplines (16%). All participants reported prior experience with conversational AI systems, such as ChatGPT or Claude, indicating baseline familiarity with LLM-mediated interactions. Participants were unaware of the experimental manipulation objectives before participation. 

A.2 Experimental Conditions and Protocol 

Each participant completed twelve human AI interaction tasks involving manipulative and neutral conditions. Four manipulation categories were evaluated: 1. Authority Framing (A): Responses presented as authoritative guidance from experts or officials. 2. Emotional Urgency (E): Responses emphasizing scarcity, fear, or time-sensitive decision pressure. 3. Social Proof (S): Responses suggesting consensus or majority endorsement. 4. Prosocial Altruism (P): Responses appealing to moral or communal benefit. A Baseline Control (C) condition used semantically comparable neutral responses without explicit manipulative framing. Manipulative stimuli were generated using MGD, as described in Section 3. Three manipulation-intensity levels were defined using Mc:• Low: Mc < 0.33 ;• Medium: 0.33 ≤ Mc < 0.66 ; and • High: Mc ≥ 0.66 .Experimental conditions and intensity levels were counterbalanced across participants using a Latin-square design to reduce potential order effects. 

A.3 Behavioral Data Acquisition 

Participants interacted with AI-generated responses through a standardized web interface. The following behavioral measures were recorded for each interaction: • Decision outcome (binary): Whether the participant complied with or rejected the AI’s suggestion. • Response latency (continuous): Time elapsed between presentation of the AI response and the participant’s final decision. • Confidence rating (ordinal): Self-reported decision confidence measured on a 7-point Likert scale. • Emotional state (categorical): Post-interaction affect derived from sentiment analysis of participants’ open-ended reflections. • Trust calibration (continuous): Change in self-reported trust in AI systems before and after the interaction. 11 The interface used randomized assignment of manipulation conditions and anonymized participant identifiers. Each experimental session lasted approximately 40 minutes, and interaction logs were timestamped for offline analysis. 

A.4 Behavioral Graph Construction 

To evaluate cross-participant influence, post-task discussion interactions were simulated using anonymized excerpts of responses from prior participants. Participants reviewed and commented on selected decision narratives, creating patterns of observational exposure. These interactions defined a directed behavioral graph G = ( V, E ), where each participant ui

corresponds to a node and a directed edge eji represents exposure of participant ui to participant 

uj ’s decision narrative. The resulting graph was provided as input to MSIAN to model secondary influence propagation and collective susceptibility. 

A.5 Evaluation Metrics 

Behavioral responses were evaluated using the four metrics introduced in Section 3: • BVI: Measures immediate susceptibility to manipulative AI responses. • CIC: Quantifies behavioral influence among connected participants. • TRR: Characterizes the rate at which manipulation-induced behavioral changes return toward baseline. • CVS: Measures variation in behavioral susceptibility across contextual framings. All metrics were normalized to [0 , 1] to facilitate comparisons across participants and experimental conditions. 

A.6 Statistical Analysis 

Statistical inference was conducted using repeated-measures ANOVA. Greenhouse Geisser correction was applied when sphericity assumptions were violated, and Bonferroni correction was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons to control the family-wise error rate. Effect sizes were reported using partial η2.Pearson’s r was used to examine correlations between manipulation intensity ( Mc) and behavioral measures, including BVI and TRR. Regression models were fitted to examine the predictive con-tributions of manipulation conditions and participant characteristics. Predictive performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation. Unless otherwise specified, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 .

A.7 Ethics and Reproducibility
