Abstract
Human preference is now central to the evaluation, guid-
ance, finetuning, and ranking of text-to-image models.
However, the susceptibility of human preferences to bias
in image generation remains underexplored. We identify a
key observation: for human faces, perceived attractiveness
overrides image quality in preference judgments, even when
comparing real photographs of identical fidelity. In a con-
trolled user study, annotators rank more attractive faces as
higher quality, even when unattractive real faces are com-
pared with attractive AI-generated ones. We examine the
downstream consequences of this bias. Firstly, simulating
Elo rankings from user study data shows attractiveness can
invert a leaderboard’s ranking, elevating synthetic attrac-
tive faces over real, unattractive ones. Secondly, the same
bias reappears in the human preference models. Editing
40 annotated facial attributes in isolation, we find that at-
tractiveness is the strongest correlate of score for four of
five human preference models (PickScore, MPS, HPSv2.1,
HPSv3), and the strongest predictor of score in regression
analysis. We further show that preference aligned genera-
tion shifts faces toward younger, more feminine faces, and
can override explicit prompts for unattractive faces, thereby
limiting user control. Finally, we explore mitigation strate-
gies, both for data collection and post training on human
preference models. These findings highlight the need for
preference models that disentangle image quality from hu-
man cognitive bias, enabling more reliable evaluation.

into the images AI systems produce, compounding well-

we are the first to identify, test, and quantify this bias across
the human preference data, reward models, and generative
outputs of T2I models. Our contributions are:
• A controlled user study demonstrating that people tend to
rank more attractive faces as higher quality, even when
image fidelity is equal or lower.
• We audit leaderboard systems, human preference models,
and preference-aligned generation, demonstrating that
each have clear vulnerabilities to attractiveness bias.
• We explore pre- and post-training mitigation through a
comparative user study and evaluations across four hu-
man preference models and five T2I models.
2. Related Work
Human Preference Models
Text-to-image (T2I) genera-
tion is now dominated by diffusion [29, 66, 67] and flow
matching [45, 47] models such as Stable Diffusion [61],
Qwen Image [76], and FLUX [42]. To further improve these
models, researchers have turned to preference alignment to
better align generation with human judgments [5, 20]. Hu-
man preference models act as reward functions rψ(y, c) that
predict human judgments of visual quality, aesthetic ap-
peal, and prompt alignment for a generated image y and
a given caption c.
Models such as PickScore [38], Im-
ageReward [78], MPS [82], and the HPS series [50, 77]
leverage large-scale human comparisons to provide their
reward signals, which are now routinely used for reward-
guided sampling [19, 35, 49, 55], RL and non-RL finetun-
ing [5, 12, 20, 56, 78], dataset filtering [2], and evaluation
[44, 52, 73–75].
However, problems have started to emerge around these
models. MJ-Bench [10] finds they can exhibit bias around
race and gender. F-Bench is a human preference dataset
for AI generated and edited faces, however, no analysis is
done on the connection between preference and attractive-
ness and at the time of writing the dataset has not been made
available [46]. Most relevant to our work, Guo et al. [25]
argues that human preference models’ focus on aesthetics
can be problematic for user control and anti-aesthetic gen-
eration (e.g., abstract artwork and negative-valence expres-
sions receive lower rewards) but do not identify the single
most significant factor biasing scores for facial imagery. We
continue this line of work in the domain of faces, finding
that attractiveness is that factor, and identify its causes.
Attractiveness Bias
The attractiveness halo effect refers
to the well-established tendency to attribute positive char-
acteristics, such as greater social desirability, happiness,
and success, to physically attractive individuals [15, 24].
Whether similar biases influence the perception of AI-
generated images remains an open question.
Makowski
et al. [51] provide an initial investigation by flashing par-
ticipants with two real face images, misleadingly asking
them to classify each as real or AI-generated. They find a
strong linear relationship between facial attractiveness and
perceived “realness,” suggesting that human judgments of
AI-generated images may be biased toward perceiving more
attractive faces as more realistic. This setting differs from
preference-data collection, which allows unlimited inspec-
tion time and presents images known to be AI-generated
rather than asking participants to guess which are real. Our
user study is designed to address this gap.
This question matters given evidence linking exposure
to idealized facial imagery with negative body-image out-
comes, from “Snapchat Dysmorphia” [60] (patients seek-
ing cosmetic surgery to match photo filters) to increased
body dissatisfaction after exposure to more attractive social-
media profiles [6, 27, 30, 79]. If T2I models default to or
exclusively generate more attractive faces, this risks rein-
forcing narrow beauty ideals at scale.
Bias in Machine Learning
Bias has been extensively
studied in large language models [26], vision-language
models [4, 11, 81, 83], image classifiers [17], and diffusion
models [21, 28, 36]. Most research focuses on demographic
attributes such as gender, age, and ethnicity, with compar-
atively less attention to attractiveness [23], however, recent
work has begun to examine how facial attractiveness can in-
fluence machine learning systems. Taylor et al. [68] find the
LAION-Aesthetic Predictor [64] scores images of women
systematically higher than images of men and LGBTQ+
individuals, and argue that reducing aesthetic quality to a
scalar score is inherently prone to encoding such bias. Doh
et al. [16] shows Stable Diffusion v2 associates attractive-
ness with trustworthiness and intelligence in portrait gener-
ation, whereas Dinkar et al. [14] finds prompts with nega-
tive beauty-related traits are more likely to produce explicit

3. Auditing Attractiveness Bias
3.1. User Study
Each human preference dataset has its own distinct ap-
proach to gathering human preference data. For example,
Pick-a-Pic [38] presents users with two images and directs
the user to “Click the image you personally prefer..”. HPD
[50, 77] gathers their data by asking users to rank 4 images,
but instructions mix different properties, directing the user
to consider “personal aesthetic appeal”, “text-image align-
ment”, and “image quality” when ranking images. Out of
all human preference datasets considered ImageRewardDB
[78] focuses least on personal preference or aesthetics in
its user instructions. Specifically, ImageRewardDB asked
users to rank 4 images according to “...image-text align-
ment, high fidelity, and harmlessness.”.
As we show in Section 3.3, the human preference model
trained on ImageRewardDB exhibits less attractiveness bias
than those trained on the other datasets. One possible ex-
planation is that instructions emphasizing “personal prefer-
ence” or “aesthetic appeal” encourage users to favour more
attractive faces, whereas instructions focused on “image fi-
delity” or “image quality” may lead to different judgments.
To test this, we conduct an anonymized user study on
Qualtrics, recruiting 51 participants. We exclude 4 partici-
pants for revoked consent or incomplete survey responses,
for a total of 47 participants. Similar to ImageRewardDB
and HPD, we ask participants to rank 4 AI generated face
images. Users are presented with one of two instructions:
1. “Please rank these AI generated images in terms of im-
age fidelity/quality.”
2. “Please rank these AI generated images in terms of your
aesthetic preference.”
Users are asked to rank 20 sets of 4 images in this way.
Users are randomly shown 1 of 5 possible sets of images
(so 5 × 20 × 4 = 400 images are utilized in the study).
Question and image ordering is also randomized. Following
[51], users are then asked to rate three subjective attributes
(0 - 100 scale) for each face: trustworthiness, familiarity,
and attractiveness. However, only half of the images are ac-
tually AI generated (using the state-of-the-art CelebA-HQ
RelayDiffusion model [72]). The other half of the faces are
real images taken from the CelebA-HQ dataset [34]. Since
users are led to believe all images are AI generated, they
should rank images according to which they think are bet-
ter generations. And since we also have a numeric score
showing how attractive participants find each face, we can
see if the attractiveness of a face influences its ranking. If
we only consider real images, we can eliminate the possible
confounder of variations in image fidelity within our rank-
ing. We assume the real images are of comparable fidelity
and inherently more realistic than generated images. See
Figure 2 for a visual overview of the user study questions.
For each ranking of 4 images we therefore consider the
two real images, referring to the higher ranked image as
the favoured face and the lower ranked images as the un-
favoured face. To determine whether participants are more
likely to rank faces more highly if they find them more at-
tractive, we formulate the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 1 (Attractiveness Bias).
H(1)
0
: Perceived facial attractiveness is not
associated with image ranking.
H(1)
A
: Images rated as more attractive are more
likely to receive a higher ranking.
To test Hypothesis 1 we use a one-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test with a significance level of α = 0.05 to
determine if the participant-level attractiveness difference
between the favoured and unfavoured faces is greater than
zero. To ensure independence of observations, we first aver-
age the attractiveness differences across all rankings within
each participant. The Holm method is applied to control
the family-wise error rate. For the “fidelity” and “aesthetic”
instructions we obtain p-values of 0.0018 and 0.0006, re-
spectively. Both are well below the significance threshold,
allowing us to reject the null hypothesis and conclude that
participants tend to rank the faces they find more attrac-
tive more highly under both instruction conditions. We also
found that participants who took more time to complete the
survey tended to show less attractiveness bias. However, the
relationship was not statistically significant.
To determine whether the “aesthetic” wording produces


more attractiveness bias than the “fidelity” wording we for-
mulate the following hypothesis:
Hypothesis 2 (Instruction Effect).
H(2)
0
: The association between attractiveness and
image ranking is the same under both
instruction conditions.
H(2)
A
: The association between attractiveness and
image ranking is stronger when participants
are instructed to rank images according to
their aesthetic preference than when instructed
to rank according to image fidelity/quality.
To test Hypothesis 2 we use a one-sided Mann–Whitney
U test with a significance level of α = 0.05 to compare the
participant-level attractiveness differences between the two
instruction conditions. As before, we average the attrac-
tiveness differences within each participant to ensure inde-
pendence of observations, but we do not apply the Holm
method as this test contains a single planned comparison.
This analysis yields a p-value of 0.028, allowing us to reject
the null hypothesis and conclude that the attractiveness dif-
ference is larger under the “aesthetic” instruction than under
the “fidelity” instruction.
Performing the same analysis on the other two subjective
attributes, trustworthiness and familiarity, revealed that im-
ages rated as more trustworthy also tended to receive higher
rankings, although the larger p-values indicate weaker evi-
dence for this association. In contrast, faces rated as more
familiar were not significantly more likely to receive higher
rankings. Furthermore, no significant difference between
the two instruction conditions was found for either trust-
worthiness or familiarity.
To summarize, our analysis suggests that humans may
have a bias toward labeling a more attractive face as bet-
ter quality. Our findings also suggest that this effect can be
reduced, although not entirely eliminated, by asking users
to rank images in terms of “image fidelity/quality”, rather
than by “aesthetic preference”. We next trace how this find-
ing propagates through leaderboards (Section 3.2), reward
models (Section 3.3), and generation (Section 3.4).

attractive faces over higher-fidelity ones.

whether this bias, aggregated over many pairwise compar-
isons, is large enough to distort the leaderboards that these
comparisons are used to build.

is LMArena. This platform allows a user to enter a prompt,

can easily override ground-truth realism, heavily skewing
preference-based leaderboards in favour of models that gen-

this section, we test this directly, examining whether the hu-
man preference models used to evaluate, guide, and finetune
text-to-image models are biased towards attractive faces.
We also include two reward models that are not trained
on human preference data, the LAION-Aesthetics predic-
tor [64] and CLIP [58], as a control.

Evaluation on Synthetic Images
We start by experi-
menting on CelebA-HQ because this dataset has a binary
label for attractive [48].
Specifically, we take 100 base
CelebA-HQ images of resolution 256 by 256 and use a pre-
trained Diffusion Autoencoder [57] to change individual at-
tributes in isolation. We use 4 randomized edit magnitudes
between −0.5 and 0.5. For each base image this produces
4 images for each of CelebA-HQ’s 40 attributes, for a total
of 4 × 40 × 100 = 16000 synthetic images. For an example
of the image edits generated, please see Figure 3.
We then run the following reward models on, and get
scores for, each image: LAION-Aesthetics predictor [64],
CLIP [58], ImageReward [78], PickScore, [38], MPS, [82],
HPSv2.1 [77], and HPSv3 [50]. For models that require a
caption we use the generic text “a photo of a face”. We are
then interested in the following question: Does increasing
the prominence of certain facial attributes result in higher
rewards?
To answer this, we calculate the correlations between
the rewards given and the edit strengths. Table 2 shows
which of the 40 attributes in the CelebA-HQ dataset most
strongly correlate with each reward.
Critically, our two
non-preference control models behave differently from the
five preference models: there is no discernible pattern for
which attributes most strongly correlate with the LAION-
Aesthetics predictor’s scores, and CLIP’s strongest corre-
lations run through attributes associated with women (e.g.
wearing earrings, heavy makeup) rather than attractiveness.
In contrast, attractive is the attribute that most strongly cor-
relates with scores from PickScore, MPS, HPSv2.1, and
HPSv3, all of which are human preference models. Im-
portantly, many of these models are finetuned from CLIP,
suggesting that this pattern only emerges from human pref-
erence alignment.
To examine the impact of the prompt on these correla-
tions, we repeat this analysis using four different captioning
strategies.
1. An empty caption “” is used for all images.
2. A constant caption “a photo of a face” is used for all
images.
3. A template caption is used based on the edited attribute;
for example, for the male attribute, we use the captions
“a photo of a man’s face” or “a photo of a woman’s face”.
4. An LLM generated caption is used for each image using
the Gemma 4 [69] model.
Figure 4 shows how these different captioning strategies
impact the correlations between edit strength and the attrac-
tive attribute. Compared to our prior results using a constant
caption, moving to LLM generated captions tends to reduce,
but not eliminate the correlation. Importantly, attractive-
ness bias persists regardless of caption used. The one no-
table exception to this is ImageReward, which already has
the lowest correlation, and for which an LLM based caption
eliminates the bias. As discussed in Section 3.1, ImageRe-
ward is also the reward model whose annotation instructions
place the least emphasis on personal or aesthetic preference,
which offers a plausible explanation for why it is compara-
tively less affected by attractiveness bias.
Although these results suggest that human preference
models may have inherited a bias for more attractive faces,
it is plausible that some attribute may produce better quality
edits due to imbalances in the training data. Next, we pro-
vide additional analysis using real image datasets, where
image fidelity should remain constant, thereby eliminating
any possible image quality confounder.

3.4. Preference Aligned Generation
Section 3.3 establishes that facial attractiveness is both
highly correlated with and highly predictive of human pref-
erence scores, but this does not yet establish a causal rela-
tionship. In this section, we therefore apply human prefer-
ence models to guide the generation process itself and mea-
sure the impact of this on facial attractiveness. To measure
facial attractiveness, we finetune DINOv3 [65] on CelebA-
HQ labels from 0 (least) to 1 (most attractive), and use the
resulting model to predict attractiveness in the following ex-
periments (see Supplementary Material for details).
To investigate how finetuning diffusion models based on
human preferences affects the attractiveness of generated
faces, we consider two approaches. First, we finetune Sta-
ble Diffusion v1.5 using AlignProp [56], with HPSv2.1 as
the reward model and prompts sampled from HPD. Second,
we finetune a CelebA-HQ DDPM model [29] using DRaFT-
K [12] (K = 1), with PickScore as the reward model and a
constant prompt strategy. Both models are trained for three
epochs, and after each epoch we generate 5000 images us-
ing 50 inference steps. For Stable Diffusion v1.5, we use
prompts from the CelebA-Dialog dataset [32] and a guid-
ance scale of 5.0. Figure 7 shows the average facial attrac-
tiveness of the images generated at each stage of finetuning.

4. Mitigation
Having established that facial attractiveness can strongly
distort human preference, we now explore bias mitigation.
In particular, we focus on human preference models, whose
continuous scalar scores offer a valuable opportunity to
apply mathematically tractable, information-theoretic de-
biasing techniques. We propose the following target be-
haviour: preference scores should depend on attractiveness
only when the prompt explicitly requests it. Our evaluation
prompts do not mention attractiveness, so here the prefer-
ence scores S should be independent of attractiveness A.
We therefore design a loss function that minimizes the
mutual information between A and S. If the mutual in-
formation I(A; S) = 0, then knowing the attractiveness
of a face provides no information about its resulting score,
thereby removing the skew toward attractive faces. Ker-
nel density estimation is used to calculate mutual informa-
tion. However, we also want the human preference scores
to change as little as possible so that they retain their useful
signal. Our loss function is therefore:

5. Discussion
The attractiveness halo effect refers to a cognitive bias
where attractive people are assumed to possess other pos-
itive attributes, such as kindness, intelligence, or success
[15, 24]. We find that the attractiveness halo effect also oc-
curs when gathering human preference data for AI images,
resulting in users ranking more attractive faces higher than
less attractive faces. Our results suggest the bias perme-
ates the T2I ecosystem, affecting leaderboards, preference
models, and preference aligned generation alike. Our Elo
simulations show that attractiveness alone can invert a face-
prompt ranking, placing synthetic images above real ones
and leaving leaderboards vulnerable to reward hacking by
models that generate more attractive rather than better faces.
Our findings may also have broader implications for
body image since exposure to attractive faces in media has
been linked to body image concerns [6, 31, 54, 79]. Sec-
tion 3.4 shows preference alignment both raises facial at-
tractiveness and narrows its diversity. As AI-generated im-
agery proliferates on social media, preference-aligned mod-
els risk reproducing a narrow beauty ideal at scale.
Finally, Section 4 shows the bias can be removed from
reward scores post-hoc, but further work is needed to pre-
vent it appearing in preference data at all. Our results sug-
gest wording matters: asking users to rank by image fidelity
produces less biased responses than asking them to rank by
aesthetic preference. Attractiveness bias also decreased the
longer users took to complete the survey, although a larger
sample is likely needed to prove the influence definitively.
The comparatively low bias in ImageReward [78] makes its
collection process of particular interest: before ranking, Im-
ageRewardDB users write text descriptions and give numer-
ical quality ratings, which together with its overall instruc-
tions may prime users to focus on image quality and en-
courage them to spend more time considering each image.
Promising directions for future work therefore include ex-
perimenting with wording, imposing time limits, and using
multi-dimensional annotation.
6. Conclusion
In this work, we show that attractive faces can skew human
preference data for AI-generated imagery. Participants tend
to rate images containing more attractive faces as better,
even when image quality is equivalent or lower. We trace
the impact of this bias through the text-to-image ecosystem,
showing that it can skew human preference models, distort
leaderboard rankings, and bias preference-aligned genera-
tion. Finally, we propose a compute-efficient approach for
removing this bias from human preference scores using a
mutual information minimization objective. We hope this
work encourages the T2I community to refine its evaluation
practices to better account for such cognitive biases.

A. Further Discussion
A.1. Societal Impact
The bias we identify has consequences beyond leader-
boards: Section 3.4 shows that preference-aligned gener-
ation raises facial attractiveness while narrowing its diver-
sity, shifting outputs toward younger, female, and lighter-
skinned faces and removing users’ ability to generate any-
thing else.
At scale this risks reproducing a narrow
beauty ideal in systems that increasingly mediate how peo-
ple see faces, with documented links to body-image harm
[6, 27, 30, 79]. Our own methodology carries risks we state
plainly: we finetune a facial attractiveness predictor and use
a FairFace-trained demographic classifier, both of which are
open to misuse in ranking, filtering, or profiling people, and
both of which encode the judgements of particular annota-
tor populations rather than any objective property of faces.
We treat them as measurement instruments for a bias audit
rather than as contributions. Finally, our mitigation is only a
partial remedy. It removes the dependence of reward scores
on attractiveness post-hoc but inherits the predictor’s own
biases and does not stop the bias entering preference data
at source, which is why we regard the finding that instruc-
tion wording measurably changes recorded bias as the more
durable result.
A.2. Limitations
Several limitations warrant caution when interpreting our
findings. First, in the user study, participants rated attrac-
tiveness after ranking the same faces, so their attractiveness
ratings may have been influenced by their earlier prefer-
ences. Furthermore, we assume that real photographs are
generally of higher image quality than AI-generated im-
ages, however, if a real image is especially blurred (as a
small number of CelebA image are) or corrupted with noise,
this may not always hold true. Directly matching image
quality across individual real and generated images is chal-
lenging, as common metrics such as FID and IS are primar-
ily designed for evaluating distributions of images rather
than the quality of individual images. Comparing real pho-
tographs with generated images therefore provides a useful,
albeit imperfect, way to investigate whether attractiveness
influences preferences when image sources differ.
Similarly, our Elo experiment uses selected comparisons
between less attractive real faces and more attractive gener-
ated faces to illustrate how attractiveness bias could affect
leaderboard rankings. It represents a constructed scenario
rather than a direct measurement of distortion in existing
leaderboards. The results should therefore be interpreted as
demonstrating a potential vulnerability rather than quanti-
fying the extent of leaderboard distortion in practice.
It is also important to note that the DiffAE editing pro-
cedure may inadvertently alter attributes correlated with
attractiveness, as well as other aspects of image appear-
ance and quality. Although the example in Figure 3 (in
the main body of the paper) suggest that the intended ed-
its can be achieved without severe visible entanglement,
we cannot guarantee that attractiveness is the only prop-
erty that changes.
This means that the attribute-editing
experiments cannot independently establish that attractive-
ness alone accounts for the observed preference differences.
However, the real-image experiments provide complemen-
tary evidence without relying on DiffAE-generated edits,
reducing our dependence on any single experimental ap-
proach.
Taken together, our experiments provide converging
evidence that facial attractiveness is associated with hu-
man preferences for text-to-image outputs and may influ-
ence reward-model scores and leaderboard rankings, but no
individual experiment conclusively isolates attractiveness
from all potential confounding factors. Further work us-
ing quality-matched comparisons, more exact text-to-image
leaderboard setups, and broader sampling of images and
participants would help establish the magnitude of these ef-
fects.

B.2. ReNO Experiments
In this section we provide further analysis on ReNO-based
[19] guided generation using HPSv2.1 [77]. As in the main
body of the text we use PixArt-α [9] as the base text-to-
image model. Specifically, we run ReNO for 50 steps us-
ing 3 distinct prompts: ”a photo of an unattractive face”, ”a
photo of a face”, and ”a photo of an attractive face”. We ap-
ply ReNO to 100 images for each prompt. In Figure 10 we
provide the mean predicted beauty (SCUT-FBP5500 pre-
diction head used) for images across all 50 steps. We can
see that preference alignment boosts the attractiveness of all
images regardless of the prompt used. We again emphasize
how this could limit user control because whatever prompt
is used, the faces generated will almost entirely appear as
very attractive. Through preference alignment, the ability
to generate unattractive faces is effectively removed.
We also provide analysis on how the optimization of
these 300 images alters the age, race, and gender of the
faces depicted. To measure this we use a pretrained clas-
sifier 2 trained on FairFace [33] to predict the age, race, and
gender of face before and after preference alignment.
According to our classifier, all faces generated before
and after generation were white. Figure 11 shows confusion
matrices describing how both age and gender change during
optimization. The majority of faces remain the same gen-
der as when they started, however, out of the faces that do
change, 61 change from male to female and only 3 change
from female to male. Similarly, most faces stay in the same
age category before and after optimization, but nearly all
faces that changes age are changed to look younger, with an
especially large focus on the 16-24 years old category. This
suggests that HPSv2.1 shows a preference for young female
faces.
All ReNO experiments use the default hyperparameters
provided in their official GitHub repo unless stated other-
wise.
