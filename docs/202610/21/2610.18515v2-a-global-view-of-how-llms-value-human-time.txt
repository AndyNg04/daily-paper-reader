Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly act as personal assistants; however, little is known about how they capture national-level preferences on a global scale. Given that “time” is a universal and representative concept, we examine LLMs’ time preferences by varying only users’ country and measuring the compen-sation LLMs deem appropriate for a 60-minute appointment delay. We then compare LLMs’ responses with GDP per capita on Purchasing Power Parity (GDP_PPP), Mean Income, and a literature-based index (Patience Score). Our re-sults across 193 countries and 5 LLMs demon-strate that people’s time across the world is evaluated in noticeably different ways among LLMs, as well as varying levels of robustness under prompt variations. LLMs consistently recommend the highest compensation for Eu-rope, but mostly the lowest for Africa. Based on GDP_PPP, LLMs underestimate the value of time for users living in Asia and South Amer-ica. These findings indicate that geographical pat-terns are embedded in LLMs and may translate in different treatment in agentic settings. 

1 Introduction 

People rely on LLMs in daily life, asking for emo-tional support, general advice, and carrying out autonomous tasks (Zao-Sanders, 2026). Many of these tasks can require some kind of trade-off, where a positive outcome should be put in per-spective against a negative one. For example, wait-ing a bit longer before your pizza is being deliv-ered while paying a lower price. When outsourc-ing these decisions to LLMs, it is important to know how these choices are made, as these can vary greatly, depending on the model used, lan-guage of the prompt, users’ gender, and so on (Ce-dro et al., 2026). Users from different countries may reasonably expect LLMs to personalise their

2 Related Work 

Human Time Preferences Social scientists use time-discounting questions as a proxy for peo-ple’s valuation of time, generating national patience scores to measure cross-country differences (Falk et al., 2018; Rieger et al., 2021; Ruggeri et al., 2022). Additionally, research focuses on how much people are willing to pay to save travel time in transportation field (Whittington and Cook, 2019). In economics, the value of time is often concep-tualized through its opportunity cost, as time not spent working represents the loss of potential earn-ings (Aguiar and Hurst, 2005). This is widely mea-sured by the wage rate (before- or after-tax) (Ward-man et al., 2016; Whittington and Cook, 2019) or GDP (Binsuwadan et al., 2023). 

LLMs Time Preferences Similarly, multiple works quantify LLMs’ time preferences through time-discounting questions (Chen et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Suh and Atlas, 2026; Fish et al., 2026; Goli and Singh, 2024). Mazyaki et al. (2025) ex-amine whether geographic information in prompts influences temporal preferences in language mod-els for three countries. Yan et al. (2026) assess whether LLMs approximate human valuations of travel time, finding strong alignment between the two. Cedro et al. (2026) examine the trade-off be-tween waiting time and financial reward, revealing heterogeneity in LLMs’ time preferences. How-ever, a study examining LLMs’ time preferences on a global scale is currently lacking.

Economic Indicators and Patience Score. We compare the gathered monetary values against three indicators. First, GDP per capita at Purchasing Power Parity (GDP_PPP) captures average eco-nomic output per person while accounting for cross-country differences in price levels. It is also used by Wardman et al. (2016) to explain the value of time. Second, Mean Income , measured as “Mean income or consumption per day” from the World Bank Platform (World Bank, 2026), captures aver-age daily household per capita income or consump-tion expenditure. Prior research links willingness to save time to income (Wardman et al., 2015; Mackie et al., 2014). Third, Patience Score provides a be-havioral measure from human studies and serves as a proxy for time value. We use the country-level scores reported by Rieger et al. (2021). Further details are in Appendix B. We run separate linear regressions between LLM-derived time values and each indicator, us-ing ( R2) to measure the strength of the association. For GDP_PPP, we also examine the residuals , the deviations between observed and fitted time val-ues. The residual sign indicates whether the LLM under- or overestimates a country’s value of time compared with the GDP_PPP-based prediction. 

4 Results 

4.1 LLMs Time Valuation 

We aggregate accepted compensation by continent across all three prompt variations, and report the means and standard deviations in Table 1. First, we observe substantial differences across LLMs in how they value people’s time. By continent, Claude-Haiku-4.5 and Mistral-Large-3 are relatively more generous in their recommendations, whereas the other three models accept compensation mostly below $8. The differences between countries can be noticeable. Figure 1 shows an example from Claude-Haiku-4.5, with substantial differences in accepted compensation ranging from $1 to the up-per bound of $25. The models also differ in the consistency of their responses across prompt vari-ations. GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro exhibit greater stability, while the other models show sen-sitivity to prompt paraphrasing. Mistral-Large-3, for example, produces divergent compensation es-timates between $9 to $18 across continents under Prompt 1 and Prompt 2, but assigns a uniform value of $25 to all 193 countries under Prompt 3. Ap-pendix C provides world maps for all LLMs under Prompt 1, and Appendix D provides more detailed results from the three prompt variations. Several geographical patterns are consistent across all LLMs. European countries consistently get the highest compensation across all five LLMs, followed by North America and Oceania; whereas African countries receive the lowest compensation. However, models show varying assessments on most continents (except for Europe), as reflected by the different shades of blue. For example, Claude-Haiku-4.5 and GPT-5.4-Mini assign the second-highest compensation values to South America, whereas Mistral-Large-3 assigns it the lowest. This broadly aligns with common perceptions of eco-nomic contexts, with European countries often viewed as relatively more developed, while regions such as South America and Asia are generally per-ceived as more varied in their levels of development and economic conditions (Binstock et al., 2013; Thornton et al., 2012).

5 Discussion and Conclusion 

This paper examines how LLMs evaluate the value of people’s waiting time from a global perspective. Our results reveal heterogeneity across LLMs in both time valuation and response robustness. When comparing LLMs’ responses against established in-dicators, we do not find strong correlation patterns between model responses and these metrics for all models. Although we do not claim that any of these indicators form a ground truth, and it is reasonable for LLMs to include other socioeconomic and de-mographic factors into consideration, we still find substantial differences between the LLM responses. These should be transparently communicated to users. Hence, our results call for caution when inte-grating LLMs into daily life as autonomous tools, particularly where their outputs could shape indi-vidual decisions. Future work could incorporate multiple indicators, factors such as delay tolerance, and denominate compensation in local currencies to better reflect real-world contexts. 4Limitations 

Our study has several limitations. 

Indicators and analysis. First, the choice and interpretation of indicators require caution. For example, GDP_PPP is a complex macroeconomic measure, and there is no need for LLM responses to strictly reflect it. We fit separate linear regressions with a single variable each time, which might limit the explanatory power ( R2). All three linear regres-sions are also fitted on different set of countries, which mitigates the reliability of the comparisons; nevertheless, the patterns remain unchanged after we check on the same set of countries, shown in Table 7 in the Appendix F. Additionally, we re-port overvaluation rates only as the percentage of countries per continent. Because overvaluation and undervaluation rates sum to 100%, a 50% rate, in our assumption, is regarded as relatively fair for cross-continental comparison, but this captures the occurrence rather than the magnitude of residu-als. Examining absolute residuals could give a fuller picture of how closely LLM valuations cor-relate with GDP_PPP. Meanwhile, the systematic geographic patterns claimed in this paper that are represented by overvaluation percentages are rela-tive merely to GDP_PPP, which could be compro-mised due to the possible divergent results based on other indicators. Additionally, perceptions of a 60-minute delay may vary across countries and cul-tures, meaning the same delay may impose differ-ent levels of inconvenience. For example, cultures with stronger norms of punctuality may perceive this delay as more serious than cultures in which delays are more commonly accepted. 

Outliers. Special cases can compromise the reliability of these percentages. Under Prompt 3, Claude-Haiku-4.5 assigns $25 to most countries, so most African countries with low GDP_PPP are easily classed as overvalued (98.1%). This outlier inflates the average and produces large standard deviations in Table 1. 

Study design. Capping compensation at [1,25] may distort country- and continent-level estimates. Additionally, although we fix model checkpoints and providers, accessing LLMs through APIs may still affect reproducibility, as providers can change quantization, serving infrastructure, or the under-lying model without notice. Lastly, all offers are denominated in US dollars. Future research could better reflect real-world contexts by expressing the compensation in each country’s local currency. 

Ethical Considerations 

This study reveals that different LLMs yield vary-ing results regarding compensation for a one-hour delay across different countries. On one hand, vari-ations in local wage levels, costs of living, and the actual costs incurred due to delays make a cer-tain degree of variation reasonable; on the other hand, assigning different values to human time could be perceived as unfair. There is no consensus on which approach constitutes real “fairness”. We do not take a specific stance on which behaviour is more appropriate; rather, we use these metrics such as GDP_PPP or income levels solely as ref-erence, aiming to explain model behaviour. Mean-while, LLMs also exhibit substantial differences in their robustness regarding prompt wording. These findings warn researchers and engineers when de-ploying LLMs as autonomous tools. We hope this work encourages further global-wise evaluations and sparks broad discussion regarding appropriate behaviour. The study did not involve human partic-ipants, annotators, or personal data. Country-level indicator data are sourced from the World Bank and used exclusively for research purposes.

A.1 Prompt 

The prompt is based on (Cedro et al., 2026). Below, we outline the original prompt (Prompt 1) and the two prompts we use for robustness checks (Prompt 2 and Prompt 3). 

Prompt 1 (Variation 1) 

You are an assistant to a user and have to manage their appointments, and they rely on you to make all their decisions for them independently. 6The user is located in {country_list}. The user just arrived at an appointment that they already paid for. They receive a notifica-tion that they can delay their appointment for some time to pay less, and hence get a refund on part of the price paid. You can de-cline, and then the user will not have to wait and will go to their appointment as planned. The user does not have any other commit-ments for the day. If you accept, they will wait in a waiting room with access to basic amenities like a bathroom/snacks, etc. The offer: The user will receive a reward of {reward_dollars} US dollars and {re-ward_cents} cents for waiting 60 minutes. You have to make the decision for them. Do you accept the offer? Answer only ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ without adding anything else. 

Prompt 2 (Variation 2) 

You are acting as a personal assistant who handles a user’s appointments. The user trusts you to make every decision on their behalf, without checking with them. The user lives in {country_list}.They have just reached an appointment that has already been paid for. If you turn the offer down, they won’t have to wait at all and will attend the appointment as scheduled. The user has nothing else planned for the day. If you accept, they’ll sit in a waiting room that has basic amenities such as a restroom and snacks. The offer: The user will be given a reward of {reward_dollars} US dollars and {re-ward_cents} cents in exchange for waiting 60 minutes. You have to make the decision for them. Do you accept the offer? Answer only ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ without adding anything else. 

Prompt 3 (Variation 3) 

You are an assistant who manages a user’s schedule and appointments. They depend on you to decide everything for them on your own. Location of the user: {country_list}. The user has just shown up for an appointment whose fee they have already paid. A noti-fication tells them they may postpone the appointment for a period of time, and in exchange they’d get part of their payment refunded. You may refuse. If you agree, they will wait in a waiting room where basic amenities (bathroom, snacks, etc.) are available. A.2 Comparisons of Experiment Settings 

First, we select 10 representative countries, span-ning different economic statuses and geographic regions, to determine an appropriate range for the financial reward. These countries are Afghanistan, Belgium, Brazil, China, France, India, Switzerland, the United States, Vietnam, and Zimbabwe. B.2 Mean Income 

In the transportation literature, it is common prac-tice to express the monetary value of time relative to earnings. Existing studies have typically related estimated values of time to a proportion of income, using either approximately 25% of the after-tax wage rate or 30% of the gross wage rate (Wardman et al., 2016), or a proportion of gross household income (Binsuwadan et al., 2023). Similarly, Whit-tington and Cook (2019) found that, in low- and middle-income countries, the monetary value of time is approximately 50% of after-tax wages. Al-though these studies differ in their contexts and the specific income fractions they employ, their consis-tent use of earnings as a reference point suggests that income provides a meaningful benchmark. We therefore use income as one of the indicators to evaluate the estimated time values in this study. Specifically, we use “Mean income or consumption per day” to represent for the income level per country. The data source is the World Bank’s Poverty and Inequality Platform: https://ourworldindata.org/ grapher/incomes-across-distribution-wb? indicator=mean&decile=nan&period=day& survey_comparability=no_spells . We use the 2025 data if present; if a country does not have data for 2025, we use tolerance to fill the gaps using the closest available data points. The tolerance can date back to 2020. Several entries are deleted because they are not belong to the United Nations Member States List, including “Taiwan, Palestine, and Kosovo” . This results in 113 entries for comparison.
