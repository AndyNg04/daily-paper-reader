Abstract 

Offl ine reinforcement learning is fundamentally limited by the coverage of the available data, while collecting additional trajectories through online exploration may be costly, unsafe, or infeasible. We study whether this limitation can be overcome without directly interacting with the underlying environment. Motivated by practical settings such as collaborator-mediated simulation, where a collaborator can provide information complementary to the o ffl ine data, we introduce query-based collaborative reinforcement learning to study how to acquire this information e ffi ciently. The learner starts with a fixed o ffl ine dataset and adaptively queries state-time pairs; the collaborator samples an action according to a fixed, possibly suboptimal policy, and the simulator returns the corresponding reward and next state. For finite-horizon tabular problems, we derive an instance-dependent information-theoretic lower bound on the additional queries required to identify an ε-optimal policy and develop a local quadratic approximation. Guided by this characterization, we develop an adaptive query-allocation algorithm whose query complexity nearly matches the local benchmark under suitable conditions. Experiments on multiple synthetic and semi-synthetic environments demonstrate the empirical e ffi ciency of the proposed method.

21 Introduction 

Collecting new trajectories for reinforcement learning (RL) can be costly, slow, or unsafe. In healthcare, for example, it is generally infeasible to deploy exploratory treatment policies on patients solely for data collection. O ffl ine RL avoids such interaction by learning from historical records, but its guarantees depend critically on the coverage of the available data [Jin et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2021; Yin and Wang, 2021]. The absence of feasible online exploration, however, does not preclude access to additional information through simulation. We study how a learner can acquire this information effi ciently through collaboration. We introduce query-based collaborative reinforcement learning , in which a learner augments a fixed o ffl ine dataset with observations from a collaborator-mediated simulator. At each query, the learner selects a state-time pair; the collaborator samples an action according to a fixed, possibly suboptimal policy, and the simulator returns the corresponding reward and next state. The learner thus controls where information is collected, but not which action generates it. Our central question is: 

Given the available o ffl ine data, when is it possible to identify a near-optimal policy through collaboration, and how should queries be allocated to do so e ffi ciently? 

This setting is motivated by systems that couple decision models with simulators. In head-and-neck cancer treatment, Wentzel et al. [2025] combine a model of physician treatment decisions with a patient simulator that predicts treatment responses and clinical outcomes. In our abstraction of such a system, a query specifies the patient state and treatment stage, while the physician model selects the treatment and the simulator supplies its consequences. Related structures arise in power-system dispatch, where a rule-based decision strategy interacts with a simulated grid [Xu et al., 2024], and in process control, where a human digital twin emulates an operator’s corrective actions in a simulated plant [Balaji et al., 2023]. The collaborator need not be uniformly informative. For example, a hospital’s records may contain treatments that an externally developed clinical digital twin rarely or never selects, while the twin may provide information about patient states poorly represented in those records. The two sources should therefore be viewed as complementary rather than interchangeable. Unlike imitation learning, our objective is not to recover the collaborator’s policy, but to use both information sources to identify a near-optimal policy for the underlying decision process. This division of control also distinguishes our setting from standard trajectory-based hybrid RL, which supplements o ffl ine data by executing exploration policies [Ball et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025; Song et al., 2022]. Here, the learner can request observations at a chosen state-time pair without first reaching that state through a trajectory. Conversely, unlike unrestricted generative-model access [Agarwal et al., 2020; Azar et al., 2012; Sidford et al., 2018; Zanette et al., 2019], it cannot directly select the sampled action. The query strategy must therefore account for both the information already available o ffl ine and the collaborator’s sampling restrictions. Uniformly querying all state-time pairs is generally ine ffi cient. The o ffl ine data may already estimate some parts of the decision process accurately, while leaving others uncertain. More importantly, not every uncertainty is equally relevant: identifying a good policy requires resolving comparisons between plausible alternatives, rather than estimating every reward and transition equally accurately. Poor coverage alone therefore need not make a state the best query location. An e ffi cient strategy should direct queries toward the policy comparisons that remain statistically di ffi cult after accounting for the o ffl ine data, subject to which observations the collaborator can supply. The sampling restriction also means that some uncertainty may persist indefinitely. If the collaborator never selects an action at a given state-time pair, no amount of querying can provide additional observa-tions of that action there. Its estimation remains limited by the fixed o ffl ine data, potentially imposing an irreducible certification limit . Contributions. We initiate the framework of query-based collaborative reinforcement learning, where an instance-dependent analysis is natural because the value of additional queries depends strongly on the o ffl ine coverage, collaborator support and the structure of the underlying MDP, which can vary substantially across instances. Hence, a worst-case analysis obscures this heterogeneity and cannot capture how the o ffl ine data and collaborator jointly reduce the remaining uncertainty. For a target suboptimality ε > 0 and failure probability δ ∈ (0 , 1), our main results are as follows. – Information-theoretic lower bound and local characterization. We derive an exact KL-based lower-bound benchmark for the additional collaborator queries required to identify an ε-optimal policy with probability at least 1 − δ. We then derive a tractable local quadratic surrogate that makes explicit the roles of policy-comparison margins, local reward-transition variability, o ffl ine coverage, and collaborator support. – Lower-bound-guided adaptive querying. We show that the same local variance structure governs the lower-bound approximation and the leading finite-sample uncertainty of policy-value comparisons. Guided by this connection, we develop an epoch-based algorithm that progressively narrows the candidate policy class and allocates queries through a local minimax design problem. The design jointly selects a reference policy and state–time query locations, concentrating observations on comparisons that remain di ffi cult after exploiting the o ffl ine data. – Finite-sample guarantees and near-matching complexity. We establish finite-query termination and high-probability suboptimality guarantees for the returned policy, covering both near-optimality certi-fication and stopping at the certification limit. In the certifiable regime, under the stated assumptions, 4the leading query-complexity term is governed by the same local quadratic characteristic, evaluated at a polylogarithmically reduced e ff ective o ffl ine sample size. We additionally establish robustness to row-wise simulator misspecification measured in total variation. Empirically, we evaluate the method on three synthetic environments (contextual bandit, multi-stage MDP, and an MDP with long-term consequences), and a semi-synthetic drug-response application. Compared with uniform and coverage-based allocation, our adaptive allocation certifies an ε-optimal policy with 3-22 times fewer collaborator queries in an e ffi cient implementation and returns better policies at equal budgets. 

# 2 Related Work 

Worst-case sample complexity in tabular RL. A large body of work studies the worst-case sample complexity of learning a near-optimal policy in tabular MDPs. In o ffl ine RL, where the learner must rely entirely on a fixed dataset, guarantees of related work necessarily depend on how well the behavior policy covers the state–action pairs relevant to an optimal policy. A sequence of works has established sample complexity guarantees under various notions of coverage, including single-policy concentrability [Shi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024]. At the other extreme, the generative-model setting allows the learner to request samples from arbitrary state–action pairs. Classical and more recent results characterize the minimax sample complexity of uniformly querying under such access [Gheshlaghi Azar et al., 2013; Agarwal et al., 2020]. 

Instance-dependent policy identification. Our work is closely related to a growing literature on instance-dependent reinforcement learning, which seeks to exploit the specific structure of the underlying MDP to obtain substantially sharper sample-complexity guarantees than worst-case bounds. Rather than characterizing di ffi culty solely through global quantities such as the size of the state–action space or worst-case coverage coe ffi cients, these results adapt to instance-specific features such as visitation probabilities, local variances, and suboptimality gaps. Hence the resulting guarantees can be much smaller than worst-case rates on favorable instances. In o ffl ine tabular RL, Yin and Wang [2021] derived variance-sensitive instance-dependent guarantees while Wang et al. [2022] showed that, under suitable coverage conditions, the sample complexity can be improved when the minimum suboptimality gap is bounded away from zero. A related line of work studies instance-dependent policy identification in online RL. For online tabular RL, Wagenmaker et al. [2022] developed instance-dependent PAC guarantees that adapt to local action gaps, thereby allowing substantially smaller sample complexity on MDPs in which suboptimal actions can be identified quickly. In contrast, Wagenmaker and Jamieson [2022] studied instance-dependent policy identification from a policy-level perspective, with complexity controlled by the suboptimal policy gap. More recently, Narang et al. [2024] showed that directly estimating value di ff erences between candidate policies can lead to substantially sharper identification guarantees than estimating each policy value separately. Instance dependence has also been studied under generative-model access. For discounted tabular MDPs, Zanette et al. [2019] derived structure-aware sample-complexity guarantees that adapt to local reward and transition variances as well as suboptimality action gaps, and can avoid an explicit polynomial dependence on the e ff ective horizon in favorable instances. 

Imitation learning and learning with expert queries. Several ideas underlying our framework also appear in the imitation-learning literature, particularly the selective use of expert supervision and the combination of expert information with other sources of data. Judah et al. [2014] studied active imitation learning, where the learner adaptively selects informative states at which to query the expert for an action, with the goal of reducing the number of expert labels. Liu et al. [2022] studied adversarial policy imitation 5with linear function approximation and, in their o ffl ine setting, combined expert demonstrations with an additional o ffl ine dataset collected by a prior policy. They showed that this supplementary dataset can improve the statistical e ffi ciency of imitation learning under suitable coverage conditions. More closely related to our use of the collaborator, Amortila et al. [2022] considered e ffi cient learning with a small number of expert-action queries. In their setting, an expert query provides only the expert’s action and the learner implements it and interacts with the environment, and the goal is to learn a policy that performs at least nearly as well as the expert rather than to identify a near-optimal policy. 

Adaptive querying and complementary information. Effi cient information acquisition is also studied beyond RL. Ding et al. [2026] adaptively select questions and respondents to infer latent group properties under budget constraints, while Liu et al. [2026] allocate reasoning samples across questions for e ffi cient test-time self-consistency. Related work also exploits complementary information: weak-strong model collaboration combines specialized knowledge with general reasoning [Jiao et al., 2025]. Our setting instead concerns near-optimal policy identification from o ffl ine data and adaptively queried simulator observations. The collaborator’s fixed action policy restricts which uncertainties can be reduced, making both query e ffi ciency and the attainability of the target accuracy central to the analysis.

Our goal is to design an adaptive procedure that accounts for the statistical limitations of both available information sources. If the target accuracy ε is attainable from the fixed o ffl ine dataset together with additional collaborator-mediated observations, the procedure should achieve the desired (ε, δ )-PAC guarantee. # 5 Algorithm Overview

# 8 Summary 

We introduced query-based collaborative reinforcement learning, a novel setting in which a learner augments a fixed o ffl ine dataset by choosing state–time queries to a collaborator-mediated simulator, while the sampled action is determined by a fixed and possibly suboptimal collaborator policy. We derived an instance-dependent information-theoretic lower bound and a local quadratic characterization. It motivates an adaptive algorithm that progressively localizes the policy class and allocates queries through a lower-bound-guided minimax design. Our analysis establishes finite stopping and high-probability accuracy guarantees and shows that, in the certifiable regime, the leading query-complexity term nearly tracks the local quadratic benchmark. Our experiments illustrate the resulting instance dependence. Across three synthetic environments and a semi-synthetic drug-response application, adaptive allocation concentrates queries on the policy comparisons that remain di ffi cult after incorporating the o ffl ine data, rather than simply on the least-covered state–action pairs. This substantially reduces the number of collaborator queries needed for certification relative to uniform and coverage-based allocation. The current theory focuses on finite tabular MDPs, a fixed collaborator, and a theory-aligned implementa-tion with fresh epoch-wise data splitting. Natural directions for future work include establishing sharper guarantees for data-reusing and e ffi cient implementations, developing more computationally scalable algorithms, extending the framework to function approximation, and studying fixed-budget settings with heterogeneous query costs or state-time-specific budget constraints. 18 References

E.1 Implementation and Protocol 

Theory-aligned version. The theory-aligned version retains the data-splitting and epoch structure of the algorithm in Section 5, on tabular layered MDPs ( H = 1 in Simulation 1), with the experimental parameter choices and stabilization rule described below. The o ffl ine trajectories are trimmed with the two-fold procedure of Li et al. [2024] at failure probability δ/ 48, and the retained data are split equally into the historical sample DG

At epoch k, with resolution εk = H21−k and the confidence factor βk computed from qk−1 = |bΠk−1|2, the algorithm collects a uniform stabilization batch at every state–time pair, executes the allocation chosen at the previous epoch, and localizes the candidate set at 5 εk /2 using that epoch’s fresh data only. Occupancies, values and sensitivities are computed in the empirical MDP of the historical data, and the variance envelope on the fresh sample. The next allocation solves the minimax design with the regularized margin max {ε − bDk , 4εk } at the smallest dyadic budget Bk with bΦ⋆k (Bk ) ⩽ 1/16. For each candidate reference policy, the continuous minimum budget and the min–max allocation are convex programs with one variable per state–time pair, which we solve with SLSQP; integer allocations use largest-remainder rounding, and the 1 /16 criterion is rechecked after rounding. A run stops when a single candidate remains or εk ⩽ ε, and returns the empirical best policy on that epoch’s fresh data. Because the collaborator plays every action with positive probability, the stops for insu ffi cient coverage of collaborator-unsupported rows and for the certification limit never occur. Baselines. Uniform and coverage run the same version of the algorithm on the same o ffl ine sample and folds as the paired adaptive run, with the same stabilization, localization, elimination (e ffi cient version), stopping and reporting rules. Only the allocation of design queries di ff ers: uniform spreads each design batch evenly over the state–time pairs, and coverage splits it in proportion to Pa πch(a | s)/(1 + Nhsa )2,where Nhsa counts all observations of ( h, s, a ) collected so far, o ffl ine and queried; neither uses gaps, values or variances. In the fixed-budget protocol both baselines also use the paired adaptive run’s epoch batch sizes and stabilization draws. In the stopping protocol each baseline solves the same design problem with its allocation restricted to its own shape, and so selects its own batch sizes. The o ffl ine-only reference returns the policy with the highest value in the empirical model of the full, untrimmed o ffl ine sample, with unobserved rows contributing zero.

Statistics, seeds and computation. Each setting uses 300 trials. Within a trial, all methods and both versions share the o ffl ine sample with its trimming and fold split, and each trial’s random streams are derived from the simulation’s seed base (20261101, 20261161 and 20261171), the setting and the trial index; both values of C0 use the same seed bases. Proportions have pointwise 95% Wilson intervals, mean regret and mean query costs have intervals of the mean ±1.96 standard errors, and paired di ff erences in recovery have intervals of the mean ±1.96 standard errors of the per-trial di ff erences. No multiplicity 94 adjustment is applied. The runs used six to nine worker processes on a single Apple-silicon laptop (Python 3.13.5, NumPy 2.5.3, SciPy 1.18.1). For each value of C0, Simulation 1 took under 7 minutes for the e ffi cient version and under 1.5 hours for the theory-aligned version, and Simulations 2 and 3 under 3 minutes each. 

E.2 Summary of Results 

Certification cost. Adaptive allocation needs the fewest queries to certify in every setting under both versions (Figure 6). In the e ffi cient version it certifies after 6 .1 × 10 3 to 2 .2 × 10 4 queries on average, and uniform and coverage allocation need 5 .9-24 .6 and 3 .6-17 .0 times as many. In the theory-aligned version it certifies after 2 .6 × 10 5 to 5 .5 × 10 5 queries, and the baselines need 5 .1-21 .2 and 3 .4-15 .8 times as many; there, in every setting, the last adaptive trial certified before the first baseline trial. The e ffi cient version’s costs are lowest at n0 = 5 ,000 in every simulation, and the theory-aligned costs barely change with n0.

Fixed budgets. At the epoch end where adaptive allocation’s smaller recovery gain over the two baselines is largest, the e ffi cient version’s adaptive allocation recovers the optimal policy more often than uniform allocation in 23 of the 25 settings and more often than coverage in 22, at the 95% level; all exceptions are at n0 = 5 ,000 in Simulations 1 and 3, where the o ffl ine data alone recover the optimal policy in 90-93% of trials. The theory-aligned version does so in all 25 settings against both baselines. These gains are the largest of several epoch-end comparisons, so they overstate the typical gain; the figures show every epoch end. Across all epoch ends, settings and both versions, no paired di ff erence in recovery is significantly below zero. 

Effi cient and theory-aligned versions. At n0 = 500 the e ffi cient version recovers the optimal policy more often than the theory-aligned one at equal budgets under all three allocations, because it decides on all data collected so far. For example, in Simulation 1 adaptive, uniform and coverage allocation recover 98 .0%, 85 .7% and 81 .7% after 3 .4 × 10 3 queries under the e ffi cient version, and 94 .3%, 79 .3% and 77 .3% after 5 .0 × 10 3 under the theory-aligned version; in Simulation 3 with H = 15 they recover 96 .0%, 77 .0% and 83 .3% after 4 .1 × 10 3 queries under the e ffi cient version, and 79 .0%, 73 .3% and 74 .3% after 4 .3 × 10 3

under the theory-aligned version. The theory-aligned version needs 12 to 74 times as many queries as the e ffi cient one to certify, because only the localization shrinks its candidate set. 110 F PRISM Application Details 

Data. We use the primary screen of the PRISM drug-repurposing resource [Corsello et al., 2020] (19Q4 release), which reports for each cell line and compound the log 2 fold change in viability relative to the DMSO control, ComBat-corrected and median-collapsed across replicates. Of the 578 cell lines in the screen, 10 fail STR identity profiling and 27 lack a response to at least one compound of a six-drug panel at 2 .5 μM; of the remaining 541, we keep every lineage with at least 30 lines, which gives the six lineages of Table 3 (284 lines). Within each lineage, lines are ordered by a hash of their DepMap identifier; the first 60% (rounded down) form a development set of 168 lines, used only to choose the drug pair, and the remaining 116 lines form the benchmark used in every experiment. The reward of a line under a drug is 1 if its log 2 fold change is at most −1 (viability at most half of control) and 0 otherwise, and the response rate r1(s, a ) of drug a in lineage s is the fraction of benchmark lines in s with reward 1.

Collaborator, queries and initial log. A query names a lineage, then the collaborator picks each drug with probability 1 /2 and returns the stored reward of a benchmark line drawn uniformly at random, with replacement, from that lineage. Each observation is therefore Bernoulli( r1(s, a )), and repeated queries resample measured responses rather than run new assays. The initial log has as many records in each lineage as the lineage has benchmark lines (116 in total, drawn with replacement) and reveals a single drug: dasatinib in half of the trials and selumetinib in the other half. It cannot compare the drugs, so the o ffl ine-only policy, the empirical maximizer on the log, plays the logged drug in every lineage and is never ε-optimal (regret about 17 percentage points).

# G Code Availability & Computing 

All simulations and experiments were run on CPU only (no GPU), on a single MacBook Pro laptop with an Apple M5 chip (10 CPU cores) and 24 GB of memory. The code is written in Python 3.13.5 with NumPy 2.5.3 and SciPy 1.18.1, whose SLSQP solver solves the allocation design problems, and the independent trials of each setting run in parallel on eight or nine worker processes. For each value of C0,Simulation 1 took under 7 minutes for the e ffi cient version and under 1.5 hours for the theory-aligned version, Simulations 2 and 3 under 3 minutes each, and each PRISM configuration at most about two minutes; in total, all runs behind the reported results took under 3 hours of wall-clock time.
