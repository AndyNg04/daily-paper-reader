Abstract 

Accurate evaluation of LLM personalization is hindered by current evaluation paradigms that over- or under-constrain the response space, yielding frequent false rejections and false acceptances. To reduce these false rates, we introduce ConQual-Audit, an audit-based framework that evaluates LLM personalization through a three-stage process: (1) extracting se-mantic constraints from user profiles and task-grounded reference; (2) qualifying constraints by pruning unnecessary constraints and recov-ering missing constraints; and (3) verifying candidate responses against the audited con-straints via natural language inference. Across PrefEval and LaMP, ConQual-Audit outper-forms nine baselines, attaining top AUCs (0.88 and 0.90) and the lowest false rate (0.21) on the leanest constraint sets at an amortizable audit cost. Crucially, this constraint audit is portable: integrating it into ExPerT and PREF helps re-vise smaller, higher-quality constraint sets to reduce their evaluation false rates. All code, prompts, and evaluation scripts are released in an anonymous repository (Anonymous, 2026)

ConQual-Audit operates across three modular stages: First , it extracts original se-mantic constraints from both task-grounded refer-ence and user preference. Second , it refines the extracted constraints through a counterexample-driven tripartite audit: (i) deduplicating redundant criteria; (ii) pruning unnecessary constraints by constructing personalized counterexamples that vi-olate candidate criteria yet remain valid; and (iii) recovering missing constraints by synthesizing non-personalized counterexamples that satisfy all ac-tive criteria. Finally , it evaluates candidate re-sponses against the audited constraint set via a premise–hypothesis inference formulation, and uni-fies constraint satisfaction and response attribution via their harmonic mean to jointly balance con-straint coverage and response recall. 

Empirical Verification. Extensive empirical eval-uations across the PrefEval Zhao et al. (2025) and LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024) benchmarks show the effectiveness of ConQual-Audit. Our framework establishes the highest evaluation performance, achieving top AUC scores ( 0.88 and 0.90 ) and the lowest overall false rates (FR = 0 .21 ) while oper-ating on the most compact constraint sets ( 2.36 and 

2.97 per query). Crucially, we show that constraint qualification is model-agnostic and highly portable: integrating our audit module into existing evalu-ators (ExPerT and PREF) systematically remove unnecessary constraints, restore missing ones, and significantly reduce downstream evaluation errors at an amortized audit computational cost. The primary contributions are three-fold: • ConQual-Audit Framework: We propose a principled, three-stage audit-based framework for LLM personalization evaluation that over-comes both over- and under-constrained eval-uation regimes through semantic constraint construction, qualification, and verification. • Counterexample-Guided Qualification: We design a tripartite audit protocol that systemat-ically deduplicates, prunes, and recovers con-straints. This mechanism yields compact, cal-ibrated constraint sets that attain top AUCs and the lowest overall false rates on LaMP and PrefEval. • Modularity and Portability: We demon-strate that constraint qualification is model-agnostic: augmenting external constraint-based evaluators (ExPerT and PREF) with our audit module consistently improves con-straint quality and lowers downstream false rates with minimal overhead. 

2 Problem Formulation & Related Work 

Problem formulation. We formalize LLM per-sonalization evaluation (Salemi and Zamani, 2025; Kumar et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2024) as a set-membership problem. Let q be a query from a user with interaction history H, let 

y∗ be a task reference for q, and let Ω be the set of all candidate responses to q. The user and the task determine the set of acceptable responses 

A⋆ = { y ∈ Ω : y is acceptable }, (1) where a response is acceptable if it follows the user’s preferences, as evidenced by H, and meets the task requirements specified by y∗. Evaluat-ing a response y means deciding whether y ∈ A ⋆.Since A⋆ is not observed, every evaluation method approximates it by an induced set bA; existing meth-ods differ in how they construct it (Figures 1 and 2). 

Reference-based evaluation (e.g., BLEU (Pa-pineni et al., 2002), ROUGE (Dasgupta et al., 2024), BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020), Mover-Score (Zhao et al., 2019)) accepts responses that are similar to a set of human annotations R ⊂ A ⋆

via a similarity function sim and a threshold τ :

bAref = { y ∈ Ω : max  

> r∈R

sim( y, r ) ≥ τ }, (2) Although annotations serve as effective anchors, they cannot encompass all forms of personalized responses. Consequently, they often reject valid re-sponses that differ in wording from the annotations, while accepting invalid responses that happen to share similar vocabulary (such as negated answers). 

LLM-as-a-judge (e.g., GEMBA (Kocmi and Fe-dermann, 2023), FActScore (Min et al., 2023), PerSE (Wang et al., 2024)) asks an LLM J

(AI@Meta, 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Team, 2025; Team et al., 2026) to score a response given the user’s history: 

bAllm = { y ∈ Ω : J(y; H) ≥ τ }. (3) LLM judges are not tied to references, but they score a response holistically, so the boundary of 

bAllm is implicit in the judge and can be neither inspected nor audited. 

Constraint evaluation (e.g., ExPerT (Salemi et al., 2025), PREF (Fu et al., 2025)) describes 

A⋆ by explicit constraints, such as rubric criteria in PREF or content aspects in ExPerT. They check responses against a constraint set C and induce: 

bA(C) = { y ∈ Ω : y |= c∀c ∈ C } , (4) where y |= c denotes that y satisfies the constraint 

c; in practice, the conjunction is relaxed to a graded score. Since the constraints specify the required content of a response rather than the exact wording, this approach accepts a variety of valid responses, and evaluation results can be traced back to the underlying constraints. However, the reliability of constraint-based eval-uation depends on the quality of the constraints themselves; existing constraint-based evaluators of-ten lack mechanisms to review these constraints during use, which can lead to the false acceptance of invalid responses or the false rejection of valid ones due to unqualified constraints. 

3 Constraint Quality Analysis 

A constraint evaluator errs when bA(C) differs from 

A⋆ (Figure 1). The difference consists of two error kinds: 1). False rejection FR( C) (i.e., the rejection of valid responses, constituting over-constraint); 2). False Acceptance FA( C) (i.e., the acceptance of invalid responses, constituting under-constraint). 

FR( C) = A⋆ \ bA(C), FA( C) = bA(C) \ A ⋆. (5) 

3.1 Necessity and Sufficiency 

bA(C) is the intersection of all constraints, to reduce over-constraint and under-constraint problems, con-straints should possess these two properties: 

1. Individual necessity (no false rejections). 

FR( C) = ∅ holds if and only if every constraint is satisfied by every valid response: 

y |= c, ∀y ∈ A ⋆ and ∀c ∈ C . (6) A constraint that some valid response violates is 

unnecessary ; each unnecessary constraint adds its own false rejections. 

2. Joint sufficiency (no false acceptances). 

FA( C) = ∅ holds if and only if the constraints together admit only acceptable responses: 

bA(C) ⊆ A ⋆. (7) Unlike necessity, this property is for the whole set. A set that violates it is insufficient , because a necessary constraint that would exclude a falsely accepted response is missing .Necessity gives A⋆ ⊆ bA(C) and sufficiency the reverse inclusion, they together recover the accept-able set exactly. 

3.2 Constraint Quality Impact on Evaluation 

The constraint evaluators compute the satisfaction rate sC (y) and accept a response if sC (y) ≥ τ :

sC (y) = |{ c ∈ C : y |= c}| |C| . (8) Weighted criteria, as in PREF, follow an analogous continuous formulation. Let n = |C| and s =

sC (y). Modifying C by adding a constraint ( +c) or removing one ( −c) perturbs the score by: 

∆s− = s − I(y |= c)

n − 1 ∆s+ = I(y |= c) − sn + 1 (9) The direction and magnitude of this perturbation depend strictly on whether y satisfies c relative to its baseline score s. For a valid response y+ ∈ A ⋆

and an invalid response y− /∈ A ⋆, this establishes three theoretical links between constraint quality and evaluation error: 

1. Unnecessary constraints inflate false rejec-tions ( FR( C) ↑). When a constraint c violates in-dividual necessity (Eq. (6)), valid responses may violate it ( I(y+ |= c) = 0 ). Adding c penalizes the valid response by ∆s+ = −s/ (n + 1) ≤ 0.If s − ∆s+ < τ , this induces a false rejection. Conversely, pruning unnecessary constraints via qualification guarantees ∆s− = s/ (n − 1) ≥ 0,directly restoring valid responses above τ .

2. Insufficient sets inflate false acceptances (FA( C) ↑). If C violates joint sufficiency (Eq. (7)), a necessary constraint c⋆ that y− violates ( I(y− |=

c⋆) = 0 ) is omitted. The absence of c⋆ inflates the invalid response’s score by ∆s− = s/ (n − 1) ≥ 0.Omitting necessary constraint can decrease the threshold distance for invalid responses, leading to false acceptances.

4 The ConQual-Audit Framework 

A low-quality constraint set can lead to increased false rejections and false acceptances. Therefore, it is crucial to audit constraints before constraint validation. ConQual-Audit is proposed to evalu-ate LLM personalization under audited constraints. As shown in ALG. 1, ConQual-Audit proceeds in three stages: (1) construction (§4.1) derives origi-nal constraints from user preference and task refer-ence; (2) qualification (§4.2) audits and improves necessity and sufficiency of the original constraints (§3.1); and (3) verification (§4.3) checks response against the audited constraints and computes evalu-ation result scores. 

4.1 Constraint Construction 

Each constraint is designed as a declarative propo-sition about the response, and y |= c holds when y

entails c. A required c is stated as a positive asser-tion (e.g., “The response recommends whole-food sources of protein.” ), which is entailed only if the response provides the required content. A prohib-ited c is stated as a negative assertion (e.g., “The response does not recommend protein powder.” ), which is entailed when the response omits or explic-itly rejects the corresponding content. Therefore, the constraints can be verified by natural language inference, during the constraint verification stage. As shown in ALG. 1, preference constraints and answer constraints are constructed by a generator 

G (line 1). Specifically, G extracts the user’s inter-ests from H, and derives one constraint for each thing the user prefers or avoids among the interests that apply to the query q. For the answer con-straint, G decomposes task-grounded reference y∗

into atomic requirements, covering what an valid answer must provide and what it must avoid.

5 Experimental Evaluation 

We evaluate ConQual-Audit by addressing three core research questions: 

RQ1 (Performance & Cost)(§5.2): Does ConQual-Audit minimize evaluation false rates more effectively than reference-based, prompt-based LLM judges, and constraint evaluation base-lines, and what is its computational trade-off? 

RQ2 (The Generalizability of Constraint Au-dit)(§5.3): Can ConQual’s qualification process be generalized as a model-agnostic filter to audit the constraints of other evaluators, and does it reduce their downstream decision errors? 

RQ3 (Case Study)(§5.4): How does ConQual-Audit reshape decision boundaries to reflect both user preferences and task-grounded references? 

5.1 Experimental Setup Benchmarks. We evaluate on two benchmarks: (1) 

LaMP (Salemi et al., 2024) (tasks 1, 2, and 4; 3,000 responses per task), testing semantic discrimination against surface form by evaluating 10 paraphrased positive answers, 10 negated answers, and 10 in-correct answers per user across 100 users; and (2) 

PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025) (200 responses across 100 users), testing explicit preference compliance via paired Qwen3-14B (Team, 2025) generations conditioned with and without the user’s preference. 

Baselines. We compare against nine baselines across three families: (1) reference-based met-rics on LaMP (BLEU-4 (Papineni et al., 2002), ROUGE-L (Lin, 2004), BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020), MoverScore (Zhao et al., 2019)); (2) 

LLM judges (GEMBA (Kocmi and Federmann, 2023), PerSE (Wang et al., 2024), FActScore (Min et al., 2023)); and (3) constraint evaluators (Ex-PerT (Salemi et al., 2025), PREF (Fu et al., 2025)). We also evaluate audited variants (ExPerT-Audit, PREF-Audit) via ConQual’s qualification stage (§4.2) to test constraint audit generalizability. 

Metrics and Implementation. Responses are accepted at threshold τ ≥ 0.5. We report False Re-jection Rate (FRR), False Acceptance Rate (FAR), their mean False Rate (FR), AUC, mean score mar-gins, number of evaluated constraints, and end-to-end token costs. To isolate methodological gains from model capability, all LLM judges and Con-Qual modules ( G, V , J) use Qwen3-8B (Team, 2025). 5.2 ConQual-Audit: Minimizing False Rate 

ConQual-Audit achieves the lowest overall false rate across both benchmarks. As shown in Ta-bles 1 and 2, ConQual-Audit reduces overall clas-sification error to FR = 0 .21 on both datasets—a 

16% reduction over the strongest baseline on LaMP (PREF: 0.25 ) and a 28% reduction on PrefEval (PerSE: 0.29 )—while dominating in AUC ( 0.90 

and 0.88 ). This improvement stems from calibrated error control. On LaMP, ConQual-Audit halves the false rejection rate of PREF (FRR = 13% vs. 26% )at a comparable false acceptance rate (FAR = 29% 

vs. 25% ), while evaluating roughly half as many constraints ( 2.97 vs. 5.43 ). On PrefEval, it reduces false acceptances(FAR = 28% vs. 45% for PerSE) without inflating false rejections (FRR = 15% vs. 

13% ). Crucially, ConQual-Audit widens the score margin between positive and negative responses to 0.57 on both benchmarks—nearly double any competing baseline ( ≤ 0.30 on LaMP, ≤ 0.32 on PrefEval). Because audited constraints isolate nec-essary and sufficient conditions, valid paraphrases systematically satisfy them while invalid responses directly violate them. 

Reference-based metrics confuses surface simi-larity with compliance. The acceptance sets bAref 

(Eq. (2)) exhibit severe failures: lexical metrics penalize benign stylistic rephrasing, falsely re-jecting 65% of valid answers on LaMP (BLEU-4 FRR = 0 .65 ), whereas semantic embedding met-rics mistake embedding proximity for alignment, accepting 100% of negated or incorrect responses (BERTScore FAR = 1 .00 , MoverScore 0.79 ). 

Prompt-based LLM judges misplace the deci-sion boundary. Unconstrained LLM judges relying on implicit boundaries bAllm (Eq. (3)) fail to cal-ibrate across task distributions. While GEMBA and PerSE show reasonable ranking capability on PrefEval (AUC 0.81 –0.82 ), they set overly permis-sive boundaries that accept 84% and 45% of non-compliant outputs (FAR). On LaMP, this behavior inverts into extreme conservatism, falsely reject-ing 47% and 62% of correct answers (FRR). De-composing responses into unweighted atomic facts (FActScore) fails entirely, hovering near chance on both benchmarks (AUC 0.48 –0.52 ). 

Constraint criteria violate necessity and suffi-ciency. Constraint baselines introduce high false rates by generating criteria that either over- or under-specify the solution space. ExPerT condi-tions constraints strictly on reference tokens; be-cause gold references are often abstract criteria (PrefEval) or brief sentences (LaMP), valid can-didate answers naturally omit arbitrary reference details. Its constraint sets thus violate necessity (Eq. (6)), falsely rejecting 92% (LaMP) and 97% 

(PrefEval) of valid outputs (FRR). Conversely, PREF constructs generic rubric criteria easily sat-isfied by standard generation quality regardless of preference adherence (mean negative score 0.81 ); its constraints violate sufficiency (Eq. (7)), accept-ing every negative response on PrefEval (FAR =1.00 ). Both evaluate more than twice as many con-straints as ConQual-Audit ( 5.11 –8.23 vs. 2.36 ), yet yield the highest false rates (FR ≥ 0.50 ). 

Inference cost and amortized verification. On PrefEval, ConQual-Audit consumes 12.6k tokens per response end-to-end, higher than single-prompt judges (GEMBA: 0.7k, PerSE: 1.9k), but one-third of atomic-decomposition methods (FActScore: 37.4k) and substantially less than auditing unvet-ted external criteria (ExPerT-Audit: 24.8k, PREF-Audit: 16.0k). Over 80% of this compute ( ∼ 10 .1ktokens) is front-loaded into one-time constraint syn-thesis and qualification per instruction. The audit systematically identifies and removes unnecessary or over-constraint constraints: it prunes 1.78 of ExPerT’s 8.23 aspects ( −19% )and 1.51 of PREF’s 5.11 criteria ( −29% ), while rarely adding new ones ( +0 .22 and +0 .05 , respec-tively). Operating on these audited constraint sets reduces overall classification error: the false rate (FR) drops from 0.50 to 0.49 for ExPerT and from 0.51 to 0.46 for PREF. For PREF, this improvement directly addresses its primary failure mode identi-fied in Table 2, reducing its false acceptance rate (FAR) down from 1.00 to 0.89 while keeping false rejections at a very low level (FRR: 0.02 → 0.03). 

Decouple constraint quality from evaluation ran-domness. To isolate the intrinsic value of con-straint pruning from downstream evaluator noise, we analyze a counterfactual setting where verifier decisions on unchanged constraints remain fixed. Under this controlled ablation, removing audited constraints strictly improves classification perfor-mance: PREF’s AUC increases from 0.82 to 0.84 (FR drops to 0.45), while ExPerT’s FR falls from 0.50 to 0.47 at a steady 0.53 AUC. In the end-to-end re-evaluation reported in Table 3, these gains are masked by test-retest instability in the underlying LLM judges: PREF’s verifier reproduces only 77% of its decisions on identical criteria across runs, introducing a baseline drift of ±0.03 AUC that ac-counts for the nominal drop to 0.78. Thus, while the audit effectively cleans the constraint space, end-to-end performance remains constrained by the variance of third-party scoring backends. 

Inference overhead and ConQual’s efficiency ad-vantage. The primary trade-off of the audit process is computational cost. Because counterexample generation and verification consume 5–6k tokens per constraint, overhead scales linearly with the size of the unvetted constraint set. Auditing Ex-PerT (8.7 aspects per response) and PREF (5.1 criteria per prompt) inflates end-to-end inference to 24.8k and 16.0k tokens per response, respec-tively—overhead that is only marginally offset by downstream scoring speedups (8%–25% fewer ver-ification tokens). By contrast, ConQual constructs and audits a compact set of only 3.9 global con-straints once per question. Consequently, ConQual-Audit achieves higher accuracy (AUC 0.88, FR 0.21) at lower total compute (12.6k tokens). The audit thus functions as an effective off-the-shelf re-finer for third-party criteria, but delivers its best cost-accuracy profile when integrated into Con-Qual’s unified pipeline. The user specifies a negative preference against ’physical sports ’in team-building activities, while the gold reference suggests ’non-physical alterna-tives’, such as ’problem-solving exercises’ or ’cre-ative collaborations’. 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we presented ConQual-Audit, an audit-based framework for evaluating LLM person-alization through semantic constraint construction, qualification, and verification. ConQual-Audit syn-thesizes candidate constraints grounded jointly in user preferences and task references, systemati-cally auditing them for individual necessity and joint sufficiency, pruning unnecessary constraints to minimize false rejections while repairing miss-ing constraints to prevent false acceptances. By evaluating candidate responses via the harmonic mean of constraint satisfaction and response attri-bution, ConQual-Audit establishes a calibrated de-cision boundary that resolves both over- and under-constrained failure modes. Empirical evaluations across PrefEval and LaMP show that ConQual-Audit achieves state-of-the-art performance, attaining top AUC scores ( 0.88 and 

0.90 ) and the lowest overall false rate (FR = 0 .21 

on both datasets) while operating on the most com-pact constraint sets ( 2.36 and 2.97 per query). Al-though constraint qualification requires an upfront token investment, this cost readily amortizes dur-ing runtime verification across multiple candidate generations. Furthermore, our case study demon-strates that qualification structurally realigns the decision boundary: pruning unqualified constraint details prevents auxiliary criteria from diluting core constraints and triggering false acceptances. Finally, our findings confirm that constraint qual-ification is model-agnostic and portable: applying the audit to existing evaluators (ExPerT and PREF) systematically refines their constraint sets and con-sistently lowers downstream false rates. These re-sults demonstrate that unvetted constraint extrac-tion routinely dilutes explicit user preferences with superficial task artifacts, establishing systematic constraint auditing as a principled foundation for reliable personalization evaluation.

AI tools were used solely for language refinement. All scientific contributions are authors’ original work. This research is supported by the Technology Innovation Institute, Abu Dhabi, UAE. Limitations 

ConQual-Audit depends on the models that play the generator, the verifier and the judge. In our experiments a single Qwen3 model fills all three roles, so their errors are correlated. Qualification is also costly. It raises the cost per PrefEval re-sponse from 3.4k to 12.6k tokens end to end, and ConQual-Audit has the highest online time of the evaluators we compare (27 s per response). The framework needs a reference answer and an ex-plicit preference to construct constraints, so it does not apply to open-ended requests that lack them. It scores a response by the share of satisfied con-straints, all weighted equally, at a fixed threshold. Finally, our evaluation covers English benchmarks with constructed positive and negative responses. Results may differ for other domains, languages and response distributions.
