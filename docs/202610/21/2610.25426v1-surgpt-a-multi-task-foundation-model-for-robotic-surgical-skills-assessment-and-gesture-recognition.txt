# SurGPT: A Multi-Task Foundation Model for Robotic Surgical Skills Assessment and Gesture Recognition 

## Parham M. Kebria, Saeid Nahavandi, Mehrdad Saif, and Fernando Bello 

Abstract — The assessment of surgical skills in robotic surgery training has traditionally relied on subjective evaluations by human experts, creating bottlenecks in surgical education and limiting scalability. We present a novel foundation model that automatically analyzes robotic surgical videos to provide comprehensive skill assessment and gesture understanding. Combining a visual encoder with a transformer architecture, the model performs three key tasks simultaneously: 1) surgical task classification (knot tying, needle passing, suturing), 2) skill level assessment on a standardized scale, and 3) natural language generation of surgical gesture descriptions. The model processes video sequences from the da Vinci surgical robot and generates human-readable text descriptions of surgical actions, similar to how language models generate text from prompts, using a multi-task learning framework with adap-tive uncertainty weighting to optimize performance across all objectives. Our transformer-based architecture incorporates positional encoding for temporal understanding and attention mechanisms for spatial-temporal feature integration. Evaluated on the da Vinci Skills Simulator dataset, the proposed frame-work demonstrates state-of-the-art performance in surgical task recognition and provides interpretable skill assessments that correlate with expert evaluations, with the model’s ability to generate natural language descriptions of surgical gestures opening new possibilities for automated surgical education, real-time feedback systems, and objective skill assessment in surgical training and certification programs. 

Index Terms — Foundation Models, Multi-Task Learning, Robotic Surgery, Surgical Skills Assessment, Transformer Ar-chitecture, Video Understanding. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The emergence of robotic systems in clinical and healthcare practices makes them a critical element in a variety of medical procedures [1]–[3]. Therefore, surgeons and clinicians need to learn, adapt, and practice the new technology. Employing all their skills, knowledge, and experience in this new schema of practice becomes a challenge, considering their limited time for training. This phenomenon requires a methodology to properly evaluate surgical skills in robotic practices [4]. Surgical skill assessment in robotic surgery training has traditionally relied on subjective evaluations by human experts, creating bottlenecks in education and restricting scalability [5]. Recent advances in deep learning have significantly ex-panded the scope of surgical scene understanding, shifting from handcrafted features and task-specific models toward end-to-end learning frameworks. In particular, transformer-based architectures have emerged as a powerful paradigm for modeling long-range temporal dependencies and complex spatiotemporal interactions in surgical videos [8]. By leveraging self-attention mechanisms, transformers can capture both local motion patterns and global procedural context, enabling amore holistic understanding of surgical scenes compared to traditional sequential models. The foundation model paradigm enables transfer learning across different surgical procedures and institutions, potentially revolutionizing surgical education through scalable, objective, and consistent skill evaluation [9]. Toward this paradigm, a wide range of techniques have been investigated to utilize expert human demonstrations to provide data-driven models with the vast amount of knowledge and context of the target procedures [10]–[12]. Surgical scene understanding introduces advanced machine learning and deep learning techniques used in image processing in the context of surgical procedures. This includes instrument detection, instrument-tissue segmentation, instrument tracking, instrument pose estimation, and tissue classification, to name a few [13]. Foundation models have shown promising performance in precision and effectiveness in the analysis of medical images and anatomical information [14]. Despite these advances, there are still drawbacks and chal-lenges in the deployment of foundation models in clinical applications that have not yet been investigated. Being large in size with the corresponding large number of parameters, foundation models are extremely data-thirsty. However, the privacy of healthcare data is a major concern, which limits the accessibility of large enough medical datasets for training such models [15]. On the other hand, the lack of interpretability and explainability in these deep models poses significant challenges to their adoption in clinically critical applications [16]. Still, foundation models provide novel benefits in the field, such as integrating multi-modal data to obtain more comprehensive diagnosis decisions [17]. Specifically, extracting diagnostic infor-mation from clinical videos is one of the areas where foundation models demonstrate unmatched benefits [18]. Moreover, surgical videos are characterized by high vari-ability due to differences in anatomy, surgeon style, viewpoint, and occlusions, while annotated datasets remain limited and costly to obtain. Specifically, for clinical adoption, surgical scene understanding systems must provide interpretable and reliable outputs that align with expert reasoning. Transformer-based foundation models, particularly when coupled with interpretable outputs such as natural language descriptions, represent a promising direction for addressing these challenges and advancing automated, scalable, and clinically meaningful surgical intelligence systems. Existing surgical AI methods are often designed for single tasks, which limits transfer across related clinical objectives. They also provide limited inter-arXiv:2610.25426v1 [cs.RO] 18 Oct 2026 pretability and rarely use uncertainty as an adaptive learning signal, reducing trust and robustness. Motivated by prior limitations, we propose a unified multi-task foundation model for robotic surgical video analysis that jointly performs gesture recognition and real-time skill assessment, combining a visual encoder and transformer for shared spatiotemporal reasoning, improved interpretability, and adaptive uncertainty weighting for better generalization and reliability. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:  

> •

Automated surgical task recognition: We propose a unified framework that accurately identifies core robotic surgical tasks, including knot tying, needle passing, and suturing, directly from video sequences.  

> •

Objective surgical skill assessment: The model provides a standardized and scalable evaluation of surgical skill levels, producing objective proficiency scores that correlate strongly with expert assessments.  

> •

Interpretable gesture understanding via natural language generation: We introduce a novel capability for generating human-readable textual descriptions of fine-grained sur-gical gestures from video data, improving interpretability and supporting automated feedback and educational ap-plications. The model processes video sequences captured from the da Vinci surgical robot and produces human-readable tex-tual explanations of surgical actions, analogous to prompt-driven language generation in large language models. A multi-task learning strategy with adaptive uncertainty weighting is employed to balance and optimize performance across all objectives. The transformer architecture incorporates positional encoding to capture temporal dynamics and attention mecha-nisms for effective spatiotemporal feature integration. Extensive evaluation on the da Vinci Skills dataset [19], [20] demonstrates that the model achieves state-of-the-art performance in surgical task recognition, while providing interpretable skill assessments that closely correlate with expert evaluations. Moreover, its ability to generate natural language descriptions of surgical gestures introduces new opportunities for automated surgical education, real-time feedback, and objective skill assessment in robotic surgery training and certification programs. 

II. S URGICAL SCENE UNDERSTANDING 

Surgical scene understanding aims to computationally inter-pret the visual and temporal context of surgical procedures by recognizing tools, actions, phases, and skill-related patterns from intraoperative data. In robot-assisted and minimally invasive surgery, this problem is primarily addressed through the analysis of surgical videos and kinematic signals to enable context-aware assistance, objective skill assessment, and intel-ligent feedback systems [4]. Surgical motion can be viewed as being similar to human language because it is made up of small, meaningful actions that occur in a structured sequence. These actions follow implicit rules and constraints, just as words and sentences do in language. As a result, methods originally developed for modeling human language and speech can also be applied to the analysis of surgical motion. At Johns Hopkins University, the Language of Surgery project studies surgical activity as a form of complex and dexterous human motion. The project aims to build curated surgical motion datasets, establish protocols for secure data storage and sharing, and develop computational models to analyze surgical activity. These models are then used to support surgical training, objective skill assessment, and research on human–machine collaboration in surgery. Surgical activity data include multiple sources of information, such as surgical tool kinematics, endoscopic video, system event logs, and additional signals such as surgeon posture, speech, and manual annotations. Together, these data provide a com-prehensive representation of surgical behavior that supports systematic analysis and learning. A central challenge in surgical scene understanding is the lack of standardized, fine-grained annotations that capture both the temporal structure and skill-related aspects of surgical activity. The JIGSAWS (JHU–ISI Gesture and Skill Assessment Working Set [20]) dataset was introduced to address this gap and has since become a foundational benchmark for modeling and evaluating surgical scene understanding in robot-assisted surgery. 

A. JIGSAWS Dataset 

JIGSAWS consists of synchronized video and kinematic recordings collected from the da Vinci surgical system during the execution of three fundamental robotic surgery tasks: knot tying, needle passing, and suturing (Fig. 1). These tasks repre-sent core psychomotor skills essential in robotic surgery and are commonly incorporated into surgical training curricula. The dataset comprises multiple trials conducted by surgeons with varying levels of expertise, categorized as novice, intermediate, and expert, to facilitate a systematic analysis of both task execution and surgical skill proficiency. A defining characteristic of JIGSAWS is its fine-grained tem-poral annotation of surgical gestures, referred to as surgemes. Each trial is segmented into a sequence of elementary gestures that capture meaningful units of surgical motion and interac-tion. The gesture taxonomy includes eleven standardized classes, denoted as G1–G11, which describe recurrent actions across tasks: G1: Reach for needle with right hand G2: Position needle G3: Push needle through tissue G4: Transfer needle from right to left hand G5: Move to center with needle in left hand G6: Pull suture with left hand G7: Pull suture with right hand G8: Orient needle G9: Use right hand to help tighten suture G10: Use left hand to help tighten suture G11: Drop suture at end of task This gesture-level decomposition provides a structured rep-resentation of surgical activity, enabling models to learn the temporal organization and hierarchical composition of surgical motion. In addition to gesture annotations, JIGSAWS provides standardized skill scores based on expert evaluations, support-ing objective assessment of surgical proficiency and correlation analysis between automated predictions and human judgment.

Despite its impact, JIGSAWS exposes several open challenges in surgical scene understanding. The dataset is relatively small by modern deep learning standards, and its annotations focus primarily on discrete gesture labels and numeric skill scores, without explicit semantic descriptions of surgical intent or con-text. Moreover, many existing methods treat gesture recognition, task classification, and skill assessment as separate problems, resulting in fragmented pipelines that fail to capture global procedural structure and limit interpretability. 

B. Skill Scores in the JIGSAWS Dataset 

In JIGSAWS, surgical skill is quantified using expert-derived global rating scores, not gesture-level labels. These scores are provided per trial and are commonly used as ground truth for skill assessment and regression/classification tasks. Each surgical trial is evaluated by expert surgeons using a standardized Global Rating Scale (GRS) adapted for robotic surgery [19]. The GRS consists of the following six skill metrics:  

> •

Depth Perception: Ability to judge spatial relationships and accurately manipulate tools in 3D space.  

> •

Bimanual Dexterity: Effective and coordinated use of both hands during task execution.  

> •

Efficiency: Economy of motion and avoidance of unneces-sary or redundant movements.  

> •

Force Sensitivity: Appropriate application of force to tissue and instruments.  

> •

Robotic Control: Smoothness and precision of tool manip-ulation using the robotic interface.  

> •

Overall Skill: Holistic assessment of surgical proficiency. Each metric is scored on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, where 1 indicates poor performance, and 5 indicates expert-level performance. These scores are typically averaged across mul-tiple expert raters to reduce subjectivity and are provided as numerical labels that support skill regression (predicting con-tinuous scores), skill classification (e.g., novice vs. expert), and correlation analysis with automated assessment models. These properties make JIGSAWS both a benchmark and a motivating testbed for unified modeling approaches. Its hierarchical tem-poral annotations, multimodal structure, and standardized skill labels provide an ideal foundation for developing models that move beyond isolated predictions toward holistic surgical scene understanding. In the following section, we build upon these characteristics to introduce a transformer-based foundation model that jointly models spatiotemporal dynamics, surgical task structure, and skill-related information within a single end-to-end framework.

F. Training Strategy 

The model training follows a two-phase transfer learning protocol. Initially, the pre-trained CNN backbone parameters are frozen for 10 epochs to stabilize the transformer compo-nents, followed by end-to-end fine-tuning of the entire network. Optimization is performed using AdamW with a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5, weight decay of 1 × 10 −5, and cosine learning rate scheduling with 20-epoch warmup. Gradient clipping at norm 0.5 prevents training instability, while mixed precision training with GradScaler enhances computational efficiency. Data augmentation strategies include horizontal flipping (50% probability), brightness and contrast adjustments within 0.8-1.2 range, and temporal downsampling (every 4th frame) to improve model robustness and generalization. 

G. Dataset and Evaluation 1) Data Structure: The framework processes the da Vinci Skills Simulator dataset containing three surgical tasks: Knot Tying (fundamental suturing technique), Needle Passing (preci-sion manipulation task), and Suturing (complete suturing pro-cedure). Each video includes RGB video streams (30 FPS, down-sampled to 10 FPS), kinematic data from robotic instruments, skill level annotations (1-5 scale), and gesture descriptions for text generation. 

2) Evaluation Metrics: Performance evaluation employs task-specific metrics: accuracy, F1-macro, and F1-micro scores for task classification; Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Pearson correlation coefficient (R²), and classification accuracy for skill assessment; and BLEU-4 score, ROUGE-L, and semantic similarity metrics for text generation quality. 

H. Model Complexity and Efficiency 

The foundation model contains approximately 25M param-eters distributed across the video encoder (VGG-16: ∼14.7M), transformer backbone ( ∼8.5M), and multi-task heads ( ∼1.8M). The framework achieves real-time inference at >30 FPS on modern GPUs with input resolution of 224 × 224 pixels, maximum sequence length of 256 frames, vocabulary size of 2000 tokens, batch size of 16 sequences, and training for 100 epochs with early stopping (patience=15), enabling practical deployment in surgical training environments. This comprehensive foundation model architecture enables unified surgical scene understanding through joint learning of task classification, skill assessment, and natural language generation, providing a robust framework for objective surgical training evaluation.

V. C ONCLUSIONS 

This work presented a comprehensive transformer-based foundation model for surgical scene understanding that suc-cessfully integrates task classification, skill assessment, and natural language description in a unified multi-task framework, achieving strong performance across all evaluation metrics with an overall score of 92.1%, perfect task classification accuracy, reliable skill assessment with high correlation coefficients, and high-quality text generation. The framework’s primary limitations include its scope, being limited to three basic surgical tasks on a single robotic platform, which limits generalizability to diverse surgical procedures and environments. Future work should focus on expanding the framework to more complex procedures across multiple surgical platforms while incorpo-rating uncertainty-aware methods to improve trustworthiness and clinical applicability.
