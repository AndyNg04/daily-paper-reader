Computational biologists increasingly delegate substantial analyses to AI agents in long-horizon, multi-turn sessions, yet most benchmarks of agents’ bioinformatics capabilities pose a single short task with a single-shot prompt. We present OmicsBench, a long-horizon, multi-turn bioinformatics benchmark for AI agents. Each of its 97 tasks links dependent subtasks into a directed acyclic graph, for 662 underlying subtasks in all. Each subtask is posed as one user turn and graded deterministically against an answer key, with optional in-context feedback on incorrect answers. Across 12 models on three harnesses, the slowest agent averages 3.6 hours per task, and no combination comes near saturating the benchmark, with mean scores of 0.43 to 0.57 out of a possible 1 without in-context feedback and 0.54 to 0.67 with it. Multi-turn evaluation also uncovers behavior that single-shot prompts cannot, as agents score lower on subtasks that depend on earlier subtasks, and in-context feedback helps agents recover some of that loss. Because deterministic scoring gives no qualitative account of how an agent failed, we also introduce an unsupervised failure mode discovery pipeline that describes, embeds, and clusters agents’ failed attempts to surface emergent failure modes. Applied to OmicsBench, this pipeline exposes qualitative shortcomings in how agents carry out bioinformatics analyses, ranging from agents applying their own statistical filters in place of the specified ones to agents writing whole answers from memory.

# 1 Introduction 

AI agents are increasingly capable of carrying out computational biology tasks that require tool use, code execution, and reasoning over biological datasets (Alber et al., 2026; Anthropic, 2026; Nair et al., 2026). Increasingly, computational biologists put these agents to work on long-horizon problems, in which raw data pass through many interdependent steps on the way to a scientific result, each building on the outputs of earlier ones. Such dependencies form a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of analysis steps, the abstraction bioinformaticians already use to define pipelines in workflow systems, such as Snakemake and Nextflow (Di Tommaso et al., 2017; Köster & Rahmann, 2012). Typically, scientists remain in the loop, delegating a substantial analysis, reviewing what comes back and requesting revisions, and only then delegating the next. Most bioinformatics benchmarks, by contrast, pose the whole analysis as a single prompt describing the desired output and grade only what the agent returns. Such benchmarks therefore rarely measure how an agent performs over a realistic workflow, one dependent subtask at a time. To address this gap, we present OmicsBench, a long-horizon, multi-turn benchmark of AI agents’ computational biology capabilities (Figure 1). Each of the 97 tasks in OmicsBench is a DAG of subtasks, 662 in all, that together make up a research-study-scale workflow, with each subtask graded against an answer key and each edge letting a subtask build on the results of earlier ones. Every task is built on a real biological dataset, and its subtasks carry that dataset from raw data through to a scientific result. The tasks are long-horizon, requiring agents to work for hours, with per-agent averages reaching 3.6 hours, 350 tool calls, and 110 million tokens per task. OmicsBench poses each subtask as one user turn of a single session, either with no feedback

Our contributions are: • A long-horizon, multi-turn evaluation framework for bioinformatics. OmicsBench poses a research-study-scale analysis as a DAG of dependent subtasks. To our knowledge, it is the first bioinformatics benchmark to pose subtasks as the turns of one session and to give in-context feedback. The framework is domain-agnostic, so any research study that is expressible as a DAG of deterministically gradable steps can be implemented as an OmicsBench task, and the benchmark scales to DAGs of arbitrary length and complexity. • A benchmark of 97 research-study-scale tasks and 662 subtasks, and an evaluation of 12 frontier models on three harnesses. We release the runner, all graders, and three tasks with their datasets, while the remaining 94 tasks form a private test set. No model/harness combination comes close to saturating the benchmark. The pipeline describes, embeds, and clusters failed attempts. It surfaces 12 emergent, bioinformatics-specific failure modes beyond those of a priori annotation, ranging from agents applying their own selection criteria to agents fabricating answers from memory. These modes identify concrete behaviors for future models to improve upon and provide valuable post-training signals. 

# 2 Related work 

OmicsBench sits between two lines of prior work, short-horizon benchmarks that ask for one small piece of analysis and long-horizon benchmarks that ask for work on the scale of a whole research study, and OmicsBench combines the scale of the latter with the granularity of the former. 

## 2.1 Short-horizon bioinformatics benchmarks 

These benchmarks evaluate self-contained pieces of work whose results are not carried into later requests. Some, including LAB-Bench (Laurent et al., 2024), LABBench2 (Laurent et al., 2026), Genome-Bench (Yin et al., 2025), BioProBench (Liu et al., 2026), BioCoder (Tang et al., 2024), BioDSBench (Wang et al., 2025), and CellVoyager’s CellBench (Alber et al., 2026), test capabilities such as biological knowledge, protocol reasoning, analysis planning, and code generation, without an agent working with a supplied dataset in a coding environment. Others ask an agent to analyze data in a coding environment and answer a question in one request. These include GenoTEX (Liu et al., 2025), BAISBench (Luo et al., 2026), BiomniBench-DA (Qu et al., 2026), CompBioBench (Nair et al., 2026), BioMysteryBench (Anthropic, 2026), DrugDiscoveryBench (Akyürek et al., 2026), and LatchBio’s SpatialBench (Workman et al., 2026b), scBench (Workman et al., 2026a), EpiBench (Muralidharan et al., 2026), VariantBench (Bhowmick et al., 2026), TxBench-PP (Le et al., 2026), TxBench-Antibody Discovery (Ramasamy et al., 2026), and TxBench-Oligonucleotide Discovery (Jacko et al., 2026). Because each item ends after one request, these benchmarks do not measure whether an agent can carry intermediate results through a research-study-scale sequence of dependent subtasks. 

## 2.2 Long-horizon bioinformatics benchmarks 

These benchmarks evaluate research-study-scale analyses, but they present the entire objective in a single request. BixBench (Mitchener et al., 2025), BixBench3 (Koch et al., 2026), GeneBench (Li & Ho, 2026), GeneBench-Pro (Li et al., 2026), BioAgent Bench (Fa et al., 2026), BioML-bench (Miller et al., 2025), SpatialBench-Long (Diks et al., 2026a), scBench-Long (Diks et al., 2026b), and the life-science tasks in 

> 2AfterQuery

Terminal-Bench-Science (Terminal-Bench-Science Team, 2026) give an agent data and a research objective, allow it to work without further instruction, and grade its final output against a published result, a reproduced or deterministic ground truth, or an expert rubric. Some of these benchmarks already structure their tasks around dependent steps, such as BixBench3’s artifact dependency graph and the cascaded decision points in GeneBench and GeneBench-Pro. Those dependent steps nevertheless remain inside a single prompt, so the benchmarks neither present successive subtasks as turns in one session nor provide in-context feedback on one step before the next depends on it. Separately, benchmarks that characterize agent failures generally assign attempts to predefined categories, leaving emergent failure modes unobserved. 

# 3 OmicsBench 

## 3.1 OmicsBench formulation 

Each OmicsBench task T = ( P, D, G ) is composed of a task prompt P , a biological dataset D, and a task DAG G = ( V, E ), in which each node v ∈ V is a subtask and each edge e = ( u, v ) ∈ E means subtask v

depends on the answer to subtask u (Figure 1A). Each subtask v = ( vp, v e) consists of a subtask prompt vp

and an evaluation set ve (Table B1). The evaluation set holds one or more deterministic graders gi, each paired with an answer key yi, so that ve = {(g1, y 1), (g2, y 2), . . . }. An agent answers the subtask prompt by writing an answer file av . Each grader in ve is assigned one field of the answer file av and scores that field against its answer key, giving one score per grader, gi(av , y i) ∈ [0 , 1] . A subtask’s score sv is the mean of these grader scores, and the task score S is the mean of all subtask scores,

To evaluate an agent on a task, we open the session with the benchmark system prompt and the task prompt, which together describe the problem and the dataset (Appendix C.1), and the task DAG is then unrolled in dependency order, with every subtask prompt becoming a user turn in that same continuous session (Appendix C.2). Because the session is continuous, the agent’s previous answer files stay on hand in the execution container, and its reasoning for those answers stays in its context. A turn ends when the agent stops working or when a time limit tmax is reached, whichever comes first, and whatever answer file is on disk at that moment is the one graded. In single-pass mode, the agent receives no feedback on its answers, and when it stops working, the next subtask prompt follows (Figure 1B). In gated mode, an answer scoring below a gate threshold τ is returned to the agent as a failure, and it is re-prompted to fix its work (Appendix C.3) until an answer scores at or above τ , or it reaches an attempt limit θ (Figure 1C, Table B2). In either mode, a subtask’s recorded score is that of its best attempt, and when the best attempt was not the last, the workspace is restored to it before the next subtask is posed (Appendix C.2). Single-pass mode measures what an agent can produce unaided, and gated mode measures how much in-context feedback allows it to recover its score (Figure 2B). 

## 3.2 Benchmark construction 

OmicsBench tasks are built from published papers and their accompanying biological datasets by a pipeline of agents and of human experts with graduate-level computational biology training (Figure 2A). Human experts first curate papers, whose reported results serve as ground truth, and their accompanying biological datasets. A writer agent then uses the papers and datasets to draft OmicsBench tasks, as in BixBench3 (Koch et al., 2026). A reviewer agent confirms that the tasks are supported by their papers and free of data leakage, or sends them back to the writer, rejecting any task that is unfixable or still flawed after three rounds of revision. Automated quality checks confirm that each task is in valid OmicsBench format, and human experts then review and edit each task, or reject it. Three of the 97 tasks are publicly released, and the remaining 94 are held back as a private test set to keep the benchmark useful for evaluating future models (Appendix E). The task datasets span a wide range of data modalities, with transcriptomic assays in 66 tasks, proteomic assays in 29, and epigenomic assays in 9, with 21 tasks drawing on more than one modality (Figure 3A).

## 3.3 Grading 

Because biological results take varied forms and rarely reduce to single values compared for equality, OmicsBench uses biologically informed graders, each built for one kind of answer (Table F1). For example, two correct enrichment analyses may order their terms differently and report slightly different p-values, so the 

enrichment_result_set grader requires the terms to match, compares p-values on a log scale, and ignores order. Each of the other 57 graders likewise checks what is biologically meaningful in its kind of answer and 

4AfterQuery 

ignores the rest, with equivalent behavior for biological data types ranging from dose-response curves to genomic intervals to docking poses. Furthermore, many types of biological entities are referred to by multiple identifiers. For example, the gene CD274 is also known as PD-L1 and B7-H1, and a string match would mark any of the three valid identifiers wrong against an answer key that uses another. OmicsBench graders therefore match genes, organisms, metabolites, microRNAs, and antimicrobial resistance genes through a reference database for each kind of entity rather than by exact string matching (Table G1). 

## 3.4 Failure mode analysis 

We characterized failed attempts with both a priori failure mode annotation and an unsupervised failure mode discovery pipeline that surfaces emergent failure modes in agents’ biological reasoning (Figure 2B). For both, we drew a single sample of 150 of the 662 subtasks, stratified by turn position and by the kind of result the subtask asks for, and analyzed every attempt scoring below τ at those subtasks. We used only single-pass trajectories, so that every failed attempt is an agent’s first and only try at a subtask. All LLM judges were Opus 5 at temperature 0 (Appendix L). Our a priori vocabulary of 13 failure modes is composed of the ten defined in the BixBench3 study (Koch et al., 2026) as well as three we added for answer sets that are too broad, too strict, or selected on the wrong criterion (Table H1). For each failed attempt, an LLM judge wrote a short narrative of the approach the agent took and how that approach produced the submitted answer, and assigned as many failure modes from the vocabulary as the evidence supported (Appendix L.1). Repeated annotation passes of the LLM judge agreed with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.76 (Appendix H). The unsupervised failure mode discovery pipeline that we built describes, embeds, and clusters failed attempts to group those that failed in the same way, surfacing emergent failure modes that a predefined set would miss. An LLM judge writes a short narrative of each failed attempt and rewrites it as a task-agnostic description of how the submitted answer diverged from the answer key (Appendix L.2). The pipeline then embeds the descriptions, reduces them with UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018), and clusters them with HDBSCAN (Campello et al., 2013), which groups only the points that lie in dense regions and leaves the rest unassigned, so it does not force every attempt into a cluster. # 4 Experiments 

## 4.1 Setup 

For this evaluation, we set the gate threshold τ to 0.7, the attempt limit θ to 3, the time limit per turn tmax 

to 1800 s, and the number of runs per task in each mode k to 3 (Table B2). We evaluated 12 models on three harnesses, with each harness pinned to one version and with reasoning effort set to high wherever the model allowed it (Appendix J, Table J1). All 12 models ran on OpenCode, and Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol also ran on their native harnesses, Claude Code and Codex, for 14 model/harness combinations. Of the three pairs of harnesses, two share a single model each and the third shares none, so we cannot make any claims as to the effect of different harnesses on performance. Every run took place in its own Docker container with no network access (Appendix K).

We treat the 97 tasks as a sample from a larger population of bioinformatics tasks, so the uncertainty we report is how much a mean could move had a different 97 been drawn. Because runs of the same task are not independent draws from that population, we averaged each task’s three runs into one score before computing any statistic, as counting them separately would understate the uncertainty (Miller, 2024). Every 95% confidence interval is the mean plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, computed from the sample standard deviation of the 97 task scores.

## 4.5 Failure modes 

The a priori failure mode distribution shows that, across all model/harness combinations, agents mostly fail in how they carry out the analysis rather than by failing to deliver an answer (Table H1, Figure 7A). Set-selection failures, in which the agent submits far more or far fewer results than the subtask requires, are the most common in every combination, at 25 to 71% of assigned failure modes. Grok 4.3 alone departs from this pattern, with synthetic or placeholder output at 20% and writing no answer at 13% of its assigned failure modes, against at most 7% and 5% for any other combination. Where other agents filter their results poorly, Grok 4.3 more often fabricates them or gives up. The 12 emergent failure modes cover 42% of failed attempts and fall into five families of related mechanisms (Figure 7C, Section 3.4, Table I1), and most of the remaining 58% are one-off mechanisms too rare to form a cluster, with the rest in the three clusters we set aside (Appendix I). # 5 Discussion 

Implications for training. OmicsBench points to three behaviors worth targeting in training. First, agents need to base their answers on the criteria that tasks state rather than on their own interpretation of them (Section 4.5). Second, they need to stay grounded in the scientific context across a session, since every model/harness combination scores lower the further a subtask sits from the start of the session and from the original data, both after its first turn and on subtasks that build on its earlier answers (Section 4.4). Third, they need to make better use of in-context feedback, as three attempts still leave most subtasks short of the gate, with the first retry worth more than the second (Section 4.4). 

Limitations. The most important limitation is that gated mode is open to reward hacking by design. Any signal that an answer has failed lets an agent search for an answer that passes rather than correct the analysis behind it, and this holds for every kind of feedback on every kind of subtask. We saw it directly on closed-choice subtasks, where agents tended to exhaust the options by guessing, and so left those subtasks out of the session and retry analyses (Section 4.4), but subtler forms are possible on any subtask, such as loosening a threshold until a gene list passes or resubmitting small variants of a failing answer. The gated gains are therefore an upper bound on how much in-context feedback helps agents recover, and the share of those gains owed to reward hacking is unquantified in this study. Furthermore, with 97 tasks across 11 biological domains and 18 subtask analysis types, each domain or analysis type holds too few tasks to compare models within it (Figure 3B, Figure 3C). Only Opus 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol ran on more than one harness, so the effect of the harness cannot be separated from that of the model. Lastly, the LLM judge was tested for reproducibility but not against human annotators. 

Future work. The clearest next step for long-horizon, multi-turn evaluation is a simulated user that answers an agent’s questions and revises its requests as a scientist would. Such a user, however, would have to be held to accurate replies, and it would cost the evaluation its determinism. Equally as pressing is measuring and limiting reward hacking under feedback, for example by comparing gated gains against retries that receive no feedback, by auditing retry transcripts for agents searching the answer space, and by designing feedback that points to the faulty step without revealing what a passing answer looks like. Additionally, more tasks in each domain and analysis type would allow models to be compared within them, and human annotation of failed attempts would test the LLM judge against human judgment. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduced OmicsBench, 97 research-study-scale bioinformatics tasks whose 662 subtasks are posed to agents as the turns of one session per task, which lets it separate a subtask’s position in the session from its place in a dependency chain in a way that single-prompt benchmarks cannot. We release the runner and graders under the MIT license and three tasks under CC BY 4.0. Across 12 models on three harnesses, no combination comes close to saturating the benchmark, every combination scores lower after its first turn and on subtasks that build on its earlier answers, and gated retries recover only part of that loss. Both of our failure analyses point to the same shortcoming in how agents carry out bioinformatics analyses, namely that they follow their own habits rather than the subtask’s instructions, most often by choosing an answer set with a criterion of their own rather than the one the subtask states.

# B Notation and parameters 

We set τ = 0 .7 as a compromise between a threshold of 1, which would fail answers with a single extra or missing member, and a threshold at or below 0.5, which would pass answers that are mostly wrong. Setting 

θ = 3 bounds the cost of gated mode at 3 times that of single-pass mode and mirrors the review-and-revise cycle a scientist would tolerate before taking over. The time limit tmax = 1800 s exceeds the typical turn time by a wide margin, so a timeout reflects unproductive work rather than a tight budget. Three runs per task ( k = 3 ) is the smallest replicate design that lets us report run-to-run variance (Figure A2) while keeping the total at 8,148 sessions (97 tasks, 14 model/harness combinations, two modes, and three runs).

# H A priori failure mode annotation 

For each failed attempt, the annotating LLM judge received the subtask prompt, the grader configuration with the answer key, the submitted answer, the score and its sub-scores, and the agent’s transcript for that turn, with model and harness names removed. It could assign at most five failure modes to an attempt, each backed by a verbatim quote from the transcript. To measure the LLM judge’s reproducibility, we annotated a separate validation set of 100 failed attempts twice with the Opus 5 LLM judge, shuffling the attempts into different batches each time. With each pairing of an attempt with one of the 13 failure modes treated as a yes-or-no decision on whether the mode was assigned, the two passes agreed with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.76 over those decisions.

# I Unsupervised failure mode discovery 

The failure descriptions were embedded with openai/text-embedding-3-large , reduced to five dimensions with UMAP (cosine distance, 15 neighbors, minimum distance 0), and clustered with HDBSCAN using leaf cluster selection and a minimum cluster size of 60, which gave 15 clusters holding 54% of failed attempts. The naming LLM judge saw each cluster’s eight most central descriptions, with three descriptions from its boundary for contrast. We set three of these clusters aside because they captured no failure of the agents. Two captured tool outages (221 and 158 attempts), and the third merged attempts that used the whole time budget without writing an answer with attempts from a single task that took gene coordinates from the wrong genome version (137 attempts).
