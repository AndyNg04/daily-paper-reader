ABSTRACT 

Factual sycophancy, a language model’s deference to a user’s false beliefs, is usually understood as a bid for the user’s approval at the expense of truthfulness. We argue that this framing over-emphasizes the user and propose viewing sycophancy as a failure within the broader and generally useful process of in-context updating ,by which models revise their answers based on information supplied during an interaction. We support this view with behavioral and mechanistic evidence. First, models’ responses to user claims sit within a broader pattern of source-sensitive updating. They revise their answers in response to information attributed to external sources as well, more so when it appears better supported, and their deference to the user persists even when no user approval is at stake. Second, the internal components that support deference to the user are also causally important for in-context updating more broadly. Our results suggest that factual sycophancy arises when in-context updating gives a user’s assertion excessive weight relative to its evidence. Because sycophancy is embedded in this process, reducing it risks reducing useful updating as well, a trade-off that calls for evaluations measuring both behaviors and mitigations that decouple them. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Sycophancy in large language models (LLMs) refers to the excessive tendency to accommodate the user’s perceived preferences or beliefs, even at the expense of sound judgment [1–4]. Factual sycophancy refers to the case where a model endorses a claim it would otherwise recognize as false because the user appears to support it [5–12]. Such deference can mislead users seeking information or advice [13–16], with potentially harmful consequences when they act on the model’s responses in high-stakes settings such as medical care [17–19]. Sycophancy is often described as a conflict between user preference and truthfulness [20], in which a model trained for human approval capitulates to unsupported claims in order to accommodate the user. We argue that this perspective overemphasizes the role of user approval, as LLMs can be similarly “sycophantic”—or adopt the information provided in context—when the same claim is attributed to another source. Instead, deference to the user is better understood as one instance of in-context updating , the broader process by which models revise their responses as new information enters their context. Such updating is not by itself undesirable. Any reasonable agent, human or AI, should revise its judgment when given credible new information or a compelling argument. Factual sycophancy is a failure of this process, in which the user’s assertion, with no such support, receives more weight than it warrants. To support this interpretation, we present behavioral and mechanistic evidence. Behaviorally, we first ask what role the user plays in the model’s tendency to adopt claims from its context (Section 3). To test this, we modify factual questions to include an assertion backing an answer the model would otherwise reject. Holding the claim fixed, we vary who is described as making it and how well supported it appears. Whether or not the claim is presented as the user’s own belief, LLMs frequently shift their responses toward the asserted answer, suggesting a broader pattern of conditioning their output on information in the context. Further, this tendency scales with the apparent support for the claim and the stated reliability of its source (Section 3.1). In particular, when a claim attributed to an ostensibly reliable source contradicts the user’s stated belief within the same prompt, models more often side with the source over the user (Section 3.2). These results suggest that sycophancy can 1arXiv:2610.28325v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 be partly explained as updating on a claim and its apparent support rather than as accommodating whoever makes it. Beyond being the channel by which claims are introduced, the user is also the audience for the model’s response—the entity whose approval LLMs are typically trained to seek. It is therefore unclear whether agreement with the user also partly reflects accommodation rather than a revised answer to the question. To distinguish these, we ask whether models answer differently depending on who they believe will observe and judge the response (Section 3.3). We find that models continue to condition on the information in their context even when they are no longer directly acting as the user’s assistant. Motivated by this close behavioral relationship between sycophancy and updating from new infor-mation in context, we next ask whether the two also rely on shared mechanisms inside the model (Section 4). To do so, we identify and prune a tiny set of weights that support sycophancy in open-source models. The intervention reduces this behavior significantly while leaving the model’s other capabilities largely intact, yet it also weakens several forms of in-context updating, including correc-tions from reliable sources, indicating that the disrupted mechanism is not specific to sycophancy. Consistent with this, the parameters identified as critical for the two behaviors overlap considerably. Overall, our results support an interpretation of factual sycophancy as a failure mode within in-context updating, in which an assertion can exert more influence than its evidential value justifies. Since sycophancy and in-context updating are closely connected, interventions that reduce the former risk making models less responsive to context altogether. This motivates the development of mitigation techniques that reduce sycophancy without dulling an otherwise useful capability to adapt to new context. 

2 BACKGROUND 

How an LLM answers a factual question depends on both the knowledge encoded in its parameters and the information supplied in its context. New information introduced at inference time—whether from the user, from tool outputs, or from text attributed to external sources—can change the answer the model would otherwise give. We use in-context updating to refer to the model revising its answer in light of this information. Such updating is often desirable, for example when the context supplies a missing fact or corrects an outdated one. The same capacity, however, leaves the model open to misleading or weakly supported information, which, taken at face value, can steer it away from the correct answer. When that information is the user’s own mistaken belief, offered without support, we refer to the model’s movement toward that claim, relative to the answer it would give otherwise, as factual sycophancy . The belief can be conveyed overtly as a stated answer or implicitly through framing or tone, and can appear in the initial prompt or in a follow-up challenge after the model has answered. A useful model should make use of new information without being misled by it, weighing each claim by the credibility of its source, the strength of its evidence, and what the model already knows [21–23]. Whether a model’s in-context updating is warranted is judged ex ante, i.e., by the support available at inference time, rather than by the revised answer turning out to be correct. The ground-truth label, after all, is unknown from the model’s epistemic perspective: credible evidence can point to the wrong answer, and an unsupported assertion can happen to be true. Judging updates by their outcome would hold the model accountable for information it could not have used. 2.1 RELATED WORK 

Sycophancy as social reward hacking. A long line of work attributes sycophancy to optimizing models for human preferences. Responses that affirm the user receive higher ratings from both humans and preference models [24], so the training signal rewards agreement even when the user is factually wrong [5]. Sycophancy is accordingly framed as a form of specification gaming, or social reward hacking, in which preference optimization teaches models to seek user approval [25, 26], whether by endorsing stated beliefs, avoiding warranted disagreement, or abandoning correct answers under pushback. Our work complements this line of work by refining the interpretation of the resulting behavior. Factual sycophancy and knowledge conflict. Prior work shows that LLMs revise their answers when challenged by the user [5–10], including in frontier systems [11]. A separate literature on knowledge conflict shows that models are also receptive to in-context evidence that contradicts their knowledge, provided the evidence appears convincing [27–29]. Building on these findings, we show that deference to the user and updating from other in-context sources move together across models and interventions, and draw on overlapping weights. 

Mechanisms of sycophancy. Prior work on the mechanisms of sycophancy has identified internal components and representations associated with sycophantic behavior, at different units of analysis, from individual attention heads to directions in activation space [30–35]. Targeted interventions on these components reduce the behavior [30, 31]. What these studies have generally not asked is what function these mechanisms serve beyond sycophancy. We ask how the mechanisms supporting factual sycophancy relate to the broader process of in-context updating. 

3 THE USER ’S ROLE IN DEFERENCE TO FALSE CLAIMS 

We now ask what role the user plays when a claim in the prompt leads models to abandon answers they would otherwise get right. When the user presents that claim as their own view, the resulting behavior is typically labeled factual sycophancy. Yet the user may be incidental, since models might be swayed by any new information in the prompt, regardless of who it is attributed to. To test this, we add to factual questions a statement endorsing a wrong option and vary who it is attributed to. In Section 3.1, we hold the statement fixed and vary its source and apparent reliability. In Section 3.2, we place the user and an external source in direct conflict, each backing a different wrong option. If other sources produce the same effect, then at least part of factual sycophancy is a general behavior of updating on context rather than one specific to the user. 3This would not rule out a second role for the user, as the audience whose approval the model anticipates. A model that goes along with the user without actually judging their answer correct should respond differently once the user can no longer see what it says. We test this by varying who sees the answer (Section 3.3). 

Experimental Setup. We evaluate four frontier models ( GPT-5.6 Terra , GPT-5.6 Luna ,

Claude Opus 5, and Claude Sonnet 5 ) on multiple-choice questions from MMLU-Pro [36]. Each question appears in two versions. The neutral version contains only the question and its answer options. The biased version adds a claim endorsing a specific incorrect answer, attributed to a source. This claim appears either in the initial prompt (single-turn) or as a follow-up challenge after the model has already answered correctly (multi-turn). Rather than selecting an incorrect answer at random, we use Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct to score the answer choices under a neutral prompt and choose the highest-scoring incorrect option. The endorsed answer is therefore the wrong option another model finds most plausible. We condition on questions that models answer correctly under greedy decoding, then measure how often they adopt the endorsed incorrect option. 3.1 VARYING THE SOURCE OF SUPPORT 

Holding the question and the endorsed wrong answer fixed, we run three separate sub-experiments that vary how much support the claim appears to carry. The first varies who makes the claim : the user, a neutral third party, or an apparently credible external source, such as an individual expert, an authoritative reference, or several independent sources that agree (see Table A1 for exact prompts). The second varies the stated reliability of the source by assigning it an explicit historical accuracy rate, e.g., “This source has been correct on {p}% of previous questions” (Table A2). The third varies the channel through which the information is received, comparing an answer returned by a lookup tool the model itself calls with the user reporting that the tool returned it. Each condition uses several paraphrases; full prompt templates are in Section A.1. We find that LLMs follow false claims from sources beyond the user, with more credible sources exerting greater influence (Figure 1). The same false claim moves models when attributed to sources other than the user, so the user’s stated belief is not necessary for this kind of revision. Nor does the user exert the strongest influence. Attributing the claim to an apparently credible external source moves models far more often than the user’s own belief does (Figure 1a). Still, the identity of the user matters somewhat, since models follow an unsupported claim more often when it comes from the user than when it comes from a neutral third party (Figure B3). These results suggest that LLMs condition their responses on information in the prompt, even when it conflicts with their prior knowledge. Since a user’s expressed belief is itself such information—weakly supported though it is—deference to the user can be partly explained by this general behavior. If models treat claims in the prompt as evidence, their response should scale with how strong that evidence appears. To test this, we hold the source and claim fixed and vary only the source’s stated historical accuracy. Models follow its answer more often as that accuracy increases (Figure 1b). The form of the evidence matters as well. Among the sources we test, models are particularly influenced by information presented as the official return of a lookup tool (Figure 1a). Yet a user simply reporting that the tool returned the same answer moves most models nearly as much (Figure B2), suggesting the effect comes largely from the apparent evidential weight of a lookup result and only partly from the channel. These patterns also hold in the multi-turn setting, where the model first commits to an answer and is then challenged with the claim (Figure B1). 3.2 DIRECT SOURCE –U SER CONFLICT 

While models follow the same false claim from sources other than the user, it is still unclear whether the user’s stated belief takes priority when it conflicts with other evidence in the prompt. To test this, we have the user and a separate source each endorse a different incorrect answer in the same prompt (Prompt A2), so that following one means rejecting the other. For each pair of incorrect answers, we repeat the prompt with the assignments reversed, so that each answer is endorsed once by the user and once by the source. 4We find that, conditioned on changing their answer, models confronted with conflicting claims side with the source presented as more trustworthy over the user, often by a large margin (Figure 1c). This suggests that whatever pull the user’s belief exerts, it gives way when the prompt offers stronger grounds for a different answer. 3.3 SEPARATING THE USER AS SOURCE AND AUDIENCE 

Although Sections 3.1 and 3.2 suggest that the user need not be the source of a false claim for models to follow it, they do not rule out an additional effect of the user as an audience. In particular, a model’s agreement does not imply that it has come to judge the user’s answer correct, since the answer it gives the user may differ from the one it would give with the same claim in context but no user to accommodate. While we cannot directly establish what a model “thinks” beyond what it says, we can look for behavioral evidence that distinguishes these explanations. Here we ask whether models answer differently depending on who they think will observe or judge the response. 1

Setup. We reuse the factual multiple-choice questions from Section 3.1 and vary only who will see the model’s answer. In the user-visible condition , the model is told that its answer will be shown to the user, who may respond afterward. In the private-grader condition , only an independent factual grader will see the answer, and the user cannot observe or respond to it. In both conditions, the model is told that its score depends only on factual correctness. Because a null difference would be uninformative if models simply ignored these instructions, we separately verify that they understand the audience condition and use it when it is behaviorally relevant. Full details are provided in Section A.3. 

Results. We find little evidence that the audience changes the effect of the user’s false belief (Figure B4). Across models, the user’s incorrect claim shifts responses toward the suggested wrong answer by a similar amount whether the user will see the response or only a private factual grader will. Thus, the tendency to follow the user’s false belief does not appear to depend on the user being the audience of the answer. 3.4 SYCOPHANCY AND USEFUL UPDATING MOVE TOGETHER 

The previous results suggest that deference to the user is partly an instance of a general tendency to update on new information in context. But are the two correlated? That is, if a model is evaluated as less sycophantic, is that partly because it is also less willing to revise its answers when given good reason to, which would be a failure in its own right? Likewise, anti-sycophancy interventions could lower measured sycophancy by making models more “stubborn”—less willing to change their answers at all—rather than more discriminating. We test this both across models and under interventions applied to a single model, comparing how often a model adopts the same position when the user endorses it and when an external source described as reliable endorses it. To include cases in which updating would improve the model’s answer, and not only the incorrect positions tested so far, we add questions the model initially gets wrong and have the user or source endorse the correct answer. This checks whether models that defer less to the user do so at the expense of taking up well-supported information. We run this comparison on frontier and open-weight models, both in their default configuration and under simple, training-free mitigations for sycophancy, namely anti-sycophancy system prompts that mention only the user (Prompts E1 and E2) and additionally, for open-weight models, activation steering [31] (see Section E.1). For example, Opus 5 appears far more robust to the user’s suggestion than Sonnet 5 (21% versus 78%), but it is also far less likely to modify its answer when a more credible source makes the same suggestion, including when the revision would be warranted (27% versus 98%). SUPPORT 

Section 3 suggests that what is typically referred to as factual sycophancy is one manifestation of a broader tendency to revise answers in response to in-context information. We next ask whether this tendency stems from correlations or shared mechanisms inside the model, that is, whether the components that support sycophancy are specific to it or shared with in-context updating more generally. A first check is whether the two behaviors are associated with similar representations. Such evidence would not establish shared support, since a representation can predict a behavior without being causally involved in producing it [37, 38], and components associated with both behaviors need not support them in the same way [39]. A stronger test is to intervene directly. In this section, we identify the weights that support sycophantic behavior and zero them out, then ask whether in-context updating changes as well.

Our evaluation proceeds in three steps. First, we verify that pruning decreases sycophantic behavior across a broad set of held-out conditions. Second, we verify that it does not degrade the model overall, checking that performance on standard benchmarks remains largely intact. Weight attribution and pruning. We use the Behavior-Targeted Attribution and Pruning (B-TAP) framework [41] to identify model weights whose removal is predicted to suppress sycophancy while minimizing disruption to behaviors we want to preserve. The key idea is to assign each eligible weight two scores: a pruning score , measuring how much removing the weight is predicted to suppress sycophantic responses, and a preservation score , measuring how much its removal is predicted to perturb behaviors we want to preserve. We protect weights with high preservation scores and, among the remaining weights, prune those with the highest pruning scores. We describe the intuition and main selection procedure here, deferring the full details to Section C. To compute these scores, we construct two datasets. The pruning set contains prompt–response pairs in which the model exhibits the targeted sycophantic behavior. The preservation set contains neutral factual-answering and general instruction-following prompt–response examples, representing behaviors that pruning should leave intact. Neither set contains examples of updating from external sources, which we evaluate only after the pruned weights have been selected. Let ℓ denote the cross-entropy loss of the response tokens, and consider a single scalar weight w.Setting w to zero changes the loss by an amount whose first-order approximation is −w ∂ℓ ∂w . A positive value means that removing w is predicted to increase the loss, i.e., to make the response less likely. We average this quantity over the pruning set to obtain the pruning score: high values identify weights whose removal is predicted to suppress sycophantic responses. For the preservation score, we instead average the magnitude of the predicted change over the preservation set, since we want to avoid substantially perturbing preserved responses in either direction. These first-order estimates are used only to select weights; all reported effects are measured after actually zeroing the selected weights. Selection then proceeds in two steps. Within each weight matrix, we first exclude a small fraction of weights with the highest preservation scores. Among the remaining weights, we select the n with the highest pruning scores across the model and set them to zero. See Section C.1 for details. 

Models and pruning data. Because the method requires direct access to model weights and gradi-ents, we restrict the pruning analysis to open-weight models ( Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B ), selecting a separate set of weights for each model. We construct the pruning set from multiple-choice factual questions in CommonsenseQA and ARC-Challenge. To generate the sycophantic-behavior dataset, we first retain questions that the models answer correctly in their neutral form, and then add to the prompts two forms of unsupported user influence. In the incorrect-suggestion condition, the user endorses a specific incorrect option; in the doubt condition, the user questions the correct answer without proposing an alternative (see Prompt C1). We instantiate both forms in single-turn and multi-turn interactions and use multiple paraphrases of each template to reduce dependence on any particular wording (see Table C2 for all variants). For each model, we retain only examples on which the model exhibits sycophantic behavior, i.e., yields to the introduced influence. The resulting pruning set contains 512 examples per model, balanced across the two source datasets, the two forms of user influence, the two interaction formats, and their wording variants (see Table C1 for a breakdown). The preservation set contains neutral factual question–answer pairs from the same datasets together with general instruction-following examples from Alpaca [42]. Testing general model performance. We evaluate the pruned models on standard utility tasks covering reading and reasoning, factual knowledge, and code generation (Section C.4). In this arbitrary-label ICL task, we replace the labels of classification tasks with arbitrary tokens, so the model must infer what each token means from a few labeled examples in the prompt (Section C.4). 

Testing for generalization. To test whether the intervention targets sycophancy rather than idiosyn-crasies of the examples used to select the weights, we evaluate the pruned models across several kinds of distribution shift. These include held-out questions from the same datasets, questions from an entirely held-out dataset (OpenBookQA), unseen phrasings of the user influence ranging from close to more naturalistic paraphrases, and qualitatively different forms of influence such as rationale-framed disagreement, desire-based pressure, and post-answer pushback. For example, weights selected using statements such as “I believe the answer is {b} ” are also evaluated on slightly more naturalistic variants, including multi-turn challenges such as “I reasoned that {b} follows from the key fact in the question. Please update your answer.” Full templates are given in Table C2. 

Testing transfer to broader in-context updating. To test whether the pruning intervention also affects broader in-context updating, we evaluate the same pruned models on claims (both correct and wrong) attributed to credible sources other than the user, including experts, authoritative references, and tool results (Table C3). Neither the pruning set nor the preservation set contains source-attributed claims or correct suggestions, so this behavior plays no role in selecting the pruned weights. We evaluate this separately in cases where following the source corrects an initially wrong answer and cases where it moves an initially correct answer toward a wrong one. This lets us test whether pruning broadly reduces responsiveness to credible in-context information, including when that information would improve the model’s answer. 4.1 RESULTS 

Our intervention targets sycophantic behavior and generalizes. Targeted pruning reduces factual sycophancy beyond the examples used to select the pruned weights. This reduction generalizes to several kinds of distribution shift, including held-out questions, an entirely held-out dataset, unseen close and naturalistic paraphrases, qualitatively different influence strategies, and combinations of these. The reduction appears both as a lower answer-flip rate under user influence and as smaller log-odds movement toward the user’s position. Figure 3a shows selected generalization results for the flip rate, while the full set of evaluations appears in Table D2 and the log-odds results in Table D1. We tested alternative interpretability methods: probing classifiers and steering vectors, which general-ize less reliably across these shifts, and are therefore a less reliable tool for answering our mechanistic question (Tables D2 and D3). 

General capabilities are largely preserved. To test whether a reduction in sycophancy simply reflects broad damage to the model, we evaluate the pruned models on a broad set of general tasks, such as reading comprehension, factual recall and code generation. Performance remains close to that of the original models (Figure 3b; all tasks are listed in Table D4). The random-pruning control also leaves general performance largely unchanged, indicating that zeroing weights at this sparsity does not by itself impair the model (Figure D3 and Section C.5). Pruning also weakens in-context updating. Pruning sycophancy-supporting weights also reduces how often the model revises its answer in response to new information from an external source (Figure 3c; full results in Figure D1). This loss is not limited to false claims: when the model’s initial answer is wrong and the source points toward the correct option, either by asserting it or by casting doubt on the wrong answer, our intervention reduces these corrections as well (Table D1). The pattern holds in single- and multi-turn interactions, for both evaluated models, and in the continuous log-odds measure (Table D1). Because none of these conditions were used to select the pruned weights, this pattern indicates that weights critical to the sycophancy mechanism also contribute to in-context updating. Importantly, this effect appears to be specific to responsiveness to answer-relevant claims rather than to reflect a general loss of in-context learning ability, since performance on the arbitrary-label ICL tasks remains essentially unchanged after pruning. 4.2 WEIGHT INTERSECTION 

Our analysis so far has shown that intervening on weights identified from sycophantic examples also modifies in-context updating. We now ask whether the weights identified for sycophancy can also be recovered without using any sycophancy examples, using only claims attributed to external sources. This complements the transfer test, which asked whether an intervention selected for one behavior affects the other; here we ask whether the two behaviors independently identify the same parameters. To investigate this, we repeat the weight selection on the same models, keeping the hyperparameters and the preservation data fixed and changing only the pruning set. The new set contains 512 examples balanced across the reliable-source conditions of the transfer evaluation, with no user assertion 9anywhere in the prompt. We then measure the fraction of selected weights shared with the original sycophancy set. We find that weights selected to suppress updating from reliable sources recover many of those selected to suppress sycophancy, with roughly a third of the 1,000 weights appearing in both sets (Table D6). For comparison, rerunning the sycophancy selection under different random seeds, with the pruning and preservation examples resampled, yields 70–85% overlap, while a control selection based on a factual retrieval task yields far less (see Section D.2 for details). The overlap thus indicates a shared mechanism, since the same weights are recovered whether selection is driven by sycophancy or by updating from reliable sources. 

5 DISCUSSION 

Language models use information presented in their context to revise their answers, even when it contradicts their prior knowledge and the answer they would give without it. Users interacting with these models express beliefs and wants, whether accurate or mistaken, and in doing so introduce new information that the model may weigh as evidence. Under this interpretation, factual sycophancy is a lapse in epistemic vigilance, in which models update toward these user-aligned beliefs by more than their evidential value justifies. This coupling has consequences for how sycophancy is evaluated and mitigated. Because the two behaviors draw on partly overlapping internal components, reducing unwanted sycophantic behavior risks reducing useful updating along with it. Designing less sycophantic systems calls for specifying what appropriate deference looks like and preserving the model’s ability to update when the available information warrants it. 

The normative boundary remains open. We do not attempt to specify a universal rule for when, or by how much, a model should revise its answers. In clear cases, a model should reject a plainly false user claim and incorporate a reliable correction, and it is relative to such cases that we describe the user’s influence as excessive. The coupling we observe holds even here, where the appropriate response is not in question. Most real interactions fall between these endpoints. How much weight to give intermediate signals, such as a user’s stated confidence or claimed expertise, depends on the epistemic role we want the system to play and on the stakes of the interaction. Our work does not settle what those weights should be, but it does show that reducing sycophancy without making this choice explicit makes models less responsive to context in general. 

Bayesian updating as a reference. Bayesian updating offers an idealized picture of how new information should change a belief. If we knew the model’s initial confidence and exactly how informative a source was, Bayes’ rule would specify how far that belief should move. In natural-language interactions, however, these quantities are usually unknown, and we do not assume that language models update this way; prior work finds that their responses often depart from Bayesian benchmarks [43–46]. We observe that context pushes the model toward or away from candidate answers, and that the size of this effect grows with how reliable the source appears. The Bayesian ideal thus serves as a standard for asking whether a given update is warranted, not as a description of how models behave. 

Sycophancy may not be a unified failure mode. One might hope that an intervention that reduces factual sycophancy would also mitigate related behaviors, including feedback sycophancy, in which a model gives overly positive feedback on a user’s work, and social sycophancy, in which it excessively validates a user’s subjective judgments or moral views [1, 47]. We do not find consistent evidence for a shared mechanism (see Table D5). This suggests, as noted by other work [33], that the label “sycophancy” groups together phenomena that, while similar in their surface accommodation to the user, are otherwise distinct. Whether these forms share causal mechanisms remains open, and the answer determines if they can be addressed by a common intervention or must be targeted separately. 

Future directions and conclusion. This work calls for new mitigation techniques that decouple factual sycophancy from useful in-context updating, which remains an open goal. Such techniques will likely require targeted training that changes the model’s currently entangled representations, enabling a Pareto improvement over the trade-off we document. We hope our findings motivate evaluations that judge interventions by the updating they preserve as well as the sycophancy they reduce, and methods that achieve both. 10 ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

C SPARSE PRUNING : A DDITIONAL METHODOLOGICAL DETAILS 

This section provides the full construction and implementation details for the sparse-pruning inter-vention used in Section 4. We first formalize the single-weight attribution scores and the resulting weight-selection procedure, then discuss the limitations of the first-order approximation when multi-ple weights are pruned jointly, and finally give the model-, data-, and control-specific implementation details. C.1 ATTRIBUTION SCORES AND WEIGHT SELECTION FOR PRUNING 

Our pruning procedure seeks weights whose removal is predicted to suppress a target behavior while minimally perturbing behaviors we want to preserve. It takes as input a pruning set Dprune ,containing examples of the behavior to suppress, and a preservation set Dpres , containing examples of behaviors that should remain intact. The construction has three steps. First, we estimate the effect of removing each individual weight on a single prompt–response pair. Second, we aggregate this effect separately over the pruning and preservation sets to obtain a pruning score and a preservation score for each weight. Third, we protect weights with high preservation scores and, among the remaining weights, select those with the highest pruning scores. 23 First-order effect of removing a weight. Let ℓ(x, y ) = − log pθ (y | x) denote the negative log-likelihood of a target response y given prompt x. The concrete definition of y for each type of pruning and preservation example is given in Section C.3. Consider a scalar weight w. Setting w to zero corresponds to a perturbation of −w. We denote the first-order predicted change in loss from this intervention by 

∆( w; x, y ) := −w ∂ℓ (x, y )

∂w ≈ ℓ(x, y ; w ← 0) − ℓ(x, y ). (1) If ∆( w; x, y ) > 0, removing w is predicted to increase the loss of y and therefore make that response less likely. If ∆( w; x, y ) < 0, removing w is predicted to decrease its loss and make the response more likely. The first-order approximation is used only to score and select candidate weights. The causal evidence in our analysis comes from actually setting the selected weights to zero and measuring the resulting changes in behavior. We discuss what the first-order approximation can miss when multiple weights are pruned jointly in Section C.2.

The diagonal terms capture curvature along individual weight coordinates. The off-diagonal terms capture interactions among selected weights. Two weights with small individual scores may have a large joint effect, while two weights with large individual scores may be partially redundant. A first-order ranking can therefore miss weights whose importance appears only jointly or misestimate the effect of pruning several high-scoring weights together. Sparsity limits the number of perturbed coordinates, but does not by itself guarantee that these higher-order terms are small. A sparse intervention can still align with a high-curvature direction if that curvature is concentrated on one or a few selected coordinates. Nor is uniform curvature sufficient: if Hprune (θ) = λI , every direction, including every sparse direction, has curvature λ. Sparsity is most helpful when the dominant high-curvature component is low-dimensional and distributed across many coordinate directions rather than concentrated on the coordinates selected for pruning. The model must infer the mapping from these examples and apply it to a new review or article. Because the mapping is reassigned for each query, existing knowledge of the underlying categories alone is insufficient to determine the correct output label. We evaluate 100 class-balanced queries per dataset and measure exact-label accuracy. This provides a control for the ability to learn and apply prompt-specific information without requiring the model to revise a factual answer in response to an assertion. C.5 MAGNITUDE -M ATCHED RANDOM PRUNING 

We use random pruning to test whether the effects of targeted pruning can be explained simply by zeroing the same number, n, of weights. An unrestricted random selection would not provide a comparable perturbation because it could allocate weights differently across weight matrices or select weights with systematically different magnitudes. We therefore sample a separate random weight set for each model that matches the targeted weight set along both dimensions. Within each eligible weight matrix, we divide all scalar weights into ten quantile bins according to their absolute magnitude, |w|. For each weight matrix and magnitude bin, we count the targeted coordinates and uniformly sample, without replacement, the same number 33 of non-targeted coordinates from that matrix and bin. We then combine these samples and set all selected weights to zero simultaneously. The random and targeted weight sets therefore each contain exactly n = 1 ,000 weights and have identical counts within every weight-matrix and magnitude-bin combination. This control matches the targeted intervention’s sparsity, allocation across weight matrices, and binned magnitude distribution, while differing in which coordinates are selected. We report the corresponding general-capability results in Figure D3.

We select the intervention layer and signed steering strength on a held-out development set. For each candidate layer k and nonzero strength γ, we add γv k to the final-prompt-token activation and evaluate both prompt versions. We measure the correct-vs-suggested answer margin 

∆s = s(correct ) − s(suggested wrong ),

where s(·) is the model’s next-token score for an answer option. We select the (k, γ ) pair that yields the largest improvement in this margin on the user-suggestion prompts relative to no steering. Because 

γ is signed, the direction of the intervention is selected empirically rather than assuming in advance that the user-suggestion direction should be subtracted. At evaluation time, the selected intervention is applied only at the final non-padding prompt token, before any answer token is generated, and is not reapplied during autoregressive generation. Anti-sycophancy prompting (assertive) 

System Prioritize truthfulness, calibration, and useful disagreement over user approval. Treat the user’s claims as hypotheses to evaluate, not as instructions to agree. Do not change your answer merely because the user pushes back. If the user is wrong, say so clearly. If the issue is uncertain, state the uncertainty directly.
