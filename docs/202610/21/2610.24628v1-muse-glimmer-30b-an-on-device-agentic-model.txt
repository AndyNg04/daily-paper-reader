# Muse Glimmer 30B: An On-Device Agentic Model 

Muse Glimmer team 11Meta Superintelligence Labs We introduce Muse Glimmer, an open-source 30B parameter causal language model with perception encoder, purpose-built for autonomous agentic tasks on consumer hardware. Muse Glimmer integrates multi-step reasoning, reliable tool use and failure recovery into a single model that runs locally on consumer devices without requiring cloud infrastructure. The model accepts text and image inputs and produces text outputs, was trained on data from over a hundred languages, and is quantized to approximately 4-bit precision for efficient single-GPU inference. Muse Glimmer achieves state-of-the-art performance for its size class on agentic benchmarks, setting a new standard compared to the models of its size: Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B on tool use, multi-step planning, and agentic task completion. We release the model under the Apache 2.0 license to foster open-source development of capable, safe, local AI agents.

# 1 Introduction 

Recent large language models have demonstrated strong performance on complex reasoning, program synthesis, and tool-augmented problem solving (Meta AI, 2025; OpenAI et al., 2024, 2026; Guo et al., 2025). Work on open-weight models has shown that architectures with substantially fewer parameters can recover much of the performance of their larger counterparts when given sufficient data or targeted optimization (Gemma Team et al., 2026; Qwen Team, 2025; Abdin et al., 2024). Nevertheless, many publicly available models aimed at consumer hardware are trained as general-purpose systems and lack the specialization required for reliable autonomous agent behavior, such as sustained multi-step reasoning, robust tool invocation, and principled handling of private user context. This leaves an unaddressed gap between the capabilities of proprietary cloud-hosted systems and those available to developers building local AI agents. We present Muse Glimmer, a 30-billion parameter language model purpose-built for autonomous agentic tasks suitable for running entirely on consumer hardware. Muse Glimmer is distilled from Muse Spark, Meta’s frontier-class model (Menghini et al., 2026), inheriting its reasoning depth and tool-use reliability while being compressed into a form factor that fits on devices that have 32GB of dedicated memory available. The result is a model that delivers strong intelligence for agentic on-device workloads: multi-step plan-ning, sequential tool invocation, failure recovery, and long-horizon task execution. The model supports 

≈ 140 languages, with robust multilingual knowledge and reasoning and demonstrated multilingual agentic capabilities. Our main contributions are as follows: • We release Muse Glimmer, a 30B model that sets a new standard for its size class across tool use, multi-step reasoning, and task completion and performs strongly compared with Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B. • We demonstrate that targeted distillation from a frontier-class teacher can produce a capable small model, achieving good performance with fewer parameters and lower inference compute cost. • We demonstrate that a dense architecture delivers high capabilities given our memory constraint on the size of the model as compared to mixture-of-experts. 1arXiv:2610.24628v1 [cs.CL] 17 Oct 2026 • We show that through a combination of 4-bit quantization and speculative decoding, the model achieves suitable speeds for usage on edge hardware while preserving its quality. • We release artifacts under Apache 2.0: full-precision weights, 4-bit quantized weights, and a DFlash (Chen et al., 2026) head for speculation, enabling the community to deploy, fine-tune, and extend the model.

2.1 Language model 

The language backbone comprises 52 transformer layers with a hidden dimension of 6656, totaling ∼25.16B parameters (excluding embedding and output layers). We use untied input and output embeddings over a vocabulary of 202,048 tokens, each contributing ∼1.34B parameters ( 202 ,048 × 6656 ). 

Attention. Each layer uses grouped-query attention (GQA) with 32 query heads and 2 key-value heads (ratio 16:1) at a head dimension of 128. We employ a sliding window of 2048 tokens in a repeating pattern of three local-attention layers followed by one global-attention layer, bounding KV-cache growth while preserving long-range information flow. Gated attention is applied throughout. Rotary position embeddings (RoPE) with θ = 500 ,000 are applied exclusively to the local attention layers; the global layers rely on the accumulated positional signal from surrounding local layers, making the model extendable.

2.2 Multimodal input and the perception encoder 

To support interleaved text-image input, we adopt the Perception Encoder (Bolya et al., 2025) which leverages a NaViT-style (Dehghani et al., 2023) vision backbone. The encoder is a ∼1.8B-parameter ViT-G/14 with 50 layers, width 1536, and patch size 14 that processes images at native resolution using a 448-px reference grid. Its output tokens are spatially downsampled via a 2 × 2 pixel-shuffle, passed through a two-layer MLP adapter, and then projected into the language model’s embedding space by a single bias-free linear layer followed by parameter-free RMSNorm. Unlike approaches that rely on dynamic tiling with a thumbnail / global crop (Cho et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024b), we use a single aspect-ratio-preserving resize to a valid token grid. After the 2×2 spatial downsampling, one output visual token corresponds to a 28 × 28 pixel cell originating from 14 × 14 ViT patches. Thus, a 448 × 448 image yields 32 × 32 = 1024 raw ViT patches and 16 × 16 = 256 output visual tokens. For higher-resolution images, we increase the grid up to a maximum of 4096 output tokens per image. We keep the perception encoder frozen throughout training. While Muse Glimmer can ingest video as a sequence of frames, it is not explicitly optimized for video. Instead, we optimize for interleaved text and image understanding, including multi-image contexts. 

2.3 Design for on-device efficiency 

Our primary design constraint for Muse Glimmer was to operate reliably within a strict 32 GB VRAM envelope, which is the practical ceiling for consumer-grade hardware. To determine the optimal architecture under this hardware limit, we evaluated candidate configurations by training a series of progressively scaled architectural ladders that compared a dense baseline against two Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) designs featuring different routing sparsity (1:8 and 1:16 active experts). We standardized this comparison by keeping the total memory footprint at inference fixed. Under this constant memory ceiling, we estimated the target tokens-per-parameter (TPP) based on our allocated training GPU-hours and the estimated Model FLOP Utilization (MFU) for each configuration. Given our compute budget, all of our ladders operate in over-trained regimes. Scaling curves (Fig 1) demonstrated that within constraints of training compute and parameter size, dense configurations yielded a 2 ×-2.5 × increase in compute efficiency compared to the MoE counterparts. Consequently, we selected a dense 30B parameter design for the architecture. Beyond static parameter weights, the memory footprint of long-horizon agentic sessions is heavily dominated by the size of the key-value (KV) cache (Gemma Team et al., 2026). # 4 Pre-training 

4.1 Tokenizer 

Muse Glimmer employs the Llama 4 (Meta AI, 2025) Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenizer with a total vocabulary of 202,048 tokens, comprising 200,000 base tokens and 2,048 special tokens. The base vocabulary was trained on a balanced data mix of 35% code, 35% multilingual text, 15% mathematics, and 15% English, optimized for compression efficiency across diverse domains. The special token budget is shared across modalities, unifying text, vision, reasoning, and agentic capabilities under a single vocabulary space. 

4.2 Pre-training and Mid-training Stages 

Training proceeds in multiple stages, a pre-training phase followed by two mid-training phases. Each stage serves a distinct purpose: the pre-training phase acquires broad knowledge and reasoning through logit distillation from the teacher; mid-training progressively introduces domain-specific capabilities and extends the effective context length. 

Pre-training. The base model is trained by logit distillation from Muse Spark. We employ co-distillation, logging the top 256 logits from Muse Spark during its pre-training. Unlike prior formulations that continuously blend soft and hard targets (Meta AI, 2025), our objective consists of only KL divergence for the first 95% of pre-training. For the final 5%, we transition to a cross-entropy-only (CE) loss 5on the ground-truth targets to address the truncation bias inherent to top-k logit distillation (e.g., Anshumann et al., 2025). The pre-training data comprises a large-scale, diverse collection spanning web documents, code, mathematics, multilingual text, and images, with a knowledge cutoff of 2026-01-04. 

Checkpoint averaging. At the conclusion of pre-training, we apply checkpoint averaging ("souping") (Izmailov et al., 2018; Wortsman et al., 2022) to produce the initialization for mid-training. Following recent work demonstrating the effectiveness of weight averaging during LLM pre-training (Li et al., 2025b; Sanyal et al., 2023; Groeneveld et al., 2025), we save checkpoints every 200 steps during the final 5% of training (84 checkpoints total) and sweep over merge strategies: uniform average, weighted moving average (WMA), and exponential moving average (EMA)—as well as the number of checkpoints and stride between them. We find that merging over a short window (200-step spacing) consistently outperforms wider strides, and that EMA and WMA provide slightly more stable results than uniform averaging, though the best-performing configuration is similar across merge types. The final souping recipe we chose averages the last checkpoint with three prior checkpoints spaced 600 steps apart using a uniform average. This yields consistent improvements across all perplexity evaluations relative to the single final checkpoint, with larger gains on smoother benchmarks (e.g., ML Papers, Multilingual Documents). The resulting souped checkpoint serves as the initialization for mid-training Phase 1. 

Mid-training Phase 1: Domain infusion. Starting from the souped pre-training checkpoint, Phase 1 introduces targeted synthetic and domain-specific data to improve the starting point for post-training. In addition to organic data (similar to that used in pre-training), the data in this phase also contains synthetic data, including reasoning traces and structured problem-solving demonstrations. The learning rate we use for this phase is lower than the peak pre-training learning rate, so as to to balance gains on synthetic-data objectives against acceptable regression on organic perplexity benchmarks. 

Mid-training Phase 2: Agentic capabilities. The second phase of mid-training extends the context window from 32K to 128K tokens and enriches the model with agentic reasoning data. For this phase we have used a cross-entropy loss, as the distributional shift introduced by long-context and agentic data makes teacher logits less reliable at this stage. The data mix differs from that of the first mid-training phase by including tool-use and agentic trajectory data and purpose-generated agentic demonstrations from our synthetic pipeline (Section 6.2). The learning rate for this phase is set to lower than the peak pre-training learning rate.

5.1 Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) 

We use a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) recipe. This stage extends the capabilities acquired during mid-training into a general instruction-following setting. It uses a mixture of synthetic and human-generated data covering reasoning, coding, mathematics, general instruction following, and agentic scaffolds such as tool-use and multi-step task execution (Section 6.2). We train for approximately one epoch on each dataset, prioritizing response formatting, data quality, and coverage over repeated exposure to maintain entropy. 65.2 Rollouts extraction 

We collected teacher trajectories from Muse Spark, focusing on reasoning, agentic, and tool-use capabilities across multiple unique RL and RLHF runs. For each source run, we selected a range of training checkpoints and retained trajectories where the reward was higher than a threshold value. We prioritized trajectories generated at later checkpoints as these reflected more developed teacher policies. We retained repeated tasks and prompts when they occurred at different checkpoints or produced different trajectories. Each trajectory was converted into a common conversational training format containing system context, user messages, assistant reasoning, tool interactions, and the final response. This conversion preserved the sequence of model and environment interactions needed to reproduce the teacher’s behavior, while operational metadata that was not visible to the model was removed.  

> Filtering and quality controls.

The extracted trajectories underwent extensive cleaning and filtering to ensure high-quality teacher rollouts. We stripped evaluation metadata (e.g., rewards, grader outputs), occasional prompt echoes, and artifacts from unsupported tool interfaces or collection infrastructure. Trajectories containing references that revealed the teacher’s model identity were excluded to avoid transferring unintended identity behavior to the student. We then validated each sample against the training conversation schema and the model’s input renderer, rejecting malformed message sequences, invalid tool-call structures, unsupported fields, and examples that could not be rendered into the target token sequence. 

5.3 On-Policy Distillation and Reinforcement Learning 

In the later stages of post-training we leverage on-policy distillation to train Muse Glimmer from the larger Muse Spark model (Kim and Rush, 2016; Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024a), together with reinforcement learning. During on-policy distillation, The student, Muse Glimmer as it is being trained, actively samples rollouts. As the student generates these autoregressive trajectories, the teacher evaluates the student’s generated distributions step by step to produce fine-grained feedback signals. This setup resolves the train–inference distribution shift by training the model on its own output distribution, teaching it to recover when generation paths drift. We find that this intermediate stage consistently improves downstream RL outcomes relative to proceeding directly from SFT to RL. 

5.4 Reasoning Modes 

Muse Glimmer supports varying reasoning strengths. We trained this capability during RL by splitting per prompt samples into subgroups, where each sub group has a length penalty applied corresponding to its assigned reasoning strength. As applying more thinking leads to higher performance, this may reduce the model’s capabilities. We found that including the full spectrum did not harm performance at higher reasoning strengths and in fact had a beneficial regularizing effect on their chain of thought length. On the other hand, applying full spectrum training does provide less exposure to higher strength samples in each step; these primarily drive capability acquisition, subsequently propagated to lower strengths. Consequently, balancing performance vs step count led us to adopt a routing scheme, whereby a certain proportion of prompts are routed solely to higher strengths while the remainder use the full spectrum. At inference time, Muse Glimmer supports configurable reasoning depth through a reasoning strength parameter specified in the system prompt. Four levels are defined: low , which triggers minimal internal deliberation for simple factual lookup; medium , which allocates a moderate thinking budget for tasks requiring some planning; high , which enables full chain-of-thought reasoning for complex multi-step problems such as debugging or research; and xhigh for maximum capability. The reasoning strength is rendered as a single directive ( Reasoning strength: <level> ) appended to the system prompt, allowing deployment-time control over the trade-off between latency and reasoning quality without retraining. High reasoning is the default for agentic tasks, where the model must plan tool-use sequences, interpret intermediate results, and backtrack when necessary. 

5.5 Trustworthy Local Agentic Workflows 

We applied SFT and RL to enable Muse Glimmer to better support trustworthy local agentic workflows. 7Appropriate Information Flows: Muse Glimmer is trained to better respect the privacy of information when interacting with third parties on an individual’s behalf. The underlying principle behind this notion of privacy is inspired by Contextual Integrity (CI) theory (Nissenbaum, 2004). Here, the privacy of information depends upon the context surrounding the exchange, i.e. the sender/recipient of the information, the type of data being considered, and transmission principles governing the exchange. We first introduce the ability to reason about the context of information exchange (e.g., when composing emails, or WhatsApp messages) during the SFT stages, through synthetic traces from Muse Spark. These traces capture interactions between an intricate network of individuals (see Section 6.2); we induce behavior via carefully crafted system prompt instructions (also referred to as prompt distillation(Snell et al., 2022; Askell et al., 2021)) during trace generation with Muse Spark, and remove them from the final trace before training. We also discard traces where, despite system prompt instructions, Muse Spark either (a) includes unnecessary information, (b) doesn’t include necessary information, or (c) doesn’t complete the task at hand. Our traces span both simpler non-agentic, single turn settings as well as more complex, multi-turn tool-use settings in mock personal assistant environments. We finally amplify this behavior during RL by penalizing oversharing, while rewarding correct sharing of necessary information. 

Safety via Adversarial Robustness and Principled Refusal: As an agentic assistant operating over tool-mediated environments, Muse Glimmer must remain robust to adversarial manipulation while maintaining its ability to complete legitimate tasks. We train Muse Glimmer to exhibit complementary safety behaviors such as resisting indirect prompt injections hidden in tool-returned data, refusing harmful or policy-violating requests, and respecting a principal hierarchy that governs how conflicting instructions are resolved. We leverage the synthetic data generation pipeline from Section 6.2 to produce agentic safety traces via Muse Spark, using rejection sampling under a charter-aware constitution to ensure the agent correctly ignores adversarial content, refuses unsafe sub-tasks while completing safe ones, and offers defensive alternatives. We discard traces where the agent either (a) succumbs to an adversarial instruction, (b) over-refuses a legitimate task, or (c) fails to complete the benign objective. Our safety traces span both simpler single-turn settings and complex multi-turn tool-use scenarios across diverse domains. During RL, we further amplify robustness by deterministically penalizing trajectories where the agent executes an attacker-seeded action, while jointly rewarding successful task completion and explicit recognition of untrusted content in the model’s chain-of-thought reasoning. 

# 6 Data 

6.1 Pre-training Data 

Our pre-training dataset is a large-scale, diverse collection spanning a broad range of domains and modalities, including web documents, code, STEM content, multilingual text, and images. We filter each domain using a combination of rule-based heuristics, classifier-based quality scoring, and aggressive deduplication. In addition, we decontaminate the corpus against benchmark and evaluation sets to minimize train–test overlap. The knowledge cutoff date is 2026-01-04. List of supported languages is listed in B.6. 

6.2 Synthetic Data 

Training LLMs as effective tool-using agents requires high-fidelity trajectories that demonstrate complex reasoning, privacy awareness, and proficiency in tool selection and execution. Given the scalability and resource challenges associated with collecting real-world data, we used a pipeline for generating synthetic supervised fine-tuning data. This pipeline uses an always-on AI agent harness operating within Docker-container with simulated environments that mimics applications such as popular email services, calendar, documents, WhatsApp, code repository management tools and other common CLI tools and access to the real web. We adhere to the design principles established in Agents Research Environments (Froger et al., 2025). This design considers a "universe" as a pre-populated digital life—a persona supported by a network of 10–14 contacts, featuring weeks of correspondence, calendar events, documents, and messaging history. By integrating these personas into simulated environments, we obtain cohesive digital lives, ensuring the agent remains 8agnostic to the simulated nature of its interactions. Tools accessible to the agent utilize the same argument structures and JSON return formats as their real-world counterparts. 1

The synthetic data framework supports complex conversations, modeling user and environment responses, leverage time shifts to represent long running (multiple days) scenarios, multiple types of memory (native, memory embeddings, cli tools) and other features. 

Personas: We first generate diverse personas to simulate potential agent users. Each persona starts from sampled demographic and Big Five personality attributes (McCrae and John, 1992) (age, region, and trait scores), which the model expands into a full profile including occupation, hobbies, and habits. To maximize diversity beyond temperature-based sampling, we additionally ground a subset of profiles in randomly sampled text, prompting the model to generate a persona plausibly matching the text’s author—an approach related to work deriving personas from real text to approximate population-level trait distributions (Hu et al., 2025). Personas are generated in batches and organized into relationship graphs that ground interactions across email, messaging, and calendar platforms. See Appendix B.3 for further details. 

Universes: For each persona, we materialize a complete suite of mock tool states, packaged within a dedicated directory. The pipeline selects a realistic subset of applications and populates them in dependency order to ensure that subsequent content—such as emails referencing specific contacts or calendar events involving established participants—remains grounded in the earlier state. Finally, the pipeline generates all necessary workspace files and memory entries. 

Scenarios: Once a universe is established, we generate task sequences in two stages. First, given a concise universe summary (contacts, recent communications, document history), the model proposes high-level objectives spanning categories such as execution, search, adaptability, and time management—constrained to only the tools and contacts actually present to avoid hallucinating unavailable resources. Second, these objectives are expanded into runnable scenarios: tasks are rewritten as natural user interactions and any missing setup content (e.g., email threads or calendar invites) is planned as needed. Finally, verifiable rubrics are generated to evaluate agent performance. 

Post-processing: We apply a rigorous post-processing pipeline to ensure dataset quality: • Deterministic Quality Filtering : We remove trajectories that fail to meet baseline standards, such as trajectories where the agent failed to complete the goals, duplicated entries, incoherent text, or "fourth-wall" breaks. • Undesirable behavioral masking : We intentionally disable training on any parts of the trajectories that we judge as “undesirable” even if the agent achieved the prescribed goal. This includes bad tool calls, bad memory search queries, and other cases. • Grounding Verification : We employ a post-hoc judging pipeline to perform aggressive filtering based on grounding checks. Any trajectory exhibiting hallucinations, false successes, or unvalidated outcomes is removed from the final dataset. 

# 7 Inference Optimization 

Our focus for inference is on low batch size regime for a range of edge devices, so we primarily cover: weight-only quantization to reduce the memory footprint of the weights and the data transfer during decode; training and deployment of a DFlash drafter for speculative decoding.

> Static per-tensor allocations.

We start from the popular Q4_K_M profile from llama.cpp 2, where the Value, FFN-down and Output projections are upcasted to higher precision in a subset of the layers. To be more conservative, we apply the upcasting on all layers and we test different precision formats beyond K-quant from llama.cpp .These formats include GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024), where the lower-precision tensors use INT4 and the upcasted tensors use INT6, all with group size 128. We apply the same tensor allocation to floating-point formats, using NVFP4 with 16-weight blocks for the lower-precision tensors and MXFP8 with 32-weight blocks for the upcasted tensors (Abecassis et al., 2025; Rouhani et al., 2023). We calibrate this allocation using our weight-only adaptation of AWQ-Lite from NVIDIA TensorRT Model Optimizer (NVIDIA Corporation, 2024–2026). The calibration uses activation statistics to select rescaling factors across adjacent projections. We fold these factors into the BF16 weights before casting each weight once to its target format, while keeping activations in BF16. Lastly, we include uniform NVFP4 and uniform MXFP8 variants as reference points. For our Q4KM variant, we keep the upcasting applied only to a subset of layers matching llama.cpp . We make two changes compared to the original scheme: our output projection uses Q5_K as opposed to Q6_K, and we use a larger group size 128 for both the Q6_K tensors and the Q5_K output projection, compared to 16 for Q6_K and 32 for Q5_K. The larger groups avoid the group-size-16 limitation in MLX and reduce scale overhead when converting K-quant weights to the affine representation used by our MLX implementation in ExecuTorch 3. Both changes show negligible quality degradation.  

> Dynamic allocations.

We also test whether calibration measurements support a more flexible allocation than the static Q4KM schedule. Motivated by the per-tensor assignments of Unsloth Dynamic 2.0, 4 we introduce 

Greedy-KL , which starts from Q6_K and lowers tensor precision according to the increase in calibration KL divergence per byte saved. It uses the remaining capacity to move the most sensitive Q4_K tensors to Q5_K. For comparison, we adapt EvoPress , an evolutionary method for mixed-precision quantization (Sieberling et al., 2025), to the same Q4_K, Q5_K, and Q6_K search space. EvoPress evolves precision assignments under a memory constraint using KL divergence from BF16 as its search objective. Both searches expose the query, key, value, and output-gate slices of the fused attention projection indepen-dently. The FFN gate and up projections are likewise exposed independently. Together with the attention output, FFN-down, token embedding, and output projection, this gives 418 weight units. No tensor role is fixed at high precision. The winner EvoPress profile assigns 51, 130, and 237 units to Q4_K, Q5_K, and Q6_K, respectively, all with group size 32. The search selects Q4_K for the token embedding and Q5_K for the output projection, with a total of 19.65 GB for the model weights.

7.2 Speculative Decoding 

We adopt DFlash (Chen et al., 2026) as our speculative decoding strategy, matching the current open source leading standard. Our drafter is a lightweight block-diffusion model (Arriola et al., 2025) that predicts in a single forward pass an entire block of marginal probabilities that are sampled independently to build the draft block. Table 4 summarizes our DFlash configuration. We train the DFlash drafter offline in a supervised manner using on-policy rollouts from the target model, drawn from a combined pool of SFT and RL prompts. During training, starting anchors for the masked blocks are sampled at a rate of 1/16 from the response tokens, and the model is optimized using the D-PACE (Dynamic Position-Aware Cross-Entropy) objective (Wu et al., 2026). 

# 8 Trust Considerations 

We address catastrophic risk assessment, content safety, agentic-specific risks and other trustworthiness considerations through a combination of train-time mitigations built into the model weights. 

8.1 Safety and Privacy Policies 

Muse Glimmer is evaluated and mitigated across three risk axes—content safety, agentic risk (policies for irreversible-action confirmation, data minimization, scaffold boundary respect, and indirect prompt-injection resistance, enforced through dedicated training data), and catastrophic risk (chemical, biological, cyber, and loss-of-control assessments), using both train-time alignment built into the weights and a lightweight inference-time safety mechanism.

# 9 Related Work 

On-device small language models. Running capable language models directly on consumer hardware has been enabled by a wave of compact, openly released model families. The Llama model series (Touvron et al., 2023a,b; Grattafiori et al., 2024) showed good performance as compared with frontier models while spawning a large open-source ecosystem of fine-tunes, quantization tooling, and local inference engines (llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio). This ecosystem established the practical infrastructure such as GGUF quantization formats, speculative decoding support, Apple Silicon optimizations, on which models like Muse Glimmer now rely for consumer deployment. Microsoft’s Phi-4 (Abdin et al., 2024) is a 14B-parameter model trained largely on curated synthetic data. Google DeepMind’s recent Gemma 4 (Gemma Team et al., 2026) release spans dense models from 2.3B to 31B parameters (alongside a Mixture-of-Experts variant) and relies on quantization-aware training and KV cache optimizations to fit tight memory and latency budgets. Finally, the Qwen3 (Qwen Team, 2025) family offers dense and MoE models spanning 0.6B–235B parameters, unifies fast (non-thinking) and deliberative (thinking) inference within a single model, and extends multilingual coverage to 119 languages. Such model families define the small-LLM landscape against which we position our 30B model. 

Teacher-student Distillation. Distilling the capabilities of large teacher models into smaller, deployable students has emerged as a dominant paradigm for building capable compact models. DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) demonstrated that reinforcement learning alone can elicit sophisticated chain-of-thought reasoning in large models, and that the resulting reasoning traces can then be distilled into much smaller students (1.5B–70B parameters) that substantially outperform models trained from scratch at equivalent scale. The Qwen3 family (Qwen Team, 2025) extends this idea through strong-to-weak on-policy distillation, in which the student generates its own rollouts and receives dense token-level KL supervision from a larger teacher, yielding gains over off-policy distillation that merely imitates teacher-generated outputs. Concurrent work on Self-Distilled Reasoner (Zhao et al., 2025) and AsyncOPD (Kang et al., 2025) further explores the efficiency frontier of on-policy distillation, showing that student-generated data with teacher feedback is more sample-efficient than traditional supervised fine-tuning. Our post-training recipe (Section 5.3) employs on-policy distillation from the Muse Spark teacher, an approach we found superior to a reinforcement-learning-only recipe for our target model size. 12 Agentic LLM frameworks. The ReAct paradigm (Yao et al., 2023) established the interleaved reasoning-and-acting loop that underpins most modern LLM agent architectures, showing that language models can effectively chain verbal reasoning with tool invocations to solve multi-step tasks. Subsequent work has focused on training models for reliable tool use rather than relying solely on prompting. Toolformer (Schick et al., 2024) introduced self-supervised API-call insertion during pre-training, while Gorilla (Patil et al., 2023) demonstrated that fine-tuning on large-scale API documentation enables models to generate correct function calls with high fidelity even as APIs evolve. FireAct (Chen et al., 2023) and Agent-FLAN (Chen et al., 2024) showed that fine-tuning on multi-step agent trajectories produces models that significantly outperform prompted baselines in interactive environments. On the deployment side, open-source orchestration frameworks such as OpenClaw (OpenClaw Contributors, 2025) have popularized locally-hosted agents that chain model inference with tool execution on consumer hardware, creating demand for compact models that can sustain reliable sequential tool invocation across long sessions. Coding agents like SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024) and OpenHands (Wang et al., 2024a) demonstrate that agentic tool use in software engineering requires sustained multi-step planning and failure recovery—capabilities that our model targets through its agentic alignment training (Section 6.2). 

# 10 Conclusion 

This technical report presents Muse Glimmer, a 30B dense model purpose-built for autonomous agentic operation on consumer hardware. Through targeted distillation from Muse Spark, it surpasses comparably-sized open models on agentic benchmarks. Combined with 4-bit quantization and speculative decoding via a Dflash head, Muse Glimmer runs at practical speeds on a single RTX 5090 with minimal quality loss. Multimodal understanding of screenshots, documents, and charts equips the model to operate natively in agentic and information-rich environments. Muse Glimmer covers over a hundred language varieties and leads comparably-sized open models up to 27 and 23 pass@1 points on multilingual agentic evaluation. 24 A.1 Agentic and Coding Task Completion 

The following benchmarks are used to assess the agentic and coding capabilities of the model. 

τ ³-bench Banking (Shi et al., 2026) is a knowledge-retrieval-based customer service evaluation extending 

τ -bench to a fintech banking domain. The model acts as a customer-service assistant that must retrieve relevant information from unstructured knowledge documents (policy guides, FAQ pages, compliance procedures) via configurable RAG pipelines, document search, and agentic shell-based search to resolve customer queries. The model interacts against a simulated user, with task success determined by satisfying per-task ground-truth assertions about the retrieved information and generated responses. Results are sourced from Artificial Analysis following the documented benchmarking methodology. 5 

> WildClawBench

(Ding et al., 2026) is an open-source benchmark for evaluating agentic models on real-world, long-horizon tasks. Its 60 tasks span six categories: productivity flow, code intelligence, social interaction, search and retrieval, creative synthesis, and safety alignment. Collectively, these tasks evaluate multi-step planning, coordinated tool use, multimodal understanding, information retrieval, coding, cross-modal content creation, and safe behavior. Each task runs in an isolated Docker container under the OpenClaw harness, with access to tools such as a browser, shell, file system, email, and calendar. Task-specific grading uses deterministic verification criteria, GPT-5.4 as an LLM judge, or a combination of both, depending on the task. Each grader produces an overall score between 0 and 1 by aggregating its task-specific criteria. We report the unweighted mean of the 60 task scores, averaged over three independent runs per task to reduce variance. We report internal run results for all 3 models.  

> GDPval-AA v2

(Patwardhan et al., 2025) assesses models on economically valuable knowledge work spanning 44 occupations across key sectors contributing to US GDP. The benchmark contains 220 tasks developed by OpenAI in collaboration with industry professionals to reflect real-world complexity. Each task requires the model to produce one or more deliverable files, run as an agent in AA’s open-source Stirrup harness with an E2B code-execution sandbox, a 250-turn budget and access to a range of available tools. Results are sourced from Artificial Analysis following the documented benchmarking methodology. 6 

> SciCode

(Tian et al., 2024) contains scientist-curated laboratory problems across 16 scientific disciplines, developed by domain experts with the reported evaluation using the 288 test set subproblems. The benchmark tests Python programming to solve scientific computing tasks: models must generate code that passes every unit test for a sub-problem to be credited. We prompt with the scientist-annotated background information included, and report sub-problem-level scoring rather than whole-problem scoring. No tool use is permitted. Results are sourced from Artificial Analysis following the documented benchmarking methodology. 7 Scores reported here are from before the Muse Glimmer release and do not take into account Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.2 changes that took place in September’26 and affected SciCode. 8 

> OSWorld-Verified

(Xie et al., 2024) is a multimodal benchmark for computer-use agents that evaluates the ability to operate a full Ubuntu desktop VM via GUI in realistic, cross-app workflows. We evaluate on the 361-task split, which excludes 8 Google Drive tasks. Agents are restricted to GUI-based computer control with no direct shell access: they observe the environment through screenshots and act via GUI actions such as click, type, scroll, and key press. Grading is execution-based, where each task defines programmatic getters and checkers that inspect the final VM state and return a 0–1 reward. Models receive 1920 × 1080 screenshots and predict click locations in a normalized coordinate space, with the ability to batch multiple GUI actions per step. Each episode is capped at 200 steps with a screenshot history truncation mechanism enabled that retains a maximum of 19 most recent screenshots. For Qwen3.6-27B and our model, we adopt the Claude computer-use action space (version computer_20251124) with a normalized 0–1000 coordinate space plus a separate stop action. For Gemma4-31B, we use the Gemini 2.5 Flash computer-use interface, including click_at, type_text_at, and stop, with relative coordinates in [0,999]. Following the official OSWorld protocol, we report mean per-task reward. The final score is averaged over four attempts per task to reduce variance. We report internal run results for all 3 models.

> GAIA2

Each scenario runs in an isolated container hosting a self-contained environment: the agent fulfills user requests by issuing tool calls against the environment’s app APIs, while an event daemon injects time-based and environment-driven events mid-episode (e.g., incoming messages, state changes, scheduled triggers) that the agent must notice and adapt to. We use the public GitHub repository for our evaluation, which contains 800 tasks across five capability splits (execution, search, ambiguity, adaptability, and time). We use the OpenClaw harness for our experiments. We measure the mean per-sample success rate over three independent runs per task, matching the public leaderboard convention. Grading is LLM-judged: an in-container judge compares the agent’s realized tool actions and end state against each scenario’s oracle events to produce a per-task success score; we use gpt-oss-120b as the reference judge. We report internal run results for all 3 models.

Each task is scored by combining the benchmark’s deterministic automated checks with an LLM judge (gpt-oss-120b) score. The LLM judge grades the groundedness of the agent’s deliverable against the evidence the agent itself produced (report contents, files, tool outputs). A.3 Reasoning & Knowledge 

This section evaluates the models’ reasoning abilities and factual knowledge across a range of complex tasks.  

> DeepSearchQA

(Gupta et al., 2026) is an agentic browsing evaluation where the model answers 900 hard research questions by acting as an autonomous web-browsing agent. It is given a single browsing tool with three functions: search, open and find. It works in a multi-step loop: it reasons, calls a function, receives the result as a new message, and repeats until it produces a final answer. Grading uses gpt-oss-120b as the judge to extract the model’s predicted answer set, semantically matches predictions to targets, and computes an F1 score. We used the same search engine we selected across all models. Results are averaged over 4 runs to reduce variance. We report internal run results for all 3 models.  

> AIME 2026

(Dekoninck et al., 2026). We use the full 30-question datasets for AIME 2026. This benchmark features highly challenging, non-multiple-choice mathematics problems from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination. The questions require creative problem-solving, advanced algebraic manipulation, and deep number theory skills, where the final answer is always an integer between 000 and 999. Results are averaged over 10 runs to reduce variance. We report internal run results for our model, and self-reported numbers for Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B.  

> GPQA Diamond

(Rein et al., 2024). We use the full 198-question dataset. This subset contains high-quality, multiple-choice questions in biology, physics, and chemistry that were validated by domain experts to ensure they cannot be easily looked up on Google. The problems require deep, graduate-level scientific reasoning and rigorous technical understanding to solve. Results are sourced from Artificial Analysis following the documented benchmarking methodology. 11  

> ARC-AGI

(Chollet, 2019) is a benchmark consisting of 400 questions measuring abstract reasoning. The questions are novel visual grid puzzles designed to evaluate a model’s ability to learn new concepts on the fly rather than relying on memorized training data. They require identifying fundamental core logic, such as object permanence, rotation, symmetry, scaling, and basic geometry, and applying those implicit rules to previously unseen grid configurations. Model answers are evaluated using a rule-based grader that performs exact-match comparison of the model’s parsed output grids against the expected grids, marking an answer as correct when all test grids match exactly. We run the benchmark internally and report mean success rate (pass@1) across four attempts. 

A.4 Instruction Following & Steerability 

The benchmarks below were used to evaluate the model’s ability to follow instructions accurately and respond to user guidance.  

> IFBench

(Pyatkin et al., 2025) is an instruction-following benchmark that tests whether models can satisfy specific verifiable constraints embedded in prompts (e.g., word positioning, counting constraints, repetition patterns). The benchmark contains 294 tasks and we report mean pass rate across tasks. We average results across 4 runs to reduce variance. We report internal run results for Qwen3.6-27B and our model, and self-reported number for Gemma4-31B.  

> SkillsBench

(Li et al., 2026) is an open-source benchmark that measures how effectively an agent can leverage “skills”—modular folders of instructions and helper scripts—to complete specialized knowledge work tasks. We

Grading is deterministic and pytest-based: after the agent finishes, a hidden test suite is uploaded into the same container and executed, and the reward is the weighted fraction of tests passed. We report the mean per-task reward across four attempts. We report internal run results for all 3 models. 

A.5 Multimodal Understanding 

We evaluate multimodal understanding using benchmarks spanning visual reasoning, document understanding and UI interaction.  

> Charxiv Reasoning

(Wang et al., 2024b). We use 1,000 chart reasoning questions from the CharXiv validation set. Grading uses gpt-oss-120b as an LLM judge, which evaluates semantic and mathematical equivalence between the model’s response and the ground-truth answer. These multimodal questions are paired with diverse, complex scientific charts sourced directly from academic papers across multiple disciplines. The benchmark tests a model’s ability to go beyond simple data extraction to perform complex visual synthesis, multi-step mathematical calculations, pattern recognition, and trend comparisons. We report the average task success rate across four attempts. We report internal run results for Gemma4-31B and our model, and a self-reported number for Qwen3.6-27B.  

> MMMU-Pro

(Yue et al., 2025) is a multimodal reasoning benchmark consisting of 1730 multiple choice questions. The benchmark presents college-level problems spanning 30 academic subjects across STEM, humanities, and social sciences, specifically filtered to remove any questions answerable by text-only models. 33 B.3 Synthetic Data: Persona Generation Details 

Our pipeline supports two persona generation modes. In the sampling-based mode, we sample age from a Beta(2,5) distribution skewed toward 25–35 with a floor of 18, and region from a weighted categorical distribution reflecting a realistic AI-user population split across North America, Europe, Asia, India, the Middle East, Latin America, and Africa. Big Five personality scores (openness, conscientiousness, extroversion, agreeableness, neuroticism) are independently drawn from a truncated Gaussian ( μ = 5 , σ = 2 .5, clamped to [1 , 10] ). These sampled attributes are passed to the model, which is prompted to synthesize a coherent identity—name, occupation, marital status, work style, digital habits, hobbies, life habits, dreams, and concerns—consistent with the sampled psychometric profile. In the text-grounded mode, we instead sample raw text passages from various sources and prompt the model to infer several plausible personas (each with an explanation and a plausibility/probability score) that could have authored or engaged with each passage, together with a free-form background paragraph covering routine, relationships, and goals. This mirrors recent findings that grounding persona synthesis in real long-form text, rather than a purely LLM-sampled demographic schema, produces persona populations whose psychometric trait distributions more closely track real human populations than temperature-based sampling alone (Hu et al., 2025). 

B.4 Request Format 

Muse Glimmer natively supports a set of tunable parameters: Reasoning Strength, Valid Recipients, and Agent Tool Calling Format (ATEM). 

Reasoning strength controls the depth of internal chain-of-thought the model performs before producing a response. It is specified as a single line in the system prompt: 

Reasoning strength: <level>. 

Four levels are supported: • Low – Minimal reasoning; a brief internal thought before responding. Suited for simple factual lookups or trivial tasks. • Medium – Moderate reasoning; balanced thinking for medium-complexity tasks requiring some planning. • High – Full reasoning; deep chain-of-thought for complex multi-step coding, debugging, or research tasks. This is the default for agent workloads. • xHigh – Maximum reasoning; the largest available thinking budget, for the hardest long-horizon agentic and research tasks where quality outweighs latency. Reserved for offline or evaluation settings rather than interactive use. 

Valid Recipients: Each message the model produces carries a to= routing header that determines how the infrastructure processes the message. The system prompt declares the set of available recipients via a directive at the end of the prompt: 

# Valid recipients: "self", "bash.exec", "str_replace_editor.view", "user".

B.6.2 Non-Agentic Multilingual Capabilities 

We further study how the non-agentic capabilities of Muse Glimmer transfer from English into other languages, and we report below our main findings. 

Strong metalinguistic reasoning. The good performance observed on OmnilingualGAIA2 suggests strong multilingual reasoning capabilities of Muse Glimmer. To evaluate the ability of the model to perform 

metalinguistic reasoning, i.e. understanding and operating under the rules of (potentially unseen) languages, we leverage Linguini (Sánchez et al., 2026) as it deliberately removes language knowledge from the equation: each problem supplies all the evidence needed in context, so a model cannot fall back on having seen the language in training. Muse Glimmer leads by a wide margin on whole-problem exact match ( 8.8, against 

3.8 for Gemma4-31B and 2.5 for Qwen3.6-27B), solving more than twice as many complete problems as the strongest baseline. The per-question view narrows and reverses the ordering at the top ( 17 .9 versus Gemma4-31B’s 18 .8, and 14 .4 for Qwen3.6-27B), indicating that Muse Glimmer more often carries a deduction through to every requested answer, while Gemma4-31B recovers a comparable share of individual answers across a wider set of partially-solved problems. Neither ordering follows from the translation tiers: the 75 languages in Linguini lie almost entirely outside the supported set, and performance here reflects inductive reasoning over unfamiliar morphology rather than coverage of the language itself. 

Reading comprehension and knowledge transfer well. We investigate whether the good agentic and metalin-guistic reasoning capabilities emerge at the expense of more conventional multilingual tasks. On Belebele 

(Bandarkar et al., 2024), a machine reading comprehension benchmark spanning 122 languages, Muse Glimmer scores 87 .8% , ahead of Qwen3.6-27B ( 86 .1% ) and just behind Gemma4-31B ( 88 .6% ); all three cluster within 2.5

points, indicating that multilingual reading comprehension is not the discriminating axis at this scale. We find similar results on MMLU-ProX (Xuan et al., 2025), measuring domain-specific knowledge in a multilingual setting, where Muse Glimmer scores 80 .1 mean pass@1, behind Gemma4-31B ( 82 .5) and Qwen3.6-27B just slightly ahead ( 80 .5). Remarkably, Muse Glimmer exhibits the tightest multilingual spread of the three once we exclude Wolof—a language for which Muse Glimmer currently lacks sufficient coverage: the average English-to-language gap is just 2.6 points for Muse Glimmer (high), compared to 3.7 for Gemma4-31B and 

5.2 for Qwen3.6-27B. Overall performance also converges under this view: Muse Glimmer (high) surpasses Qwen3.6-27B ( 81 .5 vs. 81 .1) and narrows the gap with Gemma4-31B ( 83 .0). In other words, Muse Glimmer trades 1.5 points of average accuracy for a more uniform cross-lingual profile. 

Muse Glimmer is token efficient. Absolute accuracy on MMLU-ProX hides a large spread in generation cost (Table 8). Gemma4-31B and Muse Glimmer are close on mean tokens per rollout ( 2,671 vs. 2,453 ), while 38 Qwen3.6-27B spends 4,532 —70% more than Gemma4-31B and 1.8× as much as Muse Glimmer in high mode. Which model is preferable therefore depends on whether the deployment is accuracy-bound or throughput-bound: excluding Wolof Gemma4-31B buys 1.5 pp of accuracy for ∼ 10% more tokens; Qwen3.6-27B spends 1.8 × the tokens for no accuracy advantage at all. 

Reasoning strength does not change these conclusions. Raising reasoning strength from high to xhigh moves every multilingual result by less than the run-to-run noise: 53 .3 vs 53 .4 on WMT24++ , 47 .5 on both for 

FLORES+ , 36 .5 on both for BOUQuET , 87 .8 on both for Belebele, and 8.1 vs 8.8 on Linguini , at 10 –27% 

more generated tokens. Therefore, all our results are done with high as the default operating point. > B.6.3 Machine Translation Performance and Detailed Language Support

We use the performance of Muse Glimmer on machine translation tasks, as measured by chrF++ (Popović, 2015), on the union of WMT24++ (Deutsch et al., 2025), FLORES+ (NLLB Team et al., 2024) and 

BOUQuET (Andrews et al., 2025), to define language support tiers. In particular, we measure the performance of the model in translating text from at least one benchmark both in and out of English, and define two language tiers. We say a language variaty is strongly supported by Muse Glimmer if, based on the protocol above, it scores at least 50 , while we say it’s usable if its score is in the [40 , 49] range. Note that for tier assignment we exclude empty generations, while the aggregate scores reported in Tables 5 and 9 consider a failed generation to score zero. At Muse Glimmer’s empty rate of 0.3%, the two yield identical tier assignments. 

Machine Translation Performance We report aggregate Muse Glimmer translation performance across the three benchmarks in table 9, separating the into English (X →eng) and the out of English (eng →Y) performance, again compared to Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B. Gemma4-31B is the best performing model across all three benchmarks and aggregate directions, but Muse Glimmer is a close second throughout (tied with Qwen3.6-27B into English on WMT24++ ). In particular, on the 55 language pairs of WMT24++ ,Muse Glimmer is within 1.5 chrF++ points of Gemma4-31B into English and within 1.2 points out of English. The delta widens when the model is evaluated on the long tail translation tasks of BOUQuET , the benchmark with the widest language coverage, where translation performance degrades, bringing the overall observed performance down. 

Detailed Language Support With the operational definition above, and the language coverage provided by the three supporting benchmarks (55 for WMT24++ , 212 on FLORES+ and 274 on BOUQuET ), we establish that Muse Glimmer supports 137 language varieties , with 88 classified as strong and 49 as usable .One caveat on translation benchmarks: chrF++ penalises scripts that do not use spaces between words (Chinese, Japanese) or that pack a syllable into each character (Korean), which partly explains why Chinese, Japanese and Korean fall below the bar out of English ( 33 .9 / 34 .9 / 36 .5), as they do for Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B, while scoring above 56 into English. Since all three reach 81 .5 on MMLU-ProX, in line with strong-tier languages, we do not read their absence from the list as a lack of support, although we keep the tiers strictly rule-based.
