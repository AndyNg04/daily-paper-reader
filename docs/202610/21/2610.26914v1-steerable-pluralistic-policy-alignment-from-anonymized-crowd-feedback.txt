Abstract 

Pluralistic alignment requires AI systems to learn from di-verse populations whose preferences may reflect heteroge-neous or even conflicting objectives, and to personalize their behavior to individual users. Existing approaches in plural-istic alignment often assume access to annotator identities and rely on powerful pretrained representations to model in-dividual preference variations. We propose Preference-PCA Pluralistic Learning (P3L), a novel framework for pluralis-tic alignment that jointly learns trajectory embeddings and the shared basis of preference directions via PCA decompo-sition from heterogeneous crowd feedback without requiring annotator identities. At test time, our approach enables rapid and accurate personalized reward learning and downstream policy optimization. Experiments across multiple embodied control tasks demonstrate that P3L enables steerable personal alignment that generalizes beyond the offline dataset to out-of-distribution new users and attains competitive RL and test performance, while substantially reducing computational cost and supporting variable adaptation query budgets. 

## Introduction 

As we witness growing adoption of AI systems and robotic models for downstream applications such as in industrial au-tomation (Tang et al. 2023; Kernbach et al. 2026), assistive healthcare (Bouzida et al. 2024; Lamsey et al. 2024), and household robotics (Shafiullah et al. 2023), being able to steer and align these models towards an end-user’s individ-ual preference is seen as a major challenge (Ji et al. 2023; Casper et al. 2023). One of the most popular approaches for aligning such models is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). RLHF learns a reward function from human preferences and then optimizes an RL policy under the learned reward function (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022; Kauf-mann et al. 2024). While this approach has been highly suc-cessful, it has been shown to be susceptible to over optimiza-tion (Anwar et al. 2024) and reward misidentification (Tien et al. 2023), and can become easily misaligned across diverse users in a crowd (Siththaranjan, Laidlaw, and Hadfield-Menell 2024; Poddar et al. 2024; Chhan, Novoseller, and Lawhern 2024). Recent work has tried to solve this person-alization challenge by either learning multiple reward func-

However, this assump-tion limits applicability in many real-world settings where annotator identities are unavailable, anonymized or cannot be reliably associated with preference labels. Moreover, VPL has been shown to suffer from posterior collapse (Kim and Kim 2026), a failure mode that we also observe empirically. This raises a central challenge: Can we learn from heteroge-neous crowd preferences—potentially reflecting conflicting objectives—without annotator identity, while still enabling efficient test-time personalization to new users in embodied control tasks? 

Our key idea is to decompose heterogeneous crowd pref-erences into a small set of shared preference directions. This reframes pluralistic alignment as estimating user-specific weights over a shared preference basis representation rather than learning separate reward models for each user. This representation enables personalized reward functions—and corresponding policies—to be constructed for new users at test time without requiring annotator identities, allowing rapid policy personalization from a small number of adaptive queries. Building on the preference vector framework (Luo et al. 2025), which first proposed to represent human pref-erences as directions in a latent representation space in the context of LLMs, we extend and test this idea in embodied control scenarios. Our approach jointly learns trajectory rep-resentations and preference directions from heterogeneous crowd feedback. Specifically, we train an end-to-end unsu-pervised representation encoder that embeds trajectories and we use these embeddings to extract a basis of preference eigenvectors capturing variation in user objectives. Unlike prior work such as Variational Preference Learning (VPL) (Poddar et al. 2024), which assumes access to anno-tator identities to infer user-specific rewards, our framework does not require annotator information. However, our frame-work can incorporate annotator identities when available, enabling it to operate under both anonymized and privileged settings. Overall, our approach enables a simple, flexible, and effective framework for pluralistic alignment in embod-ied control. We summarize our contributions as follows: (1) Learn-ing preference bases from heterogeneous feedback. We arXiv:2610.26914v1 [cs.LG] 17 Oct 2026 introduce a framework that learns a shared basis of prefer-ence directions from heterogeneous, potentially conflicting crowd preferences. Our method leverages an unsupervised trajectory encoder to construct latent trajectory embeddings and extracts preference eigenvectors that capture variation in user objectives without requiring annotator identities. (2) Personalized policy alignment. We demonstrate that the learned preference basis enables test-time construction of personalized reward functions and policies, allowing align-ment to individual users across multiple objectives. (3) Em-pirical evaluation. Across multi-objective control environ-ments, our method achieves strong alignment to both in-distribution and out-of-distribution users. Compared to prior work such as VPL (Poddar et al. 2024), it achieves higher test-time preference accuracy and competitive RL performance while significantly reducing training cost and allowing flex-ible adaptation to varying numbers of test-time queries. 

## Related Work 

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) 

addresses the challenge of specifying reward functions by instead learning them from human feedback. Human feed-back can take several forms, including pairwise prefer-ences (Christiano et al. 2017; Poddar et al. 2024), rank-ings (Brown et al. 2019; Myers et al. 2022), and corrective feedback (Bajcsy et al. 2017; Mehta and Losey 2024). Most RLHF methods learn reward functions from pairwise pref-erences and assume that this preference data originates from a single underlying reward function, effectively treating dis-agreement among annotators as noise. Some work attempts to account for imperfect human feedback. For example, Lee et al. (2021) introduced stochastic preference models to cap-ture noisy or inconsistent human judgments. Nevertheless, these approaches still assume that annotators ultimately share a common objective. By contrast, real-world preference data is often collected from diverse users whose objectives may fundamentally differ or even conflict. This motivates study-ing methods that can learn from heterogeneous crowd feed-back while still enabling alignment to individual users. 

Personalized RLHF and Pluralistic Alignment Recent work has begun to investigate how to model heterogeneous human preferences and personalize AI behavior to differ-ent users. Several approaches have explored preference di-versity primarily in the context of large language models (LLMs) (Luo et al. 2025; Shenfeld et al. 2025; Siththaran-jan, Laidlaw, and Hadfield-Menell 2024). These methods typically model preference variation through latent variables or preference decompositions, but focus on reward model-ing rather than policy personalization or embodied control domains. Distributional Preference Learning (DPL) (Siththa-ranjan, Laidlaw, and Hadfield-Menell 2024) studies prefer-ence learning in the presence of hidden context , where an-notator identity or other contextual factors influencing pref-erences are not observed by the reward model. While this approach explicitly models preference heterogeneity through latent contextual factors during reward learning, its primary goal is to characterize and detect hidden context rather than to construct personalized policies for new users. Only a small number of works study pluralistic alignment in embodied control tasks. Variational Preference Learning (VPL) (Pod-dar et al. 2024) models user-specific reward functions by introducing a latent variable conditioned on annotator iden-tity, requiring access to annotator IDs during training. Al-ternatively, Bahlous-Boldi et al. (2025) learns a Pareto set of reward hypotheses using lexicase selection to represent trade-offs among multiple objectives. Another line of prior work (Lin and Brown 2026) identifies shared safety princi-ples from crowd feedback but does not consider personaliza-tion or model user differences as we do. Despite many recent advances in pluralistic alignment, existing approaches (My-ers et al. 2022; Poddar et al. 2024; Bahlous-Boldi et al. 2025) either require annotator identities, explicitly model latent contexts during reward learning, or produce sets of reward hypotheses rather than directly enabling personaliza-tion for new users or learn shared skills from crowd feedback rather than personalizing for individual preferred behaviors. In contrast, our framework learns a shared basis of preference directions from anonymized heterogeneous crowd feedback. This basis representation allows personalized reward func-tions—and corresponding policies—to be constructed for previously unseen users at test time from a small number of preference queries.

Decomposed Reward Models (DRM) Luo et al. (2025) reformulate preference learning by decomposing rewards into multiple orthogonal preference directions in representa-tion space rather than learning a single scalar reward. How-ever, their analysis focuses primarily on reward prediction accuracy and does not consider optimizing policies under personalized reward functions. High accuracy on held-out preference queries does not necessarily translate to behav-iorally aligned policies, as widely documented in prior RLHF work (Christiano et al. 2017; Tien et al. 2023; Wen et al. 2025). Furthermore, DRM only tested this idea on pretrained LLM embedding models trained on internet-scale data such as Gemma-2B-RM and Llama3-8B-RM, which have under-gone multiple stages of training including pretraining and supervised instruction fine-tuning before preference align-ment (Ziegler et al. 2020; Ouyang et al. 2022). In contrast, many embodied control domains lack such pretrained repre-sentation models. A key challenge that we tackle in this paper is to learn meaningful trajectory representations and prefer-ence decompositions directly from limited task-specific data in settings where large pretrained embedding models are un-available. We show that it is possible to jointly learn the task objectives as well as user preference distribution from heterogeneous preference dataset using only self-supervised representations. 

Problem Formulation 

Learning from diverse crowdsourced feedback presents a more challenging setting than standard RLHF. Vanilla RLHF aggregates preferences from multiple annotators into a sin-gle reward function, implicitly assuming that all preference labels arise from a shared underlying objective. However, in many real-world settings different users may have funda-mentally different goals or behavioral preferences. The idea of creating AI systems that can model and serve pluralistic human values is known as pluralistic alignment (Sorensen et al. 2024). Our work specifically focuses on steerable plu-ralistic alignment , where the goal is to adapt an AI system at test time to the preferences of a specific user in the presence of heterogeneous crowd feedback. 

Steerable Pluralistic Alignment with Heterogeneous Users Consider a population of N annotators, where each user ui has an unknown ground-truth reward function ri.For each preference query m, an unobserved annotator in-dex zm ∈ { 1, . . . , N } generates a label ym ∈ { 0, 1} over a trajectory pair (τ mA , τ mB ) according to that annotator’s latent reward function rzm , where ym = 0 indicates that τ mA is preferred over τ mB . Because users may have different objec-tives, conflicting labels for the same pair of trajectories can naturally occur when the two labels are produced by different annotators. We assume access to an offline dataset of crowd preferences D = {(τ mA , τ mB , y m)}Mm=1 , where the annotator identities {zm}Mm=1 may or may not be available. 

Learning Objective Our goal is to use this heterogeneous preference dataset to learn representations of heterogeneous preferences that enable efficient test-time personalization of reward functions and policies for new users. Under this set-ting, we aim to address the following questions: (1) Can we infer personalized reward functions ˆri for individual users from heterogeneous preference data when neither annotator identities nor the underlying task objectives are known? (2) Can we optimize policies ˆπi under these inferred rewards to produce behaviors aligned with diverse user preferences? (3) Can such personalization generalize to both in-distribution users and to users whose rewards fall outside the distribution of the offline preference dataset? 

## Preference-PCA Pluralistic Learning (P3L) 

We term our proposed approach Preference-PCA Pluralis-tic Learning (P3L). The overall pipeline of our approach is shown in Fig. 1. We consider the joint problem of pre-training an expressive embedding extractor and leveraging it to learn preference vectors from diverse crowd preferences in control domains to provide personalized alignment to test-time users on both test time held out set as well as a personalized policy optimized under the personalized derived reward.

## Experimental Setup 

We first describe the environments used, followed by the baseline methods for comparison and finally present the eval-uation criteria and metrics. Figure 2: Environments MO-Highway, MO-Reacher, MO-Humanoid and MO-Kitchen. 

Environments Following the pluralism evaluation bench-mark criteria outlined in Sorensen et al. (2024), we consider multi-objective environments for steerable pluralistic align-ment. They provide a principled way to represent crowd-sourced feedback with diverse and potentially conflicting ob-jectives. Fig. 2 shows the environments used: MO-Highway and MO-Reacher from MO-Gymnasium (Felten et al. 2023), and MO-Humanoid and MO-Kitchen, described below. 

MO-Highway has a 3-dimensional reward with first re-ward dimension rewarding high-speed driving while the sec-ond dimension scores on driving in right lane and the last reward dimension represents the collision reward. 

MO-Reacher provides a 4-dimensional reward vector, where each dimension corresponds to the reward for reaching a specific goal. Some goals induce competing preferences; for example, reaching the left goal conflicts directly with reaching the right goal. 

MO-Humanoid (Chen et al. 2026) has a 5-dimensional objective space with objectives corresponding to forward velocity along the x-axis, lateral velocity along the y-axis, angular velocities of the left and right elbows, and energy efficiency. The environment has a high-dimensional contin-uous observation space of dimension 376 and an action space of dimension 17. 

MO-Kitchen is a multi-objective version of Franka Kitchen (Gupta et al. 2020) that we created, featuring a 9-DoF Franka robot that can perform several distinct ma-nipulation tasks, such as opening the microwave, moving the kettle, and turning oven knobs for different burners. This set-ting naturally induces heterogeneous user preferences such as User 1 might prefer opening the microwave, User 2 mov-ing the kettle, User 3 toggling the light switch, while User 4 prefers turning the knob to activate the top burner. MO-Kitchen is challenging because feedback from multiple users with different preferred tasks is learned jointly, requiring the framework to disentangle heterogeneous user objectives while reasoning over coordinated reaching, contact-rich ma-nipulation, and precise end-effector orientation. For MO-Kitchen, we conduct experiments and explore personalization in multiple variants, starting from personal-izing in simple setting with only 2 possible tasks, termed as MO-Kitchen-2d (opening the microwave door or moving the kettle) and then progress to challenging scenarios with 3 possible tasks, termed as MO-Kitchen-3d (microwave door, kettle, or flipping the light switch). Finally we move to the most sophisticated domain with 4 different tasks possible, termed as MO-Kitchen-4d , where each user can choose from a variety of tasks from opening the microwave door to push-ing the kettle to turning oven knobs for different burners to turning on the light switch. In each of these environments we ignore the terminal con-dition and make these tasks fixed time horizon to avoid leak-age of information and to prevent termination bias in reward learning from preferences as advised by Freire et al. (2020). Based on the number of underlying objectives in an environ-ment we report the performance across all objectives where each individual user prefers one specific objective. For sim-plicity in our experiments, we associate User 1 with a pref-erence for Objective 1 over all other objectives, User 2 with Objective 2, and so on. This is only a notational convention; our framework makes no assumption itself that user and ob-jective indices must align, and thus in general, any user may prefer any objective. 

Baselines We evaluate both our privileged and non-privileged approach against a single reward head model (Sin-gleRM) learned through conventional Bradley-Terry loss op-timization, and VPL. VPL uses adaptive queries to infer the latent preference variable on which it conditions its re-ward and policy while our approaches, inspired by (Luo et al. 2025) use HyRe (Lee et al. 2024), to infer the test-time user’s preference vector. For complete setup details with respect to number of queries, etc., please refer to Appendix A. We op-timize online RL policies under learned reward functions using Augmented Random Search (Mania, Guy, and Recht 2018) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al. 2017) from Stable Baselines3 (Raffin et al. 2021) with episodic rewards. We employ episodic rewards for two rea-sons: (1) because the preference dataset is constructed using fixed horizon trajectories and we want to mitigate termina-tion bias (Freire et al. 2020) and (2) because our reward formulation combines the PCA preference vector with a rep-resentation encoder that summarizes the full trajectory. Original VPL is an offline framework which uses Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) (Kostrikov, Nair, and Levine 2022) to learn an offline policy optimized for per timestep reward. For fair comparison to our frameworks, we learn both an of-fline IQL policy as well as an online episodic policy for VPL for all MO-environments. Since MO-Kitchen is commonly used as an offline RL benchmark, we optimize a continuous-control IQL policy on the collected offline dataset using the learned personalized reward, and evaluate the resulting pol-icy through online environment rollouts. For more details on RL optimization and hyper-parameters, see Appendix D. 

Metrics We compare the performance of our approach against earlier frameworks on these metrics: (1) accuracy on adapting to a new held out test-time user’s preferences; (2) performance of RL policy optimized under new test-time user’s preferences; (3) time taken on NVIDIA GeForce RTX-GT3090 to train these models; (4) Ablation with respect to the number of adaptive queries (For ablation with respect to training queries and number of PCA dimensions, see Ap-pendix H and Appendix F respectively); and (5) Adaptation to out-of-distribution users where we train on subset of dif-ferent users and test the adaptation to held-out new user both on test and RL policy. Note that in the Original VPL paper (Poddar et al. 2024), the adaptation query set size is referred to as context size in VPL and is noted in their appendix as the main hyperparame-ter to tune. Therefore, metric (4) directly evaluates prior work under its key adaptation hyperparameter, and we include this ablation to ensure a fair comparison against VPL. 

## Results 

In-Distribution Experiments 

Test-Time Preference Alignment Accuracy In Table 1, we present the average accuracy of adapting to test-time held out preference queries in terms of mean and standard devi-ation across 5 seeds for all environments. For the individual user accuracy of adapting to test-time held out preference queries across all environments and for more details, please refer to Appendix B. We observe that for all environments, our ap-proaches Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP and Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP outperform both Single Reward Model and VPL, where Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP makes the same assump-tions about prior knowledge of annotator identity as VPL while the Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP approach has no such prior knowledge. Across MO-Kitchen, we observe that while P3L outperforms both the baselines, its performance decreases as the number of preference dimensions increases. Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP falls from 100.0% to 84.15% to 78.44%, while Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP decreases from 90.4% to 82.67% to 72.17%. This degradation is con-sistent with the increasing difficulty of preference inference in a higher-dimensional objective space; nevertheless, both P3L variants continue to considerably outperform the base-lines, indicating that the proposed framework better captures the underlying multi-objective preference structure. Thus we show better alignment to the held out queries when learn-ing from diverse crowd feedback with no information about annotator identity. 

RL performance under Test-Time Preference Alignment 

Table 2 compares performance of Online RL policies across all frameworks except for VPL, where we also optimized an offline IQL policy, for fair comparison to Poddar et al. (2024) who used IQL. We report the average RL performance as mean and standard deviation over reward obtained over 100 rollouts across 5 seeds across all users and all seeds per model for all environments except MO-Kitchen where we report mean and standard deviation over task success rate. For more results with respect to performance across individual new users, please refer to Appendix D. We observe from Table 2 that across all environments, both of our frameworks, for the majority of users on average, deliver a more personalized policy than both the baselines. In MO-Highway (Row 1 in Table 2), we observe that VPL obtains a higher score when learning with per-timestep of-fline RL IQL policy than any other models which optimize an online RL episodic policy. However, this performance ap-pears to stem from the offline IQL policy optimization rather than the VPL reward model itself since optimizing an online ARS policy under the VPL reward, fails to achieve compara-ble performance. In all other environments, Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP, which operates under the same assumption as VPL of complete access to annotator identity, substantially outperforms VPL and Single Reward Model baselines. Moreover, P3L remains effective when this privi-leged information is removed: although Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP faces a harder learning problem and does not consistently outperform VPL, it remains competitive despite having no access to annotator identity and still surpasses the Single Reward Model baseline. Together, these results demonstrate that P3L benefits from privileged information when available while retaining strong performance under weaker and more realistic information assumptions. As the environment becomes increasingly complex from MO-Kitchen-2d to MO-Kitchen-3d to MO-Kitchen-4d (Row 4-6 respectively in Table 2), task success declines across all evaluated frameworks. This systematic decrease in perfor-mance supports our hypothesis that learning personalized policies becomes increasingly difficult as the dimensional-ity of the underlying preference space grows. Despite the growing complexity, the strong relative performance of P3L across these settings reflects its effectiveness for enabling personalized policies in embodied-control tasks. 

Training Time Comparison We report the time taken to train the pluralistic frameworks in Fig. 3a on Nvidia GeForce RTX 3090 GPU. From the table we see that VPL incurs the highest training cost whereas both P3L variants substantially outperform VPL in training efficiency across all three envi-ronments, achieving speedups ranging from approximately 29 × to nearly 2,500 ×. Notably, Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP trains approximately 176 × faster on MO-Highway, 29 × faster on MO-Reacher, and nearly 2,500 × faster on MO-Humanoid. Even without access to annotator identities, Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP achieves speedups of 61 ×, 54 ×, and over 1,000 ×, respectively. This has significant practical implica-tions with respect to computational cost, energy consump-tion, and compute budget. 

Test-Time Preference Alignment vs. Adaptation Query Budget In Fig 3b, we compare and contrast the impact of number of adaptive queries on test accuracy across our approaches against VPL on MO-Reacher. Both of our frame-works, Privileged-Traj-MaskedDP and Anonymized-CLS-MaskedDP, adapt to a new user using any number of pref-erence queries, allowing flexible adaptation at test time. By contrast, VPL requires a fixed number of queries to infer the latent preference for a new user. This stems from the VPL architecture which uses a VAE with a linear layer as first layer, rendering it unable to handle variable-sized adapta-tion query sets. Consequently, we train a new VPL model for each adaptation query size. Our results demonstrate that both our frameworks, not only work with any variable size of adaptation queries but can also achieve competitive perfor-mance of 90% accuracy with smaller number of adaptation queries as they obtain with bigger query sets; whereas VPL remains highly sensitive to the adaptation size and performs substantially worse even with significantly larger query sets of 100x per user. ## Discussion and Conclusion 

We present a framework for pluralistic alignment that enables personalized policy learning from heterogeneous crowd feed-back, both with and without privileged information about annotator identities. Experiments in multi-objective control environments show improved preference alignment, com-petitive RL performance, and significantly reduced training cost compared to prior approaches. These results suggest that learning structured representations of diverse human prefer-ences provides a scalable path toward personalized align-ment in RLHF systems. A key advantage of our framework is its computational effi-ciency. Compared to existing approaches, our method signif-icantly reduces training cost while maintaining strong align-ment performance. This efficiency is particularly important in RLHF settings, where learning from human feedback is often sample-intensive (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022). More broadly, our results suggest that heteroge-neous crowd feedback can be effectively leveraged by learn-ing a shared representation of preference directions. Once this representation is learned, personalized reward functions for new users can be constructed efficiently from only a small number of adaptive queries, providing a scalable mechanism for pluralistic alignment in RLHF systems. One limitation of our approach is pre-specification of the number of PCA components used to represent preference directions. In practice, we determine this value through ab-lation studies to capture the structure of the underlying pref-erence distribution. Future work can explore metwhods for automatically inferring number of latent objectives present in crowdsourced feedback in an unsupervised manner and also investigate incorporating richer feedback modalities, in-cluding demonstrations, rankings, and corrective feedback.

## C Ablation on Annotation Set Size for VPL 

For VPL, we tried to tune the annotation set size, also referred to as context set size in (Poddar et al. 2024) for each domain. For MO-Reacher, we swept over annotation set sizes of 6, 30, 120, 300, and 600 preference queries, where the selected number of queries are provided at once to the VAE to infer the latent user preference vector z which is then used to test alignment to new user both using test set accuracy as well as for optimizing an RL policy. For MO-Humanoid, we tuned the learning VPL over an-notation set sizes of 1,5,25 and using an annealer. We finally chose to use set size of 1 as we observed posterior collapse with bigger annotation set sizes. For Kitchen, we started with annotation set size of 30 and soon observed posterior collapse so reduced annotation set size to 20 to successfully learn the VPL-VAE encoder and decoder reward.

## I VPL Posterior Collapse 

We observed the encoder in VPL, Variational Autoencoder (VAE), begins to produce NaN values for the latent pref-erence mean z in two environments: MO-Humanoid and Franka-Kitchen. For both environments we report parame-ters that originally resulted in NaN values of the latent mean and thereby collapsing training of the VPL reward model (which is actually the decoder with input as observation and the latent mean produced by the VAE). We performed extensive hyperparameter tuning and found that reducing annotation set size, also termed as context set size in (Poddar et al. 2024), often led to more stable training. For Kitchen, the VPL training dataset is heterogeneous, com-bining trajectories across all tasks and user preferences. The training dataset contains 2,400 pairwise preference queries, and as each query can be annotated from the perspective of a user who prefers any one of the possible tasks, each query yields one label per possible preferred task, leading to 9600 preference labels. We observed this collapse for seed 0, annotation set size 30 where each trajectory in pairwise preference query is of length 280, and an adaptation prefer-ence dataset of size 180. For MO-Humanoid, we observed this when sweeping over annotation set size of 25 as well as with 5, over 1000 preference queries of length 150.
