# ABSTRACT 

In many human-AI systems, candidate actions are not executed directly but are first evaluated by another decision-making entity under a potentially different objective or decision model. Standard decision-theoretic frameworks do not require this interaction to be represented explicitly, as they define transition dynamics directly over state-action pairs, allowing evaluation to be absorbed within the transition model. We introduce a semantics-aware decision framework in which a candidate action is proposed, evaluated under an interaction context, and then resolved into an executed outcome. We formalize this through a joint action language and an interpretation function that maps each joint action and context to a semantic outcome. Transition and reward dynamics are induced through these semantic outcomes, thereby introducing an explicit representational layer for interaction semantics . Semantic outcomes provide explicit units for organizing interaction principles, which can be instantiated through application-specific evaluation mechanisms and reused across distinct interaction instances. This provides a structured interface for specifying context-dependent evaluation separately from the resulting execution dynamics, without requiring changes to the underlying planning algorithm. We instantiate the framework in shared human-AI autonomy, capturing interaction patterns such as advisory and authorization, and illustrating how interaction context influences decision making. The resulting formulation remains compatible with standard Markov decision process (MDP) planning. 

Keywords: Interaction Semantic Representation ⋅ Decision-Theoretic Modeling ⋅ Shared Autonomy ⋅ Human-AI Interaction ⋅ Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) 

# 1 Introduction 

Standard decision-theoretic models typically specify the consequences of selected actions directly through transition dynamics [1]. In many settings, however, candidate actions are first evaluated by another decision-making entity before execution. The realized outcome therefore depends not only on the proposed action, but also on the interaction through which it is accepted, modified, rejected, or otherwise resolved. This interaction pattern arises in shared autonomy, human-in-the-loop learning, and oversight settings, where humans or autonomous policies evaluate proposed actions before they affect the environment [2, 3, 4, 5]. In general, many interactive decision-making problems distinguish between proposing an action and determining whether, and in what form, it is ultimately realized. We adopt the following decision-theoretic principle. We model interactive decisions through three stages: (1) a candidate action is proposed, (2) evaluated under an interaction context, and (3) resolved into an executed outcome. The distinction is not one of expressiveness but of representation . A sufficiently expressive transition model can encode both evaluation and execution within a single dynamics. However, doing so conflates two fundamentally distinct stages of decision making: evaluating and resolving a proposed action, and describing how the environment evolves following its execution. Evaluation may itself involve distinct decision-making principles, governed by another entity’s objectives, knowledge, authority, or interaction context. When such processes are embedded within transition dynamics, their arXiv:2610.25496v2 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 From Proposal to Execution: Modeling Interaction Semantics in Shared Autonomy identification, specification, and modification may become entangled with task-level execution behavior, particularly when common evaluation principles apply across different actions. Making this separation explicit provides a dedicated interface for representing and managing these interaction processes separately from the resulting execution dynamics, while preserving the underlying planning model. Existing shared-autonomy and multi-agent formulations capture such interactions through augmented state variables, state-dependent transitions, or supervisory mechanisms (e.g., [6]). While these approaches can explicitly represent supervisory interventions, the evaluation principles shared across different actions and interaction contexts need not be exposed within the decision-process specification. We address this by introducing a semantic layer that explicitly models the interaction governing how proposed actions become executed actions. We define a joint action language for interaction-level proposals, building on joint action models [7], together with an interpretation function that maps each joint action under an interaction context to a semantic outcome. Transition and reward dynamics are induced through these semantic outcomes, which determine how proposed actions are resolved before execution. Our framework specifies where interaction semantics are represented within the decision process, rather than prescribing how the underlying evaluation mechanisms are obtained. For a given application, the interpretation over joint actions and interaction contexts determines the semantic outcomes needed to represent its interaction semantics. These outcomes constitute the semantic layer and are instantiated through domain-specific evaluation mechanisms, while planning continues over the induced dynamics. Because multiple interaction instances may realize the same semantic outcome, they naturally share a common semantic representation, enabling structural reuse independent of the particular evaluation mechanism. Our formulation preserves compatibility with standard MDP planning while making interaction semantics explicit. We instantiate the framework in shared autonomy [2, 8], where interaction is mediated by autonomy and attention, providing a concrete instantiation of interaction semantics through context-dependent evaluation. For example, an AI agent may propose an action that is executed only if the human evaluates it as acceptable. This structure captures interaction patterns such as advisory and authorization within a unified representation. In summary, our key contributions are as follows: 1. We introduce a semantics-aware decision framework with a joint action language and interpretation function that map interaction-level actions to semantic outcomes, making interaction semantics explicit. 2. We establish that semantic outcomes induce semantic regimes that enable structured transition and reward dynamics compatible with standard MDP planning. 3. We instantiate the framework in shared human-AI autonomy, showing how interaction patterns such as advisory and authorization arise as semantic outcomes within a unified interaction representation. 4. We analyze the structural implications of explicitly representing interaction semantics relative to implicit encoding, and illustrate the framework in a shared-autonomy navigation domain, demonstrating context-dependent evaluation and its influence on decision making. 

# 2 Abstract Framework 

The framework is defined at an abstract level, independently of any particular application-specific evaluation mechanism. Throughout the paper, we instantiate the interaction context using autonomy and attention to ground the framework in shared autonomy. The present formulation assumes finite, observable interaction contexts, while extensions to richer settings remain future work. 

2.1 Interaction Context 

We represent the interaction context through a structured set of variables that determine how candidate actions are interpreted within the semantic layer. This provides an explicit representation of context-dependent interpretation within the setting we consider here. 

Definition 1 (Interaction Context) Let L denote a finite set of autonomy levels and At a finite set of attention states. An interaction context is a pair 

(lau, att ) ∈ L × At, 

where lau specifies the distribution of control and decision authority between agents, and att is the attention level available for evaluating and responding to a candidate action.

2.2 Joint Action Language Definition 2 (Joint Action Language) Let Ai and Aj denote the sets of interaction-level actions available to two agents. The joint action language is the set 

Aji = Ai × Aj ,

whose elements represent primitive agent interactions. 

Joint actions constitute the primitive interaction objects of the framework. They represent interaction-level proposals between agents (e.g., proposing or responding to), but carry no semantics by themselves. Their interpretation depends on the interaction context and is determined by the interpretation function introduced next. 

2.3 Joint Action Semantics 

Not all joint actions are meaningful in every context. To capture this, we define an interpretation function that assigns a semantic outcome to each joint action under a given context. 

Definition 3 (Interpretation Function) Let Σ be a set of semantic tags ( σ), each representing a distinct semantic outcome for resolving a proposed action into an executed action. The interpretation function 

IJ ∶ ( Ai × Aj ) × ( L × At ) → Σ ∪ {} 

maps a joint action and interaction context to a semantic tag, i.e., IJ (( ai, a j ), (lau, att )) = σ, and returns  if the joint action is not valid under the given context. 

Thus, a semantic outcome represents the interaction semantics governing how a proposed action is resolved before execution. The evaluation mechanism corresponding to it determines the executed action, which in turn induces the transition, reward, and discounting. For example, in an advisory setting, a joint action representing an AI suggestion (e.g., cross-bridge ) may map to a semantic outcome within which the human evaluates the suggestion, for example, from their understanding and preference, and either accepts and executes it or rejects it. Under a different context, the same joint action may induce a different outcome, for instance, if attention is low, the suggestion may be ignored, while triggering a fallback behavior. The interpretation function is defined locally over individual joint action-context pairs. The structural reuse induced when multiple pairs map to the same outcome (next section). We say that a joint action-context pair (( ai, a j ), (lau, att )) is well-defined if IJ (( ai, a j ), (lau, att )) ∈ Σ, and invalid 

otherwise.

# 3 Formal Properties 

This section characterizes the structure induced by interaction semantics, focusing on how semantic outcomes organize the induced transition and reward dynamics. The discount factor is omitted for clarity. By Definition 3, the interpretation function depends only on the joint action and interaction context, independently of the task state x. Thus, for a fixed joint action and context, the same semantic outcome σ is selected across task states. However, the corresponding σ-indexed transition and reward functions may still depend on the full extended state and joint action. Hence, the selection of an evaluation principle is independent of the task state, while its induced dynamics remain state-dependent. Different joint action-context pairs may induce the same semantic outcome when they are “indistinguishable” at the level of interaction semantics. For example, low att may map different interactions to recovery mode. Although IJ is defined for individual joint action-context pairs, distinct pairs may share the same semantic outcome. Consequently, the local interpretation induces a global organization of the interaction space. We refer to the resulting equivalence classes as semantic regimes.

# 6 Qualitative Analysis of Interaction Semantics 

6.1 Explicit Semantics vs. Implicit Encoding Standard MDP encoding. Interaction behaviors, such as accepting or rejecting a proposed action following its evaluation under an interaction context, can be encoded directly within transition probabilities T r ji (s′ ∣ s, (ai, a j )) . In such encodings, evaluation and execution may be combined within the induced dynamics rather than represented as separate processes. For example, an advisory transition may combine the probability of accepting a proposal with the execution dynamics of the accepted or fallback action. When similar evaluation principles govern different actions or contexts, their effects may be distributed across multiple state-action transition definitions. In the worst case, an unstructured tabular encoding requires specifying transition and reward dynamics for up to ∣Sext ∣ ⋅ ∣ Ai × Aj ∣ state-action cases. This presents two potential limitations: (1) Limited explicit access: identifying and isolating common evaluation rules may require examining multiple transition definitions, and (2) Modification cost: changing evaluation behavior, e.g., based on attention, may require revisiting several state-action cases. Although a suitably factored MDP can avoid these limitations, identifying such a factorization requires the modeler to distinguish evaluation from execution and recognize shared evaluation principles within potentially intertwined dynamics. 

Semantics-aware encoding. In contrast, our formulation makes this separation an explicit modeling principle. The interpretation function IJ identifies the semantic outcome governing a joint action under its interaction context, while the corresponding evaluation mechanism determines how the proposal is resolved and induces the resulting dynamics. This provides a dedicated representational interface for specifying evaluation principles separately from task-level execution behavior. When distinct interactions share a semantic outcome, their evaluation behavior can be specified through a common mechanism while retaining state- and action-dependent execution dynamics. vii From Proposal to Execution: Modeling Interaction Semantics in Shared Autonomy Consequently, modifying an evaluation mechanism, e.g., ρ(s, a c), can update the induced behavior of the corresponding interactions without independently redefining their transition specifications. The underlying execution models can remain unchanged. The distinction therefore concerns the organization and modification of interaction models, rather than the computational complexity of planning itself. Making evaluation mechanisms explicit allows their structure to be accessed and managed directly, instead of requiring its identification and separation from the induced state-action dynamics. 

6.2 Structural Reuse Through Semantic Outcomes 

Making interaction semantics explicit does not by itself reduce specification effort. In principle, one could define a distinct mechanism for every joint action-context pair, and we now show that semantic outcomes avoid this redundancy. Interaction semantics partition joint action-context pairs into regimes Rσ = {(( ai, a j ), (lau, att )) ∣

IJ (( ai, a j ), (lau, att )) = σ}, each indexed by σ (Theorem 1). Without this structure, transition and reward must be specified for each (s, (ai, a j )) ∈ Sext × ( Ai × Aj ). With semantic outcomes, the evaluation mechanism is specified once per σ and reused across all pairs in Rσ .The reduction is therefore in the number of distinct evaluation mechanisms that must be specified: each regime Rσ

shares a single reusable mechanism. 

(Worst Case.) ∣Σ∣ = ∣ Ai × Aj ∣ ⋅ ∣ L × At ∣, thus no reduction. In structured settings, however, many pairs share an outcome, so ∣Σ∣ ≪ ∣ Ai × Aj ∣ ⋅ ∣ L × At ∣, enabling compact specification by localizing interaction behavior to reusable evaluation mechanisms associated with semantic outcomes rather than distributing it across transition definitions.

# 7 Empirical Evaluation 

Environment and Design Rationale We instantiate the framework in the navigation domain introduced in the case study to examine the interrelation between autonomy, attention, and human-robot capability asymmetry. The environment is an 8 × 9 grid with three bridges leading to isolated corridors. Crossing a bridge commits the agent to a corridor, making the initial decision irreversible. Bridge crossing and subsequent navigation are stochastic and attention-dependent, allowing attention to influence both immediate execution and long-term decision value. This design isolates the effect of interaction regimes (teleoperation, advisory, and full autonomy) and attention while minimizing confounding navigation effects. 

Structural Reuse in the Grid World The previous section established that semantic outcomes enable reuse of evaluation mechanisms. Here we illustrate this property in the implemented domain. The semantic outcome w.r.t. advisory is instantiated through a single attention-dependent evaluation mechanism, where human acceptance ρ(s, a c) depends on attention and the estimated quality of the proposed action. At (2, 2), acceptance increases from very low ( att = 1) to high attention ( att = 4) ( ref. Table 4). Because this mechanism is specified once at the semantic outcome level, modifying the acceptance model changes advisory behavior globally without modifying the planner, transition model, or individual state-action definitions.

# 8 Related Work and Modeling Positioning 

Shared Autonomy and Human-in-the-Loop Planning. Shared autonomy studies how control is distributed between human and AI agents, often via switching between teleoperation and full autonomy [12, 13, 14, 15]. These approaches treat control as an externally selected mode and assume human availability, whereas in [16], Advice-MDPs instead adapt the robot’s model to human preferences so its behavior aligns with operator intent. Recent work jointly selects robot actions and allocates human assistance under uncertainty over operator performance [8]. 

Multi-Agent and Interactive Decision Models. Multi-agent MDPs and Dec-POMDPs [17, 18, 19, 20] model coordination through joint actions that directly induce transitions and rewards, with interaction semantics encoded implicitly in the decision dynamics. Prior work on collaborative decision-making using action suggestions, as in [10], assumes that agents share a common objective. Under this assumption, suggestions provide information that updates the receiving agent’s belief, rather than being subject to an explicit evaluation mechanism governed by potentially different objectives. In contrast, our framework explicitly separates interaction evaluation from execution, allowing proposals to be evaluated under another entity’s context-dependent objectives, knowledge, or decision model. Modified-Action MDPs [21] distinguish actions selected by an agent’s policy from those ultimately executed through an action-modification mechanism, primarily to study how reinforcement learning algorithms respond to such modifica-tions. xFrom Proposal to Execution: Modeling Interaction Semantics in Shared Autonomy While sharing this distinction between selected and executed actions, our framework addresses a different modeling question: how interaction-level proposals are interpreted under context and resolved through evaluation principles that may involve another entity’s objectives or perspectives. The interpretation function makes these principles explicit through semantic outcomes σ, each admitting application-specific evaluation mechanisms. This provides a structured representation for specifying and organizing interaction semantics independently of their induced execution dynamics. The distinction is therefore not one of expressive power, but of modeling focus: rather than analyzing learning under action modification, our framework makes the principles governing interaction evaluation and resolution explicit within the decision-process specification. 

Human-Centered and Cognitive Models. Human decision making is often modeled as context-dependent and resource-bounded, as reflected in theories of bounded rationality [22], dual-process reasoning [23], and heuristic decision making [24]. These perspectives emphasize that evaluation depends on available cognitive resources and context. Theory-of-Mind and human-aware planning model agent beliefs and perspectives explicitly [25]. Our work is complementary: we introduce interaction semantics as a first-class representation of action evaluation and resolution, which can incorporate belief-based or cognitive models when needed [26, 27]. 

Modeling Positioning. Our work is motivated by shared and adjustable autonomy [28, 29, 30], where interaction is typically modeled through extended state variables and control allocation. Among existing shared-autonomy formulations, the closest is SA-RAL-MDP [6], which represents autonomy and support within an MDP and captures behaviors such as authorization, denial, and override through state-dependent transitions. The difference is representational and structural. In SA-RAL-MDP and related approaches, interaction effects are encoded implicitly in transition dynamics, often requiring specification at the level of state-action pairs. In contrast, we introduce an explicit semantic layer that maps joint actions and interaction context to semantic outcomes, thereby making interaction semantics explicit before execution. This induces a factorization in which transition, reward, and discount are defined through evaluation mechanisms associated with semantic outcomes. As a result, interaction rules can be specified, modified, and potentially learned at the level of semantic outcomes rather than redistributed across transition definitions. Thus, while overlapping behaviors can be represented in both frameworks, our formulation exposes interaction semantics as a first-class modeling construct. 

# 9 Discussion 

We introduced a semantics-aware decision framework that explicitly separates the interpretation of interaction-level actions from their induced execution dynamics. Rather than embedding evaluation and execution within state-action transition definitions, the framework provides a dedicated semantic layer in which interaction principles are identified and instantiated through application-specific evaluation mechanisms. This constitutes a representational distinction while preserving compatibility with standard MDP planning. A key structural consequence is that distinct joint action-context pairs may be associated with the same semantic outcome, allowing them to invoke a common evaluation mechanism while retaining state- and action-dependent dynamics. In standard MDP formulations, identifying such commonality requires the modeler to distinguish evaluation from execution and recognize shared evaluation principles within the transition dynamics. Our framework makes this separation an explicit modeling principle, providing a structured interface for specifying, organizing, and modifying evaluation mechanisms across different interactions. The shared-autonomy instantiation illustrates how context-dependent evaluation, particularly through attention, influ-ences interaction behavior and optimal decisions. Although a suitably factored MDP can realize equivalent dynamics, the semantic representation makes the underlying interaction structure explicit rather than leaving its identification to the modeler. Quantifying the resulting advantages in specification complexity and modification effort remains an important direction for future work. The framework requires identifying semantic outcomes and specifying their corresponding evaluation mechanisms, which may be challenging in complex domains. The present instantiation assumes observable interaction context and an application-specific acceptance model. More elaborate evaluation mechanisms could involve reasoning about another xi From Proposal to Execution: Modeling Interaction Semantics in Shared Autonomy agent’s beliefs and objectives, exchanging explanations, or engaging in further interaction to influence how a proposal is evaluated. Formalizing such multi-stage evaluation processes remains an interesting future direction. Further work also includes learning evaluation mechanisms from data, extending the framework to partially observable interaction contexts, and developing richer models of interaction evaluation and resolution.
