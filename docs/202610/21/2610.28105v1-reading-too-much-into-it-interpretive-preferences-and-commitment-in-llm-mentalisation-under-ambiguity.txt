## Abstract 

Warning: This paper contains depression and social anxiety content for research purposes. 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increas-ingly used for interpersonal advice, potentially shaping how users interpret ambiguous social encounters. Existing Theory of Mind evalu-ations typically rely on predefined answers, while uncertainty research overlooks LLMs’ preferred explanations and confidence under psychological distress. To address these gaps, we introduce MentalLoom , a novel evaluation of LLM mentalisation comprising 604 ambigu-ous social episodes, matched depression and social anxiety disclosures, and four-turn dia-logues with unchanged event evidence. Across eight LLMs, disclosures initially elicited more negative explanations overall but more positive narrator-directed interpretations. Scripted re-peated questioning showed greater emotional support, stronger positive narrator-directed en-dorsement, and higher certainty without new event evidence. These findings show that user context can reshape interpretive preferences and commitment while the underlying ambi-guity remains unresolved. 

## 1 Introduction 

Mentalisation is the capacity to interpret thoughts, feelings, beliefs, and intentions of oneself and oth-ers under uncertainty (Fonagy and Bateman, 2007). As illustrated in Figure 1, the same behaviour may admit multiple plausible explanations. The interpretation of such ambiguity is particu-larly relevant to psychological distress. Individuals with higher depressive symptoms and generalised social phobia favour negative interpretations of am-biguous, self-relevant situations (Amin et al., 1998; Berna et al., 2011) and engage in repetitive thinking (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008; Edgar et al., 2024), potentially sustaining attention to unresolved inter-actions. emotional responses (Hoppitt et al., 2010), expla-nations offered to individuals revisiting ambiguous social encounters warrant closer examination. Large language models (LLMs) increasingly par-ticipate in this interpretive process as users seek emotional support and interpersonal advice (Na et al., 2025). When asked to explain another per-son’s behaviour, LLMs may favour particular expla-nations or express confidence in an account despite limited evidence, which may influence how users understand ambiguous social encounters. This con-cern is heightened by findings that greater trust in chatbots is associated with emotional dependence and problematic use (Fang et al., 2025). These findings motivate examining how LLMs interpret interpersonal ambiguity, particularly when users disclose psychological distress or repeatedly seek explanations for the same unresolved event. Existing research leaves three gaps for evalu-ating this process. First, Theory of Mind (ToM) evaluations primarily assess LLMs’ ability to infer and predict consequences of mental states against predefined ground truths (Chen et al., 2024; Stra-arXiv:2610.28105v1 [cs.CL] 20 Oct 2026 chan et al., 2024), offering limited coverage of social events without definitive answers. Second, current emphases on accuracy and consistency met-rics overlook which explanations LLMs favour and how confidently they endorse them. Third, while re-cent work examines models’ ability to infer others’ subjective uncertainty (Sicilia and Alikhani, 2025) and single-turn interpretive closure (Yuan et al., 2026), the effects of psychological disclosures and repeated questioning remain unexplored. To address these questions, we introduce 

MentalLoom , a novel LLM mentalisation task that examines how models interpret ambiguous social behaviour under psychological distress and re-peated questioning. Grounded in validated mea-sures of depression and social anxiety, our eval-uation comprises 604 curated social episodes, matched psychological disclosures, and four-turn dialogues that revisit the same ambiguity without introducing new evidence. Across eight LLMs, psychological disclosures shifted explanations in opposing directions, increasing negativity overall while narrator-directed interpretations were more positive. Repeated questioning was accompanied by greater support and higher final-turn certainty despite unchanged evidence. These findings high-light interpretive preferences and commitment vary with psychological disclosure and dialogue history. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Mentalisation and Theory of Mind 

Existing LLM Theory of Mind (ToM) evaluations largely frame mentalisation as inference against predefined ground truths. Strachan et al. (2024) and ToMBench assess social-cognitive abilities (Chen et al., 2024), while FANToM tests infor-mation tracking asymmetries (Kim et al., 2023). Although effective for assessing correctness and consistency, they offer limited insight into which competing interpretations models favour and how confidently they endorse them when social events lack definitive answers. Recent work examines sub-jective uncertainty (Sicilia and Alikhani, 2025) and single-turn interpretive closure (Yuan et al., 2026). MentalLoom extends this work by evaluating ex-planation valence, narrator-directed framing, and certainty under social ambiguity. 

2.2 Interpretive Commitment in Dialogue 

Beyond the initial interpretation, a further question concerns whether continued interaction changes a model’s commitment to an earlier interpretation. Prior work examines belief revision with new evi-dence (Wilie et al., 2024), increase in confidence from models’ previous answers (Kumaran et al., 2026), and uncertainty loss in clinical text trans-formations (Du et al., 2026). However, how psy-chological disclosures and repeated questioning jointly shape interpretive preferences and com-mitment under unchanged evidence remains un-derexplored. MentalLoom examines these effects through matched low-mood and social-anxiety dis-closures across repeated questioning, tracking in-terpretations, certainty, and emotional support.

## 5 Results 

Analyses covered judgements across 604 ambigu-ous social episodes, 8 LLMs, 3 psychological dis-closure conditions, and 4 turns. Full results for CWIV and all labels in Appendix E. 

5.1 Initial-Turn Results Without Disclosure According to Table 1, mod-els mainly offered neutral explanations framed as possibilities. Negative explanations were more common than positive ones overall, but this pattern was reversed when interpretations concerned the narrator. Responses containing emotional support and high-certainty wording were minimal. Figure 2: (a) Explanation and (b) narrator-directed va-lence; (c) emotional support; (d) certainty (T0–T3). 

Psychological Disclosure Conditions Disclo-sure was associated with greater overall negative explanations, especially for depression. Positive explanations were slightly less frequent with de-pression but more frequent with social anxiety. In contrast, narrator-directed interpretations became more positive under both conditions. Emotional support also increased, particularly with depres-sion, while explanatory language remained pre-dominantly tentative (Table 1). 

5.2 Explanation Changes Across Turns Without Disclosure Repeated questioning was accompanied by greater emotional support and more positive narrator-directed endorsement, with CWIV peaking at T3 (Table 2). Final explanations more often expressed preferences and higher cer-tainty. Overall valence fluctuated, ending slightly more positive and less negative than T0 (Table 11). 

Psychological Disclosure Conditions Disclo-sures extended the initial pattern of greater positiv-ity towards the narrator (Figure 2), especially with social anxiety showing the highest CWIV. This pat-tern coexisted with different trajectories in overall explanation valence. Depression remained more negative than no disclosure, whereas social anxiety shifted from greater negativity at T0 to lower nega-tivity at T3. Emotional support became common, and certainty levels were relatively similar across disclosure conditions (Table 8).

## 6 Discussion 

Interpretive preferences under ambiguity 

Models favoured negative interpretations overall but positive framing toward the narrator. This divergence suggests that plausibility and expressed uncertainty alone emphasised in prior work may not fully capture interpretive preferences. Ex-planatory content, narrator framing and emotional support warrant separate consideration. 

Psychological disclosure changes interpretive framing Disclosure was associated with more negative explanations of the situation but more favourable narrator framing. This suggests that psychological distress may make adverse social interpretations more salient, while prompting reas-surance that protects the narrator’s self-image. 

Interpretive commitment can strengthen despite unchanged evidence Despite unchanged event evidence, responses after dialogue exhibited higher certainty and stronger positive endorsement. A pos-sible explanation is that models interpret repeated requests as dissatisfaction with uncertainty and re-spond with greater reassurance or decisiveness, re-flecting sycophantic tendencies. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We introduced MentalLoom, a controlled evalua-tion of LLM mentalisation under ambiguity. Psy-chological disclosures initially elicited more neg-ative explanations alongside more favourable nar-rator framing. Responses after scripted dialogue showed greater support and stronger commitment despite unchanged evidence. These patterns sug-gest that adaptation to perceived needs for reas-surance and decisiveness may make unresolved situations appear more settled. Limitations 

MentalLoom has several limitations. First , it uses synthetically generated scenarios and English-only interactions, which may not fully capture the com-plexity of real interpersonal experiences or cultural differences in mentalisation. Future work could incorporate more diverse linguistic and cultural contexts to examine the generalisability of these findings. Second , psychological disclosures and repeated questioning were simulated through con-trolled prompts. Although this design enables com-parisons under unchanged event evidence, it may not reflect how individuals experiencing psycholog-ical distress interact with LLMs in natural settings. Future research could investigate more spontaneous and extended conversations. Third , MentalLoom examines model-generated interpretations without measuring their effects on users. Consequently, shifts in explanation valence, emotional support, or expressed certainty cannot directly establish psy-chological benefits or harms. Future user stud-ies are needed to determine how these response patterns influence interpersonal beliefs, emotional wellbeing, and reassurance-seeking behaviours. 

## Ethical Considerations 

I. Data Privacy, Licensing, and Terms. Mental-Loom consists entirely of synthetic scenarios and interactions, with no authentic user exchanges or personally identifiable information included. All third-party resources, including datasets, language models, and computational tools, were employed in compliance with their respective licensing con-ditions and usage policies. 

II. Human Subjects and Privacy. Two clinical psychologists located in Australia voluntarily par-ticipated in the development and assessment of MentalLoom. Both provided informed consent to advise on evaluation criteria and assess the quality of generated scenarios and model outputs. Their contributions were limited to expert consultation and annotation, without the collection or disclosure of personal or identifying information. 

III. Data and Code Availability. The complete MentalLoom dataset, evaluation prompts, and ac-companying code will be publicly released follow-ing publication. 

IV. Intended Use. MentalLoom was developed exclusively to support academic research and the systematic evaluation of LLM behaviour. It should not be used for clinical assessment, individual pro-filing, or consequential decisions involving real individuals. Interpretations of ambiguous interper-sonal situations are shaped by cultural conventions, linguistic context, and social expectations. Accord-ingly, findings derived from this English-language benchmark may not generalise across different pop-ulations or cultural settings. Researchers are en-couraged to exercise appropriate care when using or disseminating benchmark materials, particularly where model responses contain potentially distress-ing interpersonal interpretations. 

V. Use of AI Tools. ChatGPT and Claude assisted with coding and language editing. The authors in-dependently inspected and validated all AI-assisted contributions.
