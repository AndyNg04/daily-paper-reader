## Abstract 

Agentic AI systems are deployed to execute complex tasks across domains such as writing, coding, and data analysis. As execution is offloaded to AI agents, the human role shifts toward oversight. Effective oversight requires recognizing when closer scrutiny is needed, even though flawed outputs may appear convincing. Our formative study ( 𝑁 = 12) revealed that users accepted plausible outputs without thorough evaluation, and only recognized these lapses in retrospect. To examine this systematically, we conducted a controlled within-subject study ( 𝑁 = 36) in which participants completed programming tasks with an agent that produced correct outputs, silent defects, or visible failures. Combining task outcomes, interaction logs, gaze, physiological signals, and self-reports, we examined perceived, behavioral, and effective oversight. Partici-pants were often highly confident while missing silent defects: they performed generic checks, failed to generate diagnostic evidence, or even misinterpreted defect evidence. We discuss how studying oversight across multiple dimensions can support future work on timely, task-calibrated re-engagement.

Generative AI is changing not only what people can do, but also how they are involved in the process. With a single prompt, AI agents can now interpret goals, plan and execute actions, modify artifacts directly, and return completed work. As users continue to offload task execution to AI agents, user roles are shifting towards oversight. However, ensuring that completed work aligns with user intent requires oversight that is active, not passive; users need to trace and understand the agent’s actions, and evaluate if the agent has performed their task correctly. Prior works discuss the necessity of remaining cognitively en-gaged, as past successes cannot guarantee future ones [4]. Repeated acceptance of outputs without thorough evaluation reduces op-portunities for learning and critical thinking, while leaving users less prepared to catch subtle errors and failures, or intervene when an agent’s actions diverge from their intent [18, 22]. However, as agents become increasingly capable, checking every action becomes unnecessary and costly. Moreover, with repeated successes, it be-comes harder for users to constantly remain cognitively engaged, and users may gradually fall out of the loop [1, 11, 25, 29]. We therefore employ the term effective oversight to mean recognizing when closer evaluation is needed and re-engaging at the right mo-ment . Conversely, a lapse in effective oversight would describe the phenomenon where users remain involved in an agentic workflow and accept outputs while insufficiently evaluating the substance of the agent’s work. Effective oversight is often a challenge for users, given that flawed outputs are often generated as complete and plausible re-sponses. Langer describes a similar failure to spot cues while over-looking differences that matter in the current situation [20, 21]. This characterization of effective oversight raises questions on whether users recognize lapses in their oversight, how these lapses mani-fest during interaction, and what prompts them to re-engage. To investigate oversight in agentic interaction, we first conducted a formative study ( 𝑁 = 12) on everyday AI use and discovered that lapses in effective oversight within agentic interactions often occur without the user even being aware of it. Guided by our findings, we identified AI-assisted programming as a prominent context to study oversight, and subsequently conducted a controlled within-subject study with 36 participants completing programming tasks with an AI coding agent. The agent either produced a correct solution, introduced a plausible defect without an external failure signal, or introduced a defect surfaced by a visible failure. Our findings show that participants often missed silent defects despite remaining confident, but readily identified faulty outputs when failures were visible, suggesting that visible failure cues played an important role in prompting effective oversight. Overall, this paper makes two contributions: 

• Insights on how people experienced reduced oversight with AI. Our formative study ( 𝑁 = 12) shows that users can describe reduced oversight but may not recognize it as it happens. We share several themes spanning across interaction behaviour, attention, and bodily state, as well as explore and chart the directions this exploration opens. 

• Findings on ‘oversight’ under repeated agent interac-tions based on multimodal data analysis. Through a controlled study ( 𝑁 = 36) combining task outcomes, in-teraction logs, gaze, and physiological signals, we examine how oversight changes between interactions with correct, silently flawed, and visibly failing agent outputs, and how these patterns vary with user programming experience. We reveal differences between feeling vigilant and being vigilant and discuss their implications for measuring oversight and supporting timely re-engagement. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Cognitive Engagement and Oversight in Human–AI Interaction 

Concerns about maintaining human cognitive engagement have long accompanied increasing levels of automation. Bainbridge’s “ironies of automation” highlighted how automating routine exe-cution can shift people toward supervisory roles while reducing the experience needed to intervene effectively when automation fails [1]. Subsequent work showed that reliable automation can foster complacency and reduced monitoring, while higher levels of automation can leave users “out of the loop” and less able to under-stand system state or intervene effectively [11, 29, 30]. Related work on automation bias and reliance further shows that people may defer to automated recommendations rather than independently seek or evaluate information, with reliance shaped by factors such as trust, workload, and system reliability [10, 23, 28, 31]. As sys-tems take over more of the execution, the human role increasingly shifts toward oversight. Yet sustained attention and evaluation can become more difficult as continuous involvement in the task de-creases. Generative AI extends this shift from supervising automated processes to evaluating AI-generated work. As users delegate more cognitive work to AI, studies have raised concerns about cognitive offloading and reduced critical-thinking effort [14, 22]. Delegation does not necessarily remove the need for human evaluation. Users must still decide what to delegate, assess generated outputs, and determine when further scrutiny is needed [35]. When such evalu-ation is insufficient, users may over-rely on AI and accept incorrect recommendations [2, 6]. This has motivated interventions such as cognitive forcing functions, which encourage users to engage more deliberately with a problem before or while receiving AI assistance [2, 6]. However, much of this work characterizes cognitive engage-ment through its outcomes, such as reliance on incorrect advice or task performance [2, 6], through users’ retrospective reports of critical-thinking effort [22], or by evaluating interventions designed to increase deliberation [2, 6]. Less is understood about how the quality of oversight changes during ongoing interaction , particu-larly when users remain behaviorally involved with an AI system and nothing explicitly signals that greater scrutiny is necessary. Human involvement alone does not necessarily imply effective oversight [38]: a user may continue prompting, reviewing, or ap-proving an agent’s actions while attending only superficially to details that require human judgment. We examine this in repeated interactions with increasingly autonomous AI agents.

## 2.2 From AI-Assisted Coding to Agentic Programming 

AI-assisted programming has evolved from supporting developers during coding to increasingly delegating programming tasks to AI systems. Code-completion tools operate within developer-led work-flows, leaving programmers to inspect, accept, modify, or reject generated suggestions [26]. More recent coding agents can instead take higher-level goals, plan changes, modify files, execute code and tests, and iterate toward solutions with substantially less devel-oper involvement [4, 33]. This progression represents not simply an increase in generation capability, but a redistribution of work between developers and AI systems. Studies of agentic development workflows show that agents can take substantial responsibility for execution while humans retain responsibility for reviewing, steering, and accepting their work [5, 7, 37]. Yet verification can itself be demanding. Developers must evaluate implementations they may not have directly pro-duced or closely followed [25], and repeated verification can impose cognitive load and contribute to fatigue [12]. Prior work has there-fore examined how developers critically engage with generated code and how deeper engagement might be supported [16, 18]. At the same time, studies of AI-assisted programming raise concerns that increased generation may not be accompanied by equally rig-orous validation [27]. As coding agents become more autonomous, effective oversight increasingly depends on how developers evalu-ate work they did not directly produce. This challenge is particularly consequential when an agent ap-pears to have completed a task successfully. Real-world coding-agent interactions reveal failures arising from mismatches between what developers intend and what agents interpret, implement, or report [34]. Moreover, developers can have difficulty distinguishing correct from incorrect AI-generated artifacts even while remaining confident in their judgments [17]. Some failures provide clear cues for further investigation, such as failed tests, execution errors, or visibly incorrect results. When an implementation appears plausi-ble and available checks pass, however, users must independently determine whether the evidence of success is sufficient. Less is un-derstood about whether developers recognize when such apparent success warrants deeper scrutiny and whether their evaluation can reveal errors that are not externally signaled. 

## 2.3 Modeling Cognitive Engagement from Behavior 

Understanding the quality of human oversight requires looking beyond whether users remain active in an interaction. Prior work suggests that differences in cognitive engagement can be reflected in how people behave, allocate attention, and respond physiolog-ically while performing a task [8, 19, 24]. Behavioral measures such as pauses, response timing, and action sequences can reflect changes in how users approach a task [9, 32]; gaze measures provide evidence about the allocation of visual attention; and physiological measures have been studied as indicators of workload, arousal, and mental effort [3, 19, 36]. These signals offer potential evidence about cognitive engagement, but their meaning is rarely unambiguous. The same behavior or physiological response may reflect different cognitive processes depending on the task and moment in which it occurs. Cognitive engagement is therefore better characterized by considering multiple signals and the context in which they occur, rather than interpreting individual measures in isolation [24]. This need for context is particularly important in Human–AI interaction, where users’ actions can provide task-grounded evi-dence of how they engage with AI-generated outputs. Interaction traces such as prompting, accepting or rejecting suggestions, edit-ing, testing, and sequences of actions have been used to characterize how developers interact with and evaluate generated code [16, 26]. More broadly, sequences of programming actions can reveal strate-gies that are difficult to infer from individual low-level behaviors alone [13]. For oversight, however, observable activity does not nec-essarily imply substantive evaluation. Continued interaction may reflect careful inspection, but it may also consist of routine actions that provide little evidence that the agent’s work has been critically examined. Conversely, periods of limited interaction may involve careful reading or reasoning. Thus, interpreting behavioral traces as evidence of oversight requires considering not only whether users interact, but what they are doing and how those actions relate to the task and the agent’s work. Self-report provides a complementary perspective by capturing users’ perceived effort, workload, confidence, and engagement [15, 24]. Yet subjective, behavioral, physiological, and performance-based measures need not converge [3, 19]. This distinction is par-ticularly important for human oversight of AI agents: users may perceive themselves as attentive and exhibit behaviors consistent with evaluation without actually scrutinizing the aspects of an agent’s work that require human judgment. Effective oversight therefore cannot be inferred from perceived or observable engage-ment alone. Understanding oversight requires considering these in-dicators alongside whether users perform task-relevant evaluation capable of revealing problems in the agent’s work. This motivates examining oversight across perceived, behavioral, and effective dimensions, and asking when these dimensions align—and when they diverge. 

## 3 Formative Study 

We conducted a formative interview study to understand how peo-ple experience changes in their oversight during everyday AI use. We aimed to identify when users reduce oversight, whether they recognize this change, and how it might manifest in observable behavior. This formative study provided an empirical basis for se-lecting the context, manipulations, and measurements used in our controlled study. 

## 3.1 Participants & Procedure 

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 12 regular users of generative AI (( 𝑀 age = 27 .7); 5 Females and 7 Males). Participants included four undergraduate students (F2, F3, F7, F10), four gradu-ate students (F5, F6, F11, F12), and four working professionals ([F1, F4, F8, F9]). Participants used generative AI for activities including programming, writing, learning, and information seeking. Partici-pants first described how they used AI in their everyday work. We then asked them to recall situations in which they had accepted or acted on AI output without carefully evaluating it. Follow-up questions examined what contributed to these situations, whether

participants recognized them as they occurred, and what changes they noticed in their interaction, attention, or bodily state. Partici-pants also reflected on the benefits and risks of reduced engagement and whether an external cue might help them re-engage. The full interview protocol is provided in the Appendix Section A. We used a primarily deductive coding approach, with broad analytic categories derived from the interview guide. We coded excerpts describing participants’ AI-use practices, experiences of reduced evaluation, perceived triggers and consequences, observ-able signs, and awareness of these changes. The research team regularly discussed interviews from the initial participants to re-fine the interview guide, identify recurring codes, and clarify code definitions. The first author then coded the full dataset, while a second author reviewed the resulting coding. We compared related codes across participants and organized them into higher-level pat-terns. Below, we describe these patterns and how they informed the design of our subsequent controlled study. 

## 3.2 Findings 

Oversight was calibrated to its perceived value . Participants did not consider close evaluation necessary in every interaction. Instead, they adjusted their oversight according to the perceived stakes, available time, interest in the task, and confidence in the AI. Under time pressure, participants prioritized completing the task over understanding how the output had been produced. F5 explained, “When I am rushing, I am more mindless because I just need to get it done, even if it is lower quality." Similarly, low-stakes tasks gave participants little reason to invest in verification. As F8 described, “If the task is high-priority or high-value . . . I will not directly use the output the AI gives me," whereas low-stakes outputs were more readily accepted. Repeated success also reduced the perceived value of continued checking. F1 described initially examining the system’s work but later deciding, “Once I know it is doing a good job, then I become mindless. I think, Okay, I can offload this. Let it do it." Thus, reduced oversight was not always an indiscriminate tendency to trust AI. It was often a contextual response to the apparent costs and benefits of further evaluation. Participants also recognized benefits to this offloading. It saved time, reduced effort, and allowed them to focus their attention else-where. However, they described a longer-term tradeoff involving learning and skill. F11 explained, “If we do not use AI tools, we will be behind, because others are using AI tools . . . It is a tradeoff." F12 similarly felt that AI made work faster but that “my coding skills are getting rusty because I generate everything from AI." These accounts distinguish efficient delegation from the loss of evaluation needed to learn from, take responsibility for, or intervene in the work. 

Procedural interaction did not guarantee evaluation . Partici-pants described remaining active in an AI interaction without neces-sarily reasoning about its content. They could continue prompting, approve actions, or repeatedly ask the AI to fix a problem while focusing only on whether the final result worked. F3 explained, 

“You know what the end goal is, and you know what the final result should be, but you do not care what commands or outputs the AI is giving as long as the end goal is achieved." The same participant emphasized that even a long interaction could remain mindless when follow-ups consisted mainly of responses such as "yes," "no," or "this did not work, fix this." 

Agentic tools appeared to strengthen this separation between participation and evaluation. F3 contrasted earlier copy-and-paste interactions with coding agents that iterate independently: “Now, with Codex and similar tools, you ask it one shot and it does it. You do not have to go back and forth because they iterate on their own . . . you do not think." Participants commonly evaluated only the visi-ble result. F2 described asking an agent to implement something, checking a few functions, and continuing without reading the gen-erated code. F11 similarly stated, “I do not go through much of what it is doing unless it is not working." 

These accounts suggest that the amount of interaction is not, by itself, evidence of effective oversight. Users may remain proce-durally involved while attending only to surface indicators such as whether the agent has finished, whether the output appears plausible, or whether the application runs. 

Awareness ranged from deliberate offloading to retrospec-tive recognition . Participants differed in whether they recognized their reduced engagement. Some intentionally chose not to evalu-ate low-priority work. F3 stated, “I know I am doing it. I know it is not good . . . I do not think it is an awareness problem. It is more of a discipline problem." F5 similarly described remaining in control and intentionally becoming less engaged when the task did not warrant greater effort. Other accounts indicated that reduced oversight could occur without being noticed at the time. F12 explained, "If I am really busy, I might not even realize that I am doing it mindlessly." Several participants described recognizing the problem only when an out-put violated their expectations. F6 recalled that after an AI cited an article that did not exist, the failure "snapped me out of it" and served as a reminder "to put in more effort, verify things, and check things." 

These findings complicate any simple claim that users are either aware or unaware of lapses in effective oversight. Awareness was contextual: users could deliberately offload evaluation in one situa-tion but fail to notice inadequate oversight in another. Importantly, a visible failure could expose this mismatch and reactivate scrutiny. Whether that re-engagement persists beyond the immediate failure, however, remained unclear. 

Reduced oversight had diverse observable signs . Participants associated reduced oversight with changes in how they prompted, read, verified, and distributed their attention. Short or underspec-ified prompts were mentioned frequently. F1 explained, “If I am thinking about it, my prompt is long . . . If I am mindless, I am more like, ‘Can you do this?’" Participants also described fewer substan-tive follow-ups, limited external verification, skimming summaries, and approving commands without examining them. Attention often shifted away while the agent worked. F2 de-scribed giving an agent a prompt, doing something else, and return-ing only to inspect what it had built. F5 similarly reported scrolling through YouTube while Codex worked in the background. Reported signs also extended beyond interaction logs: F3 associated reduced engagement with tiredness and slouching. No single behavior consistently represented effective or inef-fective oversight. For example, short prompts could reflect either

shallow engagement or an efficiently specified task, while extensive back-and-forth interaction could still consist of automatic correc-tion requests. The diversity of reported signs therefore motivated examining oversight through multiple signals rather than presup-posing a single behavioral marker. 

## 3.3 Implications for the Controlled Study 

The formative study was conducted with a limited pool of partici-pants, and as such, a further controlled study was necessary to better examine how effective oversight might change during and across agentic interactions. Nevertheless, the formative study helped guide the design of our controlled study in four ways. First, participants most readily described reduced evaluation in AI-assisted program-ming. Coding agents could autonomously produce complete, ex-ecutable outputs, while the correctness of those outputs could be objectively evaluated in the case of testable code generation. We therefore selected programming as a controlled setting in which be-havioral oversight and task outcomes could be examined together. Second, participants often relied on visible functionality to decide whether further evaluation was needed. We therefore designed two failure types that differed in whether the incorrect solution produced an external failure signal. Silent failures let us examine whether users initiated scrutiny independently, whereas visible failures let us examine how oversight changed when the need for human judgment was made explicit. Third, participants described failures as events that could snap 

them back into active evaluation. We therefore studied repeated interactions to determine whether re-engagement remained limited to the visible failure or carried into subsequent tasks. Finally, the reported signs of reduced oversight spanned inter-action behavior, visual attention, bodily state, and subjective expe-rience. We therefore captured multiple complementary measures in our main study: task outcomes to assess oversight effectiveness, interaction telemetry and gaze to capture observable behavior and attention, physiological signals to reflect bodily responses, and repeated self-reports to assess participants’ perceived engagement. 

## 4 AI-assisted Programming Study 

Guided by our formative findings, we designed a controlled labora-tory study in which, we examined how users’ oversight changes across repeated interactions with a coding agent and whether a vis-ible failure prompts them to re-engage more closely. We combined behavioral, gaze, physiological, and self-report measures to capture different aspects of their engagement. Smart glasses (Meta Aria Gen 2 glasses 1) were used to record gaze and egocentric activity during these interactions, while a Garmin watch was used to record physiological data. Figure 1 explains the basic study setup.

## 4.2 Programming Interface 

We developed a desktop study interface that combined a task brief, a Python code editor, and an AI coding agent (Refer to Figure 2). The task brief presented all requirements for the current feature and could be reopened at any time. The editor contained scaffolded code with syntax highlighting and supported normal indentation and direct editing. Participants could also execute their own examples in a separate Run code area. The coding agent operated autonomously: when participants requested a change, the agent’s edit was applied directly to the file. Participants could subsequently inspect, modify, test, or discuss the resulting code with the agent. Each task included hidden pre-merge checks. Participants were required to use the coding agent and obtain a passing result before handing off the feature. However, the introductory instructions explained that the checks covered only basic functionality and did not guarantee that every requirement or edge case was correct. This distinction allowed us to examine whether participants treated a passing check as sufficient evidence or performed additional verifi-cation through code inspection, customized examples, or follow-up questions.

After the first round, partici-pants were shown the results for their first three solutions before continuing to the second round. 

## 4.4 Data Collection 

We recorded participants’ prompts, agent responses, code changes, manual program executions, use of the pre-merge checks, access to the task brief, and task completion times. Participants wore Aria Gen 2 smart glasses to capture first-person video, gaze, and pho-toplethysmography (PPG). They also wore a Garmin smartwatch, which provided additional physiological measures. We separately recorded participants’ screens, keystrokes, and cursor movements. All data streams were synchronized with the study interaction logs. After each task, participants completed the NASA-TLX workload questionnaire. They also rated how thoroughly they had evaluated the agent’s suggestions and how confident they were that their final solution met all specified requirements. At the end of the study, we conducted a brief semi-structured interview where participants reflected on how they interacted with the coding agent, evaluated its outputs, and results presented be-tween rounds. We asked whether these results affected their con-fidence or verification behavior, whether they changed their ap-proach during the second round, and what consequences might arise from relying on the agent without sufficiently examining its work.

## 5.1 General Findings 

5.1.1 Sample and data quality. The core cohort comprised 36 par-ticipants, each assigned six main tasks across two rounds: two Silent Defect (SD), two No Defect (ND), and two Blatant Defect (BD) tasks. This produced 216 task instances. Two participants lacked usable outcome data for their final task. One participant recorded no in-teraction with their final SD task. The other engaged substantially with their final BD task (10 agent turns and six pre-merge check attempts), but its outcome was not recorded. Both losses occurred at the sixth and final task position, suggesting end-of-session attri-tion rather than systematic loss under a particular condition. We retained these instances where their available data supported the analysis but excluded them from analyses requiring a final task outcome. This yielded 71 SD, 72 ND, and 71 BD tasks with usable outcomes (214 in total). 

5.1.2 Task Effects. Silent-defect resolution increased from 10/36 tasks in Round 1 (27.8%) to 18/35 in Round 2 (51.4%). Because task sets were randomized across rounds, this difference was not attribut-able to a fixed task-set assignment. However, resolution still varied substantially by individual task, ranging from 8% for Conflicts to 75% for Billing. We therefore account for task identity in sub-sequent analyses. Full task-level results are reported in Appendix Table 5. 

5.1.3 Individual differences. Self-reported Python proficiency was associated with effective oversight. Silent-defect resolution increased from 16.7% among beginners ( 𝑛 = 6) to 39.1% among intermediate programmers ( 𝑛 = 23) and 57.1% among advanced programmers (𝑛 = 7; Spearman’s 𝜌 = .34, 𝑝 = .041). In contrast, self-reported experience with agentic AI tools was not associated with defect resolution ( 𝑝 > .48). Participants who frequently delegated multi-step tasks to AI were therefore no more thorough or effective than less frequent users. We also observed no association between gen-der and either measure (all 𝑝 > .56; 23 men, 12 women, and one non-binary participant). 

## 5.2 Confidence and Perceived Thoroughness Capture Different Aspects of Oversight 

After each main task, we asked participants to rate, on a five-point scale, (1) how confident they were that their final solution met all the requirements and (2) how thoroughly they had checked the coding agent’s suggestions. Although both measures concern participants’ perceptions of their own oversight, they showed notably different relationships with behavior and outcome.

## 5.3 Behavior Following an External Defect Warning 

The ND, SD, and BD conditions were designed to examine how participants behaved before and after receiving an external signal that the agent could be wrong. We expected participants to ap-proach an SD task similarly to an ND task before encountering such a signal. We further expected the failed pre-merge check and accompanying comment in the BD condition to increase scrutiny of subsequent agent output, with the strongest effect occurring on the task immediately following BD. At an aggregate level, encountering a BD warning did not im-prove subsequent silent-defect detection. Participants caught 38.89% of silent defects when SD occurred before BD and 40% when SD occurred after BD. These catch rates were statistically indistin-guishable ( 𝜒 2 = 0.00, 𝑝 = 1.00). Task duration also did not differ significantly depending on whether SD occurred before or after BD (𝑝 = .170). However, because different tasks appeared in different sequence positions and varied substantially in difficulty, the aggre-gate before-versus-after comparison cannot cleanly separate the effect of prior BD exposure from the effect of task identity. However, the overall before-and-after comparison masked dif-ferences in the timing of the subsequent silent-defect task. When a silent defect immediately followed a visible failure ( BD → SD ), participants detected 50% of defects (12/24 tasks; 12 participants). A task-level Fisher’s exact test produced a similar result (p=.023); we treat this only as a sensitivity analysis because multiple tasks were contributed by the same participants. Because task identity differed across sequence positions, we additionally compared performance after residualizing each task by its own mean resolution rate. ## 5.4 Input inactivity reflected effective oversight 

Across 21 keyboard and cursor measures, silent-defect and no-defect tasks differed in completion time but not in how much participants typed or moved after accounting for task duration. However, silent defects that were resolved contained a substantially greater propor-tion of time without typing than those that were missed (.622 vs. .425, 𝑝 < .001, Cohen’s 𝑑 = 1.29). Longer gaps without keystrokes and greater variability in typing rhythm followed the same descrip-tive pattern, whereas keystroke rate, deletions, navigation inputs, cursor travel, and cursor speed did not distinguish the outcomes. Although periods without input are compatible with participants pausing to read the implementation, revisit the requirements, or reason about possible edge cases. Evaluation effort may therefore be more visible when interaction stops than in the amount of ob-servable activity.

## 5.5 Understanding Behavioral Oversight in Agentic Interactions 

Our analysis was informed by prior work on interaction-strategy mining [13] and taxonomies of AI-assisted programming [26]. We manually coded 216 participant-authored messages to the agent, as well as code entered in the manual execution panel, to identify participants’ interaction and verification strategies. Message codes captured requests for implementation, verification, explanation, diagnosis, and correction, while scratch code indicated attempts to test the implementation with participant-generated inputs. The first author developed the codebook using a diverse pilot sample and then coded the complete dataset. A second author independently coded a stratified 24% sample, achieving an agreement of 75% (linear-weighted Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.632). Disagreements were discussed and resolved. When the meaning of a message or action was unclear, we examined the corresponding first-person video segment for context. We then combined these manually coded data with automatically recorded actions, including task-brief revisits, manual program executions, pre-merge checks, code edits, and task completion. This produced a chronological record of oversight behavior for each participant–task pair. 

5.5.1 Where Oversight Breaks Down. To characterize how partici-pants evaluated the agent’s output before completing a silent-defect task, we traced all 71 task instances through these stages (Figure 5). To illustrate, consider the Billing task, in which participants asked the agent to reconcile a member’s charges into a running total, dis-tinguishing between approved, refunded, and cancelled amounts. The seeded defect ignored refunds instead of subtracting them. Generic evaluation, such as rerunning the provided example or asking the agent to check its code, would not necessarily expose this defect. Even a task-relevant test involving cancelled charges would not reveal it. In contrast, testing an approved charge together with an equal refund would expose the error because the balance should be zero. Effective oversight required participants to notice this mismatch, correct the refund logic, and verify the revised solu-tion. The defect could also be identified directly by inspecting the code. Most instances (65/71, 91.5%) included at least one evaluative action beyond the mandatory pre-merge check, whereas six relied on that check alone. The relevance of these additional actions varied substantially. In 23 instances, evaluation remained generic, such as rerunning an existing example or asking the agent to "check" the solution without specifying what to examine. In 40 instances, participants evaluated risks associated with the task requirements. Of these, 35 produced evidence capable of revealing the seeded defect, whereas five examined plausible but unrelated risks. Even revealing evidence did not guarantee successful oversight. In five of the 35 instances, participants produced evidence exposing the defect but did not recognize its implication. Participants recog-nized the defect in the remaining 30 instances, although four did not successfully resolve it. Thus, 26 defects were resolved through task-relevant evaluation. Two remaining defects were resolved through manual code inspection and editing.

## 5.6 Summary 

Participants remained confident even when they missed consequen-tial defects, and feedback on their performance after a round did not significantly improve this calibration in the following round. Perceived thoroughness, however, reflected relative changes in ver-ification effort and defect detection within the same participant. Self-reports and multimodal signals captured different parts of this process. Confidence remained high even when silent defects were missed, while perceived thoroughness was informative mainly relative to a participant’s own baseline. Longer task duration and periods without typing were associated with successful defect reso-lution, but gaze and physiological measures did not provide reliable standalone markers. Also, we see preliminary evidence of short-lived re-engagement. Participants were more likely to detect a silent defect when it im-mediately followed a visible failure, but this advantage diminished when a correct interaction occurred between them. Given the small sequence groups, particularly the six participants in the gap condi-tion, we interpret this as suggestive rather than conclusive evidence that failure-triggered re-engagement may decay rapidly. 

## 6 Discussion 

Across our investigation, we found that effective oversight can break down even when people appear actively engaged with an agent’s work. Participants frequently expressed high confidence after missing a silent defect, and this confidence did not recalibrate after they had seen the outcomes of the previous round. Behav-iorally, participants who detected a defect tended to spend longer on the task and showed more pauses without typing. Physiolog-ical measures, however, showed substantial between-participant variation and little consistent within-participant differentiation, limiting their usefulness as general indicators of oversight while suggesting that any sensing-based intervention may need to be personalized. We also found suggestive evidence that a visible fail-ure could briefly prompt closer scrutiny of the next task, although this effect appeared to fade quickly. In the sections that follow, we

## 6.1 Cognitive Engagement and Delegation 

Cognitive engagement cannot be inferred simply from whether users remain active. Participants inspected code, ran tests, and sent follow-up prompts, but these actions alone did not show whether they had evaluated the assumptions on which correctness depended. Therefore, we coded participants’ prompts, scratch runs, tests, and inspection behavior according to whether they produced evidence relevant to the seeded defect. This coding does not capture every internal judgment, but it distinguishes generic activity from ob-servable, task-relevant evaluation. As shown in Figure 5, effective oversight required participants to identify what could fail, pro-duce evidence capable of exposing it, and interpret that evidence correctly. The interviews help explain these behavioral differences. P29 relied on the available checks: "I ran the test cases and pre-merge check, and when both passed, I assumed the solution was correct." 

Others delegated parts of the evaluation to the agent. P33 reported, 

"I mostly copied the instructions and asked it to handle edge cases," 

while P27 asked the agent to generate tests and then ran them. These actions showed that participants attempted verification, but not necessarily that they understood what risks were being tested or what remained unchecked. By contrast, P26 noticed that an important requirement had not been exercised: "The supplied example did not test case-insensitive be-havior. When I tested that requirement myself, it failed." This required recognizing the limits of the available evidence and constructing a diagnostic example. Yet generating relevant evidence was still not sufficient in every case. Some participants produced outputs that exposed a defect but did not interpret them correctly. These findings extend concerns about cognitive offloading in AI-supported work [14, 22]. Agentic systems allow users to dele-gate implementation, but users may also delegate the reasoning needed to evaluate it. Asking the same agent to "check the code" can create visible verification without an independent judgment about where the implementation may fail. Delegating test generation is not inherently ineffective. Its value depends on whether users un-derstand what was tested, recognize what remains untested, and can interpret the results. 

## 6.2 Authorship and Responsibility 

Agentic programming creates a mismatch between authorship and responsibility: users remain responsible for evaluating code they did not write or closely follow. Our formative interviews anticipated this tension. F4 explained, "If we are not acting on something, and we are letting something else act on our behalf, then it can be adverse for us in terms of being responsible for our actions." Delegating execution does not remove responsibility for the result, but it may reduce the understanding needed to evaluate it. 

> 11

The post-study interviews show how this mismatch becomes more difficult when generated code exceeds the user’s knowledge. P29 explained, "I understood the expected output but not all of the implementation, so I accepted the code and ran the test cases." P39 similarly accepted generated code because reproducing the work independently would take too long. Consistent with these accounts, programming experience was associated with silent-defect detec-tion, yet less-experienced participants were not correspondingly less confident. Coding agents may therefore enable users to produce implementations beyond what they can independently evaluate, while leaving them responsible for the outcomes. The amount of responsibility users assumed also depended on the perceived stakes of the task. In both studies, participants described relying on final outputs, accepting generated work under time pressure, and intentionally delegating thought during familiar or low-stakes tasks. P24 stated, "It depends on how serious the task is. For a high-stakes task, I would check more. These tasks felt low stakes, so I did not feel the need to put in as much effort." Such delegation may be reasonable when the consequences are limited, as time and effort are saved. The risk arises when users rely on cues such as plausible code and passing checks to decide that deeper evaluation is unnecessary, even when those cues provide incomplete evidence. This reflects Langer’s account of mindlessness: familiar cues guide behavior while distinctions that matter in the current situation are overlooked [20, 21]. Our results suggest that simply leaving final approval to the user is therefore not sufficient to establish meaningful human re-sponsibility. Participants often remained procedurally involved, yet comparatively few evaluations progressed far enough to solve and correctly interpret the silent defect. In such cases, users may retain formal responsibility without having developed the task understanding needed to exercise it effectively. For agentic sys-tems, supporting responsibility may therefore require more than preserving a final confirmation step: systems should help users maintain enough task-relevant understanding to evaluate conse-quential decisions, particularly when generated work exceeds their own expertise. 

## 6.3 Failure Cues and Effective Oversight 

Visible failure gave users a clear reason to question the agent. Sev-eral participants reported checking later tasks more carefully after encountering or learning about a failure. P42 explained, "I failed one of the three tasks even though I thought I had done well on all three. That made me think there might be a trap in the questions, so I checked more thoroughly in the second round." P28 similarly reported treating later outputs as potentially suspicious after noticing an obvious error. These accounts support our behavioral evidence that failure cues can interrupt routine acceptance and prompt re-engagement. However, this effect appeared short-lived. Silent defects were more often detected when they immediately followed a visible failure, but this advantage diminished when a correct interaction occurred between them. Apparent success may therefore restore trust and again reduce the perceived need for scrutiny. This re-sembles established patterns of automation complacency and over-reliance: reliable performance reduces monitoring, while apparent success encourages users to defer to the system rather than seek independent evidence [1, 28, 29, 31]. As discussed earlier, the depth of checking depended on its per-ceived value. Participants reduced oversight when tasks were low stakes, time was limited, or the generated code was too large or unfamiliar to inspect efficiently. Both studies also raised concerns about learning, critical thinking, and the gradual loss of opportuni-ties to reason through problems independently. P31 distinguished completion from understanding: "AI may help me finish that work, but whether it helps me learn is a different question. Often, it does not." Participants therefore valued deeper involvement in learning and research contexts, while accepting greater delegation when obtaining a working result was the main goal. This creates an important tension. Delegation can reduce imme-diate effort, but effective oversight still depends on the knowledge and reasoning needed to evaluate the resulting work. Effective over-sight should not require constant vigilance, as delegating routine work can be appropriate. The problem is miscalibration: users may not recognize when limited checking is insufficient or when pass-ing checks provide weak evidence of correctness. This was visible in the gap between confidence, observable evaluation, and defect detection. The challenge may be particularly important for less-experienced users, as coding agents can enable them to produce implementations that exceed their ability to evaluate independently. 

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Work 

Behavioral oversight is not a direct measure of internal cognitive state. Interaction logs capture observable actions, but limited ac-tivity may reflect active reasoning, while frequent activity may remain superficial. Our inactivity-related findings reinforce the im-portance of considering the timing and context of behavior rather than activity alone. We therefore characterize observable oversight rather than claim direct detection of reduced oversight or mind-lessness. Future work should validate behavioral indicators against independently coded, task-relevant reasoning at finer temporal resolution. We acknowledge several limitations in the study design. First, we used short Python tasks, seeded defects, and a controlled coding agent. This ensured comparable exposure to correct, silently de-fective, and visibly failing outputs, but did not capture the scale or uncertainty of real development and today’s capable AI agents. This may have affected how participants inspected and corrected the code, differing from their natural behavior. Future studies should examine naturally occurring failures in larger repositories using current coding agents. Second, silent-defect detectability varied sub-stantially by task, from 8% for CONFLICTS to 75% for BILLING. This difference did not emerge during piloting. Although task assign-ment and order were randomized, the aggregate findings remain specific to the defects sampled. Several exploratory analyses may have been underpowered. In particular, the visible-failure carryover comparison included only six participants in the gap sequence, and changes across rounds may also reflect learning, task identity, or position. The findings suggest that failure can prompt re-engagement, but do not establish how reliable or lasting this effect is. Larger and longitudinal studies should independently manipulate warning timing and examine 

> 12

how oversight changes as users develop familiarity and trust over time. Our study did not evaluate specific interventions, but it identi-fies directions for task-calibrated re-engagement. Future systems could clarify what passing checks establish, highlight untested re-quirements, and support users in constructing and interpreting task-relevant evidence. Such support could be explored after fail-ures, at consequential handoffs, or when existing checks provide limited coverage, while accounting for task stakes, familiarity, and reversibility. Future studies should determine whether these ap-proaches improve oversight, for whom, and at what cost to work-load and the benefits of delegation. Finally, the sensing analyses were limited by measurement qual-ity and granularity. Whole-task physiological summaries may ob-scure brief changes in engagement. Clear differences between partic-ipants also suggest that universal thresholds may be unsuitable. Our findings do not establish reliable real-time detection, but motivate event-aligned, person-specific models validated against indepen-dently coded oversight. 

## 7 Conclusion 

When AI agents execute more of the work, the human role shifts to overseeing the work. Our results show that this oversight fails in a specific, measurable way. Across 36 participants, oversight broke down not because people stopped acting, but because their actions didn’t amount to the required evaluation. Participants ran tests, inspected outputs, but never produced the one piece of evidence that would have exposed a silent defect. Sometimes, they even misinterpreted the generated evidence. Confidence was flat across tasks, even though defect detection varied substantially across tasks. In short, the code looked fine to them, leading participants to believe their evaluation was sufficient when it was not. By separating perceived, behavioral, and effective oversight, we show that no single proxy-activity, confidence, or final correctness captures whether a person is truly overseeing an agent. This does not imply users need constant vigilance; exhaustive review would forfeit the point of delegation. It is that oversight must be calibrated :users need help recognizing when the evidence in front of them can-not support the conclusion they are about to draw. Visible failures could prompt this re-engagement, but the effect was not always sustained. Through this study, we highlight a central challenge for agentic interaction: understanding when deeper oversight is needed. We identify where oversight breaks down and show how task context, interaction behavior, and person-specific patterns provide a foundation for addressing this challenge.

## A Formative Interview Protocol 

The formative study consisted of a 30-minute semi-structured inter-view conducted in person (10/12) or over Zoom (2/12). The interview explored participants’ everyday use of AI assistants, moments of reduced oversight or evaluation, perceived consequences of such interactions, observable behavioral signals, and reactions to possi-ble forms of re-engagement support. The questions below served as a guide; the interviewer used follow-up probes where appropriate. 

1. Introduction, Consent, and Demographics. The researcher intro-duced the study as an interview about people’s experiences using AI assistants during everyday work. Participants were informed that there were no right or wrong answers and that the study focused on their own experiences and perceptions. (1) What AI tools do you currently use, and for what kinds of tasks? (2) How frequently do you use AI assistants? (3) What kinds of activities do you most often use them for (e.g., writing, coding, learning, brainstorming, information seeking, or decision-making)? 

2. Current AI-Assisted Interaction Practices. Participants were asked to describe how they typically interact with AI assistants and how they evaluate or use AI-generated outputs. (1) Can you walk me through a recent example where you used an AI assistant for writing, coding, learning, brainstorming, information seeking, or decision-making? (2) When an AI gives you a response, what do you usually do with it? (3) How do you normally decide whether an AI-generated re-sponse is correct, useful, or appropriate? (4) Are there times when you inspect or evaluate the response carefully, and other times when you move forward with less checking? What tends to differ between those situations? 

3. Experiences of Reduced Oversight. Participants were introduced to the idea that, during some AI-assisted interactions, users may continue progressing through a task while devoting relatively little attention to evaluating the AI’s reasoning or output. The interviewer provided the following description: Sometimes people continue working with an AI assis-tant while doing relatively little checking or evalua-tion of what the AI has produced. They may accept, use, or move forward with an output without fully considering whether it is correct or appropriate for the task. Participants were then asked: (1) Does this description resonate with your own experiences using AI? (2) Can you recall a situation where you accepted or contin-ued with an AI-generated output without evaluating it very closely? (3) What did that interaction look like? (4) Are there particular types of tasks where this is more likely to happen? (5) What makes you more likely to reduce how much you check an AI response? 

> 14

(6) Do you always see this reduced level of oversight as negative, or can it sometimes be useful or appropriate? 

4. Perceived Consequences and Boundaries. Participants discussed whether reduced oversight matters and how they distinguish prob-lematic reliance from appropriate delegation. (1) Do you see reduced checking or evaluation of AI output as a problem? Why or why not? (2) What, if anything, can be lost when someone accepts AI output without evaluating it carefully? (3) Could this affect things such as learning, understanding, judgment, confidence, ownership, or control over the final outcome? (4) Are there situations where accepting an AI response with little evaluation is completely reasonable or useful? (5) How would you distinguish intentional delegation to an AI system from unintentionally failing to evaluate its output? (6) Does the importance of oversight depend on the task or the consequences of an error? How? 

5. Behavioral Signals and Re-engagement Support. Participants were asked to reflect on what active versus reduced oversight might look like during interaction and how easily they could re-engage. (1) When you enter a period where you are checking the AI less carefully, how long does that usually last? (2) What usually causes you to start paying closer attention again? (3) How much effort does it take for you to re-engage with the task? (4) If someone were observing your interaction, what signs might suggest that you are actively evaluating the AI output? (5) What signs might suggest that you are accepting or moving forward with relatively little evaluation? (6) At what point during an interaction would a reminder to check the AI’s output be most useful? (7) When would such a reminder feel unnecessary, annoying, or too late? 

6. Closing. 

(1) Is there anything else about how you evaluate or rely on AI-generated outputs that we have not discussed? (2) Do you have any concerns or suggestions regarding systems intended to support greater oversight during AI-assisted work? Participants were thanked for their time and participation.
