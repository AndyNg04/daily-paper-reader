A market maker’s objective is the provision of liquidity. To do so, it quotes prices to buy and to sell a given asset, earning the gap between them (spread), and carrying the risk of the position it builds up. We study market makers trained with multi-agent reinforcement learning on prediction markets, specifically Polymarket. These exchanges are characterized by their relatively thin order books whose binary contracts pay exactly 0 or 1 at a known resolution date. Three challenges underpin this research. Firstly, a maker faces adverse selection: the trades it receives come disproportionately from better-informed traders, so a policy trained on real data may learn to stop quoting. The consequences of a given quote may arrive many steps after the action. And prediction markets L2 data does not cover individual orders that a sophisticated simulator may require. We address these challenges by building a pipeline in order to reconstruct LOBSTER data from Polymarket. This data pipeline subsequently allows for the training of various market-making agents in the JaxMARL-HFT environment on 417 real markets. We focus on improving the market making capabilities of the default configuration in JaxMARL-HFT, through enriching the observation space with the book and the recent flow. We additionally make contributions with regard to reward shaping, and crucially propose a novel multi-agent decomposition of the market-making agent into a quoter and a risk manager, enabling a more sophisticated treatment of the inventory risk. We find that decomposition alone performs poorly, while reward designs intended to mitigate multi-agent credit-assignment difficulties substantially improve the resulting desk relative to the unshaped decomposition in our experiments. Our redesigned single-agent market maker quotes more frequently and incurs smaller held-out losses than the JaxMARL-HFT baseline retrained on the same data; the contributions of its jointly changed observations, actions and risk constraints are not separately identified. Our multi-agent decomposition of the seat presents our central result: careful design of the reward split gives us our best result. Our models are scored on realised end-of-episode value on the held-out set of markets. None establishes positive average portfolio value in periodic held-out evaluation, and each configuration was trained with one seed. The environment, data and protocol are the foundation for makers that quote many linked markets at once. Contents

High-frequency trading in financial markets is fundamentally a problem of sequential decision making under uncertainty. Whether the objective is to capture some market-orthogonal return (alpha), provide liquidity to the markets (market making) or allocate capital across different markets based on one’s risk tolerance (portfolio optimisation), permeating all three is a central challenge: prices form endogenously and evolve continuously as a function of the decisions of millions of market participants, each acting on private (and often partial) information. It is in this precise sense that the market price is often referred to as an information aggregator. A market maker supplies liquidity to markets by continuously quoting prices at which it would be willing to buy (bid) and sell (ask) a given asset. In doing so, it seeks to maximise its net profits while simultaneously managing the inventory risk of its filled positions. Given the dynamic nature of financial markets this is a non-trivial challenge, as the price of the asset may move against whatever the maker is holding. With the advent of electronic trading, the move to automate market making has produced a range of approaches, from closed-form stochastic control to learned policies; we review them in section 2. Information aggregation is prominently the value proposition of recently established pre-diction markets: exchanges offering binary, fixed-term contracts on a far wider range of events than the financial system covers. These venues have attracted academic attention in large part due to the novelty of their contractual offering; the term combinatorial arbitrage has emerged for configurations in which two or more contracts are logically constrained by one an-other, and participants appear to exploit such structures to extract substantial edge (Saguillo et al., 2025). Liquidity provision on these venues is independently quite interesting: given the relative thinness of their books, a competent maker is a service the exchange could benefit from (as evidenced by their recent $1 million contribution to rebates for market making on the very markets we are considering (Polymarket, 2026)). This makes market making the natural first problem to study on these novel exchanges. They also publish full historical L2-orderbook data, as well as a breadth of other freely available microstructure data that could ultimately support training foundation market-making models. This study trades a single book at a time, and in doing so lays the foundation for future work on collaborative multi-agent learners that infer and exploit the constraints linking the many books of such a venue (Saguillo et al., 2025; Kroer et al., 2016). Given the structure of the problem, it is unsurprising that reinforcement learning (RL) has been a paradigm shift for this literature. Cast as a partially observable Markov decision process, single-agent RL market making inherits the complexity of the underlying system while introducing its own family of challenges: environment design, reward specification, and often-times degenerate policies. The degeneracy that dominates this environment is the collapse to no trading, in which the agent learns not to quote, since inaction incurs a direct zero-variance return path that is otherwise costly to attain through exploration (Mohl et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). Two further difficulties shape this study. Credit assignment is temporal: a quote posted now has consequences that arrive only when the position it opened is closed, many steps later, thus disentangling the temporal credit assignment problem is non-1trivial. And the environment itself is a modelling decision: simulators that model the market using Brownian mid-prices, fill probabilities, synthetic counterparties, tend to understate adverse selection in ways that may result in an overestimation of performance, widening the Sim2Real gap, a widely acknowledged limitation in the literature (Noble et al., 2026; Lalor and Swishchuk, 2024), while the venues we trade publish only book updates, not the order-level data a faithful replay simulator needs. More recent multi-agent work has shown evidence of adversarial training improving the performance of market-making agents relative to training alone or against static rule-based participants (Spooner and Savani, 2020; Gašperov and Kostanjčar, 2021). In line with Sho-ham et al. (2007), “if multi-agent learning is the answer, what is the question?”, our agenda here is two-fold, both prescriptive and computational: we adopt multi-agent reinforcement learning as an approach to market making in adversarial settings, which in turn requires careful consideration of agent behaviour and learning under complex, partially observable stochastic game (POSG) conditions (Becker and Sunberg, 2024). Building on the novel paradigm of deep reinforcement learning at scale (Lu et al., 2022), we use JaxMARL-HFT (Mohl et al., 2025), a GPU-accelerated multi-agent environment that replays recorded order flow through a full matching engine and cuts end-to-end training time by up to 240 times against the reference implementations they compare to, ABIDES-MARL (Cheridito et al., 2025) and PyMarketSim (Mascioli et al., 2024). The scope of their research was predominantly focused on framework design, while we focus on determining improvements to their baseline market making performance in the adversarial setting. We address two obstacles in particular, noted in their paper. The first is the no-trade collapse, which we treat as a property of the environment design, in particular the reward and the action space, rather than of the learner. The second is the entanglement of quoting and inventory management in one policy, which we disentangle into a cooperative multi-agent desk: one agent quotes continuously, the other manages the quoter’s inventory position, and a reward split we control divides the desk’s profit between them. The aim of the study is to design an environment and setting conducive to legitimate market-making learning across thinly liquid and volatile markets. We use two methods to evaluate our approach. Firstly, our experimental setup scores every model on held-out (i.e. not in training) end-of-episode portfolio value. This is compared against the baseline market maker configuration of JaxMARL-HFT, retrained on the same data. Our configurations are built to isolate the effect of each design choice and thereby gain evaluative insights; what we tried and did not pursue at scale is stated, with justifications, in section 4. Our contributions are: • An enriched observation space , giving the market maker the book and its recent flow rather than the spread and its own inventory (section 3.5). • A wider action space , admitting skewed and one-sided quotes alongside the no-trade action (section 3.6). • Inventory control outside the reward , through a cap and a loss stop rather than 2a penalty, so that the reward does not pay for inaction (section 3.7.3). • A multi-agent decomposition of the market maker into a quoter and a risk man-ager, trained as independent learners, giving inventory risk its own agent and objective (sections 3.6.3 and 4.2.1). • A reward design for the desk : a general-sum split, a blend toward the desk total, and a markout term that charges the quoter for the mid’s move after each fill, which together mitigate the credit-assignment problem the split creates (section 3.7.4). • Order-level data for prediction markets , inferred from Polymarket’s L2 book updates and written in LOBSTER format (Huang and Polak, 2011) (section 5.3). The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the stochastic-control, single-agent and multi-agent literature pertaining to market making. Section 3 defines our problem, including the environment, the state, observation, action and reward spaces. It also includes our own refinements used to address challenges pervasive in the lit-erature. Section 4 presents the learners, single-agent and multi-agent. Section 5 gives the experimental setup alongside assumptions, simulator, data, models and settings and section 6 discusses the outcome of our experimentation. The central market maker problem arises from the endogenous and thereby stochastic nature of market prices. As market makers aim to capture and thereby profit from the bid-ask spread, there is no guarantee of both of their quotes getting filled, which may result in the accumulation of unwanted inventory (Fodra and Labadie, 2012). With adverse price movements in the market there is uncertainty regarding the future price of this held inventory (inventory risk (O’Hara and Oldfield, 1986)) thus requiring careful management of filled positions. The issue is further exacerbated by the adverse selection problem observed by Glosten and Milgrom (1985): informed traders exploit private information unknown to the market maker, and this information asymmetry produces adverse fills as the price rallies in a given direction. 

2.1 Stochastic control problem 

Modelling the theoretical problem of market making as a stochastic dynamic program, Ho and Stoll (1981) began the approach of deriving optimal bid-ask quotes for a monopolistic dealer in a single stock that maximise an objective function: the utility of the market maker’s expected terminal wealth. A significant refinement of this approach was offered by Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008), who combined this utility framework with the microstructure of limit order books. In particular, they leveraged notable results from the econophysics literature pertaining to the size distribution of market orders (Maslov and Mills, 2001; Weber and Rosenow, 2005; Gabaix et al., 2006) and their temporary price impact (Bouchaud et al., 2002; Weber and Rosenow, 2005) in order to model the arrival of buy and sell orders as Poisson processes tied to the market maker’s quotes, founded on Garman (1976). They first compute the agent’s closed-form reservation price, a mid-price adjusted with respect to held inventory to reflect indifference, and then derive closed-form expressions for the optimal bid and ask quotes as distances from that reservation price. Crucially, this framework relies on parametric, memoryless Poisson rate processes, creat-ing a distinct Sim2Real gap. While mathematically tractable, such models inevitably abstract away real-world microstructural features such as queue dynamics, latency and volatility clus-tering, and this reality gap in limit order book simulation leads to distorted execution costs and profit profiles that might trivialize the performance of the market maker in real-world scenarios. (Noble et al., 2026). Lalor and Swishchuk (2024) show the sharper consequence: simulating prices and order flow independently of one another omits endogenous adverse fills, thus inflating apparent profitability of such methods, and performing in an incommensurate manner in live markets. To bridge this gap while preserving mathematical tractability, subsequent literature ex-pounded on the underlying asset dynamics in order to capture more complex, observable market states. Guéant et al. (2013) solved the market making problem under inventory con-straints across arbitrary time horizons using a stochastic control change of variables. Fodra and Labadie (2012) further extended these frameworks to a broader class of mid-price mod-els, including arithmetic Brownian motion with drift and mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck 4processes, under both exponential and linear utility functions, while introducing a terminal inventory-risk penalty. These solutions remain bound to the model that makes them solvable, given that every quote is optimal with respect to the assumed dynamics, not the observed ones. To bypass this bottleneck of restrictive closed-form solutions, the quantitative literature has been converging toward reinforcement learning (Bai et al., 2025). 

2.2 Reinforcement learning 

Chan and Shelton (2001) were the first to formalise market making as a reinforcement learning problem. Their agent’s state includes both its own information such as inventory, as well as market characteristics including the imbalance of buy and sell orders, the bid-ask spread and price changes between trades. Its discretized action space allows the agent to set quote prices and sizes, while its reward mixes profit with an inventory penalty. They populate their simu-lated market with informed and uninformed traders whose orderflow responds to the maker’s quotes, so the elements of the problem we study, namely: partial information, informed flow, and a reward balancing profit against inventory, are all present in that formulation. This laid the foundation for further exploration of RL for MM. Subsequent single-agent literature explores how risk management and different learners within the broad framework established by Chan and Shelton (2001). On the value-based side, Mani et al. (2019) replace a SARSA learner with double SARSA as well as a customised temporal-difference update for the Q-function, reducing the variance of the reward and reach-ing higher profit at lower inventory than the policy of Chan and Shelton (2001). Spooner et al. (2018) leverage tile-coding to discretize the action space and reshape the reward it-self with an asymmetrically dampened profit term paying the agent for providing liquidity rather than for speculating with inventory. This reward is inherited by our baseline con-figuration (section 3.7.1). Selser et al. (2021) instead explore a penalization term for the estimated variance of the agent’s wealth, demonstrating that a deep Q-network under this reward outperforms the closed-form benchmark of Avellaneda and Stoikov (2008). Moving from value-based learners to policy gradients, Guéant and Manziuk (2019) use an actor-critic method for multi-asset market making and scale it to a hundred corporate bonds, a dimen-sionality the partial-differential-equation methods cannot reach (this lays the foundation for future work pertaining to scaled multi-agent systems across combinations of loosely correl-ated prediction market assets). We set out our own reasons for preferring the on-policy policy-gradient family over value-based learning in section 4.1.1. These methods differ as much in their environments as in their learners. Most train inside model-driven simulators, with a Brownian or mean-reverting mid-price with fill probabilities supplied by a model, similar to what was discussed in the previous section, where fills are cheap and adverse selection is a consequence of the rigour of the modelling. On the other hand, on real, replayed order flow fills are disproportionately informed, and a maker trained there often learns to stop quoting altogether, the collapse JaxMARL-HFT documents and that we address in section 3.6.2 (Mohl et al., 2025). Wang et al. (2023) reach a consistent conclusion from the model-driven side: relaxing the standing assumption that a maker quotes both sides continuously and instead allowing it to abstain, or to quote one side only, improves 5returns and risk-adjusted returns. This justifies our explicit no-trade and one-sided actions in our action space for the market maker. (section 3.6). 

2.3 Multi-agent reinforcement learning 

Reinforcement-learning for market making has been extended to several agents in three broad directions (Bai et al., 2025). The first is adversarial: Spooner and Savani (2020) train the maker against an adversary controlling the parameterization of the market model itself, allow-ing the learned policy to become robust to model misspecification; Gašperov and Kostanjčar (2021) let the adversary displace the maker’s quotes directly, reducing inventory risk and improving profit relative to training without one. Spooner and Savani (2020) deliberately choose market dynamics modelled by the optimal-control approach, allowing them to con-duct clean single-stage analysis and avoids the biases of historical data. Our choice is precisely the opposite, we accept the analytical inferiority related to recorded data and instead focus on improving the performance against real flow, given our focus is on real performance. The second direction is competitive: Ganesh et al. (2019) place the learner in a dealer market against competing market makers, showing that it learns their pricing distributions from only their own observations, thereby pricing near-optimally against competitors whose paramet-ers it never sees. The third decomposes the market-making decision: Patel (2018) splits this hierarchically between a macro-agent choosing direction and a micro-agent placing the orders in the book. Our desk (section 3.7.4) is also a decomposition of one seat into two learners, a quoter and a risk manager, but the two act simultaneously as peers on real flow, under a reward split we control, instead of in a hierarchy. The environments these systems train in divide the same way as the single-agent ones, and the division decides what a result means. Agent-based discrete-event simulators, most prominently ABIDES (Byrd et al., 2020) and its reinforcement-learning interface (Amrouni et al., 2021), populate a matching engine with modelled background traders: the market reacts to the agent, which cannot be said for historical replay, but on the other hand every counterparty is synthetic, so what the agent learns is bounded by the quality of the back-ground agent’s imitation of real flow. Optimal-control markets model the dynamics in the manner previously described. JAX-LOB (Frey et al., 2023) takes a different path: it re-plays recorded order flow, order message by order message, through a full price-time-priority matching engine, and JaxMARL-HFT (Mohl et al., 2025) extends this approach to several learning agents, building on JaxMARL (Rutherford et al., 2024). We thus chose to build on JaxMARL-HFT for two reasons. The first is realism: fills come from recorded orders resting in a real orderbook rather than from a fill-probability model or synthetic agent-based counterparties, so the adverse selection faced by our agents is reflective of the markets itself. The second is scale: ascribing to the hyper-scale paradigm recently unlocked by this line of work (Lu et al., 2022; Rutherford et al., 2024), the engine and learner compile as one JAX program, so thousands of books can step in parallel on a single GPU. This is what made our experiment matrix and broader experimentation possible. Within this environment, our taken direction is hitherto untried: a cooperative decompos-ition of the market-making seat itself. In line with the new unlock of reinforcement learning at 6hyper scale, we aim to improve upon the market maker proposed in JaxMARL-HFT, offering a refinement within its simulation environment that might further the research herein. 73 Preliminaries

3.1 Limit order books and binary contracts 

An exchange matches buyers and sellers of a given asset through a limit order book. A limit order names a side (bid/ask), a price and a size, and rests in the book until it gets filled (i.e. trades) or is cancelled. A market order trades immediately against the best resting orders on the opposite side, identical to an immediate-or-cancel (IOC) order, with the added feature that IOC’s will discard any quotes not traded immediately. Limit orders resting at the same price get filled in the order that they arrived, it is in this sense that the book runs under price-time priority. Polymarket uses a central limit order book (CLOB) with a matching engine that uses price-time priority, thus aligning with our own matching engine. At time t

the highest resting buy price is the best bid bt and the lowest resting sell price is the best ask at. Their midpoint mt = ( at + bt)/2 is referred to as the mid price and at − bt is referred to as the spread. Prices move at minimum increments called ticks (these are stated for each market on Polymarket). A market maker quotes a bid and an ask price at the same time. If both fill it earns the distance between them (spread), if only one fills it holds a position that moves with the value of the mid (mark-to-market). Its profit therefore has two parts: the spread captured on completed round trips, and the gain or loss on inventory carried between fills. The second part is the more challenging to manage. As previously established, a fill occurs because another participant chose to trade at the price quoted by the market maker, and the participants timing their trades prior to mid price moves based on private information are those a market maker would like to avoid given the potential for adverse selection(Glosten and Milgrom, 1985). The quality of a fill, and of the market maker’s quoting, therefore is predominantly revealed by the subsequent movement of the mid-price. The contracts offered on Polymarket are binary: each pays one unit if a named event occurs by the resolution date and otherwise pays nothing. It is similar in this sense to a Cash-or-Nothing digital option, except that the trigger condition is not necessarily a function of an underlying asset price. Its price sits in the unit interval with the minimum tick δ as the discrete increment, 

p ∈ { 0, δ, 2δ, . . . , 1} ⊂ [0 , 1] , (1) and reads directly as the market’s implied probability of that event. It resolves at a known deadline to zero or one. One consequence matters: time to resolution counts as much as price, given time-decay characterises these markets. 83.2 The decision model 

Market making is a sequential decision problem under uncertainty. At each step t the envir-onment holds a state st, the agent receives the observation ot, choosing an action at. The environment accordingly returns a reward rt and then updates its state to st+1 .In our context, the agent does not see the state. Since the state contains every resting order, every market participants’ intentions, and the private information that the other mar-ket participants act on, while the agent sees the book and other quantities computed from it. Each learner is trained under the single-agent view of the market, thus it is in this sense that we model our learning as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) (Kael-bling et al., 1998). The agent’s observations are obtained by treating the other participants as part of the environment, in that the observations only hold a partial view of the entire market state. Thus, the agent cannot act on exclusively ot. Instead, rather than using the full history ht = ( o0, . . . , o t), our policy conditions on a recurrent summary of it, which consists of a learned approximation of the distribution over states implied by the history. Given the other learners are simultaneously adapting to each other’s policies, the environment faced by the agents is not stationary. The partial observability here is inherent to the endogenous price formation in markets. The information asymmetry that generates the order flow is itself the unobserved part of the state, and inferring how informed the flow arriving now is precisely the substance of the market maker’s problem. This holds against the replayed participants but not within our desk configuration since the two agents of section 3.5.1 are given each other’s positions by construction. Forcing the agent to close its position at every step would make each step’s profit self-contained and restore a memoryless problem, but it would also inevitably restrict the set of policies that the agent can represent, and the best policy of the unrestricted problem, which holds a position while it works, may not lie inside the restricted set. We therefore let inventory carry across steps, thus requiring a more careful attention to the temporal credit assignment challenge: a quote placed now has consequences that appear only when the resulting position closes, possibly many steps later. Given our context, we clear the recurrent summary at every episode boundary. In partic-ular, this is done given that we have modelled each episode as one market window, and the environment subsequently draws the next window at random instead of continuing on the same market, so clearing ensures that the recurrent state does not carry over a history from an unrelated market.

3.4 State space 

The state collects two parts: a shared description of the market, and a private record for each agent. The shared part is the state described in Frey et al. (2023): both sides of the resting orderbook, each as an array of orders carrying a price, size, timestamp and an identifier for the trader who placed it. The trades executed during the step, the episode’s start time and the current clock, a counter that issues identifiers to new orders, and finally the index of the market being traded together with the step counter. JaxMARL-HFT extend this with three quantities a market maker needs: the path of the best bid and ask after every message of the step, the mid price, and the amount of time that has been covered by the steps. Our own addition is smaller and serves the desk: four fields through which an agent type can read the other agent’s position, which is used by section 3.5.1. The private part, per agent, holds the signed inventory, the cash balance, the running profit, the volume-weighted price at which the open position was entered, the distances of the agent’s own resting quotes from the mid, and two short histories used to build features, recent mid prices, and a per-step bar of open, high, low, close, volume and trade imbalance. The desk adds a table of the open positions: each row carrying a signed size, the price it was opened at, and its age in steps. 10 Everything that changes the book arrives as a message,

3.6 Action space 

The design of the action space is non-trivial as it fixes the range of possible policies available to the agent. A discretized space of possible quote placements, for instance, can only express 12 policies built from those placements. This implies that learning can then converge only to the best of that constricted action space. In contrast, a continuous space of offsets (against the mid) allows the exploration of a broader set of candidate policies: one that may contain the game’s global optimum (subject to the constraints of the problem and the observations). As such, a richer action space is more likely to contain a globally superior policy evaluated on portfolio value, but the cost incurred is learning, in particular that a larger space needs more interaction data and makes approximation much more challenging. We thus settled on two choices inside this trade-off. Firstly, we kept the action space discrete. The reasoning for this choice was two-fold. Firstly, the large size of our observation space paired with a continuous action space would have likely made the learning intractable, which corroborates the pervasive use of discretization in the literature. Secondly, with a wider and sufficiently granular discretization (namely tick-level), which is afforded to us by the abundance of Polymarket data and the JAX accelerated framework, we can attain a comparatively rich action space without requiring full continuity.

In JaxMARL-HFT, the trained market maker selects the no-trade action between 61.1% and 97.7% of the time across three seeds (Mohl et al., 2025). We refer to this as the no-trade collapse. We decide to keep this action row for three reasons. Firstly, standing aside is part of the market making role and should thus be reflected in the action space of our agent. Even in exchanges that have formally imposed market making obligations, this is legally permissible: the London Stock Exchange requires registered market makers to quote for at least 90% of continuous trading during the mandatory period, and Deutsche Börse’s cash market requires quotes during 50% of daily trading hours on a monthly average, with the requirement lifted in defined exceptional conditions (Wang et al., 2023). Our exchange has no regulation for market makers at all, so our context is especially validated. Secondly, removing this action does not change the underlying behavior leading to the no-trade collapse. If inaction is what the reward rewards, the policy will simply search for inaction out of whatever the action space provides. We deleted the row from the baseline and it did exactly that, moving most of its action mass onto the widest symmetric rows and quoting several ticks outside a book two ticks wide, where nothing fills — an observation from our preliminary experimentation. Thirdly, the reward design is particularly responsible for making inaction a desirable policy JaxMARL-HFT attribute part of the collapse in their own agent to the inventory penalty, which charges the agent for the size of the position it holds. That charge is smallest when the position is zero and thus causes a term intended to control risk to instead result in a reward for inaction (Mohl et al., 2025). The no-trade collapse is therefore a reward problem that we aim to address in section 3.7.

3.7.2 Where the basic reward falls short 

The form is simple and matches the real-world objective, but contains three properties that encourage the agent to converge to policies we do not want. • It is dominated by price noise. The revaluation term follows the step-to-step movement of the mid and thus the variance of this unrealised PnL term grows with the position. This causes the reward to be dominated by short term price fluctuations that dampen the valid signal telling the agent whether it quoted appropriately or not. 15 • It does not charge for inventory magnitude. Nothing in eq. (11) costs the agent anything for holding a large position beyond the price moving against it, so inventory can accumulate until the mid price move is significant, causing greater reward volatility. • It mishandles temporal credit assignment. The spread capture is rewarded in eq. (10), yet the identical action’s temporal consequences a few steps out are inside Φ t.With incommensurate weights, the agent may become more averse to risks taken in the present given it is not sufficiently rewarded for the consequences of the action a few steps later. Thus, it naturally may collapse into "No Trade". The standard remedy for the second problem, an inventory penalty, further exacerbates the other two stated problems. By charging the agent for held inventory, the agent during learning may opt to drive that charge predictably to zero as a possible policy. The mid moves on almost every step, so the revaluation term is noisy provided the agent holds any inventory. Therefore a policy that quotes anything at all would receive both that noisy mark-to-market and be further exacerbated by the penalty: encouraging the policy to stand aside.

A single agent holding one reward is required to trade off the quality of their quoting against the feedback on their inventory carry (often referred to as unrealised PnL). Given the two are measured in the same reward, the gradient update cannot separate the two practically different objectives/terms. Thus, splitting the desk gives each agent a smaller problem with a cleaner objective, making the problem both more tractable and closer to the intuitive separation of the roles. Rewarding both agents in a common-sum manner following the same single-agent reward would detract from the diverging policies and feedback we wish the respective agents to leverage. Yet rewarding each of them exclusively for spread capture and unrealized PnL would equally create moral hazard problems. namely, for instance a quoter rewarded for spread capture reinforces quoting without accounting for the quality of the position, with the cost of the carry accruing exclusively to the risk manager.

Section 3 defined our problem including the spaces, rewards, the desk and reasoning behind those design choices. With this requisite information covered, this section covers the learners trained on it, and justifies our design choices. We retain JaxMARL-HFT’s actor-critic foundation, including its PPO update rule and optimiser. Our changes predominantly lie in the problem definition as expounded in the preliminaries section. We also add an auxiliary prediction head (for the mid price move-ment). We maintain the same optimization such that improvements can be attributed to the design of the problem rather than a better-tuned learner. We also keep the recurrent trunk of their architecture as it stands, given the gated recurrent unit (GRU) performs comparably to similar alternatives (long short-term memory (LSTM)) on these tasks while carrying signific-antly fewer parameters and costing less per step (Chung et al., 2014; Cho et al., 2014). This decision was made in favour of allowing for more rapid iterations of experiments. We favour computational efficiency since our focus is once again on the problem design as a potential solution. 

4.1 Single-agent architecture 

4.1.1 Why a policy gradient 

Tabular learning would evidently be an intractable given the size of the observation and action spaces. As such, we first considered a value-based learner, given these methods are pervasive in the market-making literature, from the tile-coded temporal difference learners of Spooner et al. (2018) onward. A deep value-based learner uses an underlying neural network that takes the observation history as input and outputs an estimated action value for each possible action, trained to match a target built from the reward actually received plus its own estimate of the best next action’s value, the policy acts greedily on the estimates, exploring with probability ϵ. We chose not to use this approach for the following reasons: • The deadly triad. We want to avoid the deadly triad of reinforcement learning: the combination of function approximation, bootstrapped targets and off-policy maxim-isation (Sutton and Barto, 2018). A deep Q-network in this context would have all three components. Its target yt = rt + γ max a′ Q(st+1 , a ′; θ) contains the network’s own estimate of the best next action, and a parameter update can subsequently raise that estimate for an action the policy never actually takes. Never visited, the overestimation is never corrected, its value can diverge, and the divergence spreads into every other estimate that uses it as part of its target. We therefore chose an on-policy method: tar-gets are built from the actions the current policy takes, so an overestimation is corrected once the pair is visited again. • Catastrophic forgetting. The observations a learner sees in this environment are partly generated by its current policy, thereby shifting the distribution of its experience as its policy changes. This is particularly salient with our fixed-time markets given that the optimal policy halfway through the market’s life is different to the optimal 19 policy close to resolution (where time-to-resolution dominates the value). A network fitted to the most recent experiences then overwrites what it learned from earlier ex-periences, a term denoted as catastrophic forgetting (Sutton and Barto, 2018). Unlike policy-gradient methods, value-based learning is more exposed to this issue given that its targets are bootstrapped from a function approximator, so updates on visited ob-servations also shift the targets of unvisited ones. Training many market windows in parallel, each at a different point of a different market’s life, mitigates this risk for our on-policy method. • Expressiveness. An ϵ-greedy policy commits to one action per observation and spreads the remaining probability uniformly over the rest of the actions for exploration. A policy-gradient method instead parametrises the policy directly and can represent any distribution over the quote table, This matters firstly because the optimal policy may genuinely be stochastic given the latent nature of order-flow toxicity. Secondly, because its exploration will be concentrated around its current policy carrying a lower real monetary cost in a live market relative to a random uniform exploration (from 

ϵ-greedy). The drawback we accept from this approach is added variance to the gradient estimates. 

4.1.2 Proximal policy optimisation with a recurrent policy 

Proximal policy optimisation (Schulman et al., 2017) is a refinement of the actor-critic frame-work: an actor , the parameterised policy, chooses actions, and a critic estimates the value of an observation, thereby reinforcing an action outcome based on its advantage ˆAt (the return obtained relative to the critic’s estimate). A single gradient step on the policy can substantially change it and thereby degrade performance. Trust-region methods (Schulman et al., 2015) addressed this limitation by constraining each update to a region wherein the policy changes little, but this affected com-putational efficiency given a constrained optimisation (or a penalty term) had to be applied at each step. PPO therefore refined this by replacing the constraint with a clipped surrogate ob-jective. Each sample is weighted by the importance ratio ρt(ϕ) = π(at | ht; ϕ) / π (at | ht; ϕold ), of the current policy relative to the previous policy. Clipping the ratio to [1 − ϵ, 1 + ϵ] and taking the minimum of the two terms bounds how far any update can move the policy.

Its prediction error is backpropagated into the shared layers such that the embedding and GRU learn to encode the information that is predictive of mid price movement (which is fed into the actor and the critic). In the configurations noted in section 5.5 the forecast is also appended to the actor’s input, so that the policy can condition on it directly. 21 4.2 Multi-agent proximal policy optimisation

4.2.1 Independent versus central learning 

A central learner instead treats all agents as a single decision maker choosing a joint action. It largely circumvents the challenges of non-stationarity and credit assignment that arise in independent learning. We rejected it given that the joint action space would be the product of the individual action spaces of the quoter and the risk manager, which would become computationally intractable with our stated action spaces in section 3.6.3. Independence keeps each agent’s action space rich (section 3.6.1) and lets the design scale, at two costs. • Non-stationarity. A central learner controls a joint policy and thereby does not face the specific non-stationarity issue faced by the independent learner. Namely, an independent learner’s environment contains the other agents, whose policies change as they learn. This can produce cyclic co-adaptation whereby an agent changes its policy to adjust for the other agent’s policy, and in turn the other agents change their policies in response, and so on. This therefore removes any convergence guarantee and could lead to instability during learning. We opt not to correct for it given that JaxMARL-HFT reports convergence for market-making and execution agents in this setting (Mohl et al., 2025). Although real markets are themselves non-stationary, the source here is simultaneous policy adaptation and should not be interpreted as a faithful model of market adaptation. The remaining risk is that agents co-adapt to a few learned participants rather than the whole order flow; rectifying this is left for future work. • Credit assignment. Temporal credit assignment is already hard for one agent as has 22 been widely addressed in the previous sections. Multiple agents add the question of whose actions earned the reward (Foerster et al., 2018). A central learner can condition value estimates on the joint action, allowing outcomes associated with one agent’s action to be distinguished across different actions of its partner and thereby providing richer information for multi-agent credit assignment. Independent learners do not explicitly represent these joint-action values (as described in section 3.7.4), and this also motiv-ated the general-sum split of section 3.7.4 as well as the isolated rewards of the markout and exit credit. Independent learners could be given communication actions (Foerster et al., 2016) to explicitly model collaboration, but these would widen the action space and add a further layer of credit assignment given that an agent must infer the meaning of a received message and learn which message to send, which would require a further layer of reward design complexity (to ensure rewarded communication actions are dis-entangled from rewards earned through the agent’s own decisions). Instead each agent observes a block describing its partner (section 3.5.1) and infers coordination from what the partner’s actions did. The environment is our implicit communication channel as previously stated. The market maker shares its book with a learned execution agent that optimizes the execution of a scheduled order (inherited from (Mohl et al., 2025)), amounting to a mixed-motive game. We chose to keep this because an adapting learner is a more demanding opponent than replayed historical flow or a zero-intelligence agent and thereby may robustify our agents’ policies. 23 5 Experimental Setup 

This section instantiates the problem described in section 3 and the method in section 4 as a concrete experiment. 

5.1 Market assumptions 

We build on JaxMARL-HFT and inherit its market assumptions along with their subsequent limitations. This results in the following limitations acknowledged below: • No transaction costs. No fee is charged for trading which is at odds with the venue’s published rules. This was chosen in order to keep the scope of the research tractable. • Non-reactive flow. While the matching engine matches historical orders against the quotes of the agent(s), the recorded market participants do not behaviorally respond to the agents’ actions. Specifically, the endogenous behavioural response to another market participants (the agents) is absent in the historical messages: no market participant is re-pricing, withdrawing or changing its behaviour as a function of what our agents quote. This is tantamount to the assumption of negligible price impact in the market which simplifies our simulation. The execution agent partly remedies this, since it is the one participant that does continually adapts to the agents’ quotes. • The reconstructed book’s queue composition is an assumption of the data ,not something the data records. This is expounded upon in section 5.3. 

5.2 The simulation environment 

Section 3.3 described the simulator, this section fixes the quantities our experiments run it at, collected in table 1. An episode corresponds to the replay of one five-minute market from its recorded mes-sages, ending at the markets resolution. Each step advances 400 recorded messages, and an episode is 250 steps, so the stretch of recorded messages it replays (referred to as its window), is 100,000 messages long. The agents’ own messages are inserted among these and do not count toward the window. Given that a step is a fixed number of messages rather than a fixed duration, the market time an episode covers depends on how message-dense the market is. A typical held-out episode spans roughly its final two minutes. Training runs 64 parallel environments (independent copies of the simulator that take simultaneous steps and pool their transitions into a single update). Each environment then draws its next window at random from the pool of training markets. Held-out markets are never seen in training (to prevent leakage). 5.3.1 Market choice and split The instrument. We trade Polymarket’s BTC up/down five-minute binary markets. 5.3.2 From book updates to order messages 

The simulator requires market-by-order (MBO) level data (every individual order carrying an identifier). Polymarket only publishes and collects L2-orderbook data (the total resting size at each price level). We decided to remain within this framework and infer MBO data from the L2 data. Since the exchange itself only publishes and stores L2 historical orderbook data (and no MBO), this tradeoff was appropriate given the matching engine’s realism and the speed with which it runs on the hardware. 

Reconstruction. The raw L2 data (the full L2 order-book at every order-book update stamped in milliseconds) was obtained from PMXT, a data provider for prediction markets. In order to validate the integrity of their data, although its archive has already been used in published research (Nechepurenko, 2026), we separately validated its historical data against Polymarket’s own API websockets and found the two to be consistent. From timestamped L2 data one can infer an order arrival when a level’s total size rises, and an order departure when the size falls, while an accompanying trade print allows us to differentiate the order departures between cancels and fills. Since the simulator requires the individual orders behind each total (each message is an order), we synthesize them using this inference detailed above. Each observed increase becomes one order message carrying the observed time, price and size, so the output stream is the recorded feed re-encoded as orders, and the reconstructed totals equal the reported totals throughout. Only the order identities are invented, and they matter for queue position. Price-time priority fixes two of the three update rules exactly: an arrival joins the back of its queue, and a fill consumes the front. The one assumption is the cancellation: which order (in the queue) is cancelled is not in the data and with nothing to favour one position we remove one drawn uniformly at random. This is a limitation of the work, though not a departure from the simulator’s own practice: JaxLOB initialises each window from an L2 snapshot by treating every level as a single order of the summed volume (Frey et al., 2023), the same kind of assumption applied to the opening book rather than to every cancellation. A more faithful reconstruction is left to future work. The output is written as LOBSTER message and order-book file pairs (Huang and Polak, 2011) with the message vector of eq. (2), commensurate with the requirements of the (Frey et al., 2023) engine.

Given the differing reward functions and configurations across models, we cannot directly compare the performance of the models directly against their rewards. Instead, we opt for evaluating each model on a proxy metric that reflects the performance of the market-maker at the end of each market: end-of-episode portfolio value , the cash a model holds once an episode ends and any remaining position is closed. JaxMARL-HFT report average end-of-episode portfolio value as a training curve. We include this, and additionally score our models on mean end-of-episode portfolio value measured on held-out windows (i.e. our validation windows) to compare the performance of our models against unseen data. • Level and settling. We evaluate held-out portfolio value every fifth update over 64 held-out windows. We report the mean of the last five evaluations as the model’s conclusive level, and the standard deviation of the last ten serves to indicate the stability of the training process. Training curves are included where the learning process itself is of interest. • No-trade share. Portfolio value is trivially improved by not trading as previously described. We therefore report alongside every portfolio value the share of steps on which the model selects the no-trade action, and treat an improvement accompanied by a large rise in that share as withdrawal from trading rather than successful learning. • Reward decomposition. Portfolio value is separated into spread capture and profit on held inventory. This directly reflects whether a model’s quoting or its carry determ-ines its result. • Distribution across windows. For our desk models, we additionally evaluate their final policy deterministically on held-out windows and report the resulting distribution of end-of-episode portfolio value. This allows for a more granular evaluation of the performance of our design choices. These methods collectively give us an understanding of the consequences of our design choices expounded in the previous sections. Each model is run once at a fixed seed, so every dispersion is across windows rather than seeds (section 6.4). 30 6 Results

6.4 Limitations 

The shortcomings of the method and of the data are the following: 

The counterparty. Every learned configuration trains against a learned execution agent, which makes the environment non-stationary from the market maker’s perspective and means the comparisons hold under that setting rather than against replayed flow alone. A control without the execution agent was staged but cut from the scope of the paper. 

The data. One instrument family on one venue, four training days and a held-out day two weeks later. While our holdout is far later and prevents leakage, the breadth across instruments, regimes and venues is untested. 36 The evaluation. Inference is over held-out windows, not over training seeds: every reported configuration is a single seed. Since Deep RL is notoriously seed-sensitive (Henderson et al., 2018), this is noted as a limitation and a possible avenue of future work. 

The reconstructed book. Although inferred order-level messages reproduce reported aggregate depth at each update, order identities and queue positions are synthetic. Fill dynamics therefore depend on the reconstruction assumptions and cannot be equated with observed historical execution. 37 7 Conclusion 

Our study brings multi-agent reinforcement learning to market making on prediction markets. On extracted Polymarket data, we refined (Mohl et al., 2025)’s single-book market maker with book-and-flow observations, a less restrictive action space with explicit no-trade and one-sided actions, and various reward designs. We further introduced a cooperative decomposition of the market maker problem into two seats: a quoter and a risk manager, designing appropriate rewards to mitigate multi-agent credit assignment challenges. Every configuration was scored on held-out end-of-episode portfolio value and other metrics, evaluated against the published market maker (Mohl et al., 2025) which was retrained on the same data at a matched update budget. Our results support four insights: • The redesigned single-seat configuration improves held-out results. WIDE_SPOONER 

finishes at −9.6 ticks per episode against −46 .1 for PAPER_BASE , reaches its level in a quarter of the budget, and settles at a sixth of the baseline’s spread. The gain is not bought by withdrawal: it selects the no-trade action on 4 .4% of steps against the baseline’s 19 .2%, so controlling inventory outside the reward prevents the no-trade col-lapse without paying for inaction. • The desk decomposition is successful when properly designed. The bare split is the worst configuration in the paper at −72 .3 ticks, below the baseline, because the quoter is paid for fills whose losses land on the risk manager. The blend partly repairs this ( −41 .0), the markout largely ( −5.9), and their combination under the curriculum gives the best result we have ( −4.3), in the order the reward design predicted. • The repairs act disproportionately on the tail. Losing held-out windows fall from 45 of 48 under the bare split and 39 under the blend to 6 and 8 under the markout configurations, which finish positive in aggregate (+133 and +67 ticks over the 48). These deterministic-window aggregates do not establish positive expected profitability, and all configurations retain negative mean portfolio value under periodic held-out evaluation. The curriculum does not reduce the tail further. Its contribution is stability. • Independent learning did not destabilise the desk. Every adequately rewarded configuration converges, and the better configurations are also the settled ones: spread falls from 19 .4 to 3 .1 ticks across the non-desk comparison and from 17 .6 to 1 .7 across the desk ladder. This study is built to be continued, and several directions follow directly from it: • Cross-market collaboration. The venue lists many markets whose prices are logic-ally constrained by one another as mentioned in the related work section. A collab-orative desk quoting several linked books at once, leveraging the design seen in this implementation, is an interesting avenue of research. • Richer multi-agent system Giving risk manager access to other correlated instru-ments to hedge the risk of the market making agent. 38 • Training Foundation Model. Training a foundation model using the abundance of available Polymarket data together with the GPU-accelerated framework. This would allow us thereafter to investigate possible fine-tuning strategies for specific markets or types of markets. • Policy self-play and adversarial curricula. Leveraging policy self-play in order to investigate whether this results in more robust, generalizable policies and faster learning would complement well with the previous two points.
